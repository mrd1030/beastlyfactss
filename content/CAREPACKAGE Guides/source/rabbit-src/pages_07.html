Glossary, A to G

APOPThe Association for Pet Obesity Prevention, a nonprofit whose rabbit body condition chart is the 9-point scale on page {{P:growth}}.
Awake inactivitySitting still while awake for long stretches. A welfare measure rather than a mood, and associated in the research with solitary or confined housing. Page {{P:enrichment}}.
BinkyThe midair leap and twist, all four feet off the ground, of a very happy rabbit. Page {{P:behavior}}.
Bonded pairTwo rabbits that have been properly introduced and now live together, groom each other and rest in contact. Page {{P:company}}.
BruxismTooth grinding. Soft and quiet means a contented rabbit, like a cat's purr. Harsh and audible means pain, and the two are easy to tell apart once you have heard both. Pages {{P:behavior}}, {{P:signs}}.
CecotropesSoft, dark, shiny, clustered droppings, like a bunch of grapes, produced through the day and night and eaten directly from the source. A genuine part of digestion, not a problem. Page {{P:droppings}}.
CecumThe fermentation chamber after the small intestine, where gut bacteria break down fiber. It is why a rabbit is called a hindgut fermenter. Page {{P:diet}}.
Celsius (°C)The metric temperature scale, shown beside Fahrenheit throughout. A comfortable room is 16 to 22°C. Page {{P:temperature}}.
Centimeter (cm)A metric length, one hundredth of a meter and about 0.4 in. Teeth grow about 1 cm a month. Page {{P:dental}}.
CorticosteroneA stress hormone. A study measured it in rabbit droppings to show how run access and space change stress. Page {{P:enrichment}}.
CrepuscularMost active at dawn and dusk. It is why permanent run access beats an hour scheduled for the household's convenience. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
Culture and sensitivity testA lab test that grows bacteria from a swab to identify the germ and show which antibiotics work against it. Page {{P:flystrike}}.
DewlapThe fold of skin under the chin, larger in does. A permanently damp dewlap points at drooling, and so at dental disease. Pages {{P:growth}}, {{P:dental}}.
Doe and buckAn adult female and an adult male rabbit. Pages {{P:hay}}, {{P:neuter}}.
E. cuniculiShort for Encephalitozoon cuniculi, a parasite many pet rabbits carry silently. When active it can cause head tilt. A vet can test for it and treat it. Page {{P:conditions}}.
EnterotoxemiaToxins flooding the bloodstream after certain antibiotics wipe out the gut bacteria and a toxin-producing Clostridium takes over. It can kill within 48 hours. Page {{P:vaccine}}.
Fahrenheit (°F)The temperature scale used first in this package. A comfortable room is 61 to 72°F, and about 80°F is the heat line. Page {{P:temperature}}.
Fecal pelletsThe round, dry, brown droppings you see in the litter box, made mostly of undigested fiber. Page {{P:droppings}}.
FenbendazoleA deworming drug. A 28-day course is the treatment for an active E. cuniculi infection. Page {{P:conditions}}.
FlystrikeAlso called myiasis: fly eggs laid in soiled fur hatching into maggots that eat into the skin. Can kill within 24 to 48 hours. Page {{P:flystrike}}.
Foot (ft)A length of 12 inches, about 30 cm. Used for pen and enclosure sizes. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
Friendship indexA measure of how close two rabbits are, used in the companionship study. It fell as the age gap between pairs widened. Page {{P:company}}.
GI (gastrointestinal)Relating to the stomach and intestines, the digestive tract. Page {{P:stasis}}.
GI stasisThe gut slowing or stopping. The most common life-threatening emergency in pet rabbits, and the reason for the 8-to-12-hour rule. Pages {{P:stasis}}, {{P:signs}}.
Gram (g)A metric weight, one thousandth of a kilogram, the unit a kitchen scale shows. The weekly weight log is kept in grams. Page {{P:growth}}.
Gram-positive bacteriaA large group of bacteria that includes most of a rabbit's gut bacteria. Antibiotics aimed at them can wipe out the gut flora. Page {{P:vaccine}}.

Glossary, H to Z

Hemolytic anemiaA shortage of red blood cells because they are destroyed faster than they are made. Onion, garlic and chives can cause it. Page {{P:treats}}.
Hindgut fermenterAn animal that digests fiber in the cecum, after the small intestine. It is why the gut must keep moving and why fiber is not optional. Pages {{P:diet}}, {{P:hay}}.
Inch (in)A length of 2.54 cm. Used for sizes and for how fast teeth grow. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
IUCNThe International Union for Conservation of Nature, which keeps the global list of threatened species. The wild European rabbit is listed as Endangered. Page {{P:profile}}.
Kilogram (kg)A metric weight of about 2.2 lb. Water intake is figured per kilogram of body weight. Page {{P:treats}}.
KitA baby rabbit. Page {{P:diet}}.
Lumbar spineThe lower back. An unsupported, struggling rabbit can fracture it with its own hind legs. Page {{P:handling}}.
Meter (m) and square meter (m²)A metric length of about 3.3 ft, and the metric measure of floor area, 1 m² being about 10.8 sq ft. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
Milliliter (mL)A metric volume, one thousandth of a liter, about a fifth of a teaspoon. A rabbit drinks about 120 mL per kilogram a day. Page {{P:treats}}.
Millimeter (mm)A metric length, one tenth of a centimeter. An incisor with nothing to wear against can grow 1 mm a day. Page {{P:hay}}.
Molar spurA sharp point on an overgrown back tooth that cuts the tongue or cheek. Not visible from the front, which is why a vet needs X-rays and sedation to see it. Page {{P:dental}}.
MotilityThe movement of food through the gut. When it slows, gas builds up and the bacteria shift: GI stasis. Page {{P:stasis}}.
OvariectomySurgical removal of the ovaries, the spay operation, possible as a simple procedure in a rabbit under a year old. Page {{P:neuter}}.
Pasteurella multocidaA bacterium most adult rabbits carry without trouble, and the usual cause of snuffles when stress or poor ventilation lets it take hold. Page {{P:flystrike}}.
PDSAThe People's Dispensary for Sick Animals, a United Kingdom veterinary charity, cited on the Sources page for flystrike.
PododermatitisSore hocks: pressure sores on the underside of the hind feet, caused by wire or hard flooring, or obesity. Page {{P:conditions}}.
Pound (lb)A weight of about 0.45 kg. Body weights and food portions are given per pound. Page {{P:greens}}.
RHDV2Rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus type 2. Highly contagious, frequently fatal, and carried on food, water and anything else it contaminates, so indoor rabbits are not automatically safe. A vaccine protects against it. Page {{P:vaccine}}.
RSPCAThe Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, a United Kingdom animal welfare charity, cited on the Sources page for housing, behavior, health and travel.
SnufflesUpper respiratory infection, most often Pasteurella multocida. Look for crusted front paws as much as for a runny nose. Page {{P:flystrike}}.
Square foot (sq ft)A measure of floor area, 1 sq ft being about 0.09 m². The space standard is given in it. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
Styptic powderA powder that stops bleeding from a nail trimmed too short. Keep it open before you start. Page {{P:molt}}.
Tablespoon (tbsp)A cooking measure of about 15 mL. A week's treats are 1 to 2 tbsp. Page {{P:treats}}.
TrancingFlipping a rabbit onto its back until it goes still. Measured heart rate and stress hormones show this is fear, not relaxation. Don't. Page {{P:handling}}.
Uterine adenocarcinomaA cancer of the uterus. In some strains, 80% of unspayed does develop it by 3 years of age, which is why spaying is near-routine. Pages {{P:conditions}}, {{P:neuter}}.
VCAVCA Animal Hospitals, a United States veterinary hospital network that began as Veterinary Centers of America, cited on the Sources page.
X-penA foldable metal exercise pen. The current standard for rabbit housing, in place of a cage or hutch. Page {{P:enclosure}}.

Veterinary manuals and clinical references

  • Merck Veterinary Manual: management of rabbits; housing of rabbits; nutrition of rabbits; bacterial and mycotic diseases of rabbits; and the pet owner page on selecting a rabbit. The 61 to 72°F range and the 150 temperature-plus-humidity limit, water intake, maturity by breed size, sexing, the uterine cancer rationale for spaying, the culture before snuffles treatment, the E. cuniculi test at the first exam, the 3-hour fasting limit and the 2 to 3 hour eating bar after surgery, and the healthy-rabbit checks.
  • VCA Animal Hospitals (VCA, originally Veterinary Centers of America): health problems in rabbits; dental disease in rabbits; gastrointestinal stasis in rabbits. The 80°F ceiling, bladder stones, sore hocks, tooth growth, the 4 to 12 week dental schedule, and why clippers must never be used on teeth.
  • PetMD: gastrointestinal stasis in rabbits, for the eight-hour figure, the roughly 70% survival rate and the three to five day recovery; and the Flemish giant rabbit page for giant breed size.
  • Lafeber Company: tips for grooming your rabbit, for the 6 to 8 week nail and scent gland interval. North Carolina State University College of Veterinary Medicine: caring for your pet rabbit, for bowl versus bottle and the sore chin.
  • Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (RSPCA): rabbit environment, health, behavior, bonding, transport and holiday care pages. Cleaning, twice-daily rear-end checks, breed risks, body language and leaving a rabbit with a sitter. People's Dispensary for Sick Animals (PDSA): fly strike in rabbits, for what to do and not do.
  • Association for Pet Obesity Prevention (APOP): rabbit body condition score chart. Avian and Exotic Animal Clinic: the RHDV2 vaccine, for the starting age, interval and side effects.

Research

  • Companionship counts: investigating social housing conditions and welfare in privately owned rabbits, Applied Animal Behaviour Science. A study of run access, hutch size and time of day and their effects on behavior and stress hormone levels in pair-housed pet rabbits, Applied Animal Behaviour Science.
  • A study of single versus paired housing and rabbit body temperature and behavior in a shelter in the United Kingdom, Animal Welfare. A study of environmental enrichments and the behavior of growing rabbits housed in collective pens.
  • Bosco-Lauth and colleagues (2024), Viruses: vaccination against rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus 2 using a baculovirus recombinant vaccine provides durable immunity in rabbits. The 12-month booster and the trial deaths.
  • Irving-Pease and Larson (2018), Trends in Ecology and Evolution: the correction of the story that rabbits were domesticated by decree.

Husbandry references
