Diet by age

The diet changes twice: once off alfalfa, and once into old age.
StageFeeding
Nursing kits
birth to about 7 weeks		Mother's milk, and she nurses just once, rarely twice, a day for 2 to 10 minutes. Rabbit milk is unusually rich, so kits can go roughly a day between feeds. A nest left alone is normal, not abandoned. Weaned by around 8 weeks.
Transition

Juvenile
7 weeks to about 6 months		Unlimited alfalfa hay and alfalfa-based pellets: the extra calories and calcium support growth. Vegetables are added gradually from about 3 months, one new item at a time.
Changeover
roughly 4 to 7 months		Alfalfa is phased out in favor of grass hays, and pellet portions are reduced gradually over several weeks as unlimited grass hay takes over as the base of the diet. Do this slowly.
Adult
7 months to about 6 years		Unlimited grass hay at 80 to 85% of the diet. Measured pellets, roughly 1/8 to 1/4 cup of high-fiber, timothy-based pellets per 5 to 6 lb (2.3 to 2.7 kg) of body weight daily. At least 3 varieties of leafy greens a day (page {{P:greens}}). Treats capped at about 1 to 2 tbsp, once or twice a week.
Senior
about 6 years and up		Unlimited grass hay continues. Alfalfa may come back if the rabbit is underweight, the treat allowance is higher, and feeding becomes individual and vet-guided rather than by the table.
Fresh water at every stage Water must be available at all times, by bowl or bottle. Which is better is on page {{P:treats}}.
Change food slowly, always There is no gut-loading or calcium dusting here, that is a reptile practice. The rabbit equivalent of getting it wrong is changing food too fast. A sudden switch of hay, pellets or vegetables upsets the gut bacteria in the cecum, the fermentation chamber, and can bring soft stool or a stasis episode. Make every change over a week or two.
The one figure to hold on to Past 8 hours without food or droppings, a rabbit should be seen by a vet. At 12 hours or more it is an emergency, so 8 is when you make the call and 12 is when you are already late. Fasting beyond about 24 hours risks fatty liver disease. Same-day vet contact is the safe default the moment a rabbit skips a meal or stops producing normal droppings, at any age (page {{P:signs}}).
Section 03 · Feeding

Hay: the base of the diet

If you take one thing from this package, take this page.

Grass hay should be 80 to 85% of what an adult rabbit eats, available 24 hours a day, never rationed. It does two jobs at once, and both are the jobs that kill rabbits when they are not done.

Job one: the gut

Rabbits are hindgut fermenters, and the gut has to keep moving. Long-strand fiber is what moves it. Low fiber is the biggest risk factor for GI stasis (page {{P:stasis}}).

Job two: the teeth

Teeth grow about 1 cm (0.4 in) a month, and an incisor with nothing to wear against can grow 1 millimeter (mm) a day. Grinding long hay wears them down. Pellets are concentrated and do not do this.

Making hay take time

Hay is also the cheapest enrichment here, and presentation matters. Stuff it into cardboard tubes, hang it in bundles, scatter greens through it, and keep a rack over the litter box so eating and toileting happen in the same corner, which is where the rabbit wants them anyway. One rabbit-sized bundle is a rough guide to what a rabbit eats in a day.

A rabbit not eating hay is a problem, not a preference The two usual reasons are that pellets or treats are filling it up, in which case cut those back, or that eating hurts, which means dental disease and a vet visit (page {{P:dental}}). Both need acting on. If anything in a rabbit's budget gets cut, it should never be the hay: every other part of the diet can be adjusted later, and teeth that have already overgrown cannot.

Pellets

A fresh, high-fiber, plain timothy-based pellet, not a colorful mix with seeds, nuts or dried fruit. Mixes are linked to obesity and dental disease, because the rabbit eats the sweet pieces and leaves the fiber. Pellets are measured for adults, never free-fed the way hay is.

Keep the weight honest Rabbits pick the richest parts of a meal, which is why they gain weight easily on pellets and treats. Weigh weekly and feel the body monthly (page {{P:growth}}).

Water

A rabbit drinks about 120 mL per kilogram of body weight a day, roughly twice what a dog or cat of the same size drinks. A 6 lb (2.7 kg) rabbit takes about 11 fluid ounces (325 mL). Rabbits drink significantly more from an open bowl than from a sipper bottle, so a heavy, flat-bottomed bowl is the better default, and a giant needs a big, heavy one that is checked and refilled more than once a day.

  • A rabbit that gets a sore, damp chin from a bowl does better with a bottle. Rabbits chew the valve of a bottle, so a stainless steel one lasts longest.
  • Whichever you use, change the water every day, and wash a bowl rather than topping it up.
  • Dehydration slows the gut directly. Greens that carry moisture help, and in a blackout, keep water in more than one container.
The never-feed list, in one place Avocado · chocolate · onion, garlic and chives · rhubarb · muesli-style mixes · bread, crackers, cereal and yogurt drops. Iceberg lettuce is not toxic, but skip it too. Anyone feeding the rabbit should have this list.

Growth, weight & body condition

A change in weight is often the first sign of illness, and a slow drift is easy to miss by eye.

Maturity follows breed size

Rabbits reach sexual maturity at about 3.5 to 4 months in small breeds such as the Polish Dwarf and the Dutch, 4 to 4.5 months in medium breeds, and 6 to 9 months in giants, so the timing of a spay or neuter follows breed size more than the calendar. Adult weight runs from about 2.5 lb (1.1 kg) to more than 20 lb (9 kg), and healthy for your rabbit is its own figure, not a breed average.

Weigh weekly, feel monthly

Weigh once a week on a kitchen scale that reads in grams and write the number on the owner log (page {{P:ownerlog}}). Once a month, feel the body as well, because a thick coat hides fat. Body condition is scored on a 9-point scale, and 4 to 5 is the usual ideal for an adult.

ScoreWhat the hands find
1 to 3, thinRibs, spine and hips feel sharp under little or no padding. The back is flat or sunken, and the belly is tucked up.
4 to 5, idealRibs and spine are easy to feel with gentle pressure under a light, even layer. The back is gently rounded, the rump feels firm and filled, and the belly has a slight tuck.
6 to 7, overweightRibs and spine take firm pressure to find. The back is broad and rounded, the dewlap (the fold of skin under the chin) is noticeably padded, and the belly has lost its tuck.
8 to 9, obeseRibs and spine are very hard or impossible to feel. The rump bulges, the dewlap is large and folded, and the belly sags.
A home score is an estimate Anything outside 4 to 5, or any doubt, is a question for a rabbit-experienced vet before the diet changes. Weight alone cannot show whether a change is fat or muscle.

What to do with the numbers

  • Gaining: cut pellets, treats and fruit first, and never the hay. Rabbits pick the richest parts of a meal, which is why they gain easily on pellets, fruit and treats.
  • Losing: weight loss is a reason for a vet visit, not a diet tweak. Dental disease is the usual first suspect (page {{P:dental}}).
  • Any change, either way: make it gradual. Never cut food to the point that a rabbit stops eating, which is the stasis emergency (page {{P:stasis}}).
Giants Giants such as the Flemish are prone to obesity, so hold them at the lower end of the pellet range and let hay make up the difference. Their flooring and weight checks matter more, because extra weight lands on the hocks.
