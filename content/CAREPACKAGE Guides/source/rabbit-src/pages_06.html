Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

Look in the litter box. Quantity and shape, five seconds (page {{P:droppings}})
Top up hay so it is never empty, and refresh the water
Measured pellets and the day's greens, at least three varieties (page {{P:greens}})
Clean the toilet area and any accidents (page {{P:cleaning}})
Run time, ideally permanent, and at dawn and dusk if it is not
Look at the rear end, fur and skin, twice a day in warm weather (page {{P:flystrike}})
Thirty seconds of watching: posture, breathing, eyes, appetite, how it moves

Weekly

Weigh in grams and log it (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Clean the whole enclosure
Hands-on check: incisors, jawline, hind feet, rear end, lumps (page {{P:dental}})
Brush at least weekly, and daily instead during a molt

Every 6 to 8 weeks

Nail trim, with styptic powder to hand
Scent gland check, in the same session

Monthly

Feel the body for condition, not just weight (page {{P:growth}})
Review the log for slow trends rather than single readings
Rotate or add one piece of enrichment (page {{P:enrichlog}})

Seasonal

Molts come roughly every three months: brush daily and push hay and water hard (page {{P:molt}})
Summer: watch the room against the 80°F (26 to 27°C) line, and check the rear end twice a day (page {{P:temperature}})
Winter: watch for drafts and damp bedding, not the thermometer alone

Twice a year

Wellness exam, and ask the vet to look at the back teeth (page {{P:redflags}})

Once a year

The vaccine booster, paired with one of the wellness exams (page {{P:vaccine}})

Power outages & heat waves

A rabbit needs no equipment running to survive, which makes a blackout mostly a temperature problem, and heat is the one to worry about.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Keep feeding. This is the one place the reptile rule runs backward: a rabbit that stops eating for a day is in more danger than one that is a few degrees cool. Hay and water stay in front of it the whole time, and water goes in more than one container.
  • In a summer outage, freeze bottles of water for the rabbit to lie against, use cool wet towels, shade the enclosure, and move it to the coolest room, usually a tiled bathroom or a basement. Cucumber, melon and apple help with water. Read the thermometer instead of guessing.
  • In a winter outage, keep the pen draft-free, away from windows and doors, and raised off the floor. Add plenty of dry bedding hay or straw, extra hay for insulation, and check that the water has not frozen, including the ball in a bottle nozzle.
  • Move the rabbit out rather than wait, once the room is past the safe band and you cannot bring it back. Decide at hour two, not hour eight.

Don't

  • Don't treat a cool rabbit as a reason to stop feeding.
  • Don't plunge an overheated rabbit into cold or ice water.
  • Don't assume it has recovered once it looks calmer. A vet visit is still needed after heat stress.
Outage kit Frozen water bottles, towels, a battery fan, a thermometer, extra hay and bedding, a carrier, and your vet numbers written on paper.

In a heat wave

The line is about 80°F (26 to 27°C), and temperature plus humidity should stay at 150 or less. Check the room through the afternoon, since a house without air conditioning keeps climbing. Keep the rabbit out of direct sun, move to the coolest room, and use airflow and a frozen water bottle wrapped in a towel. A rabbit living outdoors comes inside for a heat wave.

Leaving town, transport & moving

A rabbit travels badly and notices every change, so the planning matters more than the equipment.

Leaving town

Appetite, drinking, droppings and weight are checked every day, and the rear end twice a day in warm weather, and that job passes to a sitter while you are gone. Having someone look after the rabbit in its own home is usually best, because it keeps things familiar. Walk the sitter through the pet-sitter sheet in person (page {{P:sitter}}), tell your vet that someone else may bring the rabbit in, and make sure the sitter knows what normal droppings look like. A rabbit that must stay elsewhere takes some used, unsoiled nesting material from home.

Transport and vet trips

  • A sturdy, hard-sided, well-ventilated carrier the rabbit cannot chew through, with room to lie down and turn around. A wire cage flexes under braking and lets the rabbit watch every passing car, which for a prey animal is a stream of predators.
  • Drape a towel over most of the carrier, and give it somewhere to hide: a cardboard box or small tunnel tucked inside. Add a toy or a handful of clean bedding from home so it smells familiar.
  • Line the carrier with hay, and offer water. A rabbit that nibbles on the way has a gut that keeps moving, and a long stretch with nothing to chew is a stasis risk.
  • Secure the carrier in the car, in the cabin, out of direct sun, never in a closed trunk, and never left in a parked car, even briefly.
  • Take a bonded partner along even when only one is ill, and keep the trip short where you have a choice of vet.
  • Moving house is a stress, a known stasis trigger. Keep the diet identical, set the pen up first, and watch the droppings closely.

What not to do Don't buy toys instead of solving space or company: the research measured that gap and toys were not what closed it. Don't take away a hiding place to add a new object. Don't change three things at once, or you will never know which one the rabbit responded to.
