Red flags & finding a vet

What to watch for, what to tell the vet, and how to find one before you need one.

Before anything else on this list Rabbits need a rabbit-savvy exotic vet, not a standard small-animal clinic. This is not snobbery about credentials: rabbit physiology, anesthetic protocols and drug tolerances differ enough that a vet unfamiliar with the species can miss or mishandle everything in this section. Find one before you need one, ideally through a local rabbit rescue.

Go to a vet today

What to have ready when you call

When the rabbit last ate and what, when you last saw normal droppings and what they look like now, its current weight and the trend from the owner log, whether it is spayed or neutered, anything that changed recently (food, housing, a new pet), its vaccination status, and any medication given.

Finding a vet

Ask a clinic how many rabbits it sees, whether it follows exotic-species fasting rules for surgery, and who covers emergencies after hours. Not every emergency clinic sees rabbits, so find out in advance which one near you does, and write the numbers on the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}}). Book a wellness exam twice a year: it catches dental changes and weight drift before they become emergencies, and the vet should look at the back teeth, which cannot be seen from the front.

GI stasis: what it is & why rabbits get it

Ask an experienced rabbit vet what kills more pet rabbits than almost anything else and this is the answer: the gut simply slowing down or stopping.

What it actually is

Rabbits are hindgut fermenters. The digestive system relies on continuous movement to process a high-fiber diet and to produce two kinds of droppings, including the nutrient-rich cecotropes the rabbit eats directly (page {{P:droppings}}). When gut motility, the movement of food through the gut, slows or stops, gas builds up, painful bloating follows, and the balance of gut bacteria shifts dangerously. Untreated, that cascade can become life-threatening within 24 to 48 hours of onset.

Why rabbits specifically

Unlike many pets, a rabbit cannot comfortably skip a meal or two. Its gut needs near-constant input to keep moving, and going without food for even half a day can be enough to slow motility in a rabbit that is stressed or unwell. That is the opposite of how most mammals are built, and it is why a rabbit cannot tough out an off day the way a cat or dog can.

Triggers

TriggerWhy it matters
Low-fiber dietInsufficient hay slows motility over time. The single biggest risk factor, and the one entirely in your control.
Dental painMakes eating uncomfortable, and reduced intake alone can start the cascade.
StressA loud environment, a new pet or a vet visit can be enough to slow digestion.
DehydrationReduces gut lubrication and motility directly.
Pain from any other causeA rabbit in pain from any source often stops eating, which starts the same cascade.
A heavy moltSwallowed fur plus dehydrated gut contents can form a mass that is hard to pass (page {{P:molt}}).
Some medicationsA few drugs used for other conditions slow gut motility as a side effect. Ask when anything new is prescribed.
Stasis even on a good diet Hay is the single biggest prevention factor, but it does not make a rabbit immune. Stress, dehydration, dental pain or an unrelated illness can all trigger stasis in a rabbit with an excellent diet. Stasis is also not quite the same as bloat: bloat, severe gas buildup, is one dangerous consequence of stasis, but stasis can also show up as reduced motility with minimal gas.

GI stasis: signs, what to do & prevention

The signs arrive in an order, and the first one you can see without touching the rabbit.

Early warning signs, in the order they usually appear

  1. Reduced or absent fecal output. The earliest and most reliable sign, and the reason a daily glance at the litter box is worth more than any other habit in this book.
  2. Smaller, oddly shaped, or fur-strung droppings in the hours before output stops entirely.
  3. Reduced appetite, picking at food without refusing it outright.
  4. Hunched posture with the belly pulled in, the classic sign of abdominal pain.
  5. Teeth grinding (bruxism) at a harsh, audible pitch, distinct from the soft tooth-purring of a relaxed rabbit.
Why wait and see is dangerous here With most illnesses, a day of monitoring at home is reasonable. With GI stasis, that same day can be the difference between a rabbit that recovers fully and one that does not. A rabbit that has produced no droppings in 8 to 12 hours, or has stopped eating entirely, needs a same-day visit to a rabbit-savvy or exotic vet, not a "let's see how tomorrow goes."

What treatment looks like

Vet treatment typically means fluids under the skin, pain management and motility medication, along with identifying whatever triggered the episode, because stasis is very often the visible end of something else, most commonly dental pain. Syringe-feeding a critical care formula is often part of recovery, but it should be guided by a vet: force-feeding a rabbit with a real blockage rather than a slowdown can make things worse, and the two look similar from outside. About 70% of rabbits survive GI stasis, and most uncomplicated cases resolve with veterinary treatment over three to five days, but delayed cases can need days of hospitalization.

While you are getting to the vet

  • Call first, and give the facts: when it last ate, when you last saw normal droppings, what changed.
  • Go the same day.

Don't

  • Don't syringe-feed without veterinary direction.
  • Don't use pineapple juice, gas drops or any home remedy in place of the appointment. They are outdated, with no reliable evidence behind them, and they waste critical time.
  • Don't wait for the morning to see if it passes.

Prevention, which is most of the answer

  • Unlimited grass hay at 80% or more of the diet (page {{P:hay}}).
  • Fresh water always available, and greens that carry moisture.
  • Diet changes made gradually over a week or two, never overnight.
  • A low-stress home, and a daily glance at the litter box to see that output looks normal in size and quantity.
  • Brushing through heavy molts, so less fur is swallowed.
  • Twice-yearly wellness exams, since dental pain is the most common hidden trigger.

Dental disease

One of the most frequent hidden causes of appetite loss, and one that can develop silently.
CauseRabbit teeth grow about 1 cm (0.4 in) a month, as much as 4 to 5 in (10 to 13 cm) a year, and an incisor that does not meet its partner can grow 1 mm a day. Long-strand fiber wears them down by grinding, so a diet short of hay lets them overgrow. Molar spurs form on the outside edges and cut the tongue or cheek, and incisors can stop meeting at all. Heredity adds to it: dwarf and lop-eared breeds are overrepresented, whatever the diet.
SignsDrooling, or a permanently damp chin. Dropping food while trying to eat. Weight loss. Pawing at the mouth. Trouble grooming. Mouth sores or abscesses.
ResponseA vet exam. The roots and back teeth cannot be seen from the front, so a vet confirms the problem with skull X-rays. Treatment is trimming the teeth under anesthesia with rotary tools, and in severe cases extraction.
RecoveryDental exams and trims are repeated every 4 to 12 weeks for the rest of the rabbit's life, because the teeth keep growing. The prevention is unchanged: unlimited hay, from day one and for life.
Why this page matters for the rest Dental pain is a trigger for GI stasis. A rabbit whose mouth hurts eats less, and a rabbit that eats less has a gut that slows. If your rabbit has had a stasis episode with no obvious cause, the teeth are the first place to look, which is why a full workup matters more than treating the stasis alone (page {{P:signs}}).

What you can check at home

Lift the lips once a week. The top incisors should meet the bottom pair cleanly, with the top just in front.
Watch the rabbit eat a piece of hay. It should take it and grind steadily, not pick it up and drop it.
Look for a damp chin, drooling, or weight loss on the weekly weigh-in.
Keep the twice-yearly wellness exams, and ask the vet to look at the back teeth.
Never trim a rabbit's teeth yourself Nail clippers and wire cutters fracture the tooth, which leads to abscesses in the tooth or gum and enormous pain. This is a veterinary procedure under anesthesia with rotary tools. There is no home version of it.

Flystrike & snuffles

Flystrike (myiasis)

CauseFlies lay eggs on soiled or damp fur, most often around the rear, and the eggs hatch into maggots within hours. The rabbits at risk cannot keep their rear end clean: a rabbit with diarrhea, tooth pain, arthritis or obesity, one living in a dirty enclosure, or one with an open wound. It is most common in warm weather but can happen all year.
SignsFly eggs or maggots in the fur, patches of wet fur and fur loss around the rear, lethargy, eating less, a bad smell, or collapse. The maggots can do extensive damage in a day and kill a rabbit within 24 to 48 hours.
ResponseAn emergency vet appointment right away, for even a single maggot, at any hour. Do not try to pick the maggots off or treat the rabbit at home, and do not dunk it in water to drown them: both cause pain and can send the rabbit into shock. The vet clips the fur, cleans the skin and removes the maggots, often under anesthesia, then gives pain relief and medication for the rest. In severe cases the damage is too great to save the rabbit.
PreventionLook at the rear end, fur and skin every day, and twice a day in warm weather. Remove soiled litter and wet bedding every day. Keep the rabbit slim and moving so it can groom itself, have any wound checked, and ask the vet about a fly deterrent in summer. A rabbit with a permanently mucky bottom has a hidden cause, since dental pain and arthritis both stop a rabbit grooming.

Snuffles (upper respiratory infection)

CauseOften Pasteurella multocida, a bacterium most adult rabbits carry without trouble, though other bacteria do the same job. Stress, poor ventilation, or ammonia from a dirty enclosure lets it take hold.
SignsA thin discharge from the nose and eyes that thickens over time, sneezing, wheezing or noisy breathing, loss of appetite, lethargy, and the giveaway owners miss: crusty, matted fur on the insides of the front paws, from a rabbit wiping its own nose repeatedly.
ResponseCall the vet promptly: it is contagious between rabbits. A vet should confirm it with a bacterial culture and a sensitivity test (a lab test that grows the bacteria to identify them and show which antibiotics work) rather than treating on the signs alone, because not every strain causes disease and only some antibiotics are safe for a rabbit (page {{P:vaccine}}).
PreventionFix the ventilation and the cleaning schedule alongside the medication. Pneumonia cases rise with the ammonia level in the enclosure.
Open-mouth breathing is always an emergency A rabbit breathing through its mouth is a same-hour call, whatever else is going on.

Sore hocks, bladder stones, uterine cancer & a parasite

Sore hocks (pododermatitis)

Pressure sores on the undersides of the hind feet, from wire or hard flooring or inadequate bedding, and made more likely by obesity. Rex rabbits and giant breeds are most prone. It starts as fur loss, red skin and swelling, then becomes ulcers and open sores, and untreated it reaches the bone. Treatment is anti-inflammatory medication, foot wraps and a flooring change. Prevention is solid, soft, dry flooring and a rabbit at a healthy weight, and catching the bald-patch stage is worth the weekly look.

Bladder sludge and stones

Caused by too much calcium in the diet, for instance from alfalfa fed to an adult, or from a lot of kale, spinach or dandelion greens. Signs are decreased appetite, lethargy, weight loss, teeth grinding and frequent urination, and stones are removed surgically. Grass hay for non-breeding adults is the prevention (page {{P:hay}}).

Uterine cancer

A significant risk in unspayed does. In some strains, 80% of unspayed does develop uterine adenocarcinoma, a cancer of the uterus, by 3 years of age, and it is the main reason vets spay a doe that will not be bred. Spay early, before it becomes a concern rather than after (page {{P:neuter}}).

Encephalitozoon cuniculi (E. cuniculi)

A parasite carried by many pet rabbits, often with no signs. When it becomes active it can cause head tilt, and it contributes to recurrent GI stasis episodes. A head tilt can also come from an inner ear infection, and telling the two apart is the vet's job. Vets often test for it at the first exam, and when the test shows an active infection the treatment is a 28-day course of fenbendazole, a deworming drug, started before signs appear.

The pattern across these conditions Very little here is treated better than it is avoided. Sore hocks are prevented by soft, dry flooring and a healthy weight, bladder stones by a grass-hay diet and water that gets drunk, and uterine cancer by a spay. That is the argument for the twice-yearly exam.
ConditionCatch it by
Sore hocksLooking at the undersides of the hind feet every week for fur loss and red skin
Bladder stonesNoticing frequent urination, teeth grinding or weight loss, and asking about calcium in the diet
E. cuniculiAsking about a test at the first exam, and treating a head tilt as a vet visit
Uterine cancerNot waiting: a spay removes the risk, so it is a decision rather than a screening

Rabbit hemorrhagic disease vaccine & antibiotics

RHDV2 and the vaccine schedule

Rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus 2 (RHDV2) reached the United States in 2018 and has since spread through wild and pet rabbits nationwide. It kills fast: in a vaccine trial, 18 of 19 unvaccinated rabbits exposed to it died within 10 days. The virus travels on food, water and anything else it contaminates, so an indoor rabbit is not out of reach.

StepWhen
First doseFrom 4 weeks of age
Second dose21 days after the first. Protection is full about two weeks after it.
Annual boosterOnce a year. Vaccinated rabbits in a duration study held protective antibody levels for a full 12 months, and a booster at that point raised them sharply.
Side effectsUsually limited to a mild fever, some lethargy, or a small swelling where the needle went in.
CostThe two-dose starter series runs about $80 to $180, and each annual booster about $60 to $75. The higher prices usually bundle a full exam, a nail trim or a microchip.

Pairing the yearly booster with one of the twice-yearly wellness exams keeps it from slipping.

Antibiotics and enterotoxemia

A routine antibiotic that is safe in a dog can be fatal in a rabbit. Certain antibiotics aimed at gram-positive bacteria, the large group that includes most of a rabbit's gut bacteria, wipe out the resident gut flora, and a toxin-producing bacterium, Clostridium, takes over: enterotoxemia, toxins flooding the bloodstream from a gut that has stopped working as a barrier. Rabbits move the fastest: lethargy, a rough coat, greenish-brown feces caked around the rear, and death within 48 hours. A rabbit can look healthy in the evening and be dead the next morning.

Antibiotic class or drugDocumented risk
Penicillins (penicillin, ampicillin, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate)Contraindicated in rabbits
Lincosamides (clindamycin, lincomycin)Contraindicated in rabbits
Macrolides (erythromycin, tylosin)Documented in guinea pigs and rodents; treat as risky in a rabbit and ask before use
CephalosporinsOral use is risky in rabbits

Oral dosing carries more risk than an injectable form of the same drug, because it passes straight through the gut where the bacteria live. Bring the drug list to the appointment, or simply ask: "Is this antibiotic one of the ones known to cause fatal diarrhea in rabbits, and have you prescribed it to a rabbit before?" A vet experienced with exotics will have an immediate answer.

If it starts during a course Any diarrhea, appetite loss or lethargy that starts during or shortly after an antibiotic is a call-the-vet-now situation, not something to monitor overnight. Do not wait for the next scheduled dose. Stopping the antibiotic is usually the first step the vet will want to take.

Reading droppings & cecotropes

The litter box is the best diagnostic instrument you own, and checking it takes five seconds a day.

Rabbits make two kinds

Fecal pellets

Small, brown, roughly round and somewhat dry, made mostly of undigested fiber. These are what you see in the litter box, in large quantities, all day.

Cecotropes

A cluster of small, soft, shiny pellets like a dark brown mulberry or a bunch of grapes, coated in mucus. They pass at several points through the day and night, and the rabbit eats them straight from the source.

Cecotropes are not an accident and not a sign of anything wrong. They carry B vitamins and microbial protein that the gut bacteria made but that were not absorbed on the first pass, and eating them is a genuine part of rabbit digestion. A rabbit that cannot eat them eventually becomes malnourished, so do not stop the rabbit doing it.

What the litter box is telling you

What you seeWhat it points to
Plenty of round, even, dry pelletsNormal output for a rabbit eating enough hay.
Fewer, smaller or oddly shaped pelletsThe gut is slowing. Check what the rabbit has eaten today and act now rather than waiting for the count to reach zero.
Pellets strung together with fur or debrisSlowing motility, an early stasis sign. In a molt, brush daily and push hay and water (page {{P:molt}}).
No droppings at all for 8 to 12 hoursSame-day vet visit (page {{P:signs}}).
Soft, mushy pellets, or uneaten cecotropes stuck to the fur or floorToo much rich food (pellets, treats or fruit) shifting the gut bacteria, or a rabbit that cannot reach them because of obesity, arthritis or another physical problem. Cut the rich food first, then look for a reason it cannot reach them.
Unformed, watery stoolTrue diarrhea. It is rare in adults and can point to parasites or inflammation, so a vet should see the rabbit. In a baby rabbit it is an emergency.
Uneaten cecotropes are not diarrhea They get mistaken for it, but true diarrhea is the unformed, watery kind, and an adult with a soft stool usually has a diet problem rather than an infection.
The five-second habit Know what your rabbit's normal output looks like, and look in the litter box every time you pass it. You are not counting, you are looking for a change in quantity or shape. It is the earliest signal this species gives you, and it is free.
