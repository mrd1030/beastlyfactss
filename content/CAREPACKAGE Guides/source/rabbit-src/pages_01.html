How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

Units used throughout Every temperature and size is given twice: degrees Fahrenheit (°F) with degrees Celsius (°C), inches (in) and feet (ft) with centimeters (cm) or meters (m), square feet (sq ft) with square meters (m²), and pounds (lb) with kilograms (kg). The weight log is kept in grams (g), the unit a kitchen scale shows. Food is measured in cups and tablespoons (tbsp), water in milliliters (mL), and prices are in United States dollars.

If you haven't set up yet

Read pages {{P:profile}} through {{P:cleaning}} in order before buying anything, and the arrival and spay pages ({{P:arrival}} and {{P:neuter}}) before you bring a rabbit home. Two decisions cost the most to reverse: how much space the rabbit gets, and whether it is spayed or neutered. Get both right first, then use the Budget & Shopping List on page {{P:budget}}.

If your rabbit is already home

Audit what you have against the Setup Checklist on page {{P:checklist}}. If hay is not unlimited and the bulk of the diet, fix that this week: it is the most protective habit available to you. If your rabbit is off its food right now, do not read further. Go to page {{P:stasis}}, then call a vet today.

Why this package exists Rabbits are sold as a starter pet and kept like a hamster, and almost everything that goes wrong follows from that one mistake. They are not a cage animal, and they are not a cheap animal once the vet is counted. This package reflects current welfare standards and peer-reviewed research, cross-checked against the sources on page {{P:sources}}.
Not a substitute for A rabbit-savvy exotic vet. This matters more for rabbits than for most pets: rabbit physiology, anesthetic protocols and drug tolerances differ enough from a dog's or cat's that a general small-animal clinic can miss or mishandle the conditions in Section 07. Find one before you need one, ideally through a local rabbit rescue.

Cost overview

Initial setup

$93 to $185 for the equipment, plus $150 to $500 or more for a spay or neuter, and $25 to $100 for an adoption fee that often includes the surgery. Every line is on page {{P:budget}}.

Every year after

$840 to $1,835: food and litter at $55 to $130 a month, two wellness exams, and the yearly vaccine booster. The first year runs $1,103 to $2,625, without the adoption fee.

The number that catches people out A GI stasis workup alone can exceed $1,500, and rabbits need an exotic vet whose rates are higher than a general clinic's. Keep $500 to $1,000 set aside separately from the routine budget. Rabbits hide illness well, and when something goes wrong it goes wrong quickly.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • They eat some of their droppings on purpose. Cecotropes are soft pellets eaten straight from the source, carrying B vitamins the gut bacteria made on the first pass. A rabbit that stops eating them is telling you something (page {{P:droppings}}).
  • Their teeth never stop growing, about 1 cm (0.4 in) a month, as much as 4 to 5 in (10 to 13 cm) a year. Hay is not a bedding material with a nutritional bonus, it is the dental equipment.
  • Rabbit milk is so rich that a mother nurses once, rarely twice, a day for 2 to 10 minutes. Kits (baby rabbits) go roughly a day between feeds, so a nest left alone is usually normal, not abandoned.

The species, breeds, size & lifespan

What you are buying, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

One species, more than 300 breeds

Every pet rabbit, from a dwarf to a giant, is the same species: Oryctolagus cuniculus, the European rabbit. In the wild it lives in large underground warrens with strict social hierarchies, on grassland, meadow, farmland and forest edge, eating grasses, herbs, root vegetables and bark. It is a social animal that digs, which is why a single rabbit in a hutch is a compromise and why the digging in your living room is not misbehavior.

The monk and the Lent myth The old story says rabbits were domesticated at a stroke around the year 600, when a pope ruled that newborn rabbits counted as fish and could be eaten in Lent. A graduate student sent to find the decree found that none exists. The story traces to a passage written in the year 584 about a man who ate young rabbits during Lent. Irving-Pease and Larson published the correction in 2018: domestication was a long process with no single starting point.

Lifespan

A rabbit kept indoors typically lives 8 to 12 years, and some reach 14 or even 16. Outdoor rabbits average 3 to 5, because of predators, weather and problems that go unnoticed without close daily contact. A wild rabbit lives 1 to 2 years. Plan for the whole span: a rabbit bought on impulse can outlast a lease, a school year and a dorm room.
