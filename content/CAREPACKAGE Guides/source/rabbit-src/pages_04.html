Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & building trust

Rabbits are more fragile than almost any first-time owner expects, and getting this wrong breaks bones.
Why support is the whole thing A rabbit that struggles against an unsupported hold can fracture its own lower back, the lumbar spine. Its hind legs are powerful enough to do that, and without full body support that power works in exactly the wrong direction. This is not a handling preference. It is the single most important physical thing to get right with this species.

Picking one up

  1. One hand on the chest just behind the front legs, the other under the hindquarters. Both at once, never one then the other.
  2. Lift with both regions supported, and bring the rabbit straight in against your body, not out at arm's length.
  3. Stay low to the ground for the whole session, so a struggle ends in a short drop rather than a long one.
  4. If it struggles, lower it back down immediately, in one smooth motion, in a straight line. Do not try to keep your grip.

The "football hold" is worth learning: the rabbit's head tucked into the crook of your elbow, its body supported along your forearm. Vets use it because it is secure and low-stress at once. For a quick look at the eyes, ears or teeth, vets use another hold: one hand behind the rabbit so it cannot back up, the other lightly covering its eyes and steadying the head so it does not bolt forward. It is the lowest-stress restraint there is.

Never Lift a rabbit by the ears, ever. Never rely on the scruff alone to carry its weight: even where a grip at the neck is used to steady an animal, the body has to be supported underneath.

Skip trancing

You will see advice about flipping a rabbit onto its back to calm it, sometimes called trancing. Don't. Research measuring rabbits in that position found significant increases in respiration, heart rate and stress hormones. The stillness is a fear response the animal cannot escape, and it looks calm to the untrained eye, which is why the myth lasted. Repetition makes it worse: a rabbit turned over often goes still sooner because it has learned what is coming, and it tends to hide more and explore less afterward.

Building trust instead

  • Most rabbits are calmest with all four feet on the ground, and many never learn to love being picked up even once they trust you. That is normal.
  • Sit or lie down at the rabbit's level and wait. Let it come to you rather than reaching in. Hand-feed a green or a herb at floor level.
  • Aim for a rabbit that is relaxed around you, not one that tolerates being carried. Pick-ups can stay rare.
  • Train it to come when called and to walk into its carrier for a treat. A rabbit that rarely has to be scooped up makes vet trips far easier.
  • Nail trims, grooming and health checks happen with four feet on a stable surface, never with the rabbit on its back. Long sleeves help, because hind nails can badly scratch an unprotected arm.

Stress signals to stop on

Thumping a hind foot, a tense and rigid body, wide eyes, fast and shallow breathing, harsh tooth grinding, freezing in place, bolting or kicking. If you see these, set the rabbit down in one smooth motion, straight down and never dropped, and give it space.

A habit that points to a problem

Those are in-the-moment signals. A longer-running pattern is a different matter: circling the enclosure over and over, chewing the cage bars, over-grooming to the point of bald patches, constant hiding or freezing as the baseline, and any change in eating, drinking, activity or litter habits. These are signs of stress or illness. Rule out health first, then look at space, company and enrichment (pages {{P:enclosure}}, {{P:company}}).

Normal, not naughty

  • Hiding is how a prey animal rests, so every rabbit needs somewhere to retreat.
  • Digging is a burrowing instinct. A rabbit without anywhere to dig gets stressed, and picks somewhere you would rather it didn't.
  • Chewing comes from teeth that never stop growing, and it slows after neutering without ever stopping.
  • Marking territory with chin secretions, urine and droppings is part of how a rabbit feels at home.
  • Being most active at dawn and dusk is the crepuscular rhythm, and it is why exercise scheduled for the household's convenience misses.

Enrichment & common mistakes

The enrichment aisle is full of things to put in a hutch. The evidence points somewhere much less commercial.

What the research says

  • Companionship. A study of social housing in privately owned rabbits found solitary rabbits showed less behavioral diversity and more awake inactivity than socially housed ones. A larger age difference between paired rabbits went with a lower friendship index, a measure of how close two rabbits are, which is a practical steer when choosing a companion.
  • Space, measured in a stress hormone. A study of pair-housed pet rabbits found that run access, hutch size and time of day all affected welfare-relevant behavior and fecal corticosterone. Space registers in a stress hormone, which is why permanent access beats a scheduled hour.
  • Structure. In group-housed rabbits, an elevated platform let animals rest more comfortably and explore more. Somewhere to get on top of, and somewhere to get underneath, is worth building in from the start.
Awake inactivity A still rabbit reads as a contented rabbit from the sofa, and the measure exists because the two states look identical from across a room. A rabbit sitting motionless and awake for long stretches in a small space is showing the pattern the research associates with poorer welfare, not relaxing.

Priority order

  1. A neutered, bonded companion, close in age
  2. Permanent run access or a rabbit-proofed room
  3. Unlimited hay, presented so it takes time to eat
  4. A dig box
  5. Platforms to get on and cover to get under
  6. Chewing and shredding material: bamboo sticks, untreated willow, plain cardboard
  7. Novel objects, last, and they are the cheap part

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happens
Buying the cage sold as a rabbit cageIt is a fraction of the current standard. Confinement shows up as awake inactivity, which owners read as a calm rabbit.
Pellets as the main foodLow fiber is the biggest stasis risk, and pellets do not wear the teeth the way hay does.
One rabbit and toys instead of companyThe research measured that gap, and toys were not what changed it.
Not spaying or neuteringIn some strains, 80% of unspayed does develop uterine cancer by 3 years, plus marking, aggression and failed litter training.

Companionship & bonding

The highest-value thing you can do for a rabbit, and the one that takes the longest to do properly.

A neutered, bonded pair does things a single rabbit does not: mutual grooming, resting in contact, and simply more behavior of every kind. A single rabbit with an attentive owner is a compromise, not an equivalent, and this is the finding the research most directly supports.

Choosing a companion

  • Neuter both first. Hormones sabotage bonds that would otherwise work.
  • Closer in age is better: the friendship index dropped as the age gap widened.
  • A neutered male with a spayed female is the pairing usually recommended.
  • Temperament matters more than size or breed.

How bonding goes, stage by stage

StageWhat you do
1. Neuter, then waitA male can stay fertile for up to six weeks after neutering, and hormones take weeks to settle, so wait about four weeks after surgery before the first meeting.
2. Vaccinate and quarantineThe newcomer gets its vaccination against rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus 2, RHDV2 (page {{P:vaccine}}), and two weeks in a separate space before it shares air or bedding with the resident.
3. Side by sideTwo enclosures next to each other with a barrier, so the rabbits can see and smell each other and each can hide. Expect about a week of unrest.
4. Neutral groundShort, supervised meetings in an area neither rabbit has lived in, lengthening day by day, ended before either is tired of it.
5. Moving inOnce they spend one to two hours together every day without trouble, they can share a living space.
It is working when They lie together and groom each other. Bonding takes weeks, and going too fast is how it fails.
Separate and go back a stage if There is mounting of heads, biting, or chasing that does not stop. A bond is also failing when one or both rabbits lose weight or develop new behavior problems over the following weeks.

After the bond

A bonded pair travels together. A familiar rabbit alongside helps a rabbit stay calm in an unfamiliar place, so the partner comes to vet visits and on car trips even when only one is ill (page {{P:outage}}). A prey animal separated from its companion in a strange place carries two stresses instead of one.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a rabbit, quarantine & the first vet visit

Most of what decides the next ten years is settled before the rabbit comes home.

What a healthy rabbit looks like

Look atHealthy
Fur and skinClean and shiny, with no bald patches or wounds. Wet or matted fur under the chin or around the tail can signal illness.
Eyes, nose and earsEyes and nose clear. Ears clean, not crusty.
BehaviorAlert, curious, and willing to move without stiffness or limping.
The placeClean. Ask what the rabbit has been eating and how it has been handled.

Visit in the early morning or the evening, when rabbits are most active, to judge personality. Larger rabbits are often calmer and sturdier, and smaller ones more delicate. Selling or giving away baby rabbits under 2 months old is illegal in many states, and a rabbit from a rescue is often cheaper in the end: adoption fees of $25 to $100 frequently include the surgery and an initial vet check.

The first vet visit

  • Find a rabbit-savvy exotic vet before the rabbit arrives, and book the first exam for as soon as the rabbit is home (page {{P:redflags}}).
  • For a rescue rabbit, ask when it was last vaccinated, and bring what you know about its diet.
  • The exam covers weight, body condition and the mouth. A full dental exam of the back teeth takes sedation, so a rabbit that is eating normally may not need one.
  • Ask about a blood test for Encephalitozoon cuniculi (E. cuniculi), a parasite many pet rabbits carry (page {{P:conditions}}), and about the vaccine schedule (page {{P:vaccine}}). Vets often test for the parasite at the first exam.
  • Ask the vet to confirm the sex, and book the spay or neuter if it is not already done.

Quarantine

A newcomer that will share space with a rabbit already at home is vaccinated first and then waits two weeks in a separate space before they share air or bedding. Keep the diet exactly as the seller or rescue fed it for the first days, and change it over a week or two, because a sudden food change is a classic stasis trigger. Then the bonding stages on page {{P:company}} begin.

Settling in A new home is a stress. Give it hay, water and quiet, with the litter box already stocked with hay over it, and let it come to you rather than reaching in.

Sexing, spay & neuter

Not optional for a pet rabbit, and the cheapest route is often an adoption that includes it.
The number this page exists for In some strains, 80% of unspayed does develop uterine cancer by 3 years of age. That is the main reason vets spay a doe that will not be bred, and why spaying is treated as close to routine rather than as an elective extra. Spaying removes the uterus before the cancer can start.

Sexing

A vet can sex a rabbit by gently pressing the genital area to show a slit-like opening in a female or the penis in a male. A buck's (adult male's) testicles descend at 10 to 12 weeks, so a young rabbit can be hard to read. Ask the vet to confirm the sex at the first exam, before any pairing, because opposite-sex pairs breed unless both are altered.

What it changes

Benefits
FemalesRemoves the uterine cancer risk. Hormonal marking stops, so litter training works. Chewing settles.
MalesNo health benefit, but worth it for behavior: an intact buck is in constant libido, mounts other pets and objects, and may be aggressive toward other rabbits.
PairsBoth rabbits are neutered, which makes bonding possible at all (page {{P:company}}).

Timing and cost

A doe's simple ovariectomy (removal of the ovaries) is possible under a year old, and the usual time is at maturity, around 6 months, before fat builds up around the uterus.

Choosing who does it, and what to expect

Ask whether the clinic follows exotic-species fasting rules. A rabbit cannot vomit, and its stomach is never empty, so a fast longer than three hours is neither needed nor recommended, and a short one-to-two-hour fast to clear the mouth is the norm. A clinic that says withhold food overnight, the way it would for a dog, may not do many rabbits. Afterward, hay, vegetables and water go in front of the rabbit as soon as possible, and eating again is the number that matters: a rabbit that has not eaten within two to three hours needs its pain relief reviewed, not more waiting.

Molting, grooming & nails

Rabbits groom themselves thoroughly and cannot vomit, which is the whole reason this page exists.

Molting

Rabbits molt roughly every three months, and the timing and length of each shed vary, so one molt can pass almost unnoticed while the next fills the brush every day. A heavy molt is normal. The risk is the fur the rabbit swallows while grooming: a cat coughs up a hairball, and a rabbit cannot. Fur has to travel the whole digestive tract and come out in the droppings. That works unremarkably until the gut slows for another reason, such as low fiber, dehydration, stress or dental pain, and a heavy load of fur meets a gut that is not moving it. True blockages are uncommon and stasis usually has a separate trigger, but that is why a heavy molt is a stretch to watch closely rather than a shedding nuisance (page {{P:signs}}).

  • Brush at least weekly, daily during a molt. Every hair on the brush is one the rabbit did not swallow. Angora and other long-coated rabbits need daily brushing all year.
  • Push hay and water hard through a molt. Fiber and fluid keep swallowed fur moving.
  • Normal shedding is even across the body. A bald patch that does not fill back in, redness or scabbing under missing fur, or clumps coming out outside a molt is a reason to ask a vet.

Nails

Trim every 6 to 8 weeks. Overgrown nails are uncomfortable to walk on and curl back into the pad. The quick is the blood vessel through the center of the nail, a pink line on a pale nail. On a dark nail, hold a flashlight behind it to see where the quick ends. Keep styptic powder out and open before you start, for the nick that eventually happens: pressing it on for a few seconds normally stops the bleeding, and it is not a reason to stop trimming.

A rabbit that fights every trim does better wrapped snugly in a towel with one foot worked free at a time, and with one or two nails done per sitting, so it does not associate the whole job with an ordeal.

Scent glands, baths and the rest

  • Scent glands. A rabbit has a scent gland on either side of the anus that needs periodic cleaning. Have a rabbit-savvy vet or groomer do it, or show you the technique, because the area is sensitive and easy to irritate. Check it in the same session as the nails, every 6 to 8 weeks.
  • Never a full bath. Rabbits groom themselves, and a soaked coat holds moisture and cold, and the struggle is stressful. Spot-clean a dirty patch with a damp cloth on that spot and dry it thoroughly.
  • A mucky rear end needs the cause found, not just cleaning: diet, teeth, weight or arthritis (page {{P:flystrike}}).
Make grooming the health check Every session is a chance to run your hands over the whole rabbit: lumps, scabs, sore hocks, a damp chin, a matted rear, a change in weight. Doing both at once means the check actually happens.
Section 06 · The Law

Is a rabbit legal where you live?

Legal without a permit almost everywhere in the United States, with two rules that are genuinely unclear.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet rabbit is legal with no permit in 50 and unclear in 2: no jurisdiction is recorded as barring it or as requiring a permit for a pet.

WhereStatusThe rule
MinnesotaUnclearMinnesota Rules 6216.0250 designates the European rabbit, Oryctolagus cuniculus, a prohibited invasive species with no strain or domestication qualifier, and Minnesota Statutes § 84D.05 bars possessing one except under a permit. None of the permit categories is pet-keeping, and the state's invasive species page restates the rule. What the rule says and what the state does about house rabbits are different questions: ask the state natural resources agency before you buy or move.
NevadaUnclearNevada Administrative Code 503.110(1)(d)(5) names Oryctolagus cuniculus but labels the row "Wild European Rabbit", and unlike the chinchilla and rat rows it carries no domesticated-races wording. Pet rabbits are plainly sold in the state, but the text does not settle it. Clark County, Las Vegas and Henderson are stricter than the state, so check the municipality first.
