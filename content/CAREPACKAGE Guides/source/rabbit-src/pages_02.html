Enclosure size, type & where it goes

A pen is the bedroom. The exercise space is the house.

The standard is at least 8 sq ft (0.75 m²) of enclosure combined with at least 24 sq ft (2.2 m²) of exercise space for one or two rabbits, with a minimum of 5 hours a day to run in it. In practice that means a large foldable metal exercise pen (an x-pen), 4×4 ft (1.2×1.2 m) or bigger, not a cage the rabbit is let out of occasionally. The wire hutch sold as a "rabbit cage" is a fraction of this, and current welfare standards consider it inadequate.

Permanent access beats scheduled access. A rabbit let out for an hour a day spends the other twenty-three confined, and the research on page {{P:enrichment}} measured time-of-day effects for a reason: rabbits are crepuscular, most active at dawn and dusk, and want the room then, not when it suits the household.

Scaling up for large and giant breeds

The figures above describe an average rabbit. A rabbit's sleeping and resting area should be about four times its own size, and a cage used as a bedroom should run 3 to 4 times its stretched-out length. A 4×4 ft (1.2×1.2 m) pen suits most rabbits but not a giant. A Flemish Giant weighs 15 to 20 lb (6.8 to 9 kg) and stretches to 2.5 to 4 ft (0.8 to 1.2 m), so its base enclosure needs to be at least 36 in (91 cm) wide, 36 in (91 cm) tall and 120 in (3 m) long, about 30 sq ft (2.8 m²) on its own, and that size doubles for each additional rabbit. Floor time outside it matters just as much.

Never House a rabbit in an aquarium or any solid-walled container. Ammonia from urine builds up in still air and irritates the airways over time, which is the slow road to snuffles (page {{P:flystrike}}). Wire floors are out for a different reason, covered on the next page.

Flooring, the litter box & litter

Soft under the feet, hay over the box, and the right litter in it.

Flooring

Skip wire flooring entirely. It contributes to sore hocks (page {{P:conditions}}). Vinyl or linoleum over plywood, washable rugs, or fleece all give better traction and are easier on a rabbit's feet. A waterproof tarp under the whole setup protects your actual floor from the inevitable accidents.

Weight changes the question. A heavy rabbit puts more pressure on its hocks, so a solid, cushioned floor such as fleece over a yoga mat is the standard for giants. Carpet is abrasive and best avoided, and the floor has to be kept clean so urine and droppings do not cake onto the feet.

Litter training, which is easier than it sounds

Rabbits eliminate almost reflexively while they eat, so the trick is not teaching a new behavior, it is routing an existing one. Put the hay directly in or immediately over the litter box, and most of the training does itself.

Use a big box

A large, cat-litter-box-style container, not the small corner boxes marketed for rabbits. It needs room to sit and eat at once.

Right litter

Paper-based or compressed wood-pellet litter, with the hay on top or in a rack directly above it.

Spay or neuter

If training stalls with an intact rabbit, this is often the reason. Spaying or neutering improves litter habits meaningfully (page {{P:neuter}}).

Never use these litters Clumping cat litter, which can cause serious problems if eaten, and pine or cedar shavings, whose aromatic oils irritate the airways. Both are sold in the small-pet aisle, and both are wrong here.

Temperature, heat & cold

No heat lamp and no dial to check: the room is the whole climate system.

Rabbits are far better at cold than at heat. They cannot sweat, and they shed heat mainly through the ears, where blood vessels dilate to carry warmth away. That is a much smaller radiator than a human's whole sweating skin, which is why a room that feels fine to you can already be too warm for a rabbit.

RangeWhat it means
61 to 72°F (16 to 22°C)The comfortable range. A normal indoor room needs no intervention.
About 80°F (26 to 27°C)The line. Heat risk climbs from here, so keep the room at or below it. Move to the coolest room and do not wait to see how the rabbit copes.
Temperature plus humidity of 150Add the room temperature in °F to the humidity percentage and keep the total at 150 or lower. Eighty-five degrees at 65% humidity adds up to 150, so a room that does not feel unbearable can already be a risk.
ColdA rabbit tolerates subzero outdoor temperatures with proper shelter. Indoors, a draft or damp bedding causes far more cold-related illness than a steadily cool room.

Heat stress arrives in an order

  1. Fast, shallow or open-mouth breathing. Sometimes visible as panting.
  2. Drooling or salivating, which can leave the fur around the mouth wet. Red or very hot ears often come with it.
  3. Weakness and reluctance to move, a rabbit lying stretched flat and hard to rouse.
  4. Severe signs: tremors, convulsions, collapse or unconsciousness. This is an emergency, and the first sign, not the fourth, is the point to act.

Do

  • Move the rabbit to the coolest room, ideally with air conditioning or good airflow away from direct sun.
  • Offer cool, not ice-cold, water. Do not force it.
  • Cool it gradually: a cool, damp towel or a light wipe of cool water on the ears and feet.
  • Call a vet straight away. Heat stress can look like it is resolving and then relapse.

Don't

  • Don't plunge a rabbit into cold or ice water. The shock can send an already-stressed animal into collapse.
  • Don't assume it will recover on its own once it looks calmer.
  • Don't leave a rabbit in a parked car, even briefly. A closed car crosses into heat stress well before it feels warm to you.
When the power fails in summer A frozen water bottle wrapped in a towel gives the rabbit a cool surface to lie against, which is not the same as a cold-water plunge. The full blackout plan is on page {{P:outage}}.

Indoors versus outdoors

A lifespan decision, not a preference, and the gap is larger than almost any other choice in this book.
WhereTypical lifespanWhy
Indoors8 to 12 years, some to 14 or 16You see the rabbit daily and closely, which is how illness is caught in the hours that matter.
OutdoorsAbout 3 to 5 yearsPredators, extreme weather, and health problems that go unnoticed without daily close contact.

The second half is what people underestimate. A rabbit outdoors is exposed to predators and heat, and it is also out of sight. The window between a rabbit going off its food and being in genuine danger is measured in hours (page {{P:signs}}), and nobody notices it from indoors.

If a rabbit does live outside

RiskWhat to do
PredatorsA backyard hutch does not protect a rabbit. Dogs, feral cats, raccoons and coyotes bend wire and open latches, and the mere presence of a predator can send a rabbit into a panic that ends in injury. Most predators hunt at night, so an outdoor rabbit comes inside each evening. By day, a sturdy covered pen is enough if people are close by.
DiggingDirt in a yard pen holds harmful bacteria and invites tunnels the rabbit can vanish into when it needs help. Give the digging instinct a box of clean straw instead.
HeatShade and airflow first, because rabbits overheat. Wet towels hung over the side of the enclosure, or ice frozen in cardboard cartons set inside, cool a rabbit that has to sit out the heat.
PoisonsA rabbit cannot tell a poisonous plant by taste or smell, and lawn chemicals and fertilizers are poison too. Check every plant in the yard, and keep the rabbit in a pen indoors or in the yard until the poisonous ones are gone.
InattentionA rabbit that is fed and watered but rarely handled or checked develops problems nobody notices until it is too late. Look at the rear end once or twice a day for flystrike (page {{P:flystrike}}).
Bring it in Even a well-kept outdoor rabbit comes inside at night, in extreme weather, in a heat wave and whenever it is ill, and its owner expects to catch problems later than with an indoor rabbit.

Rabbit-proofing the room

Chewing is not naughtiness. It is a set of teeth that never stop growing doing their job.

Proof the space rather than fighting the instinct. A rabbit loose in the house will bite through anything that looks like a cord or a string, and the shock from a live electric cord is the most immediate danger it faces. That makes cords the single most important step in preparing a room, and it is why the pen is ready before the rabbit comes home: it protects the rabbit while you assess the rest.

The room checklist

Every cord the rabbit can reach in split plastic tubing, or blocked off with furniture, outlets included
Cords that run across the rabbit's path, or under the couch, handled first: they are the most tempting
Every houseplant identified, and the poisonous ones moved out of reach
An uncarpeted floor where possible, since carpet fiber swallowed in quantity can block the gut
A chew and dig outlet next to each place the rabbit favors
Hay, plain wood, cardboard or untreated straw mats within reach

Why each one matters

HazardWhat to know
Electric cordsRabbits have an instinct to sever any cord or string. Watching a rabbit is not enough, because a bite through a cord takes a moment.
HouseplantsA rabbit cannot judge a plant by taste or smell, so it will eat a poisonous one that is within reach.
Carpet and synthetic fiberSwallowed in quantity, it can block the digestive system, and a blockage is an emergency.
Young rabbitsA rabbit under a year old jumps up, over and into furniture and misjudges falls, so it needs watching until it settles. A mature rabbit is less adventurous and can have unsupervised freedom after a period of observation.
Give the behavior somewhere to go Proofing alone moves the problem. Pair every block with an outlet: a dig box by the carpet corner a rabbit favors, and bamboo sticks, untreated willow or plain cardboard where it chews. A rabbit with nowhere to dig or chew picks somewhere, and not the somewhere you would choose. Neutering also lessens the urge to redecorate (page {{P:neuter}}).

Cleaning & hygiene

Frequent cleaning is health care as well as housekeeping.
How oftenWhat
Every dayClean the toilet area. Remove soiled litter and any wet bedding. Wash the water bowl rather than topping it up. Look at the rear end, fur and skin, twice a day in warm weather.
About once a weekClean the whole enclosure. Do it more often if the rabbit soils the space, and more often again with each additional rabbit you keep together.

A cleaning day, step by step

Empty the toilet area and wash it with a non-toxic cleaner
Make sure everything is completely dry before the rabbit goes back in
Put a little of the used bedding back, so the space smells familiar
Restock hay in the rack and over the litter box
Keep the toilet area apart from where the rabbit sleeps
Why a clean-out can upset a rabbit A clean-out disrupts a rabbit's routine, which is stressful for a prey animal. The used bedding left in is what keeps the space recognizable.

What dirty actually costs

ProblemHow it follows from a dirty space
Respiratory diseaseAmmonia from built-up urine and manure irritates the airways and sets a rabbit up for snuffles. Pneumonia cases rise with the ammonia level in the enclosure (page {{P:flystrike}}).
FlystrikeIn a warm spot, the mess draws flies, and flies lay eggs on soiled fur. A rabbit with a permanently mucky bottom has a cause, such as diet, teeth, weight or arthritis, that needs finding, not only cleaning.
Sore hocksThe floor has to be kept clean so urine and droppings do not cake onto the feet (page {{P:conditions}}).
