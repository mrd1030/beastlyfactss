Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling with plain water & no soap

The risk runs the direction people do not expect: not the frog hurting you, but you hurting the frog.

This is one of the few frogs that tolerates being picked up, and most White's tree frogs handle well once they are used to it, more so than the great majority of pet frogs and toads. What makes technique matter is the skin. Amphibian skin is permeable and absorbs what it touches, including oils, lotions, soap residue and chemicals from your hands. Even the natural oils and salts on clean human skin can damage it.

The technique

StepHow
HandsWash thoroughly with plain water only, no soap, immediately before handling, and rinse well so no lotion, sanitizer or soap remains
Or glovesPowder-free vinyl gloves (nitrile is a second choice), never latex, wetted with dechlorinated water. After lotion, cleaning chemicals or a manicure, gloves are the safer choice however well you have washed. Deaths have been reported in tadpoles exposed to latex, nitrile and, to a lesser extent, vinyl, so vinyl is the cautious choice for a froglet
Pick-upLet the frog walk onto a flat, wet hand rather than grabbing it, and support its body. Sit low, over something soft, in case it jumps
SessionRoughly 5 to 15 minutes, and no more than about twice a week
AfterwardBack into the enclosure, then wash your hands with soap and running water, away from the frog

First-timer mistakes

  • Handling with lotioned or chemical-exposed hands. By far the most common and most damaging mistake.
  • Handling too often, which adds stress even to a tolerant species.
  • Wetting hands or gloves with tap water straight from the faucet instead of treated water.
  • Treating handling as enrichment. For an amphibian it mostly is not; it is something the frog tolerates.
Salmonella, and children under 5 Healthy-looking amphibians carry Salmonella in the gut with no sign of illness, and it spreads hands and surfaces to mouth rather than by bites. Wash with soap and running water after touching the frog, its water or anything in the enclosure. Keep the frog and its equipment out of the kitchen. Children younger than 5 should not handle the frog or touch its enclosure at all, since they are the most likely to become seriously ill. Older children are supervised and wash every time.
What to expect A sedentary animal, so do not expect a lot of movement during a session. This is a calm, low-drama frog by amphibian standards. A frog that struggles or leaps away every time is asking for shorter sessions or a break, and a new frog gets none at all until it is through quarantine on page {{P:arrival}}.
Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Shedding, color, calling & normal behavior

Most of what worries a new owner is normal. This page sorts the normal from the worth-a-look.

Skin shedding you will rarely see

A White's tree frog sheds the whole outer layer of its skin on a regular cycle, and at room temperature that cycle is short: about every 2 days at 73 to 91°F (23 to 33°C) and every 3 to 4 days in cooler conditions. The frog pulls the skin off and eats it as it comes away, so a keeper rarely sees a shed happen and almost never finds shed skin lying in the enclosure. Shedding also does a job: it is one of the ways a frog clears microbes from its skin.

Color

A normal White's tree frog ranges from light blue-green through emerald to almost gray, often with scattered white or gold spots along the sides, and it can change shade somewhat. Its green is blue pigment under a yellow layer, and where that yellow layer is thin the frog looks blue. That is also why the species was named caerulea, blue: the first specimens shipped to England in 1790 had turned blue in preservative.

Calling, sleeping and sitting still

Males call, and call through the year, so a frog heard calling is a male. The species is nocturnal: it sleeps through the day pressed against cover, often tucked behind bark or leaves, and wakes in the evening to hunt. Long stretches of sitting in one place are normal for an animal this sedentary. In the wild, in the dry season, these frogs coat themselves in a secreted cocoon that holds moisture in.

A quick look at feeding time Evening feeding is the best health check you have, because it is when the frog is awake and moving. Clear eyes, a good grip, a quick strike at the insect, and smooth skin with no red flush: that takes ten seconds to see and catches most of the problems in Section 06 early.

What the research says

No enrichment study exists for this species. The closest and most useful one is on red-eyed tree frogs, another arboreal tree frog: Michaels, Antwis and Preziosi (2014) found that providing plants affected body size, growth rate and the bacterial communities on the skin, and the frogs showed a strong preference for planted areas over bare ones. That is a fitness result rather than an observer scoring activity, which is rare for amphibians: a 2014 review of the whole topic found only fourteen relevant primary research papers. Skin bacteria matter because they are part of how a frog defends itself against pathogens, chytrid included.

Priority order

1. Dense planting at several heights
2. Broad perches near the top of the enclosure
3. Humidity with real drying cycles, and treated water
4. Prey released into cover, not presented
5. Varying where and when food appears
6. Same-size company, if you want a group
7. Novelty, last, and gently
Move the food An ambush feeder will happily sit in one spot and be fed the same way forever. Release insects into the planting at night and vary where they go in. In dart frogs, simply changing the position of the food dish increased activity, which for an animal this sedentary is a real result from a free change, and more movement is part of the obesity fix on page {{P:obesity}}.
Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a frog, quarantine & the law

The healthiest frog is chosen well, moved gently and kept apart until it has proved itself.

Choosing one

Buy captive-bred. It is widely available, and wild-caught frogs often carry parasites and disease and adjust less well to captivity. A standard animal runs $20 to $60. Blue and other designer colors cost more, but a bluish frog in a store is not proof of a blue morph: the green comes from blue pigment under a yellow layer, the shade shifts, and a frog at a morph price should come from a breeder who can say what its parents were.

Pass on any frog showing listlessness, weight loss, cloudy eyes, skin discoloration or redness on the belly and legs, swelling, labored breathing, or loose stools. Ask what it has been eating and when it last ate, and watch it take an insect if you can.

Bringing it home

StepHow
Move it in a containerA small clear container, never a net, so the frog is never forced through mesh. A ventilated tub with a moist paper towel works for the trip home
Temperature firstLet the container sit beside the new setup until both read the same before the frog moves across. A fast temperature swing is one of the most common ways a new arrival gets stressed
Then leave it aloneLights, gradient and humidity cycle running for a few days before it arrives; no handling until quarantine ends

Quarantine: six to eight weeks

A new amphibian is kept apart for 6 to 8 weeks, the usual period. The shortest in use is 30 days, and 60 is preferred. Use its own tub or enclosure with paper towel substrate, its own spray bottle, dish and tools, in a different room if you can, and look after the established frogs first and the new one last. If it shows any illness partway through, the clock resets rather than pausing.

Use the window for a first vet exam: a fecal test for parasites and, ideally, a skin swab test for chytrid, the fungus that causes chytridiomycosis. Chytrid is the reason quarantine is close to mandatory for amphibians: the pet trade is a documented route by which an amphibian chytrid fungus has already spread between continents.

Never release a frog outdoors Not a healthy one, not an unwanted one. A captive frog carrying a pathogen becomes a wild exposure event the moment it is loose. A frog you cannot keep goes back to a breeder, a rescue or a reptile and amphibian society.

Where it is legal

No state-by-state review of this species has been done for this package, so it carries no state table. Check before you buy. Rules for nonnative amphibians run from wide open to closed: Nevada's list clears all nonindigenous amphibians except bullfrogs, and Oklahoma exempts nonindigenous reptiles and amphibians from its import permits. At the other end, Hawaii allows an animal only if it appears on its approved lists and bars anything never written down, Massachusetts runs a closed list of exempt species, and the District of Columbia's list of allowed animals names no amphibian at all. Your state wildlife agency is the answer that counts, and a city can be stricter than its state.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Group housing by size, sexing & breeding

They tolerate company well. The one rule is not optional.
Every frog in a group is a similar size A White's tree frog will attempt to swallow anything it can fit in its mouth, including a smaller frog. A mixed-size group is a feeding accident waiting to happen. Juveniles grow at different rates, so a group that started matched needs checking as it grows.

Setting up a group

  • Space: 24×18×24 in (61×46×61 cm) for two to four frogs of a similar size, against 18×18×24 in for one.
  • Cover: enough planting and cork that each frog can be out of the others' line of sight, and enough high perches that nobody is pushed to the floor.
  • Feeding: forceps feeding lets you see that each frog eats its share, so a dominant eater does not get fat while a shy one thins out. Check every frog against the ridge test on page {{P:bodycondition}}, not just the group.
  • A new frog goes through quarantine before it meets the group, page {{P:arrival}}. If one frog falls ill, it comes out to its own tub until the vet has seen it.

Most frogs for sale are juveniles, and a juvenile usually cannot be sexed: the adult features may not have appeared yet, and the species takes about two years to mature.

What happens if a pair breeds

A male and female kept together can breed. In captivity breeding follows the wet season the frogs get in the wild, and keepers who want eggs trigger it by imitating heavy summer rain. The male grips the female from behind in amplexus, which can last for days, and she lays 150 to 300 eggs in still water. Hatching starts 28 to 36 hours after laying.

The tadpoles are the long part. In good conditions some finish metamorphosis in 2 to 3 weeks, most are out of the water within 2 months, and a few stay tadpoles for up to a year. The froglets then take about two years to reach breeding age. That is hundreds of animals needing homes, food and space.

Company without the project Keep two of the same sex. Because juveniles are hard to sex, that usually means buying adults whose throats show which is which. Two males will call; two females are the quiet option.
