Section 04 · Handling, Company & Behavior

Choosing a frog, quarantine & the law

The healthiest frog is chosen well, moved gently and kept apart until it has proved itself.

Choosing one

Buy captive-bred. It is widely available, and wild-caught frogs often carry parasites and disease and adjust less well to captivity. A standard animal runs $20 to $60. Blue and other designer colors cost more, but a bluish frog in a store is not proof of a blue morph: the green comes from blue pigment under a yellow layer, the shade shifts, and a frog at a morph price should come from a breeder who can say what its parents were.

Pass on any frog showing listlessness, weight loss, cloudy eyes, skin discoloration or redness on the belly and legs, swelling, labored breathing, or loose stools. Ask what it has been eating and when it last ate, and watch it take an insect if you can.

Bringing it home

StepHow
Move it in a containerA small clear container, never a net, so the frog is never forced through mesh. A ventilated tub with a moist paper towel works for the trip home
Temperature firstLet the container sit beside the new setup until both read the same before the frog moves across. A fast temperature swing is one of the commonest ways a new arrival gets stressed
Then leave it aloneLights, gradient and humidity cycle running for a few days before it arrives; no handling until quarantine ends

Quarantine: six to eight weeks

A new amphibian is kept apart for 6 to 8 weeks, the period veterinary guidance calls typically adequate. Zoos run a 30-day minimum and prefer 60. Use its own tub or enclosure with paper towel substrate, its own spray bottle, dish and tools, in a different room if you can, and look after the established frogs first and the new one last. If it shows any illness partway through, the clock resets rather than pausing.

Use the window for a first vet exam: a fecal test for parasites and, ideally, a skin swab test for chytrid, the fungus on page {{P:chytrid}}. Chytrid is the reason quarantine is close to mandatory for amphibians: the pet trade is a documented route by which an amphibian chytrid fungus has already spread between continents.

Never release a frog outdoors Not a healthy one, not an unwanted one. A captive frog carrying a pathogen becomes a wild exposure event the moment it is loose. A frog you cannot keep goes back to a breeder, a rescue or a reptile and amphibian society.

Where it is legal

No state-by-state review of this species has been done for this package, so it carries no state table. Check before you buy. Rules for nonnative amphibians run from wide open to closed: Nevada's list clears all nonindigenous amphibians except bullfrogs, and Oklahoma exempts nonindigenous reptiles and amphibians from its import permits. At the other end, Hawaii allows an animal only if it appears on its approved lists and bars anything never written down, Massachusetts runs a closed list of exempt species, and the District of Columbia's list of allowed animals names no amphibian at all. Your state wildlife agency is the answer that counts, and a city can be stricter than its state.

What the research says

No enrichment study exists for this species. The closest and most useful one is on red-eyed tree frogs, another arboreal tree frog: Michaels, Antwis and Preziosi (2014) found that providing plants affected body size, growth rate and the bacterial communities on the skin, and the frogs showed a strong preference for planted areas over bare ones. That is a fitness result rather than an observer scoring activity, which is rare for amphibians: a 2014 review of the whole topic found only fourteen relevant primary research papers. Skin bacteria matter because they are part of how a frog defends itself against pathogens, chytrid included.

Priority order

1. Dense planting at several heights
2. Broad perches near the top of the enclosure
3. Humidity with real drying cycles, and treated water
4. Prey released into cover, not presented
5. Varying where and when food appears
6. Same-size company, if you want a group
7. Novelty, last, and gently
Move the food An ambush feeder will happily sit in one spot and be fed the same way forever. Release insects into the planting at night and vary where they go in. In dart frogs, simply changing the position of the food dish increased activity, which for an animal this sedentary is a real result from a free change, and more movement is part of the obesity fix on page {{P:obesity}}.

Shedding, color, calling & normal behavior

Most of what worries a new owner is normal. This page sorts the normal from the worth-a-look.

Skin shedding you will rarely see

A White's tree frog sheds the whole outer layer of its skin on a regular cycle, and at room temperature that cycle is short: about every 2 days at 73 to 91°F (23 to 33°C) and every 3 to 4 days in cooler conditions. The frog pulls the skin off and eats it as it comes away, so a keeper rarely sees a shed happen and almost never finds shed skin lying in the enclosure. Shedding also does a job: it is one of the ways a frog clears microbes from its skin.

Color

A normal White's tree frog ranges from light blue-green through emerald to almost gray, often with scattered white or gold spots along the sides, and it can change shade somewhat. Its green is blue pigment under a yellow layer, and where that yellow layer is thin the frog looks blue. That is also why the species was named caerulea, blue: the first specimens shipped to England in 1790 had turned blue in preservative.

Calling, sleeping and sitting still

Males call, and call through the year, so a frog heard calling is a male. The species is nocturnal: it sleeps through the day pressed against cover, often tucked behind bark or leaves, and wakes in the evening to hunt. Long stretches of sitting in one place are normal for an animal this sedentary. In the wild, in the dry season, these frogs coat themselves in a secreted cocoon that holds moisture in.

Section 05 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & finding a vet

Hardy for an amphibian, but amphibian illness moves fast, and treatment options narrow the longer it waits.
Call a vet today if The frog is lethargic and losing weight, especially if it is new or has been near a new animal. The belly or legs show redness or pinpoint bleeding. Skin is coming away in patches, or there is excess mucus. It cannot turn itself upright, or the body or limbs are swollen. Breathing is labored. It has been exposed to a cleaner, spray or pesticide. The jaw looks misshapen, a leg is bent, or it twitches or cramps after moving.
Book this week if The eyes look cloudy. The eardrum ridges are folding over. It has refused food with the temperature and humidity confirmed in range. Shed skin is lying uneaten. Stools have been loose, or absent, for longer than its normal pattern. The nose is scraped from rubbing on the glass.

Finding the right vet

Not every exotic vet sees amphibians, and amphibian medicine has real limits, which makes the vet you choose matter. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians runs a find-a-vet directory. Call ahead and ask whether the practice treats frogs, and whether it can run a skin swab for chytrid. An exam typically costs $50 to $150, and an exotic emergency visit fee alone often starts around $250 before treatment. Annual exams are recommended for exotic pets, and one during quarantine catches what arrived with the frog.

Getting the frog there

A well-ventilated plastic container with a moistened paper towel on the bottom, the lid covered or closed, kept at a steady temperature and out of the sun. The full transport routine is on page {{P:outage}}.

Obesity, fatty eyes & fatty liver

The defining health risk for the species, and the one entirely in the keeper's hands.

Obesity shows up in this species more than in almost any other pet frog, driven by the same enthusiastic appetite that makes it so satisfying to feed. It is the most common problem in captive adults, and unlike most of this section it is fixed at home.

Obesity
CauseOverfeeding, and fatty feeders in particular: waxworms and superworms as anything more than a weekly treat, free-feeding, and a bare enclosure that gives a sedentary frog no reason to move. Many amphibians keep eating as long as prey is available, so the frog will not stop on its own
SignsFat bulging visibly over the tympanum, the ridges above it sagging or folding over, and fat building in the armpits. General roundness is not the sign: a moderate shoulder roll is normal for this frog
ResponseThe ridge test on page {{P:bodycondition}}, then a diet change at home: fewer insects per sitting, fatty items cut out, and more planting and height to climb through
RecoverySlow and steady, judged monthly on the ridges and photos rather than by the week. Once ridges read defined, go back to the normal adult schedule on page {{P:feeding}}, not the old one
Fatty eyesCorneal lipidosis
CauseCholesterol and lipid deposited in the cornea, from a diet too high in fat
SignsA cloudy look to the eyes, or whitish plaques on them. In an obese frog, fat in the crests above the eyes can also start to block its vision
DiagnosisA vet exam. Cloudy eyes have other causes too, including the corneal swelling of a bacterial infection on page {{P:redleg}}, which is a far more urgent problem
RecoveryTreatment is largely ineffective, but correcting the diet may stop it getting worse. That is the strongest argument for catching weight gain at the ridge stage
Fatty liverHepatic lipidosis
CauseSustained obesity raises the risk of this serious fatty liver condition
SignsAn obese frog that also looks enlarged through the body from fat laid down inside it, loses its appetite, or becomes lethargic
ResponseA vet visit. Advanced cases with liver involvement are past a home diet fix
RecoveryDepends on how far it has gone, which is why the monthly ridge check exists
The whole prevention plan, in one line Adults 2 to 3 times a week, 3 to 4 insects, waxworms once a week at most, a dense tall enclosure, and a look at the eardrum ridges every month.

Chytridiomycosis

The fungal disease behind amphibian declines worldwide. In a pet, an emergency that quarantine prevents.

Chytridiomycosis is caused by the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, Bd for short. It is known to affect hundreds of amphibian species, frogs, toads and salamanders alike, and has been linked to wild population declines going back to the 1970s. It infects the skin, the organ a frog drinks and partly breathes through.

Chytrid
CauseBd, carried in by an infected or contaminated animal, its water, or equipment that touched either. A new frog that skipped quarantine is the usual route
SignsLethargy and weight loss. Brown to red discoloration of the skin, and reddening of the belly that can look like red-leg. Excessive skin shedding and excess mucus. Swelling, abnormal posture, incoordination, and losing the ability to turn upright. Sudden death can be the only sign
DiagnosisA real-time PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test on a swab of the skin, a lab test that detects the fungus's DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid, the genetic code). It is rapid and it is the test to ask for. A skin scraping under the microscope can show the organism, but it is not always seen
TreatmentVet-prescribed antifungal baths, typically itraconazole daily for about 10 to 11 days. Keeping the frog at the warm end of its range, as the vet directs, may help halt the infection
RecoveryIf it is diagnosed early, treatment can be effective. The vet decides on retesting. Everything the frog touched is disinfected or replaced, and the frog stays apart from any others until cleared
If you suspect it Isolate the frog at once in a clean tub with paper towel and treated water, wash your hands, use separate tools, and call the vet the same day. Do not wait to see if it eats. Do not move any water, plants or decor from that enclosure into another. Keep the frog in its normal range, within the 70 to 85°F (21 to 29°C) gradient, until the vet sets a treatment temperature.

Prevention is the real defense

  • Quarantine every new amphibian for 6 to 8 weeks, page {{P:arrival}}, and ask for a chytrid swab during it. Treatment options are limited once the disease takes hold.
  • Never share water, plants or tools between enclosures without disinfecting them.
  • Buy captive-bred, from a seller who quarantines.
  • Never release a frog, and never dump enclosure water outdoors where wild amphibians live.
The shed connection Frogs shed their skin every few days, and shedding helps clear skin microbes. In chytrid-infected frogs, heavier infections go with faster sloughing. So a frog suddenly shedding far more often than usual, or with shed skin lying uneaten, is a reason to book a swab.

Red-leg syndrome & bacterial infection

Usually traced back to a dirty or poorly ventilated enclosure, exactly the kind of thing an owner controls.
Red-legBacterial dermatosepticemia
CauseBacteria, typically Aeromonas, with Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, Citrobacter and others also found. These live in the environment and take hold in a frog stressed by dirty water, a stale dish, constant high humidity and poor ventilation
SignsA red flush on the pale skin of the legs and belly, progressing to pinpoint bleeding under the skin and red-purple patches. Lethargy, loss of appetite, emaciation, skin ulcers. Cloudy eyes can come with it, from corneal swelling
DiagnosisA vet takes blood, or fluid from the body cavity if there is any, for culture, so the antibiotic is matched to the bacteria. Chytrid can look similar and may be tested for at the same visit
TreatmentAggressive and immediate: antibiotics chosen by the culture, often with antifungals and fluids. A full review of the enclosure goes with it
RecoveryGuarded. Even with early, aggressive treatment the likelihood of death is high, which is why prevention and a same-day call matter. A frog that recovers goes back into a setup that has been fixed, not the one that made it sick
Red on the belly or legs is a same-day call Isolate the frog in a clean tub with treated water and paper towel, keep it at its normal temperature, and phone the vet. Do not try antibiotics or salt at home.

Bacterial and skin infections

CauseEnclosures kept too humid without adequate ventilation, or a water dish that is not cleaned often enough. A scraped nose or a wound gives bacteria a way in
SignsDiscolored or reddened patches, sores, excess mucus, swelling, a frog that is dull and off its food. Shed skin left uneaten can be an early hint
DiagnosisAlways a vet when signs appear: skin infections in frogs can be bacterial or fungal, and the treatment differs
RecoveryThe real fix is usually in the setup itself: humidity that dips on page {{P:humidity}}, a dish emptied daily on page {{P:water}}, and ventilation that is never sealed off
The husbandry pattern behind all of it Overfeeding drives obesity. A dirty or under-ventilated enclosure drives red-leg and the other bacterial infections. Skipping quarantine is how chytrid gets in. All three are preventable, and a reliable thermometer and hygrometer catch the humidity drift before it becomes an infection.

Metabolic bone disease & vitamin A

Two nutritional diseases, both prevented by the supplement schedule and the UVB tube.

Metabolic bone disease, also called nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, is frequently observed in captive amphibians. The frog's body pulls calcium out of its own skeleton to keep its blood level up, and the bones soften and deform.

Bone diseaseMetabolic bone disease
CauseToo little calcium and vitamin D3 in the diet, missing or expired UVB, and insects with an inverted calcium to phosphorus ratio: undusted, un-gut-loaded crickets are the classic route
SignsEarly on, twitching or spastic cramping after exertion, and weakness. Later, a misshapen or droopy jaw, broken long bones, a curved spine, bent limbs, bloating, and sometimes a cloacal prolapse (tissue pushed out of the vent, the frog's single rear opening). Advanced cases end in tetany
DiagnosisA vet exam and a review of the diet, the supplement routine and the UVB tube's age. Bring the jar and the date the tube went in
TreatmentCorrecting the husbandry, plus calcium given by mouth daily for around 30 days at a dose the vet sets, with UVB provided
RecoveryWeeks to months, and permanent deformities may remain. The aim is to stop it progressing
Prevention is cheap and simple Gut-load the insects, dust them on schedule (adults about once a week, juveniles about twice), use calcium with D3 if there is no UVB, and replace the UVB tube every 6 to 8 months. Page {{P:feeders}} and page {{P:uvb}}.

Vitamin A deficiency: short tongue syndrome

CauseToo little vitamin A reaching the frog, usually a diet of unsupplemented insects
SignsLethargy and wasting, and a frog that strikes at prey and misses because its tongue no longer works properly: the tongue's lining changes and it stops catching
DiagnosisA vet exam and diet history. The classic story is a frog that is plainly hungry and keeps failing to catch
RecoveryVet-directed supplementation and a corrected diet. Do not dose vitamin A at home: excess has been suggested to interfere with vitamin D metabolism, so more of everything is not the answer
Why it is easy to miss A frog with early bone disease still looks round and still eats. The first real clue is often behavior, weakness or a cramp after exertion, rather than appearance. Weak limbs, a droopy jaw or twitching are a vet visit, not a supplement change and a wait.
