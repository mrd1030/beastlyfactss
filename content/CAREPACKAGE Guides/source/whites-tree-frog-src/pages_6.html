Glossary, A to H

AmplexusThe mating grip, a male holding a female from behind. In this species it can last for days. Page {{P:group}}.
ArborealTree-dwelling. Why the enclosure is measured in height and the best perches go near the top.
BdBatrachochytrium dendrobatidis, the chytrid fungus behind chytridiomycosis. Page {{P:chytrid}}.
BioactiveA living, planted substrate that breaks down waste. An option for the floor of the enclosure.
CarotenoidsPigment compounds amphibians cannot make for themselves, one reason a multivitamin is part of the diet.
ChloramineA tap water disinfectant that does not leave water on standing. Needs a conditioner to split it. Page {{P:water}}.
Cloacal prolapseTissue pushed out through the vent, the frog's single rear opening. A sign of advanced bone disease. Page {{P:mbd}}.
CoelomThe body cavity. In an obese frog it looks enlarged from the fat laid down inside it.
Corneal lipidosisCholesterol and fat deposited in the cornea, seen as cloudy eyes. Page {{P:obesity}}.
D3, vitamin D3The form of vitamin D a frog makes in its skin under UVB light and needs in order to use the calcium in its food. Pages {{P:uvb}}, {{P:feeders}}.
DechlorinatorA water conditioner that neutralizes chlorine and chloramine. Everything that touches the frog goes through one.
DermatosepticemiaA bacterial infection of the skin that has spread into the blood. Red-leg is the common form.
DNADeoxyribonucleic acid, the genetic code in every cell. The chytrid test looks for the fungus's DNA. Page {{P:chytrid}}.
Gut-loadingFeeding the insects a calcium-rich diet for 24 to 72 hours before they go to the frog. Page {{P:feeders}}.
Hepatic lipidosisFatty liver disease, the serious end of obesity.
HygrometerA humidity meter, read mid-height to track the humidity cycle. Page {{P:humidity}}.

Glossary, I to Z

IUCNThe International Union for Conservation of Nature, whose Red List rates this species Least Concern.
Metabolic bone diseaseSoftened, deformed bones from too little calcium, vitamin D3 or UVB. Page {{P:mbd}}.
NocturnalActive at night. Why misting, feeding and health checks happen in the evening.
Nuptial padThe dark pad at the base of a breeding male's thumb, used to grip a female.
PCR swabPCR, polymerase chain reaction, is a lab test that finds DNA. On a skin swab it detects the chytrid fungus. Page {{P:chytrid}}.
PVCPolyvinyl chloride, the ordinary rigid plastic used for plumbing pipe, cut into tube hides. Page {{P:budget}}.
QuarantineKeeping a new frog apart for 6 to 8 weeks before it shares space or tools with any other amphibian. Page {{P:arrival}}.
RanavirusA viral disease of amphibians causing swelling and bleeding under the skin, frequently fatal. Page {{P:minor}}.
Red-legA bacterial infection, typically Aeromonas, named for the redness on the legs and belly. Page {{P:redleg}}.
Rostral abrasionA snout scraped raw from rubbing on glass or screen. Page {{P:skin}}.
RO waterReverse-osmosis water: filtered so far that almost nothing is dissolved in it. Never used for a frog without remineralizing. Page {{P:water}}.
Short tongue syndromeA tongue that no longer catches prey, from vitamin A deficiency. Page {{P:mbd}}.
SloughThe outer skin a frog sheds every few days and eats. Page {{P:behavior}}.
T5 HOT5 high output: a slim fluorescent tube 5/8 inch across, the usual UVB source. The percentage on the box is its UVB output, and 5 to 7% keeps it gentle. Page {{P:uvb}}.
TetanyUncontrolled muscle spasm, the advanced stage of metabolic bone disease.
TympanumThe round external eardrum behind the eye. The fat ridge above it is the body condition check. Page {{P:bodycondition}}.
UVBUltraviolet B, the band of ultraviolet light a frog uses to make vitamin D3, which it needs to use calcium. Page {{P:uvb}}.
UV index (UVI)UV is ultraviolet, the invisible light that includes UVB. The UV index (UVI) measures UVB strength at a point. Roughly 0.7 to 1.6 where this frog basks. Page {{P:uvb}}.
Vocal sacThe stretchy throat skin a male calls with: grayish and wrinkled on a male, plain white on a female.

Sources

Where the numbers in this package come from, so you can check them yourself.

Veterinary references for this species

  • Dr. Gregory Rich, Avian and Exotic Animal Hospital of Louisiana: White's Tree Frog Care Sheet. The ridge test, feeding by frog size, the 12 hour light cycle, heat outside the enclosure, aged and conditioned water, cleaning with plain hot water, the bark and paper-strip furnishing tips.
  • Dr. Michaela Betts, Exotics Keeper Magazine: A Vet's Care Guide for White's Tree Frogs. Feeding and dusting frequency by age, the clinical signs of obesity, a UV index of 0.7 to 1.6, signs of chytrid and red-leg, quarantine and chytrid testing.
  • Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital: White's Tree Frog Care. The 90°F (32°C) basking ceiling and 65°F (18°C) night floor, dish depth, the froglet feeding row, and the warning signs of illness.
  • Tree of Life Exotic Pet Medical Center: White's Tree Frog Care. Light cycle, humidity with drying periods, gloves, and the conservative 10 to 15-plus year lifespan.
  • VCA Animal Hospitals (originally Veterinary Centers of America): Frogs as Pets. Adult size, and this species as one of the few frogs suited to handling.

Amphibian medicine and husbandry

  • Merck Veterinary Manual: Infectious Diseases of Amphibians. Chytrid signs, PCR diagnosis, itraconazole baths and warmth as part of treatment; red-leg organisms, signs and culture; ranavirus.
  • Merck Veterinary Manual: Noninfectious Disorders of Amphibians. Metabolic bone disease signs and treatment, vitamin A deficiency, obesity, corneal lipidosis, rostral abrasions.
  • Merck Veterinary Manual: Environment and Husbandry for Amphibians. Ventilation, water free of chlorine and chloramine, UVB replacement at 6 to 8 months and glass blocking UVB, cedar and pine mulch, the 6 to 8 week quarantine, bleach disinfection, carotenoids.
  • Merck Veterinary Manual: Clinical Techniques in Amphibians. Transport container, what to bring to the vet, moistened gloves.
  • Dr. Douglas Mader, Reptiles Magazine: How to Recognize and Prevent Medical Ailments in Amphibians. Signs and response for dehydration, overheating, toxic exposure, gastric overload, corneal lipidosis, red-leg prognosis and parasites.
  • LafeberVet, McDermott and Pollock: Amphibian Handling and Restraint. Moistened gloves, minimizing handling, glove-type cautions.
  • Cramp and colleagues (2014), Conservation Physiology, and Ohmer and colleagues (2017), Scientific Reports. Skin sloughing intervals and the link between faster sloughing and chytrid load.
  • Michaels, Antwis and Preziosi (2014), PLoS ONE: plant cover and fitness in red-eyed tree frogs. Hurme and colleagues: enrichment for dendrobatid frogs.
  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention: Reptiles and Amphibians. Salmonella and the under-5 rule.
  • Smithsonian's National Zoo and Animal Diversity Web, University of Michigan: lifespan, clutch size, hatching and maturity.
  • Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians: find-a-vet directory.
  • Zoo Med Laboratories: the ReptiSun 5.0 and 10.0 UVB T5 HO product pages. Tube lengths and wattages.
