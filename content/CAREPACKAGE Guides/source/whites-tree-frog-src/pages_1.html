Why this package exists The White's tree frog is sold as the easy frog, and next to most amphibians it is: calm, sturdy, and one of the few that tolerates being picked up. The trouble is that its easiness gets read as low maintenance. A frog that eats everything offered gets fed everything, a care sheet that says "high humidity" gets followed with an enclosure that never dries, and tap water goes into the mister because it is what comes out of the faucet. Each of those is a common way this species gets sick. Every figure here is cross-checked against veterinary sources, listed on page {{P:sources}}, and every temperature and dimension is given in both imperial and metric.
Not a substitute for A veterinarian who sees amphibians. This is a husbandry reference for day-to-day care, and anything urgent is a professional call, not a page in this book. Amphibian medicine has real limits, which puts most of the weight on getting the setup right, and it also means the vet you need is not the nearest one. Find one before you need one, and write the number on page {{P:emergency}}.

Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameLitoria caerulea. Also called the dumpy tree frog or Australian green tree frog. Some recent references file it as Ranoidea caerulea
Native rangeNorthern and eastern Australia and southern New Guinea. Forest and woodland, and often around houses, where lit windows draw insects
Adult size3 to 4.5 in (7 to 11 cm). Females run larger: about 4 in (10 cm) against a male's 3 in (7.5 cm)
Captive lifespanAbout 16 years on average, with one recorded at 21 in human care. More conservative figures say 10 to 15-plus. Plan on well over a decade
ActivityNocturnal. Sleeps through the day tucked against cover and hunts in the evening
DietLive insects. Crickets as the staple, juveniles fed daily, adults 2 to 3 times a week. Page {{P:feeding}}

TemperatureCool side 70 to 75°F (21 to 24°C), basking area 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C), mid-70s°F (about 24°C) at night

Conservation statusLeast Concern on the IUCN Red List, the global list of species at risk kept by the International Union for Conservation of Nature. Buy captive-bred

Cost overview

The frog is $20 to $60 for a standard animal, more for a blue or other designer color. Itemized properly, the equipment comes to $289 to $528, and the terrarium is the biggest single line. With the frog, first supplements and a first vet exam, plan on $374 to $758. Page {{P:budget}} has every line.

Ongoing

Feeders, supplements, UVB tubes and water conditioner run $178 to $360 a year, about $15 to $30 a month, with substrate and a little electricity on top. An exam is $50 to $150, and an exotic emergency fee alone often starts around $250 before any treatment.

The number that should drive the decision Not the price. The lifespan. A frog bought for a twelve-year-old is very likely still alive when that child is finishing college. The purchase is the cheap part of this animal, and the routine on page {{P:routine}} is what you are actually signing up for.
Section 02 · Housing & Environment

The vertical enclosure & where it goes

An arboreal frog measures its home in height. The floor is where it goes to drink.

18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) is the standard minimum for one adult, with the 24 in as height, because this is a climbing species that spends its time off the ground. A 20-gallon-equivalent enclosure is comfortable for a single frog. For a small group of two to four, all of a similar size, step up to 24×18×24 in (61×46×61 cm). A front-opening glass terrarium with a mesh top is the usual choice: the door lets you work at frog level, and the mesh is where the UVB and heat sit.

The lid has to fit and lock. Those toe pads climb glass without effort, and an escaped frog in a heated house dries out fast. Height only counts if it is furnished: sturdy branches, broad leaves and cork mounted high, so the frog can sit near the top, supported and out of sight.

Where the enclosure goes

Out of direct sunA glass box in a sunny window heats far past the basking target, and a frog cannot leave. The heat on page {{P:temperature}} comes from a controlled source, never the window

The temperature gradient & heating

A gentle, reliable gradient. Intense heat is not the goal with this species.
Zone°F°C
Cool side, low in the enclosure70 to 7521 to 24
Basking area, high on one side80 to 8527 to 29
Nightmid-70sabout 24
Do not let the warm end pass9032
Act if anywhere falls below6518

The two bold lines are the limits a veterinary care sheet gives for this species: a basking area that does not exceed 90°F (32°C) and a night that does not fall below 65°F (18°C). They are the edges, not targets.

Why gentle

A frog sets its own temperature by moving between warmer and cooler spots, so the gradient exists to give it a choice rather than to push heat into it. A frog that wants the warm branch will use it, and one that wants to cool down needs the low, cool side to go to. In most homes the room already supplies the cool side, and the only equipment needed is a small source that lifts one upper corner into the basking range.

The heat source

OptionHow it is used
Low-wattage basking bulbA small bulb over the mesh at one side, outside the enclosure. A higher-wattage bulb overshoots the safe range in a box this size
Side-mounted heat matOn the outside of one side wall, not under the floor, since this frog lives high up and the substrate holds water
ThermostatEither source runs through one, so a hot room or a failed timer cannot cook the enclosure. Its probe goes where the frog actually sits
Two readingsDigital, one high at the warm end and one low at the cool end, from a two-probe unit or a second thermometer. Analog dials drift, and one reading tells you nothing about a gradient
Never inside the enclosure Every heat source sits outside the glass or on top of the mesh. A frog climbs everything, and a bulb, mat or heat rock it can reach is a burn. Its belly skin is thin and permeable, and a sluggish, cold frog does not move off a hot surface fast enough.

Section 02 · Housing & Environment

The humidity cycle that dips

The detail first-time keepers get backwards: too wet, all the time, causes more disease than a dip between mistings.

Hold a baseline of 50 to 60%, let misting push it to 70 to 80% once or twice a day, and then let it fall back. Mist in the evening, when this nocturnal frog wakes up, so the spike arrives when it is active and drinking off the leaves. Constant, unbroken high humidity is directly linked to bacterial and red-leg problems, the conditions on page {{P:redleg}}, so resist the instinct to keep everything perpetually damp. The waxy coating on this frog's skin is part of why it tolerates drier air than most tree frogs.

Holding the cycle in practice

  • Read it twice. A digital hygrometer mid-height, away from where you spray. Read it an hour after the evening mist and again the next afternoon. Those two numbers are the cycle; a single reading whenever you walk past tells you almost nothing.
  • Mist the surfaces, not the frog. Leaves, glass and cork, with dechlorinated water from page {{P:water}}, until they bead.
  • Too dry between mistings? Cover part of the mesh top, add live plants, or keep the substrate lightly damp. Change one thing, then wait two days to judge it.
  • Never dries? Uncover mesh, mist less, and check the substrate is moist rather than soggy. Condensation all day, a sour smell or mold are the signs.
Ventilation is half of humidity Moist air that never moves is what bacteria want. Veterinary husbandry guidance for amphibians puts adequate ventilation at 1 to 2 fresh air changes an hour, which in a home terrarium means a mesh top or vents that are never fully sealed. Live plants help both ways: they hold humidity and they freshen the air.

The water that is safe to mist with

A frog drinks through its skin. Whatever is in the mister and the dish goes straight into the animal.

Use only dechlorinated tap water or spring water, for the dish and for misting. Never distilled or reverse-osmosis water. Distilled and RO water are usually not electrolyte-balanced, and without rebalancing they can be fatal to an amphibian. That sounds backwards, since purer water seems safer, but an animal that exchanges water and salts through its skin is not helped by water with nothing in it.

WaterVerdictWhy
Tap, with a conditionerUseThe practical default. Pick a conditioner that states it neutralizes both chlorine and chloramine
Bottled spring waterUseFine for the dish and the mister. Check the label says spring, not distilled
Tap, left standingOnly ifStanding open for 24 to 48 hours lets chlorine leave. It does nothing to chloramine, which needs a conditioner such as sodium thiosulfate to split it. Unless you know your supply uses no chloramine, treat the water anyway
Fresh, untreated tapNeverChlorine and chloramine are fine for people and harmful to permeable skin
Distilled or RONeverNot electrolyte-balanced. Can be fatal without remineralizing

There is no published hardness target for this species. The rules with real backing are the ones above, plus water free of ammonia, pesticides and heavy metals. Treat the water before it goes in, not after.

The water dish

Wide enough for the frog to sit in and soak, and shallower than the frog is tall when it sits with its legs folded. Tree frogs are weak swimmers and a deep dish can drown one, a froglet especially. A heavy, smooth dish that will not tip is easiest to keep clean.

Empty, rinse and refill it every day, or every other day at the very least. The frog soaks and defecates in it, and a dish left to sit is the infection source behind the bacterial and skin problems on page {{P:redleg}}. Refill with treated water at room temperature, never straight from the faucet.

Keep a jug of ready water Treat a gallon jug at a time, label it, and use it for both the mister and the dish. It makes the right water the convenient water, and it is the water you will want on hand during a power cut, page {{P:outage}}. A dedicated spray bottle that has never held cleaner or plant food is part of the same habit.
A frog living in its dish is telling you something Soaking is normal. A frog that sits in the water most of the day and night is usually too warm or too dry, so check the gradient on page {{P:temperature}} and the cycle on page {{P:humidity}}. If both read right, it is a reason to look harder at the frog: page {{P:redflags}}.
