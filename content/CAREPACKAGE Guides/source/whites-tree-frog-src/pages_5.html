Emergency & quick targets

Call the vet now if Lethargy and weight loss, above all in a new frog. Redness or pinpoint bleeding on the belly or legs. Skin coming away in patches, or excess mucus. Cannot turn itself upright. Swelling of the body or limbs. Labored breathing. Exposure to a cleaner, spray or pesticide. A misshapen jaw, bent limbs, or twitching after moving. Found dry, tacky and limp.

First 30 days

The first month sets the habits. Quarantine runs past it, to six to eight weeks.

Day 1

Move the frog in a container, matching temperature before it crosses over
Photograph it from the side for a ridge baseline, and weigh it in a lidded tub if you have a gram scale
Fill in page {{P:emergency}} and the equipment dates on page {{P:equiplog}}
Lights off and leave it alone

Week 1

No handling. Evening misting, and check the hygrometer an hour later and the next afternoon
Offer the first feeding in the evening once it has settled, and count what is eaten
Dish emptied and refilled daily with treated water
Watch for normal stools, and note anything odd on page {{P:ownerlog}}

Week 2

First vet visit inside quarantine: fecal exam, and ask about a chytrid skin swab
Settle the feeding days and the dusting day, and write them on page {{P:emergency}}

Week 3

Adjust the humidity cycle if it is not dipping back to 50 to 60%
Start releasing insects into the planting rather than presenting them
Still no handling, and still the established frogs first and this one last

Week 4

First monthly ridge check against the day 1 photo, page {{P:bodycondition}}
Review the feeding counts. Ridges sagging already means fewer insects
Fill in the pet-sitter sheet, page {{P:sitter}}, before you need it

Weeks 5 to 8

Quarantine continues to 6 to 8 weeks; the clock resets if it shows any illness
Act on the fecal result rather than filing it
Once quarantine is done and the frog is eating well, first short handling sessions with wet hands, page {{P:handling}}
If it is joining others, check sizes match before it goes in, page {{P:group}}
A quiet first month is a good first month A new frog that hides by day, eats in the evening and sheds out of sight is doing everything right. The things to watch for are the red flags on page {{P:redflags}}, not a lack of activity.

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

Mist in the evening with treated water as the frog wakes
Empty, rinse and refill the water dish
Spot clean droppings off leaves, branches and glass
Glance at both temperatures and the hygrometer
Juveniles: feed. Pull leftover insects a few hours later

Two or three times a week

Adults: 3 to 4 gut-loaded insects in the evening, and count them
Handling, if any: 5 to 15 minutes, twice a week at most

Weekly

Dust: adults about once, juveniles about twice
Wash bark, moss or paper lining as needed; scrub the dish
Check every branch and perch is still anchored

Monthly

Ridge check and side photo, logged on page {{P:ownerlog}}
Sniff the substrate: replace soil that smells or stays waterlogged
Move the feeding spot or a perch for a little novelty

Every 6 to 8 months

Replace the UVB tube, even if it still lights

Yearly

Wellness exam with a fecal test
Supplements past their date replaced

Seasonal

Summer: is the enclosure out of the sun? Know how you will keep the warm end under 90°F (32°C)
Winter: check the night low against 65°F (18°C), and watch that heating dries the air
What a good week looks like The hygrometer at 70 to 80% an hour after the evening mist and back to 50 to 60% by the next afternoon. The cool side 70 to 75°F (21 to 24°C), the basking area 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C). A clean dish every morning. A frog that sleeps high up by day, strikes cleanly at its insects in the evening, and leaves no shed skin lying around.
If you only keep one habit Count the insects. Overfeeding is this species' most common problem, and it happens one generous handful at a time. A number on the log each feeding day is what turns the ridge test from a surprise into a trend.

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

The two edges are 65°F (18°C) and 90°F (32°C). Most of this page is keeping the frog between them, and damp.

When the power goes out

  • Put a battery thermometer where the frog sits. Act if it is heading below 65°F (18°C).
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold in the heat that is left.
  • Or move the frog in a ventilated tub with damp paper towel to the warmest room, usually one without exterior walls.
  • A wrapped hand warmer or hot water bottle beside the tub, never touching the frog.
  • Keep misting by hand with your stored treated water: the dish and the humidity still matter.
  • No candles, gas stove or open flame near the enclosure. Hold off feeding until warmth is back.
  • More than a day below 65°F (18°C): board the frog with a vet or a friend who has power.

When it gets too hot

  • Turn the heat source off first. In a heat wave the room is the heater.
  • Get the enclosure out of any sun and away from exterior walls, to the coolest room in the house.
  • More air: uncover the mesh, and run a fan across the room, not into the enclosure.
  • Make sure the dish is full of fresh treated water at room temperature, and mist the surfaces.
  • A frozen bottle wrapped in a towel can sit beside the enclosure, never inside it.
  • Warm end past 90°F (32°C) with a frog that is hyperactive, uncoordinated or lethargic, the signs of overheating: cool the room, give it fresh treated water at room temperature, and call the vet.

One tier stricter than a desert reptile, because the misting and the dish are daily jobs. The hand mister and treated water are what sitters most often get wrong: leave the jug labeled.

Transport and vet trips

  • A clean, well-ventilated plastic container with a moistened paper towel on the bottom and the lid on. No loose substrate and no water dish to slosh.
  • Set it inside a dark bag, box or small cooler to hold the temperature steady and keep the view calm.
  • In cold weather warm the car first, and on a trip of more than about 30 minutes below 65°F (18°C) add a wrapped hand warmer beside the container, not touching it. In hot weather cool the car first and keep the container out of the sun.
  • Never leave the frog in a parked car, hot or cold. Check it at stops rather than only on arrival.
Outage kit, packed before you need it Two hand warmers or a hot water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated tub with paper towel, a full jug of treated water, and the vet and emergency clinic numbers on paper, not only in a phone that might die.

Ridges: write U for none visible (underweight), OK for defined, O for sagging or folding (obese). Humidity: the reading an hour after the evening mist, and the next afternoon's.

Why bother logging For this frog, the feeding count and the ridge reading are the early warning system. A frog drifting toward obesity shows up as a column of generous numbers weeks before it shows up as fat over the eardrum. Sixteen rows is four months, long enough to see a real trend rather than a bad week, and it is the first thing a vet will want to see.
