Cost overview

Initial setup

$175 to $495 for equipment, plus $150 to $215 for the first vet exam and fecal test. The gecko itself is $20 to $45 for a wild type, more for a morph.

Monthly ongoing

$32 to $91 for insects, supplements, substrate, electricity and averaged vet care. Most owners land between $250 and $700 a year all in.

Budget for the worst case too An annual wellness exam runs $85 to $105. A sick or emergency visit runs $100 to $800 or more, and diagnostics or impaction surgery push it higher again. Treat a small emergency fund as part of the setup cost, not an optional extra.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Unlike most geckos, leopard geckos have true eyelids and can blink. They also lack the toe pads other geckos climb glass with.
  • That fat tail is roughly a quarter of the gecko's body mass and a third of its length. It is a working fat reserve, which is why tail thickness is the best health check you can do at a glance.
  • Over 100 color morphs exist in captivity, and pigment matters practically: pale morphs burn under UVB a normally pigmented gecko handles fine.

Housing & enclosure

36×18×18 in (91×46×46 cm) is the current minimum for one adult, and bigger is always fine. The 20 gallon long tank most older care sheets still name, roughly 30×12×12 in (76×30×30 cm), is no longer considered adequate: it is too short to hold a real temperature gradient from end to end, which is the whole point of the enclosure. Hatchlings can start in something as small as a 10 gallon while they are small, then move up.

Leopard geckos are terrestrial and cannot climb smooth glass, so floor space matters far more than height. Go for a front-opening, well-ventilated enclosure with an escape-proof top. House one gecko per enclosure: they are not a social species, two males will fight, and even females that seem to tolerate each other compete for the warm hide.

Never Run a heat mat without a thermostat. An unregulated mat is a genuine burn and fire risk, and thermal burns from an unregulated heat source are one of the most common injuries a reptile vet sees in this species. Skip hot rocks entirely: they heat unevenly and burn geckos that are sitting on them for warmth.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Hides should be snug enough that the walls touch the gecko's sides. An oversized hide gives no security, and a gecko without security stops using the warm end properly.
  • Add rock, slate or cork for nail filing and low climbing. Leopard geckos are not arboreal, but they explore every inch of floor and will use a ledge.
  • The humid hide belongs on the cool or middle side. Directly over the mat it dries out in hours, and cooks in between.

Temperature, humidity & the three hides

ZoneTarget (°F)Target (°C)
Warm hide floor (belly heat)88 to 9231 to 33
Ambient warm side80 to 8427 to 29
Cool side70 to 7821 to 26
Night drop65 to 7018 to 21
Humidity30 to 40% through the enclosure, 70 to 80% inside the humid hide only

Measure the warm hide floor with an infrared thermometer, pointed at the surface the gecko lies on rather than the air above it. Use digital probes for ambient air and humidity, mid-enclosure. Stick-on analog dials are inaccurate enough to be worse than nothing.

The three hides are the husbandry, not the decoration

A warm dry hide, a cool dry hide and a humid hide are the minimum, and the humid hide is the one people skip. Line it with damp sphagnum moss or damp paper towel, genuinely damp rather than swampy or bone dry. A gecko uses it in the days before a shed to soften the old skin, and it prevents most of the stuck shed on toes, tail tips and eye caps that becomes a vet visit later. Leopard geckos thermoregulate by moving between zones rather than basking, so an enclosure that is warm end to end is as much of a problem as a cold one.

Humidity: the detail people get wrong

Keep the enclosure itself at 30 to 40% and put the moisture in one box rather than in the air. Raising whole-enclosure humidity to reach 70% is not a substitute for a humid hide, it just adds a respiratory infection risk. If the room is very dry, add a second damp hide rather than misting the tank.

UVB: optional, and worth doing anyway

Leopard geckos do not require UVB the way a bearded dragon does, and were kept without it for decades. The research has moved: controlled work found even brief low-level exposure, as little as two hours a day, measurably raises blood vitamin D3 through the skin. Keep the output low, a UVI of 0.5 to 1.5 at the warm hide and 0.5 to 0.7 for pale morphs. Distance is on page 7.

Thermostats, probes & UVB distance

The thermostat is the safety device

A heat mat has no idea how hot it is. Left unregulated it will climb well past the target on a warm afternoon, and because a gecko seeks belly heat it will stay sitting on the hot spot rather than moving off it. That is how thermal burns happen. An on/off thermostat is right for a mat, which changes temperature slowly; a ceramic heat emitter reacts faster and wants a pulse proportional or dimming unit that eases output down before it overshoots. Set it to the warm hide floor target, not to the room, and re-check with an infrared gun after any change of season, substrate depth or hide.

Probe placement decides whether the number means anything. The probe belongs flat on the floor inside the warm hide, taped down so the gecko cannot shift it, on the same surface the animal lies on. A probe stuck to the glass wall, hanging in the air, or sitting on the cool side reads a different temperature than the one that matters, and the thermostat will happily hold the wrong number all year.

Timers and the light cycle

Put the lights on a plug-in timer so day length never depends on you remembering. Twelve hours on and twelve off is the standard cycle; some care references run the lights up to 14 hours in summer. Skip colored night bulbs entirely, red and blue included: they disrupt sleep in a crepuscular animal. If the room falls below the night floor, use a ceramic heat emitter, which gives heat with no light, on its own thermostat.

UVB distance and probe placement

Distances are for a low-output tube, a 6% or 5% T5 HO, mounted over the warm third. Keep the gecko no closer than 10 in (25 cm) to the lamp, and treat a mesh lid as a 30 to 50% loss of output, which means the tube sits closer to hold the same reading. Higher-output desert bulbs are the wrong tool for this species. Replace UVB tubes every 12 months even if they still light.

Measure, don't assume An infrared thermometer costs a fraction of one vet visit and settles the two questions that matter most: what the warm hide floor actually reads, and whether it holds at 3 p.m. on a hot day. Check it monthly and after any change. Keep a second thermometer away from the thermostat probe and read it daily too: a thermostat stuck on, or a probe the gecko has shifted, gives no other warning.

Substrate, furnishings & handling

Substrate

Paper towel or tile are the safest choices and the right default for anyone newer to the hobby: they remove impaction risk entirely and make a stool check trivial. Bioactive soil and sand mixes work, but they are an advanced setup, not a starting point.

Never Calcium sand, or any fine loose sand. It is marketed as digestible and is not; a gecko licking it for calcium or catching a mouthful with an insect is the classic route to impaction. Crushed walnut shell and corncob bedding carry the same risk and add nothing.

Furnishings checklist

Warm dry hide, snug, directly over the mat
Cool dry hide at the far end
Humid hide with damp moss, cool or middle side
Shallow water dish the gecko can climb out of
Slate, rock or cork flat for nail filing and low climbing
Shallow dish of plain calcium, left in permanently

Handling

Wait one to two weeks after a new gecko comes home, as a benchmark rather than a countdown: do not start until it has stopped bolting for cover and is eating normally. A gecko under about 3 in (8 cm) waits until it reaches roughly 6 in (15 cm). Then start with 5 minute evening sessions every other day, building to 10 to 20 minutes. Let it walk onto your hand rather than grabbing from above, and stay low so a jump is a short fall.

  • Never lift a leopard gecko by the tail. They drop it, called caudal autotomy, and the tail is roughly a quarter of the body mass and a third of the length. It regrows in 4 to 8 weeks but comes back shorter, thicker and visibly different.
  • No handling during a shed, and none for about 48 hours after a large meal. Chirping, squeaking, rapid tail waving or a fast retreat all mean the session is over.
Salmonella Healthy reptiles shed Salmonella without being ill, and one negative test clears nothing. Not a turtle problem: a CDC investigation into pet-gecko infections, closed January 2026, counted 113 cases across 36 states. Wash with soap after every contact with the gecko or its enclosure. Never clean gear in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink or a bath people use; keep a plastic tub for it. Do not kiss the gecko. Under-fives, over-65s, pregnancy and a weakened immune system are the risk groups, and the CDC line for under-fives is not to handle reptiles at all.

First days in a new home

Run the enclosure for a day or two before the gecko arrives, so the thermostat has been checked at its warmest and coldest. For the first week leave the animal alone apart from fresh water and a daily look: no handling, no rearranging, no worrying about refused meals. Offer food each evening anyway. Hiding all day and refusing food are normal after a move; weight loss over two weeks is not.

Diet & feeding by age

Leopard geckos are obligate insectivores: live invertebrates and nothing else. Their nutrition is decided less by which insect you pick than by what that insect ate first (page 11) and what you dusted it with.

Supplement schedule

Plain calcium is the routine dusting; calcium with D3 is a twice-weekly backup for a setup without UVB, and it is one or the other, not both stacked. If you are running UVB and still dusting D3 at every meal, drop the D3 to once a week or stop it.

Water and the calcium dish

Fresh dechlorinated water in a shallow dish the gecko can climb out of, refreshed daily and scrubbed weekly. The separate plain calcium dish stays in permanently: geckos self-regulate from it, and one suddenly eating a lot of it is worth noticing.

Never feed Fireflies, which are genuinely toxic, wild-caught insects of any kind, wild hornworms (bright green, as opposed to the safe blue-green captive-bred ones), or any fruit or vegetable. The full never-feed list is on page 12.

Commercially raised only, gut-loaded first, and no wider than the space between the eyes.

Sizing and presentation

Prey wider than the gap between the eyes is the second most common impaction cause after loose substrate. Hatchlings take insects around 1/4 in (6 mm); after that, measure against the gecko rather than the calendar. Tong-feeding controls the portion, keeps insects out of the substrate, and stops a gecko learning that fingers arrive with food.

Gut-loading: what to feed the feeders

A gecko's nutrition is mostly decided 24 to 48 hours before the insect is ever offered.

Feeder insects are a poor calcium source, and the problem is the ratio rather than the raw amount: phosphorus competes with calcium for absorption, and the accepted target for insectivorous reptiles is roughly 2 parts calcium to 1 part phosphorus, or 1:1 at minimum. Unsupplemented crickets sit near 1:9. Mealworms are worse, in the region of 1:14. An insect is a container, so what is in its gut when the gecko eats it gets eaten too. That is the whole mechanism, and it only works if the diet you put in front of the insect is genuinely calcium-rich.

Gut-load foodTierNotes
Commercial high-calcium gut-load dietStapleBuy one sold on its calcium content rather than one that just says "gut-load" on the front. The reliable option, and it works for crickets as readily as roaches.
Collard greensStapleCalcium-dense and low in oxalates, which is the combination that actually moves the ratio.
Mustard greens and turnip greensStapleSame profile as collards. Rotate them so the insects keep eating.
Dandelion greensStapleExcellent, and free if the lawn is genuinely unsprayed. If in doubt, buy them.
Butternut squashStapleCalcium-dense, holds moisture, and insects take it readily.
Calcium-fortified water gel or cubesStapleInsects drown in an open dish. A fortified gel gives water and calcium through the same route, and insects drink even when they will not eat a dry diet.
Carrot, potato, appleOccasionalKeeps insects alive and hydrated, which is worth doing. As a calcium intervention it does close to nothing, so it is not gut-loading on its own.
Spinach, beet greens, Swiss chardNeverThey read as healthy and do contain calcium, but they are high in oxalic acid, which binds that calcium into a form nothing can absorb. Self-defeating as a gut-load.

Timing: 24 to 72 hours on the diet, with 48 hours the figure most often cited. Too short and the insect has not eaten enough for its gut contents to matter. Too long stops helping, because gut-loading is not cumulative, an insect only carries what is in its digestive tract right now. Insects start voiding those contents as soon as they leave the food, so feed them out within a few hours of taking them off it. Running a standing colony is simpler: leave the gut-load available all the time rather than treating it as a two-day event before each feeding.

Gut-load and dust, not one or the other A properly formulated high-calcium gut-load can in principle get an insect to an acceptable calcium to phosphorus ratio on its own. In practice, dust as well: you cannot see how much of the diet a given cricket actually ate, and dusting is the cheap insurance that covers the gap. Page 9 has the schedule.

Why a leopard gecko stops eating

  1. The warm side is too cool. The most common fixable cause by a distance. Check the warm hide floor before anything else.
  2. A shed is coming. Many geckos go off food for a day or two either side of one. Appetite returns when it is done.
  3. Brumation. A natural winter slowdown cued by cooler temperatures and shorter days, with reduced activity and brief waking to drink.
  4. Stress. A new enclosure, too much handling, or an incompatible tankmate. It resolves when things settle.
  5. Breeding season. Both sexes may eat less during courtship, and gravid females often eat less as they near laying.
  6. Prey boredom. Some geckos lose interest in a repeated feeder and pick straight back up on something different.
  7. Illness or impaction. Refusal alongside bloating, straining, loose stool, poor shedding or sunken eyes points at a medical cause, not a cycle.
When a refusal becomes a vet visit Watch the tail, not the calendar. Estimates for "still probably fine" in a healthy adult run from about two weeks to a month, and no one agrees on a day count. A tail that has visibly thinned over a couple of weeks is the concrete signal. Go sooner for lethargy beyond normal brumation behavior, bloating, straining, incomplete shedding, sunken eyes, or wrinkled skin. Juveniles have far less margin.
The never-feed list, in one place Fireflies and lightning bugs. Wild-caught insects, spiders and centipedes of any kind. Wild hornworms, the bright green ones. Fruit, vegetables, purees and baby food. Crested gecko diet and other fruit-based powders. Anything larger than the space between the gecko's eyes. Anything from a bait shop.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • One hide instead of three. A single hide forces the gecko to choose between feeling safe and being the right temperature, and it removes the humid hide that prevents most shedding problems.
  • A heat mat with no thermostat, or a probe in the wrong place. Both produce the same result: a surface that reads fine when you check it and climbs past 100°F (38°C) when you do not.
  • Mealworms and waxworms as the staple diet. The fastest route to an obese adult, and obesity in this species shows up as fat pads at the neck and armpits long before the owner calls it a problem.

Enrichment, by priority

Leopard geckos are one of the few reptiles with real published enrichment research behind them, and it is specific enough to change what is worth buying. Enrichment comes after correct belly heat, hides and humidity, never instead of them. In this order:

1. Thermal choice and the three hides

Thermal enrichment produced one of the two strongest responses in the Bashaw study. A warm dry hide, a cool dry hide and a maintained moist hide are what let a gecko pick its own microclimate without giving up cover. This is the foundation; nothing below compensates for missing it.

2. Feeding and hunting presentation

The other strong result. Tong-feeding, short supervised insect hunts inside the enclosure, a rolling dispenser that sheds insects as it is nudged, and simple timed-release prey containers all beat a static bowl. Feed in the evening, when the animal is naturally hunting.

3. Low climbing structure and cover

Cork flats and tubes, low sturdy branches and stacked slate give somewhere to rest off the floor and visual cover that still allows movement. Height matters far less here than for an arboreal species.

4. A dig box

A single box of damp, safe substrate satisfies real digging behavior, which shows up especially in some females, without turning the whole floor into loose substrate. Paper towel or tile stays the safe primary surface.

5. Light novelty rotation

A new piece of cork, a mild rearrangement. Some value, genuinely last on the list, and not a substitute for anything above it. Rotate monthly rather than weekly; a gecko that has to re-learn its enclosure every few days is not being enriched.

6. What the research says does not work

Mirrors and other visual enrichment were the weakest of the five types tested. Not harmful, just close to pointless, and the same goes for exercise wheels. Geckos in enriched housing showed better welfare indicators than those in sparse setups, and chose the enriched side in preference tests, so the answer is more of items 1 to 4, not novelty for its own sake.

Sexing, growth & body condition

Telling male from female

Two features, both just in front of and behind the vent, and both easier to read as the gecko approaches maturity at 9 to 18 months. Males show a row of enlarged preanal pores in a V shape ahead of the vent, which produce a waxy secretion used for scent marking, and a pair of hemipenal bulges at the tail base just behind it. Females have pores that are much smaller or absent and no bulges. Lift the tail gently to about 45 degrees in good light rather than restraining the animal, and never hold the tail itself. Hatchling sexing at a shop is a guess; recheck at six months and again at a year.

Growth reference

Ranges are deliberately wide and healthy geckos fall outside them. What matters is the trend: steady gain for a juvenile, a stable weight for an adult. Weigh weekly in grams on a kitchen scale and log it on page 30.

AgeTotal lengthWeight
Hatchling3 to 4 in (8 to 10 cm)2 to 8 g, sources differ

6 months5 to 6 in (13 to 15 cm)25 to 60 g
18 months and up (adult)8 to 11 in (20 to 28 cm)40 to 80 g
Adult male vs femaleMales 8 to 10 in, females 7 to 8 inMales 60 to 90 g, females from about 45 g

Published adult lengths run from 6.5 to 11 in (17 to 28 cm) depending on the source, which is a real disagreement rather than a typo; see page 34. Most geckos reach full length by about 18 months and keep filling out for another six months after that.

Body condition at a glance

Underweight

Tail narrower than the neck, or visibly thinning week to week. Hip bones and spine standing out, sunken eyes, loose skin when there is no shed due. Rule out low warm-side temperature, parasites and cryptosporidiosis (page 17) before simply feeding more.

Ideal

Tail as thick as the neck at its base, full and rounded, with no bony ridge along the spine or hips. Skin smooth, eyes full, weight stable week to week in an adult and climbing steadily in a juvenile.

Overweight

Fat deposits at the neck and in the armpits, a tail noticeably wider than the head, belly wide from above. Almost always a mealworm and waxworm diet. Cut the fatty feeders and stretch the interval, do not fast the gecko.

When a weight change is a vet visit A tail that is thinning while the gecko is still eating is the one that needs an appointment quickly, because that pattern is the signature of cryptosporidiosis (page 17). Any steady loss across three or four weekly weigh-ins is worth a call, as is a juvenile that stops gaining. Bring the Owner Log; a trend line tells a vet more than a single reading.

Females, eggs & egg binding

A female kept entirely alone can still produce eggs. This page is for every female owner, not just breeders.

Eggs without a male

Female egg-laying reptiles can produce eggs with no male present, so every female is at risk of being unable to pass one. Those eggs are infertile and will never hatch, but the physical process is identical, and so is the calcium demand behind it. A mature female can lay several clutches in a season, typically two eggs at a time with roughly a month between clutches, and a female in that cycle draws heavily on her calcium reserves. This is the practical reason the plain calcium dish stays in the enclosure permanently.

Signs a female is gravid or about to lay

  • Two pale, oval shapes visible through the skin of the belly, most obvious when she is on glass or held briefly over a light.
  • Restlessness and repeated digging, especially in a corner or in the humid hide.
  • Appetite dropping off in the days before laying, then returning afterwards.

Give her somewhere to lay

Inadequate nesting is a recognized cause of egg retention in lizards, and it is the easiest one to remove. Put a lay box in as soon as she looks gravid: a plastic tub with an entry hole cut in the lid, filled 3 to 4 in (8 to 10 cm) deep with damp, holdable substrate such as coco fiber or damp sphagnum, kept on the warm or middle side. Damp enough to hold a burrow shape, not wet. Leave her alone with it, since disturbance during laying is exactly what makes a female hold on to eggs.

After she lays Remove infertile eggs; they mold. Offer food and water, and expect appetite to bounce back within a day or two. Weigh her: a laying female loses weight, and knowing how much this time makes the next cycle readable. If she is producing clutch after clutch, ask a reptile vet about it rather than treating it as normal, because repeated laying is a genuine calcium drain.

Egg binding (dystocia) is a same-day call

Egg binding is a female straining but unable to pass eggs. Laying should be complete in under 48 hours; past that, or at any point she looks stressed or unwell, it needs a vet rather than more waiting. Causes include an inadequate nest site, poor calcium status, oversized or misshapen eggs, illness, and incorrect temperature or humidity. Treatment ranges from correcting the nest site through calcium and hormone injections to surgical removal, and the earlier it is caught the further down that list it stops.

  • Straining, a swollen or protruding cloaca, restlessness that does not settle, repeated digging with nothing produced.
  • Lethargy, depression, refusing food while still visibly gravid, or any tissue protruding from the vent, which is an immediate emergency.

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a reptile-experienced vet promptly if you see A tail thinning to a stick, with or without weight loss. A soft, spongy or visibly deformed jaw. Tremors or twitching limbs. Any breathing difficulty, wheezing, clicking, or open-mouth breathing at rest. Drooling, or pus or cheesy material around the mouth. Why temperature comes up first

A leopard gecko cannot generate its own body heat, so the warm side is not a comfort setting, it is what powers digestion and the immune response. A warm hide reading a few degrees low produces refused meals, slow or absent stools, an animal that hides all day, and eventually a real infection, and every one of those looks like a different problem. That is why a vet asks for the number before anything else, and why the first thing to check at home is the surface the gecko lies on. Stick tail disease & metabolic bone disease

Cryptosporidiosis, or "stick tail disease"

Cause: a parasite, most often Cryptosporidium varanii, spread between geckos by contact and by feces. It is severe, highly contagious, and currently has no cure. Leopard geckos are the most commonly diagnosed reptile for it, and it is estimated that around half of captive leopard geckos may carry it, many with no symptoms at all. Signs: a tail that thins out to a stick, weight loss and emaciation despite a normal or even increased appetite, undigested food in the stool, foul-smelling feces, and a bloated or distended belly. Diagnosis: a fecal PCR test through a reptile-experienced vet. False negatives happen, so one clear test does not fully rule it out. Response: isolate the gecko immediately, with separate tools and hands washed between animals, and get the test done. Recovery: the main drug, paromomycin, manages the condition rather than curing it. Mortality for symptomatic cases runs around 50%, and a gecko that has not responded within a month generally has a poor outlook.

The prevention that actually works Quarantine every new gecko for 3 to 6 months before it shares a room, a tool, or a hand with an existing animal. That is the Merck figure for a new reptile; 60 to 90 days is the hobby floor. A separate room rather than a second tank on the same shelf, since two tanks in one room share air. Paper towel substrate throughout, which is not only easy to strip: it is the diagnostic surface, the thing that makes mites, an abnormal stool and a regurgitated meal visible where loose substrate hides all three. Its own tongs and dishes, and always fed and cleaned last. Asymptomatic carriers spread this without ever looking sick, which is exactly why quarantine, not observation, is the control.

Metabolic bone disease (MBD)

Cause: chronic calcium or vitamin D3 deficiency, and it is common in this species precisely because geckos are so often kept without UVB, without consistent supplementation, or without adequate heat. All three contribute, and this species appears to rely on UVB and correct temperature to process calcium rather than on dietary supplementation alone. Signs: trembling or twitching limbs, difficulty walking, a soft or deformed lower jaw sometimes called rubber jaw, pathological fractures, twisted or bent limbs, difficulty eating, lethargy and weight loss. Diagnosis: a vet exam with bloodwork and x-rays. Recovery: caught early, correcting calcium, D3 and heat reverses the course; advanced cases leave permanent skeletal deformity even after treatment.

Concrete threshold Any tremor, any softness or asymmetry in the jaw, or any new difficulty walking is a vet appointment this week, not a wait-and-see. In the meantime handle the gecko as little as possible and take out anything it can climb and fall from: weakened bone fractures on a drop that would normally mean nothing.

Impaction, stuck shed & respiratory infection

The three husbandry-driven problems, in the order they turn up.

Impaction

Usually caused by swallowed loose substrate, most often sand, or by prey too large to pass safely. Low warm-side temperature, dehydration and parasites all make it likelier, because a gecko that cannot digest at speed cannot move a mouthful of substrate along either. Watch for a bloated abdomen, appetite loss, weight loss and no normal bowel movement. Warm shallow baths and very gentle belly massage help mild cases; a true blockage is a surgical problem. That surgery is the reason paper towel and tile are the recommended substrate for anyone who is not already confident, and the reason prey width is measured against the gap between the eyes rather than guessed.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Caused by low humidity or a vitamin A deficiency, and it collects in the same three places every time: the eye caps, the toes, and the tail tip. A ring of retained shed around a toe acts as a tourniquet and can cut off circulation completely, which is how geckos lose digits. Never peel dry shed off. Soak the gecko in shallow lukewarm water for 10 to 15 minutes, then work the softened skin off with a damp cotton swab, and repeat over a couple of days rather than forcing it in one go. Retained eye caps, a gecko rubbing its face or holding an eye closed, are a vet job, not a home one. A maintained humid hide prevents nearly all of this, and a check of the toes, tail tip and eyes after every shed catches the rest early.

Respiratory infection

Far less common in a correctly kept leopard gecko than in a damp one, which is exactly why raising whole-enclosure humidity to fix a shedding problem is the wrong move. The cause is nearly always cold, damp, or both: a cool warm side, poor ventilation, or an enclosure misted like a rainforest. Signs are clicking or wheezing when the gecko breathes, open-mouth breathing at rest, mucus or bubbles around the nose or mouth, and lethargy. This is a vet visit rather than a husbandry fix on its own, but fix the husbandry at the same time: get the warm side to target, drop enclosure humidity back to 30 to 40%, and confine the moisture to the humid hide.

The common thread All three of these are readings, not bad luck. Impaction traces back to substrate and prey size, stuck shed to one damp box, respiratory infection to temperature and ventilation. Any gecko showing one of them should have its warm hide floor, cool side and enclosure humidity measured the same day, because the fix is usually in those three numbers.

Parasites, eye caps, tail loss & other problems

Less dramatic than the last two pages, and far more common.

Internal parasites

Most geckos carry a low parasite load without trouble; it becomes a problem when the animal is stressed, cold or already ill. Loose, foul or unusually frequent stool, undigested food, weight loss with a normal appetite, and a gecko that fails to thrive are the signs. Diagnosis is a fecal exam, which is why a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag turns a first vet visit into a real baseline. Bring one to the wellness exam even if nothing looks wrong: several of the conditions in this section, cryptosporidiosis especially, are present long before they are visible.

Retained eye caps and eye problems

A gecko holding one eye closed, rubbing its face on the substrate, or with a visibly cloudy eye outside the pre-shed window needs looking at. The usual cause is retained shed under the eyelid, sometimes with a vitamin A deficiency behind it, and it can progress to infection. Do not attempt to remove anything from an eye at home. A weekly multivitamin that includes vitamin A is the preventive half of this.

Cystic calculi (bladder stones)

Listed among this species' common presentations in veterinary references. It shows as straining, a swollen lower abdomen, appetite loss and difficulty passing stool or urates, and it is diagnosed on x-ray. It is not something to manage at home, but it is worth knowing the name, because "straining with nothing produced" in a male gecko is easy to misread as constipation.

Tail loss and regrowth

A dropped tail is a stress response, not an injury you caused by accident, and it happens when a gecko is grabbed by the tail, badly frightened, handled roughly or attacked by a cagemate. Move the gecko onto clean paper towel immediately, keep the enclosure especially clean while the stump heals, skip handling for one to two weeks, and watch the stump for swelling, discharge, discoloration or a foul smell, any of which mean a vet. The tail regrows in 4 to 8 weeks, shorter and thicker than the original, and the gecko is otherwise fine. The real cost is the fat reserve it carried, so keep a closer eye on weight for the next couple of months.

Burns and prolapse

Burns come from unregulated heat, hot rocks, or a mat with a failed thermostat, and they present as blistered, reddened or blackened skin on the belly and feet. Turn the heat source off, fix the regulation, and get it seen: reptile burns become infected easily. A prolapse, pink or red tissue protruding from the vent, is an emergency in every case. Keep the tissue moist with clean lukewarm water or sterile saline, do not push it back in, and go the same day.

Shedding, brumation & behavior

The shed cycle

Shedding, properly ecdysis, is how a reptile grows and clears old or damaged skin. The pre-shed phase runs roughly 5 to 14 days: colors go dull and greyish, the skin looks dry, and the gecko hides more and often goes off food. Then it comes off in a day, usually at night, and most leopard geckos eat the shed as they go, so an owner who never sees one is not missing anything. Humidity is the single biggest factor in whether it comes off cleanly, and rough surfaces, cork, slate, a textured hide, give the gecko something to rub against to start the peel.

Shed week routine Check the humid hide is genuinely damp and top it up. Leave the gecko alone: no handling during a shed. Skip a feeding if it is refused rather than pushing food. The day after, check the toes, the tail tip and both eyes in good light. Anything still attached gets a lukewarm soak, not a fingernail.

Brumation

A natural winter slowdown cued by cooler temperatures and shorter daylight, not something you have to induce. A brumating gecko is less active, eats little or nothing for weeks, and wakes briefly to drink. That is normal in a healthy adult, and juveniles should not be allowed to brumate deeply. The one rule that keeps it safe: weigh the gecko every couple of weeks through it. A brumating gecko lives off its tail reserve and should not be losing significant body mass, so real weight loss during a suspected brumation is a vet visit, not a season.

Sounds and body language

Leopard geckos are more vocal than most lizards. A bark or chirp is not automatically distress and often just means the gecko wants food or wants you further away. A squeak, a scream (most common in juveniles), rapid tail flicking or waving, fast retreats and refusing food afterwards all mean a handling session has gone on too long. A slow, deliberate tail wave is different: it is the stalking and territorial display, not a complaint. Learning the difference is most of what makes handling go well.

Quick differentiator Brumation looks like a gecko that is quiet, hiding and off food but holding its weight and still bright-eyed when disturbed. Illness looks like the same behavior with a thinning tail, sunken eyes, or a gecko that is hard to rouse at all. Weight is what separates them, which is the whole reason for the log on page 30.

A desert species still drinks. Most of a leopard gecko's water comes from the dish and from the humid hide it sits in before a shed, and the rest from its food, which is why a mostly-mealworm diet is a hydration problem as well as a fat one.

Power outages, travel & transport

A leopard gecko's normal night low is around 70°F (21°C). Act once it is heading below 60°F (16°C), and remember a small gecko loses heat faster than a heavy-bodied snake in the same room.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Put a battery or wind-up thermometer in the enclosure so you know the real number rather than guessing.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, and move it to the warmest interior room.
  • Set a wrapped hot water bottle or a commercial hand warmer beside the gecko, never touching it, and stop feeding insects until heat is reliably back.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, gas stoves, or open flame anywhere near the enclosure.
  • Don't put a heat pack in direct contact with the gecko. A cold, sluggish animal cannot move away fast enough and will burn.
Outage kit Two or three hand warmers or a hot water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated tub, and both vet numbers written on paper.

  • Never leave the gecko in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly.

Enrichment safety Never leave feeder insects loose in the enclosure overnight, never use wild-caught insects, and never treat a shed cycle as handling time. Glossary

AutotomyVoluntary tail loss. In a leopard gecko the tail regrows in 4 to 8 weeks, shorter and thicker than the original.
BrumationA reptile's winter slowdown: reduced activity and appetite triggered by cooler temperatures and shorter days. Not hibernation, and not weight loss.
CrepuscularMost active at dusk and dawn, which is why evening is the right time to feed and handle.
CryptosporidiosisA contagious parasitic infection with no cure, known as stick tail disease for the wasting it causes. Page 17.
DysecdysisAn abnormal or incomplete shed. Usually a humidity problem, occasionally a vitamin A one.
DystociaEgg binding: a female unable to pass eggs she has produced. A same-day veterinary problem. Page 15.
EcdysisThe normal shed cycle, from the dull pre-shed phase through to the skin coming off.
EctothermAn animal that takes its body heat from its environment. It is why the warm hide reading is a health number, not a comfort setting.
Gut-loadingFeeding a feeder insect a calcium-rich diet for 24 to 48 hours before it is offered, so the gecko eats the contents too. Page 11.
Hemipenal bulgeThe paired swelling behind the vent that marks a male. Page 14.
Humid hideA closed box of damp substrate at 70 to 80% humidity inside, in an enclosure that is otherwise 30 to 40%. The single most useful furnishing in this setup.
ImpactionA blockage of the digestive tract, usually from swallowed substrate or oversized prey. Page 18.
Metabolic bone diseaseSkeletal weakening from chronic calcium or vitamin D3 deficiency. Preventable, and permanent once advanced. Page 17.
Obligate insectivoreAn animal that must eat insects and cannot use plant food at all. That is a leopard gecko.
Oxalic acidA compound in spinach, beet greens and chard that binds calcium into an unusable form, which is why they are the wrong gut-load. Page 11.
Preanal poresThe V-shaped row of pores in front of the vent, enlarged in males and used for scent marking. Page 14.
Thermal gradientThe warm-to-cool range across the enclosure that lets a gecko choose its own temperature. Without it the animal cannot thermoregulate at all.
UrateThe soft white portion passed alongside stool: a reptile's version of urine. Hard and yellow means dehydrated.
UVIUV index, the measured strength of UVB at the animal's position. A leopard gecko wants 0.5 to 1.5, and less for a pale morph.

Where sources genuinely disagree this package prints the range rather than picking a side: adult length runs from 6.5 to 11 in (17 to 28 cm) across veterinary, species and growth-chart references, hatchling weight from 2 to 8 g, and adult feeding frequency from every other day to every five.
