Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Red flags & finding a vet

A hardy animal that hides decline for a long time, then declines quickly.

Russian tortoises are hardy, and most of their health problems trace back to a handful of husbandry basics that are within an owner's control. The pattern is worth learning as a pattern: weak UVB and too little calcium cause metabolic bone disease; cold, damp or drafty conditions cause respiratory infection; temperature, humidity and diet through the growing years decide whether pyramiding happens; and an unscreened new tortoise brings parasites and viruses in with it.

Go to a vet now Wheezing, nasal discharge, open-mouth breathing, or lethargy (respiratory infection, which does not clear on its own) · straining while making noise (a urate blockage) · watery, jelly-like urine, or straining to urinate · a crack, puncture or bleeding in the shell · soft, discolored or foul-smelling patches on the shell · a soft or deformed shell with weak limbs · a female weak, flat or straining with no progress · drooling or frothing on a hot day

Finding a vet

Find a reptile-experienced vet before you need one, not on the day. An exotic-animal vet is meaningfully more reliable for this species than a general clinic. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory of its members. Write the number on the emergency card. A routine exotic-vet checkup runs around $75; illness that needs tests or medication commonly runs $150 to $300 or more.

What to bring and say

  • The weight log, and the weight today.
  • The basking surface and cool-end readings as measured, not as set, and the age of the UVB tube.
  • What it eats, in rough proportions, and any supplements.
  • When it last ate, drank, and passed droppings and urates, and a fresh dropping in a sealed bag. A fresh sample matters, because some parasites are easiest to see while they are still moving.
  • Whether another tortoise or reptile has joined the household.

Metabolic bone disease & pyramiding

The two conditions that are written permanently into the shell.

Metabolic bone disease

Metabolic bone disease (MBD) is among the most common conditions in pet tortoises. It comes from too little UVB or too little calcium in the diet: without UVB the tortoise cannot make the vitamin D3 it needs to absorb calcium, so however much calcium is in the food, the shell and skeleton go short.

  • Signs: a soft or deformed shell and weak limbs.
  • What to do: see a vet, every time. Correcting the UVB and the calcium is part of the treatment, not a substitute for it.
  • Prevention is the whole story, and it is not optional: a linear T5 HO tube at a UVI of 3.0 to 4.0, replaced on schedule, plus a calcium source from the start, calcium lightly on food and a cuttlebone in the enclosure.

Pyramiding

Pyramiding is abnormal, raised growth of the scutes, the plates covering the shell, into cone shapes instead of a smooth curve. The condition is not completely understood. It occurs commonly in young tortoises kept at the wrong temperature and humidity, or fed an imbalance of calcium, phosphorus or protein. There is no single settled cause: low humidity during growth and the growth rate itself are both implicated. What is certain is that the growing years decide it, and that it does not reverse afterward.

What existing pyramiding means Raised scutes are permanent, so treat existing pyramiding as a record of past conditions rather than something to fix. What you can still change is the next growth, at the edges of each scute. Correct the diet, the temperatures and the humidity, and watch the new edges grow in flat.

Respiratory infection & herpesvirus

The urgent one on this list, and the one that can take a whole group of tortoises.

Respiratory infection

Wheezing, nasal discharge, open-mouth breathing and lethargy are the signs. The causes are temperatures running chronically low, too much humidity across the enclosure, and drafts, which is why ventilation and a dry surface are health measures rather than comfort ones. Vitamin A deficiency can bring it on too.

It does not clear on its own A respiratory infection in a tortoise needs a vet and antibiotics, and it does not wait. While you arrange care, raise the temperature and make sure the tortoise is drinking. Do not raise the humidity, which is the common and wrong instinct.

Herpesvirus

Tortoise herpesvirus is confirmed in Russian tortoises and in many other true tortoises. It spreads mostly by mouth, through shared water, substrate and hands, and there is no cure: treatment only supports the animal and controls the infections that follow. Survivors carry the virus for life and shed it on and off, most of all after brumation.

Look forNotes
Nose and eye dischargeA mild case can look like nothing more than a touch of eye irritation

Because a carrier can look entirely well, watching for symptoms is not screening. That is why every new tortoise does a tested quarantine of at least six months (page {{P:arrival}}), and why a new tortoise never goes in beside an established one of another species either.

Mycoplasma, the runny nose that keeps coming back

Mycoplasma agassizii is the bacterium behind upper respiratory tract disease in tortoises, and it has the same carrier problem: it has been found in captive tortoises with no symptoms at all. A runny nose that comes and goes is never just a cold in a tortoise. It is a reason for a vet visit and for keeping that animal apart from any other.

Shell rot, shell trauma & abscesses

The shell is living bone, and it gets infected and injured like any other tissue.

Shell rot

Shell rot shows as soft, discolored or foul-smelling patches on the shell. It is a bacterial or fungal infection that usually gets in through a cut or a scrape, and an overly damp enclosure often makes it worse, one more reason to keep the surface dry. Untreated it can spread into the bloodstream as septicemia, which is life-threatening. It is a vet case from the first patch.

Shell trauma

A shell has its own blood and nerve supply, so a crack or puncture is a wound, not cosmetic damage. The commonest cause is the household dog: chewing a tortoise like a toy leaves punctures or breaks whole sections away, sometimes exposing the organs underneath. Another tortoise's bites land on the neck and feet.

  • Same-day vet for any crack, puncture or bleeding. A fresh fracture in a well-fed tortoise can usually be repaired; waiting invites bone infection.
  • Never leave a tortoise and a dog together unsupervised, whatever the dog's record, in the house or the yard.
  • Falls count too. Handle low, sitting or kneeling, because a drop can be fatal.

Abscesses

A tortoise abscess is a hard, tumor-like lump rather than a soft pocket, because the pus inside is thick and dry, close to cottage cheese. The classic sites are the ear, which shows as a large swelling on the side of the head just behind the eye, and the mouth, including the roof of it. Many trace back to vitamin A deficiency (page {{P:vitamina}}).

They do not drain on their own A vet opens and cleans out the abscess, flushes the site, cultures the bacteria to choose the right drug, and follows with antibiotics. Waiting does not shrink it.

Parasites & Hexamita

Common, mostly invisible, and the reason every new tortoise is screened.

Gut parasites

Roundworms and protozoa (single-celled parasites) are fairly common in tortoises, especially wild-caught ones, and often cause no signs at all. A fecal test is worth doing for every newly acquired tortoise, symptoms or not, and at every yearly exam after that. Repeated watery droppings are a classic sign of protozoal or parasitic disease and a reason to test sooner.

Hexamita parva: the kidney parasite

Hexamita parva is a flagellate, a single-celled protozoan that swims with whip-like tails, not a worm, and it attacks the kidneys and urinary tract rather than the gut. Russian tortoises and their relatives in the genus Testudo are notably affected. Hallinger and colleagues' 2018 study of pet tortoises in Germany found it invading the kidney tubules, and described long-running infection as able to cause severe kidney inflammation, tissue death and gout, occasionally fatally.

Vitamin A, bladder stones & beak overgrowth

Three conditions from three corners of husbandry: the diet, water, and the texture of food.

Vitamin A deficiency

A diet built on iceberg lettuce or a poor-quality commercial food leaves a tortoise short of vitamin A, and the shortage changes the skin and the glands that make mucus. The signs are easy to blame on something else: poor appetite, lethargy, swollen eyelids often with a pus-like discharge, a swelling at the ear from an abscess, respiratory infection, and kidney failure late on. A vet treats it with vitamin A by mouth or injection while the diet is fixed. Dark leafy greens, weeds and orange vegetables carry the carotenoids a tortoise turns into vitamin A, which is why a weed-based diet prevents it.

Urate blockages and bladder stones

A cloacolith is a stone of urates, the crystalline part of the urine, lodged in the cloaca, the single opening that waste and eggs leave by. A large one blocks it. A tortoise with one strains to pass a bowel movement and makes noise as it strains, which is a true emergency: a vet removes it under sedation with pain relief, breaking it down and flushing it out. A soak will not shift a stone that is already wedged.

Bladder stones are common in tortoises and are made almost entirely of urate salts. In Takami and colleagues' 2021 survey of 101 tortoises at three Japanese veterinary hospitals, Russian tortoises were 8.9% of the cases, and every stone analyzed was ammonium acid urate. Chronic dehydration is the mechanism most often pointed to: a tortoise's kidneys cannot concentrate urine, so a thirsty or overheated one pulls water back out of the bladder and the urate crystallizes. The same survey could not tie stones to diet. Straining and a poor appetite were the commonest signs, some tortoises showed none until the stone was large, X-ray finds it, and surgery removes it.

Prevention for both is water: a dish always available, the soak on schedule, and a moist hide.

Beak overgrowth

A tortoise's beak grows all the time and wears down on tough food. An overgrown one can come from metabolic bone disease, a misaligned jaw, a diet of soft foods, or an old injury, so a beak that keeps overgrowing is a symptom to investigate rather than a grooming job. Since soft food is one of the causes, the coarse weeds and hay of a proper diet are the prevention.

Trimming is a vet's job The beak has its own blood and nerve supply. A trim done wrong causes severe pain, bleeding and lasting disfigurement.

Reading droppings, urates & hydration

A free health check every day, and the soak is where you get to see it.

A tortoise passes two things together through its single opening: the solid dropping, and urates, the white to cream paste a reptile makes instead of liquid urine. Urates are the best hydration gauge an owner has. Many owners never see them on a normal day, because they land in the substrate and get buried, which is why the soak, when most tortoises drink and empty themselves, is the time to look.

Gritty urates are the sign to catch

Chronically gritty, granular, off-white urate is clear evidence of extended dehydration, and it comes before the urate blockage and the bladder stones on page {{P:vitamina}}. When a soak turns it up, review the water dish and the soak schedule now rather than waiting to see.

Hydration, in four routines

  • A water dish, sunk level with the substrate, washed and refilled daily.
  • The soak on schedule: hatchlings daily, juveniles twice a week for 10 to 15 minutes, adults about weekly for 10 to 20 minutes, lukewarm and no deeper than the elbows, never unattended.
  • A moist hide and damp depth in the substrate, which is the real fix for a tortoise that drinks hard at every soak.
  • Fresh, water-rich food: weeds and greens carry moisture that hay alone does not.
What a soak should produce A soaking tortoise drinks, urinates and defecates. That is the point of it. If the tub is still clean when the soak ends, the animal had nothing to give, and that is the reading to act on.
