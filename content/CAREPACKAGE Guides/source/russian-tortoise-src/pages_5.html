Brumation: the decision and the protocol

Programmed into the species, optional indoors, and dangerous done casually.

Russian tortoises are physiologically programmed to brumate. In their native range they are active for only about three months of the year, spending the rest underground in burrows 20 to 50 cm (8 to 20 in) deep where the soil sits at 6 to 10°C (43 to 50°F). Captive tortoises keep the drive, and many slow down, refuse food and dig in as autumn arrives whatever the enclosure is doing.

The genuine choice indoor keeping gives you By maintaining full lighting, heat and feeding year-round, an indoor keeper can keep a Russian tortoise active through winter rather than brumating it. This is a real option that many species do not offer, and it is a perfectly legitimate way to keep this animal. Wild-caught adults in particular may refuse food or show real stress if prevented from brumating, so the choice is easier with a captive-bred animal, and the honest position is that both routes are defensible and that the wrong route is a half-hearted one.

Do not brumate a tortoise that is

  • Underweight, or has lost weight this year
  • Under about two years old, or newly acquired
  • Showing any sign of respiratory infection, however mild
  • Carrying an untreated parasite load
  • Recovering from any illness or injury
  • Not health-checked by a vet in the last few months

A pre-brumation veterinary health check is worth doing before a first brumation and after any year with a health problem in it. A tortoise that goes into brumation sick does not come out.

The protocol, if you say yes

  1. Weigh and record, and check the shell, eyes, nose and mouth. This is the baseline every later weighing is measured against.
  2. Stop feeding, two to four weeks before the temperature comes down, while heat and light stay on. The gut has to be completely empty: food left fermenting in a cold tortoise is a route to serious illness. Keep offering water and soaking through this period.
  3. Wind the temperature and photoperiod down gradually over a week or two, rather than moving the animal straight into the cold.
  4. Hold it at 40 to 55°F (4 to 13°C) for 2 to 4 months, in a ventilated box of dry substrate in a place that stays reliably in that band. A dedicated refrigerator with a thermometer and a daily door-opening for air exchange is the method most experienced keepers now use, because a garage or a shed swings with the weather.
  5. Check weekly. Weigh the animal. A loss of more than roughly 1% of body weight a month, or any waking, urination, discharge or discomfort, ends the brumation early.
  6. Warm up gradually, over a few days, then straight back to full basking temperature and UVB.
  7. Soak immediately on waking, and daily for the first week. Rehydration comes before food. Expect the first meal to be refused and the first droppings to take several days.
Two things that kill brumating tortoises Food left in the gut. The fasting period is not optional and it is not a suggestion. Temperature that drifts. Too warm and the animal burns reserves without being properly dormant; below freezing and it suffers permanent eye and tissue damage or dies. A brumation with no thermometer on it is a gamble.

Reading droppings, urates & hydration

A reptile passes two things, and the second one is the more useful.

A tortoise produces feces, the brown or dark green solid, and urates, a separate white or off-white portion that is the crystalline waste a reptile excretes instead of liquid urine. It also passes clear liquid urine. Each tells you something different, and the urates in particular are the best hydration gauge an owner has. The weekly soak on page {{P:humidity}} is when most tortoises pass both, which makes it the easiest time to look.

Gritty urates are the sign to catch Thick, sandy, chalky urates mean the animal is running chronically dry. It is the earliest and easiest warning this species gives, it precedes the urate blockage that is a true emergency, and it is fixed with the two cheapest interventions in this book: more soaking and a genuinely moist hide. Look at the soak water every week.

Hydration

A tortoise from an arid environment is very good at conserving water, which means dehydration is well advanced before it is visible. The signs are sunken eyes, loose or wrinkled skin at the limb sockets, thick urates, reduced appetite, and lethargy. The interventions are all routine rather than emergency:

  • A shallow water dish, sunk level with the substrate, changed daily. Many tortoises drink far more than their keepers expect once the dish is easy to walk into.
  • A weekly soak of 20 to 30 minutes in shallow warm water reaching the bottom of the shell, more often for hatchlings, newly acquired animals, and any tortoise with thick urates.
  • A moist hide, remoistened every few days. Page {{P:humidity}}.
  • Water-containing food. Fresh weeds and greens carry real moisture; dry hay alone does not.
Soaking is also a health check A tortoise in a soak is level, still, and easy to look at from every angle. Use the twenty minutes: check the eyes and nose, look at the shell top and bottom, feel the limb sockets, and inspect what ends up in the water. It is the single most efficient routine in this book.

The law & the four-inch rule

The species is legal almost everywhere. The rule everyone quotes is not what they think it is.

Of the species we have checked state by state, the Russian tortoise has the fewest restrictions in the United States. No state bans it. One state requires a permit. Hawaii, which bans almost everything, expressly allows it. The rule that actually shapes how these animals are bought and sold is federal, says nothing about the species, and is misquoted more often than any other rule in the pet trade.

What the four-inch rule actually says It is 21 CFR 1240.62, an FDA regulation from 1975 written to reduce Salmonella transmission to small children. Its operative sentence is that viable turtle eggs and live turtles with a carapace length of less than 4 inches shall not be sold, held for sale, or offered for any other type of commercial or public distribution. Sold, held for sale, offered for distribution. Nothing about owning one, and nothing about the species: it catches a hatchling Russian tortoise for the same reason it catches a hatchling red-eared slider.

Then comes the part almost nobody quotes. Subsection (c) lists exceptions, and the second covers the sale, holding for sale and distribution of live turtles and viable turtle eggs not in connection with a business. A private keeper rehoming a hatchling is not a business; a breeder selling them is. That clause is why hatchlings move hand to hand through private keepers while shops stock only animals past the four-inch line, and it is the real reason a Russian tortoise you buy is usually already a few years old.

Conservation status, and why it should matter to a buyer Testudo horsfieldii is Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List and CITES-listed, and large numbers of wild-caught animals have entered the trade labeled captive-bred or ranched. A cheap adult at an expo is very often a wild-caught import, with the parasite load and disease risk that carries (page {{P:parasites}}). Captive-bred costs more and does not draw on a wild population.

Rules change, and this page is a summary rather than legal advice. Confirm your own state and city before buying, and check import rules before crossing a border with a CITES-listed animal.
