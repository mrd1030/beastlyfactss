Weeds, grazing & growing your own

The cheapest and best food for this species grows in the cracks of a pavement.

Supermarket greens are bred to be tender, watery and mild, close to the opposite of what a steppe grazer evolved to eat. Weeds are tougher, higher in fiber and minerals, more varied, and free. A keeper who learns six weeds by sight has better food than one spending $200 a year on salad, and the variety matters: the wild diet runs to over 80 plant species.

WeedHow to recognize it, and notes
DandelionThe staple. Deeply toothed leaves in a flat rosette, hollow stem with milky sap, single yellow flower. Leaves and flowers both, in quantity. High in calcium
Plantain (ribwort and broadleaf)Ribbed parallel-veined leaves in a rosette, with a bare flower spike. Grows in every lawn and path edge. Excellent, and tortoises usually take to it immediately
White and red cloverThree leaflets with a pale crescent, ball-shaped flower. Good, but higher in protein than most weeds, so a component rather than a base
Sow thistleTall, hollow stem with milky sap and clasping leaves, small dandelion-like flowers. A favorite. Do not confuse with prickly lettuce, which is also fine
Hawkbit, cat's ear, chickweedHawkbit and cat's ear are dandelion lookalikes with hairy leaves and branched stems, and all are fine to feed, which is why the lookalike problem hardly matters. Chickweed is a low sprawling mat with white star flowers: fine, but watery
MallowRounded lobed leaves, pink-purple flowers. Leaves and flowers both, and it is a good hot-weather grower
Vetch, hawksbeard, bramble leaf, hibiscus leafAll safe and all worth adding for variety
Where you must not pick Anywhere sprayed with herbicide, pesticide or fertilizer, including a neighbor's overspray. Within a few meters of a road, where exhaust residue and salt accumulate. Anywhere dogs are walked. Any verge a council or contractor maintains. Farmland margins. And never pick something you cannot name: buttercup, ragwort, foxglove, hemlock, ivy, deadly nightshade and daffodil all grow alongside the good weeds and several of them are lethal. If you are not certain, leave it.

Growing your own

A seed tray or two of a tortoise forage mix, sown in rotation, turns cut greens into living plant the animal crops itself, which is as close as a captive setup gets to grazing. Three trays on a windowsill on a two-week rotation keeps a single tortoise supplied through most of the year. Outdoors, sow forage mix straight into the pen and let the animal graze as it walks; that is the single biggest quality-of-life change available for this species. A pot of dandelion and plantain in a sunny corner covers the winter.

Drying weeds for winter Dandelion, plantain and clover all dry well. Cut on a dry day, spread in a single layer somewhere warm and airy out of direct sun until crisp, then store in a paper bag rather than plastic. Crumbled into hay it turns a dull winter diet into a varied one, and it costs an afternoon in June.

Fruit, protein, supplements & the never-feed list

The short page that prevents most of the health section.
Never feed a Russian tortoise Any animal protein. Dog or cat food, boiled egg, cheese, meat, insects, mealworms. Excess protein drives pyramiding and burdens the kidneys, and none of it belongs in a steppe herbivore.
Fruit. Fruits are inappropriate for arid-habitat species: the sugar disrupts gut flora, causes loose droppings and encourages the wrong fermentation. If you feed any at all it belongs under 5% of the diet, and zero is a defensible answer for this species.
Bread, pasta, grain, cereal, cat treats, "tortoise" biscuits.
Iceberg lettuce. Almost no nutrition, and linked to vitamin A deficiency in tortoises fed on it. Page {{P:minor}}.
Toxic plants: avocado, rhubarb leaves, buttercup, ragwort, foxglove, daffodil and other bulbs, ivy, hemlock, nightshade, azalea, oleander, yew.
Anything from treated ground. Page {{P:weeds}}.

Why protein and fruit matter so much here

A tortoise's shell grows continuously, in layers, at whatever rate the diet allows. Fed richly, it grows fast and unevenly, and the scutes lift into the raised cone shapes called pyramiding, which is permanent (page {{P:mbd}}). Fed on tough, fibrous, low-nutrient plants, it grows slowly and smoothly, which is what it does in the wild. The commonest owner instinct, that a growing animal should be fed generously and well, is the exact instinct that deforms this one.

Supplements

SupplementSchedule
Calcium without D3A light dusting on food at most feedings. This is the daily one
Calcium with D3Twice a week at most, and less or none for a tortoise with strong UVB and outdoor time, since D3 is what the UVB is for. Never at every feeding
Reptile multivitaminOnce a week or so, herbivore-appropriate, with no added phosphorus
CuttleboneLeft in the enclosure permanently, for the tortoise to gnaw at will. Also helps keep the beak in shape. Page {{P:minor}}

Supplement schedules vary by keeper and by how much natural sun the animal gets, so treat this as the starting point and ask your reptile vet to review it at the annual exam. A tortoise living outdoors in summer needs less added D3 than one under a bulb in January.

Do not dust with D3 and then add a D3 multivitamin Vitamin D3 is fat-soluble and accumulates, and oversupplementation causes its own problems. Read both labels, count the D3 sources you are actually giving, and remember that a strong UVB fixture is one of them.
Water is a supplement too A shallow dish always available, changed daily, plus a weekly 20 to 30 minute soak in shallow warm water. Hydration does as much for smooth shell growth as any powder, and a dehydrated tortoise is the one that pyramids and the one that forms the urate blockages on page {{P:minor}}.

What enrichment means for a tortoise

The behaviors this species is built for are walking, grazing, digging and thermoregulating, so enrichment is mostly a matter of building an enclosure those can happen in rather than adding objects to it. A Russian tortoise explores its whole territory every day, pushes things over, and rearranges what it can move. An enclosure that looks tidy the next morning is usually one that is too small or too empty.

Terrain and structure

  • Flat stones, cork flats, gentle slopes and low platforms, which do get used rather than walked past.
  • Large hollow logs and visual breaks, so the space is not one flat open plain.
  • Multiple hides facing different directions, one warm, one cool and moist.
  • A deep dig area, or a dedicated dig box.

Routine and change

  • Scatter feeding in new places daily or every other day.
  • Living plants to crop rather than cut greens in a dish. Page {{P:weeds}}.
  • Rearrange non-critical decor every one to four weeks, without moving the hides themselves.
  • Supervised outdoor time whenever the weather allows. Page {{P:outdoor}}.
Leave the hides where they are Rearranging the scenery is enrichment; moving the shelter is stress. A tortoise builds a mental map of where safety is, and shifting the hide means it has to find safety again from scratch. Change the route, not the destination.
Two things that look like enrichment and are not A mirror or a reflective surface. A male reads a reflection as a rival and will ram it. A companion added for company. This is a solitary species, and page {{P:handling}} is the reason.

Sexing, growth & body condition

Straightforward to sex once mature, and worth weighing every month for forty years.

Sexing

Russian tortoises are not reliably sexable as hatchlings or small juveniles; the differences appear as the animal matures, generally somewhere past about 4 in (10 cm) of shell. Once they do appear they are clear.

Growth, and why slow is right

There is no growth chart for this species that is worth printing, because a healthy Russian tortoise grows at whatever rate its diet and its season allow, and the wild pattern is slow and interrupted. Fast growth is a warning rather than an achievement: a tortoise that gains rapidly on a rich diet grows the raised, cone-shaped scutes of pyramiding, which never flatten out again. What you are watching for is smooth growth, with new keratin appearing as flat pale rings around the edge of each scute and the surface of the shell staying even.

The three things to check monthly Weight, on a digital kitchen scale in grams. Write it down. Trends over years are what this record is for, and the reason a tortoise's owner log runs for decades.
Shell surface. Run a finger across the scutes. Smooth is right; a growing ridge at the scute margins is the beginning of pyramiding and it is a signal to reduce food and increase hydration and exercise now, while it still matters.
Shell rigidity. Press very gently at the rear edge of the carapace and along the plastron. A healthy adult shell does not flex. Any give at all in an animal past hatchling size is metabolic bone disease until a vet says otherwise. Page {{P:mbd}}.

Body condition

A tortoise in good condition feels heavy for its size when lifted, the skin around the limbs looks full rather than loose, and the animal can withdraw its head and legs fully without the limbs looking like they barely fit. Signs of poor condition are a light, hollow feel, loose wrinkled skin at the limb sockets, sunken eyes, and lethargy. Because so much of the animal is shell, weight is a better measure than appearance, and the jump-to-conclusion figure worth knowing is that any sudden weight loss in a tortoise is significant, since these animals lose weight slowly by nature.

Females, eggs & egg binding

A female with no male in the house can still produce eggs, and she still has to pass them.

A mature female develops and lays eggs on her own hormonal schedule, whether or not she has ever met a male. Those eggs are infertile, but the physical process is identical and so is the risk if she cannot complete it. Every owner of a female should know what a gravid female looks like and what to do when the laying does not happen.

Signs a female is gravid

  • Restlessness and pacing, often out of character, and walking the perimeter repeatedly.
  • Digging test nests. Repeated shallow excavations, often abandoned, usually in a warm spot.
  • Reduced appetite as the eggs take up room.
  • A firm, rounded feel if a vet palpates the soft area in front of the hind legs. Do not attempt this yourself; pressing on eggs can break them.
Provide a nest site before she needs one A female with nowhere acceptable to dig will hold eggs, and holding eggs is how a straightforward event becomes an emergency. Give her a deep area of slightly damp substrate, at least as deep as the shell is long and ideally 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm), in a warm and undisturbed corner. Outdoors, a mound of soil in a sunny spot works. She may reject several sites before choosing one, and being watched puts many females off entirely.
Egg binding, or dystocia A female straining, restless for days, digging obsessively and then stopping, going off food, becoming lethargic, or producing fluid or blood from the vent is a same-day veterinary case. So is a female you know is gravid who has been trying to nest for more than a couple of days without laying. Left alone this ends in a ruptured oviduct, peritonitis or death. Treatment ranges from correcting the nest site and giving fluids and calcium, through hormonal induction, to surgical removal of the eggs. It is not something to wait out.

What to do with the eggs

With no male, they are infertile and can be discarded once she has finished and covered the nest. Let her finish: a female interrupted mid-clutch may retain the remainder. If a male has had access they may be fertile, and incubating them is a decision to produce more forty-year animals into a market where rehoming is already hard.

Calcium is what this costs her Producing a clutch draws heavily on a female's calcium reserves, which is the practical link between this page and page {{P:mbd}}. A laying female needs her calcium supplementation kept up, a cuttlebone permanently available, and her UVB fixture in date. A female laying repeatedly on a marginal setup is the classic route to metabolic bone disease in an adult that was fine for years.
