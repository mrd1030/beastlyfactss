Section 03 · Feeding

Diet by age & how much

A grazer of tough, fibrous plants, and a tortoise that will happily eat far more than it needs.

In the wild this animal moves through low vegetation, taking a bite here and a bite there of soft-stemmed, water-rich grasses, flowers and leaves, high in fiber and low in energy. Fiber is not filler: microbes in the gut ferment it, a major nutrient source for a tortoise. Almost every captive feeding mistake replaces that diet with something richer.

AgeHow much and how often
Hatchling and growing juvenileAs much as it will eat, every day
Nearing adult sizeA limited portion every day, roughly a pile the size of its own shell
AdultThe same shell-sized daily portion as a starting point, adjusted to the tortoise in front of you
Every ageGrass hay in front of it all the time, unlimited. It is the fiber that never runs out, and the one thing never rationed

The check is the tortoise

Russian tortoises tend toward overweight in captivity, and the test is what happens when one withdraws into its shell. A tortoise that looks chubby as it pulls back, with flesh crowding the shell openings, is being fed too much: cut the amount back. That is the direction of error to expect. Underfeeding a tortoise that grazes is unusual; overfeeding one that would happily eat all day is the default.

How to put it down

  • Graze first, bowl second. Safe growing plants beat anything chopped into a dish, because grazing spreads intake across the day and keeps the fiber high.
  • Mix the food together rather than laying items out separately. Given the choice, a tortoise eats selectively and leaves the fiber.
  • On a tile or flat stone, so the animal is not swallowing substrate with every bite.
  • In several spots, moved daily or every other day, so it walks and searches.
Section 03 · Feeding

Weeds, hay & growing your own

The best food for this species grows free in an ordinary lawn.

Supermarket greens are bred for water content and mild flavor; weeds are tougher, more fibrous and more varied, and they cost nothing. Learning seven common weeds by sight is the single change most likely to improve a captive tortoise's diet. For a tortoise, a weed rotation can be the bulk of the diet, not an addition to it.

WeedHow to recognize it, and notes
DandelionA flat rosette of deeply toothed leaves, one bright yellow flower on each hollow, leafless stem, and milky sap. The closest thing to a default staple. Feed leaves, flowers and stem
Broadleaf and narrowleaf plantainA low rosette with strong veins running the length of each leaf and a thin flower spike. Broadleaf has wide oval leaves, narrowleaf long narrow ones. Not the banana relative
White and red cloverThree leaflets, often with a pale chevron, and a round white or pink-red flower head. Higher in protein, so a rotating item rather than the daily bulk
Sow thistleSoft, lobed leaves with milky sap and small dandelion-like flowers on a taller branching stem. Its spines are soft
HawkbitA dandelion lookalike with hairier leaves and branched stems carrying several flowers. Interchangeable with dandelion for feeding, so a mix-up carries no risk
Common mallowRounded, slightly lobed leaves on long stalks, low and sprawling, with small pink to purple flowers. Soft and popular, and it self-seeds
ChickweedSmall oval leaves in pairs on a thin trailing stem, tiny white star flowers, and a single line of fine hairs down the stem. Grows through cooler months

Start with dandelion and plantain, which are hard to confuse with anything dangerous, then add the rest as you learn them. Rotate two or three as a base, add clover a couple of times a week, and mix in the others as the seasons bring them.

Where not to pick

  • Roadsides and anywhere near traffic, where exhaust residue, runoff and winter road salt settle.
  • Dog-walking verges and park lawns: parasite contamination, and frequent herbicide on public turf.
  • Anywhere sprayed with herbicide or pesticide, and the mowed edges of farmed fields, which sit in spray drift.

Growing and drying your own

A garden bed or large pots you know have never been treated solve the location problem. Dandelion, plantain, mallow and chickweed establish readily from seed, and clover is sold as ground cover. Tortoises tend to eat a plant down to the roots, so grow forage somewhere else and bring it in, or sow a tray of tortoise forage seed mix for living plants to crop. Harvest a little at a time and plantain and mallow regrow within a couple of weeks. Surplus can be air-dried on a screen out of direct sun, or in a dehydrator on its lowest setting, and stored airtight. Dried weeds are a fiber source like hay, an addition to fresh food rather than a replacement.

Fruit, protein & the never-feed list

The short page that prevents a large part of the health section. Print it for the fridge.
Never feed a Russian tortoise Any animal protein: meat, dog food, cat food, insects. This is a herbivore, and that is not a soft preference.
Fruit: high in phosphorus and low in calcium, the opposite of what this animal needs. It stays off the menu altogether.
Iceberg lettuce, or a poor-quality commercial food as the diet: the classic route to vitamin A deficiency.
Spinach, chard and beet greens as staples: their oxalate binds calcium in the gut and can promote bladder stones.
Any plant you cannot positively identify, and anything picked from sprayed, roadside or dog-walked ground.
Buttercup and oleander, named below.
Supplements with added phosphorus, or with added vitamin D unless a vet asked for it.

Why protein matters twice

Animal protein has no place in the diet, and it also does a slower kind of damage. Protein-rich food pushes fast growth, and rapid growth on a high-protein diet is one suspected driver of pyramiding, the raised, cone-shaped scutes that never flatten again. The condition is not completely understood, and it is not purely a feeding fault, but protein and overfeeding are the parts a keeper controls at the dish (page {{P:mbd}}).

Two plants to know on sight

PlantWhy, and how to tell
ButtercupGrows in exactly the lawns and meadows where clover and dandelion are picked, so a careless handful can include it. Listed as toxic to dogs, cats and horses, with mouth ulcers among the signs. Its flowers are glossy and waxy where dandelion and hawkbit are matte, and its leaves are lobed like a hand rather than growing in a flat rosette
OleanderA common hedge and border shrub, not a lawn weed, but a clipping can end up in a forage pile or an outdoor pen. One of the plants most consistently named as dangerous to animals. Keep it out of reach entirely

Toxic plant lists are built on dogs, cats and horses, and plant toxicity in reptiles is barely studied. That argues for more caution, not less: if a plant is not one you have positively identified as safe, it is not food.

Treats

There is no treat food for this species. Fruit, the usual treat for other reptiles, is on the never list above, and the variety a treat would add comes from the weed rotation instead.

Calcium, multivitamin & water

A nutrient to supply reliably, not maximally, and water that a tortoise really does drink.

Calcium runs the shell and the skeleton, and a deficiency is one of the serious problems in this species: a soft shell, shell and bone deformities, kidney damage, and worse if it goes far enough. There is also a ceiling. Wild diets for these tortoises can run a calcium to phosphorus ratio at or above 4:1, but the target in captivity sits nearer 2:1, because too much calcium causes medical problems of its own.

SupplementHow
Calcium carbonateSprinkled lightly onto food. Calcium citrate works too. Choose one with no added phosphorus
CuttleboneLeft in the enclosure for the tortoise to gnaw. A small, cheap calcium source that lasts a good while
Reptile multivitaminSprinkled lightly on food. Herbivore-appropriate, with no added phosphorus
Vitamin D3Only if a vet asks for it. A tortoise under a working UVB tube makes its own, and D3 builds up in the body

A laying female draws heavily on her calcium, which is one more reason to keep the calcium, the cuttlebone and the UVB tube in date (page {{P:eggs}}). Ask your reptile vet to look over what you give at each yearly exam.

Water

Tortoises drink, and the belief that they do not is a persistent one that causes real harm. Without water, urates build up and can reach dangerous levels, and chronic dehydration is the main route to the bladder stones and urate blockages on page {{P:vitamina}}. Provide water in a shallow bowl, something like a plant saucer, sunk so its lip sits level with the surrounding surface. It doubles as the soaking dish.

Soaking

A dish is an offer; a soak is a guarantee. In warm water a tortoise drinks, through the mouth and through the cloaca, then empties itself, which is why the soak is where most keepers see urates at all.

WhoHow oftenHow longWater
HatchlingDaily15 to 30 minWarm and shallow, elbow deep
JuvenileTwice a week10 to 15 minLukewarm, no deeper than the elbows
Indoor adultAbout once a week10 to 20 minLukewarm, no deeper than the elbows
  • Never leave it alone. A tortoise that tips onto its back in an inch of water cannot right itself and can drown.
  • Warm to the hand, never hot. Fill a few degrees warm, since it cools while the tortoise sits in it. A chilled tortoise sits still and does not drink.
  • A dedicated plastic tub, emptied into the toilet. Never the kitchen sink or a bathtub people use.
  • A tortoise that drinks hard at every soak is living in an enclosure that is too dry. The fix is the humid hide and the substrate, not more soaks.

Growth, weight & body condition

Slow and steady is right, and the scale tells you more than the eye.

There is no growth chart worth printing for this species, because a healthy Russian tortoise grows slowly and unevenly with the seasons. A hatchling starts at about 42 to 50 mm (1.7 to 2 in) of shell and 18 to 23 g, and an adult ends up 5 to 10 in (13 to 25 cm) long. What you are watching for along the way is steady, unhurried gain.

Too fast

Rapid growth on a rich, high-protein diet is one suspected driver of pyramiding, which is permanent. A young tortoise is not to be pushed along.

Too slow

Growth that stalls does damage as well. A young tortoise is not to be starved either; steady gain on the scale is the target.

Too heavy

Flesh crowding the shell openings as it withdraws means it is being fed too much. In this species overweight is the usual direction of error.

Weighing

  • Weigh in grams on a digital kitchen scale, at the same point in the routine each time, and write it down. Because so much of the animal is shell, the scale shows a change long before your eye does.
  • Weigh a young tortoise regularly, weigh any new tortoise on arrival for a baseline, and weigh every one to two weeks through brumation.
  • Read the log as a trend: steady gain in a youngster, a steady weight in a settled adult. A loss you cannot explain goes to the vet with the log.
