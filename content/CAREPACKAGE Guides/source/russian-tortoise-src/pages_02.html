Enclosure size, type & where it goes

Floor area, an open top, and solid walls. Height hardly matters.

Floor area is the number that matters, because this is an unusually active tortoise that walks its whole enclosure every day and rearranges anything light enough to move. A 4×2 ft (120×60 cm) table, about 8 sq ft, is the absolute indoor minimum for one adult, and it is a floor rather than a goal: below it the enclosure is failing, and at it the job is not finished. Aim for at least 12 sq ft, and treat 7×3.5 ft (210×105 cm), about 24.5 sq ft, as the adult working size. Each extra tortoise needs at least 10 sq ft more.

Seen from above. The basking lamp and the UVB tube sit together over the warm end, so the tortoise gets heat and UVB in the same place. The cool end stays genuinely cool, with the moist hide and a deeper dig zone. Hides, stones and dishes sit away from the walls.

An open table, not a glass tank

An open-topped tortoise table is strongly preferred over a closed glass aquarium, for two separate reasons. Glass ventilates poorly, and damp air held against an arid-adapted animal is a cause of respiratory infection. And a tortoise that can see through a wall it cannot pass tends to pace along it. Neither problem is fixed by buying a bigger tank.

  • Walls solid and smooth, at least a foot (30 cm) high. Wire or mesh invites climbing, and this species climbs better than its shape suggests.
  • Nothing makes a ladder. Hides, rocks, dishes and heaped substrate stay away from the walls, and corners are checked for a foothold.
  • Open on top indoors, with room above for the lamps at their mounting distances and no shelf or lid in the light path.

Where it goes

Pick a room that holds at least 60°F (16°C) at night, or plan for a ceramic heater on the enclosure. Keep it out of drafts, which are one of the triggers for respiratory infection, and out of direct window sun: window glass blocks the UVB, so the sun through it adds heat and nothing the shell needs.

Temperature, basking & night lows

A real gradient, measured on the surface, in an unusually cold-hardy species.

A tortoise cannot make its own heat, so the enclosure offers a range and lets the animal choose: a hot basking spot at one end and a genuinely cool end at the other. If the whole table reads the same, the animal cannot regulate itself, whether that reading is too high or right on target.

A cold-hardy species, within limits

Russian tortoises are one of the most cold-tolerant tortoises kept, and a night drop to around 60°F (16°C) needs no added heat in most homes. Sixty is still the floor. A room that will not hold it needs a ceramic heater on a thermostat, since a heat source that gives off light is wrong at night.

The heat source

  • A halogen basking bulb over the warm end is the daytime heat source indoors. Tortoises kept outdoors in mild climates often need no added heat at all.
  • Distance is the adjustment. Raise or lower the lamp to set the surface temperature, then hold it there with a dimming thermostat. Wattage is a starting guess you verify, not a number to trust.
  • Keep every lamp at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest point the tortoise can reach, including the top of a hide or a rock it can climb.
  • Basking lamp and UVB tube together, at the same end, so heat and ultraviolet light arrive in the same place, as they do in sunlight.
Two thermometers, two jobs The infrared gun reads the basking surface; the digital probe thermometer reads the cool end. They are measuring different places on purpose and should read differently. A gradient is the goal, not a matching pair of numbers.
In summer, heat is the danger Overheating is one of the five ways an outdoor tortoise dies (page {{P:outdoor}}). Indoors, check the enclosure on the hottest afternoon of the year, not the morning, and keep it out of a sunroom and out of direct sun through glass.

Thermostats, timers & thermometers

The equipment that holds the targets, and the three items first setups leave out.

A heat source with no thermostat draws full power with no ceiling, and a basking surface under it can climb well past what a reptile can tolerate. A thermostat measures the temperature at one point and cuts or lowers power once that point reaches the set value. Three items on the shopping list, the dimming thermostat, the infrared temperature gun and the gram scale, are the ones most first setups skip, and they are exactly the equipment that prevents thermal burns and catches slow weight loss.

ControllerWhat it doesSuits
Dimming thermostatLowers the power to a bulb instead of switching it off and onThe halogen basking bulb. The right choice here
Pulse proportionalSends short pulses of power to hold a steady averageA ceramic heater for night heat
On/offCuts power fully at the set point, restores it fully below itSlow heat sources only, never a basking bulb

Where the probe goes

The probe sits where the tortoise is, at the basking spot, never up near the fixture reading room air. Makers disagree on whether it belongs on the basking surface itself or in the air a few inches below the bulb, so follow your own controller's instructions and then verify the result with the infrared gun. Hold the probe in place with a probe clip or a suction cup, never tape. A probe that slips and reads the wrong spot is how a correctly set thermostat still produces a burn.

The rest of the kit

  • A timer on the lights, so the day length holds whether or not you are home. The cheapest item on the list.
  • An infrared temperature gun for the basking surface. It reads a surface from a distance, which no other instrument here does.
  • A digital probe thermometer at the cool end, plus a hygrometer for humidity.
  • A digital scale reading in grams, for the weight log that runs for the tortoise's whole life.
  • A UVI meter, optional, the only way to measure the UVB at the basking spot rather than assume it.
How thermostats fail Most fail obviously: the heat stops and the enclosure cools. The dangerous failure is rarer, a thermostat stuck on, or a probe that has slipped off its spot. A second, independent reading checked every day, the infrared gun on the basking surface, catches either one before it becomes a burn or a cold snap.

UVB: tube, strength & mounting distance

The one piece of kit you cannot substitute, and the one that fails invisibly.

UVB on the skin is how a tortoise makes vitamin D3, and vitamin D3 is how it absorbs calcium to build and keep a shell. A young tortoise short on UVB grows a soft, deformed shell, and that damage is permanent. The difficulty is that UVB output falls away well before the visible light dims, so a tube that is obviously working can be delivering very little.

RequirementDetail
TubeA linear T5 HO tube, 10 to 12% strength, in a reflective fixture. Coil and compact UVB bulbs are not adequate for this species
Length and positionA third to half the enclosure length, over the warm end alongside the basking lamp
TargetA UVI of 3.0 to 4.0 at the basking area
Mounting distanceWith mesh between tube and tortoise, its back sits 14 to 16 in (35 to 40 cm) below the tube. With no mesh, 17 to 20 in (43 to 50 cm)
ReplacementEvery 6 to 12 months depending on the model, never later than 12, on the calendar rather than when it looks dim. Write the date on the equipment log (page {{P:equiplog}})
Visible lightA bright 6500 K daylight lamp alongside. K, kelvin, is the scale for the color of light; 6500 K is white daylight
Day lengthOn a timer: 12 hours on and 12 off, or a seasonal swing from about 14 hours in summer to 10 in winter

Three ways to deliver no UVB while believing you are

  • The wrong bulb. A coil or compact bulb is not a substitute for a linear T5 HO tube over a tortoise.
  • Glass or plastic in the path. Window glass blocks essentially all UVB, and most clear plastics block all of it or close to it. A sunny window gives heat and light, not UVB. A mesh screen costs some output, which is what the two mounting distances above allow for.
  • A tube past its date. Output decays from the day it is switched on. Distance matters too: UVB falls off sharply, so a fixture correct at 12 in (30 cm) may deliver almost nothing at 20 in (50 cm).
Sunlight beats every bulb Unfiltered natural sunlight outdoors, in weather that suits the species, delivers UVB no lamp matches, and as little as 1 to 2 hours a week of it can have a marked effect on a reptile's health. It does not replace the indoor tube, because the weather does not cooperate for most of the year. Doing it safely is the subject of page {{P:outdoor}}.

Humidity, substrate & furnishings

An arid species that still needs somewhere damp, and soil deep enough to dig in.

Two humidities, not one

Keep the air at the open surface relatively low, 40 to 50% for an adult. A juvenile runs moister, 40 to 75%, lower by day and higher at night. High humidity across the whole enclosure is a real cause of respiratory infection, so resist the instinct to keep everything damp. But the tortoise lives in two climates: a dry, well-ventilated surface it walks and basks on, and a genuinely humid microclimate underneath, in a burrow or a moist hide, where it spends much of its day and does most of its shedding. Build both. A hide lined with damp peat moss on the cool side is the minimum. An enclosure that is dry everywhere, with no damp retreat and nothing to dig into, produces a dehydrated tortoise, and that is a more common failure in this species than one that is too wet.

Digging is close to a welfare need Burrows are how this species buffers heat, cold and dryness in the wild, so an enclosure with no diggable zone is missing something the tortoise actively needs, not a nice extra.

Furnishings

  • At least two hides, facing different directions: one warm and dry, one cool and moist. Terracotta half-pots, wooden hide boxes filled with loose substrate, cork flats, or a partly buried hide the tortoise can dig out further all work.
  • Terrain: hollow logs, flat stones, low platforms and gentle slopes, which get used rather than walked past, and which break the space into routes. All of it away from the walls.
  • A water dish: a shallow bowl, something like a plant saucer, sunk so its lip sits level with the surface. It doubles as a soaking dish.
  • A feeding tile or flat stone, so food is not eaten off the soil.

Outdoor pens & escape-proofing

The richest setup this species can have, and the one it will try hardest to leave.

Whenever local temperature and security allow, a secure outdoor pen is the best enrichment there is for this species: real sunlight, real soil to dig, varied ground and plants growing where it walks. A pen shaped around the species is hilly and rocky, drains well, has shrubby cover such as lavender, rosemary and cistus, and is sown with an edible wildflower mix so the tortoise grazes what grows there. But a pen that is not dig-proof, climb-proof and predator-proof is a bigger risk than staying indoors. Wild Russian tortoises dig burrows up to 2 m (about 6.5 ft) long.

ElementWhat it needs
WallsSolid and opaque, never mesh or chain link, about 2 ft (60 cm) high with a lip of about 4 in (10 cm) projecting inward along the top
Buried barrierWalls sunk at least 12 in (30 cm) into the ground, in concrete or buried wire mesh
TopCovered with mesh. Dogs, foxes and raccoons come in at ground level, crows, ravens and birds of prey from above
ShadePermanent: a solid roof section or a shelter, not a shadow that moves off by mid-afternoon
ShelterAn enclosed house that stays dry, with runoff draining away from the entrance, checked after storms
WaterNever deeper than the tortoise's head is tall, with a slope or ramp out from every side it can enter
PlantsEvery plant in or reachable from the pen checked before the tortoise goes in (page {{P:never}})

The five ways an outdoor tortoise dies

  • Overheating. A desert tortoise, a species built for heat, starts drooling heavily at a body temperature of 103°F (39.5°C), and 109°F (43°C) is lethal: under 7°F between the two. A tortoise drooling or frothing goes into shade at once and to a vet the same day.
  • Predation. In Segura and colleagues' 2020 study of wild spur-thighed tortoises, a related species, young tortoises under 75 mm (about 3 in) of shell were taken by ravens, which caused every recorded death on open ground with little cover. Anything that small needs a covered pen and cover to duck under.
  • Escape. Digging under or climbing a corner. Walk the perimeter regularly and look for fresh digging along the base.
  • Drowning. A pool or dish it can get into and not out of, or an upended tortoise in water.
  • An unsafe plant growing in or over the pen.
When it comes back in The floor outdoors is the same 60°F (16°C) as indoors, but a 60°F afternoon usually means a night in the low fifties with wind and damp on top. Bring the tortoise in while the daytime reading is still comfortably above the floor, and act on the forecast rather than the afternoon thermometer.

Cleaning & hygiene

A little every day, a scrub when something is soiled, and a full change of soil a few times a year.

Cleaning and disinfecting are two different jobs. Cleaning is the physical removal of droppings, food and grime; disinfecting kills what is left on the surface afterward, and it only works on a surface that has already been scrubbed, because a film of dirt shields bacteria from the disinfectant.

WhenWhat to do
DailyPick out droppings and urates (the white part of the waste) as soon as you see them, and any uneaten fresh food. Empty, wash and refill the water dish.
As it happensLift out any soiled patch of substrate and replace it.
WeeklyWipe down dishes, hides and the feeding tile. Dampen the moist hide if it has dried out.
Every 3 to 6 monthsMove the tortoise to a secure temporary tub, take everything out, scrub and disinfect the table, hides and dishes, and refill with all-new substrate.

Disinfecting safely

  • Household bleach at 1:32, which is half a cup of bleach per gallon of water, works against bacteria and many viruses.
  • It needs 10 minutes of wet contact: apply it to the scrubbed surface and keep it wet for the full time rather than wiping it straight off.
  • Rinse with plenty of clean water until no bleach smell remains, then let everything dry completely and air out. Plan on a full day of airing before the tortoise goes back in.

Where to do it

In a utility sink, a dedicated plastic tub, or outdoors. Never in the kitchen sink, a bathroom sink, or a bathtub people use, and never anywhere food is prepared. Wash any bedding, decor or gear separately from dishes used for people. The soaking tub is a dedicated plastic tub too, emptied into the toilet.

Why the rules are strict Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella bacteria in the gut and shed them in droppings without being ill, and a negative test does not change that, because shedding comes and goes. The route into people is hands and surfaces to mouth. Wash your hands with soap after every contact with the tortoise, its enclosure, its water or its substrate (page {{P:handling}}).
During quarantine A new tortoise has its own dishes, hides, tools and cleaning brushes that never cross to another tortoise's enclosure, and it is cleaned and handled last each day, after every other reptile in the house (page {{P:arrival}}).
