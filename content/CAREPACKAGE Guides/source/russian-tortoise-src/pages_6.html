The forty-year number At $365 to $715 a year, a Russian tortoise costs roughly $15,000 to $30,000 over a forty-year life, before a single illness. Sick or emergency care with tests and medication commonly runs $150 to $300 or more per episode, and this animal will have episodes. Nobody budgets a tortoise this way and everybody should: the purchase price is the least significant number on this page.

Electricity moves most: a cold house costs meaningfully more to keep a basking spot in, and a tortoise outdoors for half the year costs almost nothing to heat. Save on the table, which is a plywood box anyone can build, on substrate bought as topsoil rather than as a reptile product, and on food, which grows free in a lawn (page {{P:weeds}}). Do not save on the UVB fixture, the thermostat, or the vet: a coil bulb costs a fraction of a T5 kit and produces the deformity on page {{P:mbd}}.

First 30 days checklist

In order. Most of week one is letting it settle and watching what comes out.

Before it arrives

Enclosure built, filled, lit and running for several days
Basking surface measured with the infrared gun and adjusted to 95 to 100°F
Cool end confirmed genuinely cool with a probe thermometer
UVB mounted at the right distance, date written on the bulb
Reptile vet found and the number written down
Quarantine room ready if you already keep reptiles. Page {{P:parasites}}

Days 1 to 7

Weigh on arrival and write the number down. This is the baseline for the next forty years
Handle only to move it in and to soak it. Otherwise leave it alone
Soak daily this week, 20 minutes, shallow warm water. Newly moved tortoises are usually dehydrated
Offer food daily and do not worry if it is refused for the first few days
Watch for the first droppings and urates, and keep a fresh sample for the vet
Check the basking temperature again with the animal actually in the enclosure

Days 8 to 14

Veterinary health check plus a fecal test, symptoms or not. Page {{P:parasites}}
Ask the vet to confirm sex if the animal is large enough, and to review the supplement schedule
Weigh again. A newly moved tortoise often dips slightly, then recovers
Switch the diet gradually toward weeds and hay if it arrived on something else. Page {{P:diet}}

Days 15 to 30

Weekly soak established, and the weekly weigh-in logged. Page {{P:ownerlog}}
Scatter feeding started, in different places each day. Page {{P:mistakes}}
Weeds identified in your own area, or a forage tray sown. Page {{P:weeds}}
First monthly shell check done. Page {{P:shell}}
First supervised outdoor session if the weather allows. Page {{P:outdoor}}
Long-term carer named and written on page {{P:emergency}}
What normal looks like by day 30 Out and walking the enclosure in the morning, basking for a stretch, eating within a few hours of food appearing, digging into the substrate or a hide by late afternoon, and producing formed droppings with soft white urates every couple of days. A tortoise that hides constantly, refuses food for weeks, or has not passed anything is not settling in, it is unwell. Page {{P:redflags}}.

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Ten minutes a day, half an hour a week, and one decision each autumn.

Every day

Fresh food, scattered in new places, on a tile or stone
Water dish emptied, rinsed and refilled
Remove yesterday's uneaten greens and any droppings
Look at the tortoise properly: gait, eyes, nose, and whether it basked
Check the basking surface reading and the cool end
Damp the moist hide if it has dried out

Every week

Soak, 20 to 30 minutes, and use it as a health check. Page {{P:poop}}
Weigh and log the number. Page {{P:ownerlog}}
Top up hay and refresh the forage trays
Turn and refresh the substrate surface; replace any soiled patch
Rearrange non-critical decor, leaving the hides where they are
Walk the perimeter of any outdoor pen looking for digging

Every month

Shell check, top and bottom, under good light. Page {{P:shell}}
Check the shell margin for the ridging that starts pyramiding. Page {{P:sexing}}
Full substrate turn or partial replacement, and a wash of dishes and hides
Review the weight log as a trend rather than as this month's number
Check the UVB bulb's replacement date. Page {{P:equiplog}}

WhenWhat changes
SpringWeeds return. Start picking, sow forage trays, and begin outdoor sessions once daytime temperatures allow. A tortoise coming out of brumation is soaked daily for the first week
SummerThe dangerous season. Check the enclosure and the pen at the hottest part of the afternoon, confirm permanent shade, and never leave a tortoise in a conservatory, greenhouse, car or unshaded pen. Page {{P:heat}}

Power outages, travel & transport

A reptile depends on an outlet for the one thing it cannot make: body heat.

A healthy adult Russian tortoise tolerates one ordinary cool night without harm. The risk is duration, not the single low reading, and this species has more margin than almost any other reptile kept: it tolerates 60°F (16°C) comfortably, and the point to act is a sustained period below 60°F outside an intentional brumation. Pushed meaningfully below its preferred range for more than a day or two, a reptile becomes more susceptible to bacterial infection and stops eating, which is the textbook setup for the respiratory infection on page {{P:respiratory}}.

During an outage

  • Move the enclosure to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold in the heat that is left.
  • A wrapped hot water bottle or a commercial hand warmer beside the animal, never touching it directly and never underneath it.
  • Stop feeding if the enclosure is genuinely cold. A reptile cannot digest below its working temperature, and food sitting undigested in a cold gut is a real problem. This is the opposite of the rule for a mammal or a bird.
  • Keep water available, and keep soaking on the normal schedule with warm water heated another way.
  • No candles, gas stoves or open flames near the enclosure. The fire and carbon monoxide risk outweighs the cold, which this species handles well.

Outage kit, packed before you need it: two or three hand warmers or a hot water bottle, two thick towels, a battery or wind-up thermometer, a ventilated tub sized for the animal, and your reptile vet and 24-hour exotics numbers written on paper rather than saved on a phone that may die.

Travel and being away

Trip lengthWhat a healthy adult needs
1 to 2 nightsOften fine alone. Lights on timers, thermostat running, water dish full, and a generous scatter of greens and hay before you leave

Over a weekA daily or every-other-day sitter who has done a practice visit, or boarding with a reptile-experienced vet or shop. Never a general boarding kennel

Name someone, tell them, and write it on page {{P:emergency}}. Tortoise rescues exist because this conversation usually happens too late.

Glossary

Terms used earlier in this book, in the sense they were used.
BrumationThe reptile equivalent of hibernation. A period of cold dormancy, 2 to 4 months in this species, and optional indoors. Page {{P:brumation}}
CarapaceThe upper shell
CloacaThe single opening for waste and reproduction. Cloacolith: a hardened urate blockage in it
Ferguson ZoneA classification of how much UV a species seeks out in the wild, used to match a bulb to an animal rather than buying the strongest one
GravidCarrying eggs. Fertile or not
HerpesvirusA viral disease of tortoises with no cure, high contagion, and near-total mortality in unquarantined groups. Page {{P:respiratory}}
Hexamita parvaA parasite of the kidney and urinary tract found frequently in this species. Page {{P:parasites}}
MBDMetabolic bone disease, from a calcium and phosphorus imbalance, inadequate UVB, or both. Page {{P:mbd}}
MycoplasmaThe bacterium behind chronic upper respiratory tract disease in tortoises: the runny nose that never fully clears
PlastronThe lower shell
PyramidingRaised, cone-shaped scutes from overfeeding, excess protein or chronic dehydration. Permanent. Page {{P:mbd}}
SCLStraight carapace length. The standard measurement, taken in a straight line rather than over the curve
ScuteOne of the keratin plates covering the shell. They grow at their margins, which is why the shell is a record
SCUDSepticemic cutaneous ulcerative disease, an older term for severe shell infection. Page {{P:shell}}
T5 HOA high-output linear fluorescent tube, the correct format for reptile UVB. Page {{P:uvb}}
UratesThe white, semi-solid crystalline waste a reptile passes instead of liquid urine. The best hydration gauge an owner has. Page {{P:poop}}
UVIUV index, the measured strength of UV reaching a point. Target 3.0 to 4.0 at this species' basking site
21 CFR 1240.62The FDA four-inch rule. It restricts commercial sale, not ownership. Page {{P:legal}}
