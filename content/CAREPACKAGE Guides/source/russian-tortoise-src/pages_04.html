Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & hygiene

An observation animal with a personality, picked up for a reason rather than for its own sake.

Russian tortoises tolerate being picked up, but they neither need it nor seek it out, and most do not enjoy it. Treat this as an animal you watch and feed rather than one you hold. Handling is for health checks, soaks, weighing and moving the tortoise, and most of the risk in those moments comes from a fall. Many tortoises do come to accept a gentle rub on the shell, and many will take food from a hand: that is the relationship to build.

Picking one up

  • Scoop from below, a palm under the plastron (the lower shell), instead of grabbing the shell from above.
  • Then hold the shell with two hands, so the body and the legs are both supported. Firm but gentle.
  • Work low, sitting or kneeling over the floor or a table. A fall can do serious damage to a tortoise and can be fatal.
  • Keep it brief and purposeful, and put it back where it can walk straight into cover.
  • Wash your hands before and after, every time.

Salmonella, plainly

Healthy reptiles, tortoises included, carry Salmonella bacteria and shed them in their droppings without being ill, and a negative test does not clear an animal, because shedding comes and goes. The risk is not a bite. It is hands, water and surfaces carried to the mouth. The four-inch rule on page {{P:legal2}} was written for exactly this reason.

  • Wash with soap right after touching the tortoise, its enclosure, its water, its dishes or its substrate.
  • Keep it out of the kitchen entirely, off counters and away from anywhere food is prepared or eaten.
  • Never clean its gear in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink or a bathtub people use. A dedicated tub does the job.
  • No kissing the tortoise, and no eating or drinking while handling it.

Children under 5Should not handle or touch the tortoise or anything from its enclosure at all. Avoidance, not just extra care

Daily rhythm, courtship & living alone

What a normal day looks like, what the loud moments mean, and why one tortoise is usually the right number.

A normal day

In the wild this species is active in two short windows a day, mid-morning and late afternoon, and spends the rest of the time in its burrow. A captive tortoise that basks, grazes, walks its enclosure, digs in and then disappears for hours is keeping its own schedule. It is an unusually active animal for a tortoise: it walks the whole of whatever floor it is given and rearranges anything light enough to move. It also climbs and digs better than its shape suggests, so the enclosure is checked for escape routes as a routine.

Courtship, the loudest thing it does

A courting male circles a female and bites at her head and legs, then stretches his neck out stiffly, stares her in the eye and jerks his head up and down in quick succession. Mating comes with high-pitched squeaks from him. In the breeding season males turn on each other and fight hard.

Living alone

  • Never two males. Males do not tolerate each other. They fight viciously, and the loser ends up injured, flipped onto his back, or chronically stressed and prone to disease.
  • A male with a female is hard on her: he circles and bites at her head and legs, and with nowhere to escape she takes that attention constantly. The pair will also probably produce eggs.
  • Two females are usually more tolerant, but not always: one can become a bully. A shared enclosure needs at least 10 sq ft more floor for each added tortoise, and enough hides, plants and logs to break every sightline.
  • One is the default. House a tortoise alone, or two females with caution.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Giving digging, grazing and exploring somewhere real to happen, and the errors that cause most trouble.

Enrichment copies the wild pattern: a burrow to disappear into, a daily round between shelter and forage, and a diet that changes constantly. It never replaces correct UVB, temperature and enclosure size, which come first.

Digging

  • A deep zone of 6 to 12 in (15 to 30 cm) or more of soil mix, or a dedicated dig box.
  • A partly buried hide, a terracotta half-pot or a box of loose soil gives it a start it can dig out further.

Grazing

  • Scatter food in several spots instead of one bowl, and move the spots daily or every other day.
  • Tuck greens into the folds of a crumpled towel or a forage mat.
  • Pots of safe weeds, or a tray of tortoise forage seed mix to crop as a living plant.

Exploring

  • Several hides facing different directions, plus hollow logs, cork flats, flat stones, low platforms and gentle slopes that make routes.
  • Move one non-essential item every 1 to 4 weeks, depending on the tortoise. Leave the warm and cool hides where they are: they are its security.

Outdoors

  • Supervised or fully secure outdoor time whenever weather and security allow is the richest enrichment there is (page {{P:outdoor}}).

Choosing a tortoise, quarantine & the first vet visit

What a healthy one looks like, and why a new tortoise lives apart for months.

Choosing

Buy captive-bred from a reputable breeder whenever possible. Wild-caught animals, and sales where nobody can say where the tortoise came from, carry a real head start on disease: imported tortoises arrive stressed and carrying parasites, and die at a higher rate. Then look at the animal itself.

Look forWalk away from
Active and alertInactive or lethargic
Feels heavy for its sizeSunken or closed eyes
Pulls its head and legs into the shell when picked upAny discharge from the nostrils or eyes
A smooth shell, not cracked, pitted or missing scutesSoft or infected-looking patches on the shell

The first vet visit

Book a reptile vet before or right after pickup, and have the tortoise examined within a week at the latest. Every new tortoise gets a fecal test for gut parasites, symptoms or not; roundworms and protozoa are common, especially in wild-caught animals, and often cause no signs at all. After that, an exam at least once a year with a fecal test at every visit.

Quarantine: six months, not weeks

Tortoise herpesvirus is the reason. It is shed in mouth secretions and spread mostly by ingestion, so a shared water dish, substrate or hands moving between animals can carry it. There is no cure, survivors carry it for life, and a carrier can look healthy for months. In Marenzoni and colleagues' 2018 outbreak investigation, the source tortoise had finished a full year of quarantine and showed nothing for three years beyond an occasional wet nose; when it broke, 75% of the twenty tortoises exposed died. Time alone caught nothing. Testing would have.

  • At least six months, and six months to a year is the real range, apart from any other tortoise or turtle.
  • A separate room or airspace, not a second enclosure in the same room.
  • Its own dishes, soak tub, hides and brushes, which never cross to another enclosure.
  • Fed and handled last each day, with hands washed or gloves changed between animals every time.
  • Tested, not just timed: a full exam at the start and the end, and during it a blood test (serology) and a PCR test (polymerase chain reaction, a lab test that finds a germ's genetic material in a swab), the blood test repeated several weeks later because antibodies take time to appear.
  • A fecal screen before the tortoise joins any other, which is also how the kidney parasite Hexamita parva is kept out of a group (page {{P:parasites}}).

Sexing & females that lay

Easy to tell once grown, and a female with no male in the house can still lay eggs.

Telling males from females

FeatureMaleFemale
TailMuch longer, usually carried tucked to one side. The quickest cue in this speciesShorter
Vent positionFarther out toward the tail tipCloser to the shell edge
Plastron (lower shell)Concave, dished inward. The most useful cue on most adult tortoisesFlat, leaving room for eggs to pass
Size and shellSlightly smallerSlightly larger, with flared scutes

None of these are precise on a young animal, where the plastron has barely dished and the tail difference is slight, so treat an early call as provisional and check again as it grows. A vet or an experienced keeper can confirm. Sexual maturity comes at around 10 years, and a mature female can lay two or three clutches in a season. Sex matters before any pairing, because a male and a female together will probably produce eggs.

A female doesn't need a male to lay

A female builds eggs on her own cycle and still has to pass them whether or not a male was ever involved. The eggs will not hatch, but the process and its risks are the same. An unmated female is in fact the likelier one to hold a clutch, since it is usually fertilized eggs that get laid. So every female owner, not just breeders, needs a plan for where she lays.

A nest site before she needs one

A missing or unsuitable nest site is one of the causes of egg retention, and an entirely preventable one. The fix is a deep, loose area of substrate she can dig a hole her own body length into and fully bury the clutch, there all the time rather than built once she starts pacing. If the everyday substrate is too shallow, a deeper section or a separate container filled well past the normal depth works. With nowhere acceptable to dig, a female may simply hold her eggs, for up to six months, waiting for somewhere better.

Signs she is carrying eggs

  • Restlessness and test-digging, often abandoned and started again over days.
  • Eating less, sometimes nothing for days or weeks, while staying bright, active and alert. That last part is what makes it normal.
Do not press on her belly Pressing on her belly to feel for eggs can crack one inside her, and a cracked egg is a vet emergency. If you think she is carrying eggs and something seems wrong, a vet confirms with imaging instead of hand pressure.

Egg binding

Egg binding, or dystocia, is a female unable to pass her eggs. The question is the animal, not the calendar.

Telling normal laying behavior from a problem is genuinely hard in tortoises, because a female can hold eggs on purpose for weeks and look exactly like one that is stuck. Digging and straining are not the useful signal. What separates the two is whether she stays bright, active and alert between attempts.

SignLikely a nest-site problem you can fixLikely dystocia: vet the same day
AppetiteReduced, sometimes absent for days or weeksAbsent, and it does not come back
AlertnessBright, active and responsive despite not eatingWeak, lethargic or unresponsive
Digging and strainingRestless test-digging that pauses and resumes over daysStraining that goes on with no progress toward laying
AbdomenRounder, otherwise normalDistended, and it stays that way
Given a proper nest siteOften resolves once she has somewhere acceptableNo improvement even with a good site

Why it happens

Nonobstructive: fixable at home

No suitable nest site, the wrong temperature or humidity, a poor diet, or dehydration. These are husbandry gaps, and they can improve once the gap is fixed. Simply giving her a quiet area with the right substrate can be enough to let her lay.

Obstructive: a vet case

An oversized or misshapen egg, scarring, a misshapen pelvis, or a narrowed oviduct. These are physical blockages: no better setup fixes them, and a vet needs imaging to find them.

Eggs held into autumn In a species that brumates, eggs held into late summer or early autumn may be the body preparing for the winter slowdown rather than dystocia. A female holding eggs going into fall while still bright and eating is a different picture from one that has stopped eating and gone flat, whatever the date.

What a vet does

Imaging comes first, because the plan depends on whether there is a blockage. Without one, supportive care, fluids by vein or into the bone, calcium and vitamin injections, and a hormone such as oxytocin to make the oviduct contract, can help her pass the clutch herself. The dose is a species-specific veterinary decision, never something to estimate at home. With a real obstruction, surgery is the usual answer, since no hormone moves a physical blockage. Left untreated, prolonged retention carries serious downstream risks, and a difficult clutch can leave infection behind.

Brumation: the decision & the protocol

Built into the species, optional indoors, and dangerous done casually.

Brumation is the reptile winter slowdown: cooler temperatures and shorter days push a tortoise into weeks of near-dormancy. Russian tortoises are built for it, and wild-caught adults especially may refuse food or show stress if stopped. Indoors there is a real choice: full lighting, heat and feeding all year keeps the tortoise active instead. Both routes are defensible; a brumation drifted into without the steps below is not.

Do not brumate a tortoise that is Underweight · showing any respiratory signs · sick, or recovering from illness in the last year · under about 4 years old · not vet-checked this autumn. That tortoise overwinters warm, never below 60°F (16°C), and tries again next year.

The protocol, if you say yes

StepWhat to do
1. Vet checkEvery autumn, with a fecal parasite screen. A hidden infection or parasite load becomes serious once the metabolism drops
2. The fastStop feeding 1 to 3 weeks before the cool-down, the longer end for a bigger tortoise, with the heat kept on at 70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C) for the whole fast so the gut empties. Food left in a cold gut rots
3. Cool downAfter the fast, heat off, then down by roughly 9°F (5°C) a week into the band below
4. Hold35 to 50°F (2 to 10°C), as close to 41°F (5°C) as you can. Below 35°F (2°C) or above 60°F (16°C) is unsafe: too cold risks blindness or death, too warm burns through the reserves it needs to wake
5. LengthA ceiling, not a target: 10 weeks at most for a small tortoise and 14 for a large one, or a shorter 6 weeks to 3 months
6. WeighEvery 1 to 2 weeks. About 1% of body weight a month is normal; a 1,000 g tortoise losing about 10 g a month is fine. Past 7% in total, end it
7. WakeWarm slowly: room temperature for the first few hours, then heat. Once it is alert, a warm soak of at least 30 minutes, up to twice a day. Easy, succulent food first

Where it sleeps

  • A dedicated fridge, used for nothing else: a probe thermometer rather than the dial, the door opened about 30 seconds a day for air, and a bowl of water in the bottom for humidity.
  • Or an insulated box: a rodent-proof, padded outer box with mesh over its air holes, and an inner box filled with polystyrene chips, dry leaves or shredded paper. No hay or straw.
  • Never unmonitored cold. A tortoise left to find its own spot in a garden or garage faces freezing, dehydration and rodents with nobody reading a thermometer.
End it now if Weight loss passes the action point · any discharge from the nose, eyes or vent · the temperature dropped below freezing, even briefly · any sign of illness. After waking, a tortoise that has not urinated or eaten within about a week sees a vet.

Section 06 · The Law

The four-inch rule & the trade

The rule that actually shapes how these tortoises are sold is federal, and it is not the one people quote.

Almost everyone has heard some version of it: turtles under four inches are illegal. That is not what the regulation says. The rule is 21 CFR 1240.62 (Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, section 1240.62), a 1975 regulation of the US FDA (Food and Drug Administration) written to reduce Salmonella infection in small children. It says that live turtles and viable turtle eggs with a shell under 4 in (10 cm) long may not be sold, held for sale, or offered for any other kind of commercial or public distribution.

  • It is about selling, not owning. Nothing in it stops anyone keeping a small tortoise.
  • It is about size, not species. It catches a hatchling Russian tortoise for the same reason it catches a hatchling red-eared slider.
  • It exempts private keepers. Subsection (c) excludes sale and distribution not in connection with a business. A private keeper rehoming a hatchling is inside the rule; a shop doing the same thing is not.

That exception is why hatchlings move hand to hand through private keepers while shops stock only animals past the four-inch line, and it is the real reason a Russian tortoise you buy is usually already a few years old. New York City and Montana write the same four-inch line into their own rules.

Why Colorado is the surprise

Colorado allows what its lists name and nothing else, and the tortoise family is on the list, so a tortoise looks cleared. But the whole reptile block carries a qualifier: native to the tropical and subtropical region, fixed at 26 degrees either side of the equator. The Russian tortoise comes from Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Afghanistan and Iran, far north of that line, and the state's own reptile list records it as native outside the tropics. A family-level clearance is never the end of the question.

International trade

The species is on Appendix II of CITES, where trade runs on export quotas that the countries it comes from set for themselves, and it is assessed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. It has been traded heavily since the mid-1970s, and a wild-caught supply continues alongside captive breeding. Crossing an international border with one is a CITES question as well as a state one: check the import rules before you travel.

What actually constrains you Since the law barely does, plan around what the species imposes: a digger that will tunnel out of a pen that looks secure, a real UVB requirement indoors, and a lifespan long enough that rehoming is a real possibility rather than a hypothetical.
