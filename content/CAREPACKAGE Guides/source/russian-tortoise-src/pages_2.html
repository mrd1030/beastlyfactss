UVB, lighting & the equipment that controls it

The one piece of kit you cannot substitute, and the one that fails invisibly.

UVB drives vitamin D3 synthesis, which drives calcium absorption, which builds and maintains a shell. Without it a tortoise develops metabolic bone disease however much calcium is in the food (page {{P:mbd}}). What makes it dangerous is that output falls away months before the bulb looks dim, so a fixture that is visibly working can be delivering nothing useful.

RequirementDetail
Bulb typeA linear T5 HO tube in a reflective fixture. Arcadia 12% or Zoo Med ReptiSun 10.0 are the standard choices
LengthRoughly a third to half the enclosure length, mounted over the warm end alongside the basking lamp
TargetA UV index of 3.0 to 4.0 at the basking area, measured with a UV index meter
Mounting distanceWith mesh in the path, the tortoise's back sits 14 to 16 in (35 to 40 cm) below the tube. With no mesh, 17 to 20 in (43 to 50 cm)
ReplacementEvery 12 months, on the calendar, not when it stops lighting. Write the date on the bulb and on page {{P:equiplog}}
Visible lightA bright 6500K daylight lamp alongside. Tortoises are diurnal and a dim enclosure suppresses activity and appetite
Photoperiod12 hours on, 12 off, on a timer. Lengthen toward 14 in summer and shorten toward 10 in winter to track the seasons
Three ways to deliver no UVB while believing you are A coil or compact bulb. They concentrate output into a narrow cone, have been associated with eye and skin irritation, and are not adequate for a species that needs a UVI of 3 to 4 across a basking area.
Glass or plastic in the path. Glass removes essentially all of it, which is also why a sunny window is not sunlight to a tortoise. Mesh costs a substantial fraction, which is what the two mounting distances above are for.
A bulb over a year old. Output decays continuously from the day it is switched on.

The equipment that controls the rest

  • A timer for the lights, so the photoperiod holds whether or not you are home. The cheapest item on the list.
  • A dimming thermostat or lamp dimmer on the basking bulb, holding the surface temperature through changing room conditions. An on/off thermostat is the wrong controller for a basking lamp, because it flickers the light.
  • An infrared temperature gun for the basking surface. Under $25, and it settles what the other instruments cannot.
  • A digital probe thermometer at the cool end, plus a hygrometer. Page {{P:humidity}}.
  • A UV index meter, optional and expensive, and the only way to know rather than assume. Borrow one if a local keeper has it.
Sunlight beats every bulb Unfiltered natural sunlight delivers UVB no lamp matches, so a few supervised hours outdoors in warm weather is worth more than any equipment upgrade. Page {{P:outdoor}} covers doing it safely. It does not replace an indoor fixture: the weather does not cooperate for most of the year.

Humidity, substrate & the moist hide

An arid species that still needs somewhere damp, and an open disagreement about the number.
Two credible sources, two different figures General care guidance for this species, ours included, gives ambient humidity of 30 to 50% and warns that high ambient humidity across the whole enclosure is a real cause of respiratory infection. The ReptiFiles species care sheet gives 40 to 75%, lower during the day and higher at night, following what the animal experiences in a burrow. Both agree on the shape of the answer even where the numbers differ, and the shape is what matters: a dry, well-ventilated open surface with a genuinely humid microclimate available underneath and inside a hide. A tortoise in the wild spends most of its life in a burrow that is far damper than the steppe above it. Build both, and let the animal choose.

In practice: keep the open surface dry and the air moving, keep the deeper substrate slightly damp rather than bone dry, and provide at least one hide packed with moist sphagnum or peat moss on the cool side, remoistened every few days. That combination gives hydration and clean shedding without a permanently damp enclosure, which is what actually causes the respiratory and shell problems on pages {{P:respiratory}} and {{P:shell}}.

Substrate

UseNotes
50/50 topsoil and coconut coirThe standard mix. Holds a burrow shape, holds a little moisture at depth, and is cheap in bulk. Buy topsoil with no added fertilizer, pesticide or wetting agent
Sandy loam, or a topsoil and play sand blendCloser to the native substrate. A minority of sand in a soil mix is fine and useful
DepthAt least 4 in (10 cm), which is the minimum for the animal to dig a pallet, and 6 in (15 cm) or more in at least part of the enclosure is better
Avoid: pure sandLinked to mobility problems and impaction, and it does not hold a burrow
Avoid: heavy wet clay soilsWrong for a desert species and slow to dry
Avoid: newspaper or rabbit pellets aloneNo digging, no thermal buffering, and nothing the animal can do with them

Digging is not decoration Burrowing is how this species buffers temperature and humidity, and a substrate too shallow to dig into removes a behavior the animal actively needs rather than merely enjoys. If the main substrate cannot go deep enough, build a dedicated dig box: a plastic tub of damp soil mix sunk into the enclosure works, and it will be used constantly.

Soaking and water

Keep a shallow water dish available at all times, sunk level with the substrate so the animal can walk in and out and cannot tip. Change it daily; tortoises defecate in water routinely. Beyond that, soak the tortoise in shallow warm water for at least 20 minutes, two to three times a week indoors, more often for a young or newly acquired animal. The Tortoise Trust ties that frequency to reducing bladder-stone risk. The water should reach the bottom of the shell and no higher. Most tortoises drink and pass urates during a soak, which is also when you get the best look at both. Page {{P:poop}}.

Outdoor housing & escape-proofing

The best enclosure this species can have, and the one it will try hardest to leave.

Where climate and security allow, an outdoor pen is the richest environment this species can have: sunlight, real soil to dig, weather, and edible plants growing where it walks. Many keepers treat the pen as standard and the indoor table as the winter arrangement.

Building a pen

ElementWhat it needs
WallsSolid rather than mesh, so it cannot see through and pace, at least 12 in (30 cm) above ground with nothing climbable against them
Buried barrierWalls dug in at least 12 in (30 cm) of concrete, paving or mesh. VCA gives 6 to 12 in; build to the deep end, because this species tunnels out of pens that look secure
WaterA dish with a sloped edge it can walk out of. VCA sets the line by depth, not size: never higher than the tortoise's own head
ShadePermanent through the hottest part of the day, from a structure rather than a plant that may be cut back. Heat is the real outdoor danger
ShelterA dry insulated house it can retreat into in cold or wet weather, with a low entrance and hay or dry leaves
SubstrateDiggable soil, not lawn over hardpan, with a dig area 6 in (15 cm) deep or more
PlantingEdible weeds sown across the pen so it grazes as it walks. Page {{P:weeds}}
Cover overheadA secure top, not just tall walls: dogs, foxes and raccoons come in low, corvids and birds of prey from above. A 2020 study of wild spur-thighed tortoises put 75 mm (about 3 in) of shell as the size above which ravens stopped being a threat, and on open ground ravens took every recorded young-tortoise death. Anything smaller needs a lid and cover to duck under
Five ways an outdoor tortoise dies Heat. No permanent shade at 2 pm in July.
A dog. Shell trauma from a household dog is among the commonest serious injuries in pet tortoises, and it is usually the family's own.
Escape. Digging under, or climbing a corner. Corners are ladders: round them off.
Drowning. A pond, a deep bowl, or an upended tortoise in a dish it cannot right itself out of.
Pesticide, and the wrong plant. Sprayed lawn, treated soil, slug pellets, overspray. Check every ornamental in or over the pen: oleander and the rest of page {{P:never}} do not stop being toxic outdoors.

When outdoors is not an option

Indoor keeping is viable for this species for life, which is its main advantage over the larger tortoises. It needs the UVB fixture on page {{P:uvb}} to be right rather than approximately right, and it makes the enclosure size on page {{P:housing}} matter more, because no garden is making up the difference. A supervised session outside in warm weather bridges the two.

Take the temperature out there too Pen surfaces reach temperatures a lawn does not. Check a paving slab, a stone and the shaded soil with the infrared gun on the hottest afternoon of the year. If a surface is too hot for your hand, it is too hot for a plastron.

Handling, temperament & why males live alone

An observation animal with a personality, not a lap animal.

Russian tortoises are active, curious and genuinely responsive to their keepers: many approach confidently, recognize the person who brings food, and rearrange their enclosure overnight. None of that makes them a handling pet. Being picked up is a predator experience for an animal whose entire defense is staying on the ground inside its shell, and over-handling causes real, measurable stress. Keep physical contact purposeful, brief and infrequent, and get the relationship from watching and from feeding instead.

Picking one up, when you have to

  • Approach from the front or the side where it can see you, and pause. A hand descending from above is a bird.
  • Support the whole shell from underneath with both hands, keeping the animal level and low to the ground.
  • Never tip it vertically or onto its back. That compresses the lungs against the organs and is genuinely dangerous, not just unpleasant.
  • Never lift by a leg or the front of the shell, and never pull a retracted head or limb.
  • Sit on the floor. A dropped tortoise cracks, and a cracked shell is a page {{P:shell}} problem.
  • Wash your hands before and after. Reptiles carry Salmonella as normal gut flora, and it is the reason a tortoise is a poor pet for a household with children under five, adults over 65, anyone pregnant or anyone immunocompromised.
Salmonella, plainly Healthy tortoises shed Salmonella without being ill, and one negative test clears nothing, because shedding is intermittent. The route is hands, then face or food. Wash after every contact with the animal, its enclosure, dish or substrate. Never clean gear in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink or a bath people use; keep a plastic tub for it. Do not kiss the tortoise. The CDC line for under-fives is not to handle reptiles or their environments at all. This is the reason behind the four-inch rule on page {{P:legal}}, and a real risk rather than a regulatory relic.

Housing more than one

House alone by default. This is a solitary species that does not benefit from company the way a social mammal does, and an owner adding a second tortoise for the first one's sake is usually creating a problem. If you do keep more than one:

  • Never two males. Males are genuinely territorial and will ram, bite legs and flip one another, sometimes fatally. This is not a personality question and it does not resolve with more space.
  • Two females can work, with caution, in a large enclosure with several hides, several feeding stations and sight breaks between them. Watch for one animal monopolizing the basking spot.
  • A male with a single female is a problem for the female, who gets pursued and mounted relentlessly. A group of several females to one male spreads that, which is a breeding arrangement rather than a pet one.
  • Never mix species. Different husbandry needs, and pathogens that one species carries harmlessly can be fatal to another. Page {{P:parasites}}.
Better than they look, at escaping Despite the reputation, Russian tortoises climb and dig well and are persistent about both. They will scale a corner, push under a barrier, or work at the same weak point for weeks. Assume any enclosure is escapable until it has held for a season, and check the perimeter as a routine job rather than after the first escape.

Diet: what a steppe grazer actually eats

High fiber, low protein, low sugar, high calcium. And less of it than you think.

The dietary rule for Testudo species is short: low protein, low carbohydrate, low sugar, high calcium, high fiber, and that combination is the primary key to deformity-free growth. In the wild a Russian tortoise eats from a range of over 80 plant species across 27 families, almost entirely weeds, leaves and flowers. It is a grazer of tough, fibrous, low-nutrient plants, and every problem on page {{P:mbd}} comes from feeding it something richer.

The quantity nobody mentions In nature this species typically feeds on no more than about ninety days out of three hundred and sixty five, and even then spends roughly fifteen minutes grazing a day. Captive tortoises are fed every day, generously, on food far richer than steppe weeds, which is why overfeeding is the single most common husbandry error in this species and why pyramiding is so widespread. Feeding less, and feeding coarser, is closer to correct than most keepers are comfortable with.

How to feed

  • Scatter feed rather than using a bowl. Put greens in several places so the animal has to walk and search, which is closer to grazing and doubles as enrichment. Page {{P:mistakes}}.
  • Feed on a flat stone or a tile where you do scatter feed, so soil and substrate are not swallowed with every mouthful.
  • Adults: feed daily, in a portion roughly the size of the tortoise's shell, and expect it to be eaten within a few hours rather than picked at all day. A fasting day once a week does an adult no harm at all and reflects how the animal eats in the wild.
  • Hatchlings and juveniles eat daily and more, with no fasting days, because they are building shell.
  • Remove uneaten fresh food the same day. Hay can stay.
