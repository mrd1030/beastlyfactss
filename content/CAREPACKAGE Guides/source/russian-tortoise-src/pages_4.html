Health red flags & what to tell the vet

A hardy animal that hides decline for years, then declines quickly.

Almost every health problem here traces back to a handful of husbandry basics, and the pattern is worth learning as a pattern: weak UVB and low calcium cause metabolic bone disease; cold, damp or drafty conditions cause respiratory infection; overfeeding and poor hydration cause pyramiding; an unscreened new animal brings in parasites and viruses.

Metabolic bone disease & pyramiding

The two conditions that are written permanently into the shell.

Metabolic bone disease

MBD is among the most common conditions in pet tortoises, and it comes from an imbalance of calcium and phosphorus caused by an improper diet, a lack of adequate ultraviolet light, or inadequate protein intake. Without usable D3 the animal cannot absorb calcium regardless of how much is in the food, so it draws calcium out of its own skeleton and shell to keep blood levels workable.

Signs Misshapen shell. Deformed leg bones. Slow growth. A shell that stays flimsy beyond about six months of age. Weak limbs, an inability to lift the body clear of the ground when walking, or a wobbling gait. In severe cases, fractures that occur with no real trauma. Advanced cases can leave permanently misshapen bones or a permanent shell deformity even after treatment.

Prevention is the whole story: a T5 HO UVB tube at UVI 3.0 to 4.0 replaced yearly, calcium without D3 on most meals, a cuttlebone always available, and a high-calcium low-phosphorus plant diet. Treatment is veterinary, involving injectable calcium and often vitamin D alongside correcting the husbandry. Do not treat a suspected case with supplements alone: calcium given without addressing the D3 pathway is not absorbed.

Pyramiding

Pyramiding is abnormal raised growth of the scutes, so the carapace carries individual sections that look like small pyramids instead of lying flat. It is associated with suboptimal temperature and humidity and with imbalances of calcium, phosphorus and protein, and in practice the drivers are excess dietary protein, overfeeding, and insufficient hydration or humidity. It is entirely preventable and completely irreversible: once the scutes are raised they stay raised for the animal's life. A tortoise with mild pyramiding that is otherwise well cared for can live a normal lifespan, so it is a permanent record of past husbandry rather than an ongoing illness. Severe pyramiding does restrict movement and lung capacity.

Respiratory infection & herpesvirus

The urgent one on this list, and the one that can take a whole collection.

Respiratory tract infection

Caused by Mycoplasma, other bacteria, or viruses, and often secondary to vitamin A deficiency. The husbandry triggers are chronically low temperature, excess humidity across the whole enclosure, and drafts, which is why the ventilation argument on page {{P:housing}} and the humidity argument on page {{P:humidity}} are health arguments rather than comfort ones.

Signs, and this does not resolve on its own Excess mucus in the mouth, seen as bubbles at the mouth, nose or eyes. Nasal discharge. Lethargy. Loss of appetite. Wheezing. Extending the neck to breathe. Open-mouth breathing or gasping. Any of these is a veterinary appointment now, not a wait-and-see, and treatment is antibiotics given orally, by injection or as nose drops. Severe cases need hospitalization with fluid therapy and assisted feeding.

While you arrange care, raise the warm end toward the top of the normal range, make sure the animal is hydrated, and remove any source of damp or draft. Do not raise the humidity, which is a common and counterproductive instinct.

Herpesvirus, and why quarantine is not optional

The reason a new tortoise never joins an existing one Testudo tortoises, this species included, are susceptible to epidemics of viral disease and should be treated as potential high-risk carriers of herpes-type organisms. An infected animal is highly contagious and can appear healthy for long periods; in a group with no quarantine, mortality can approach 100%. Signs when they appear include a runny nose, swelling, a white or yellow plaque in the mouth and on the tongue, difficulty swallowing, lethargy and neurological signs. There is no cure. Treatment is supportive and a survivor remains a carrier for life.

What follows from that is simple and absolute. A new tortoise is quarantined in a separate room, on separate equipment, for months rather than weeks, and is screened by a vet before it goes anywhere near another tortoise. Page {{P:parasites}} sets out the full protocol. Never buy a tortoise on impulse from a table at an expo where animals from many sources have been sitting together, and never accept a tortoise from a household whose other animals you have not asked about.

Ranavirus, and mycoplasma

Ranavirus infections also occur in chelonians and, like herpesvirus, often prove fatal. Mycoplasma causes the chronic upper respiratory tract disease familiar to tortoise keepers: a persistent runny nose that comes and goes for years, worsens under stress, and is very difficult to clear. All three are arguments for the same two habits: quarantine properly, and never mix species or sources.

A runny nose is never just a runny nose in a tortoise Owners describe it as a cold and wait. There is no tortoise equivalent of a cold that resolves by itself. Persistent nasal discharge is either an infection, a chronic mycoplasmal disease, or vitamin A deficiency, and all three need a vet and none improve with time.

Shell rot, trauma & abscesses

The shell is living bone with a skin over it, and it gets infected like any other tissue.

Shell infection, or shell rot

Shell infections, also called shell rot and historically SCUD, or septicemic cutaneous ulcerative disease, are caused by bacteria, fungi or parasites entering through trauma: a scrape, a crack, a burn, or a damp patch that has softened the keratin. An overly damp enclosure is the commonest predisposing condition, which is one more reason for the dry surface on page {{P:humidity}}.

Signs, and why this is not cosmetic Soft, discolored or foul-smelling patches. Pitting, flaking or lifting scutes with damp tissue underneath. A discharge or an unpleasant smell. Deep ulcerations can become life-threatening if not treated aggressively, and shell rot that reaches the bloodstream causes septicemia, which is a genuine emergency. Diagnosis involves microscopic analysis and culture to identify the organism, because a bacterial and a fungal infection need different drugs.

Treatment is cleaning and debridement, medication chosen for the organism, and correcting the husbandry that allowed it. Do not treat a suspected shell infection with a household antiseptic and hope: superficial treatment over an established infection seals it in.

Shell trauma

The commonest cause of shell trauma in a pet tortoise is a dog in the same household. A dog does not need to be aggressive: chewing a tortoise like a toy produces punctures and crushing injuries, and a shell is bone with a blood and nerve supply, so it hurts and it bleeds. Any damage can lead to life-threatening bone infection, and severe injuries expose internal organs.

  • Never leave a tortoise and a dog in the same space unsupervised, including a garden, including a dog that has always ignored it.
  • Other sources: a fall from a height, a heavy object, being stepped on, a lamp burn, or a heat mat under the animal.
  • Any crack, puncture or bleeding is a same-day veterinary case. Keep the animal clean and dry, do not apply glue, epoxy, resin or tape, and do not submerge it.

Abscesses

Abscesses in tortoises present as hard, tumor-like swellings rather than the soft pockets a mammal forms, because the pus is thick and dry. The classic sites are the ear and the mouth: a large swelling on the side of the head just behind the eye is an aural abscess, and swellings in the roof of the mouth also occur. They are frequently related to vitamin A deficiency (page {{P:minor}}). Treatment is surgical drainage or removal, flushing with a medicated solution, culture to identify the organism, and antibiotics. They do not resolve without intervention and they do not drain on their own.

The monthly shell check Once a month, with the animal on your lap over a towel: look at the whole carapace under good light for soft spots, discoloration, lifting scutes and cracks; turn it over briefly and check the plastron the same way, especially where it meets the substrate; check both sides of the head behind the eyes for swelling; and press gently at the shell margins for give. It takes two minutes and it catches all three conditions on this page early, when they are all treatable.

Parasites, the fecal test & quarantine

Fairly common, mostly invisible, and the reason every new tortoise is screened.

What they carry

Roundworms and protozoa are common gastrointestinal parasites in tortoises, particularly in wild-caught animals, and in many cases a parasitic infection causes no clinical signs at all. Severe infections cause diarrhea, weight loss and failure to thrive. Oxyurid (pinworm) burdens have been found to correlate with calcium deficiency and metabolic bone disease as well as with kidney disease, mainly in juveniles, which is one more reason not to treat a parasite load as a separate issue from husbandry.

Hexamita parva, the one specific to this species Hexamita parva is a highly damaging parasite of the renal and urinary system and has been identified frequently in Testudo horsfieldii. Untreated it causes rapid weight loss, dehydration and terminal renal failure. Treatment is oral metronidazole, reported at 260 mg/kg, and it needs a vet: the dose is calculated on body weight and the diagnosis needs a urine or fecal examination looking for the right organism. A Russian tortoise losing weight steadily with no obvious cause deserves this specifically ruled out.

The fecal test

Every newly acquired tortoise gets a fecal test, whether or not it has symptoms. Collect a fresh dropping, keep it in a sealed bag in the fridge, and take it to the vet within 24 hours; a sample from a soak is fine. Repeat annually thereafter, and repeat immediately for any unexplained weight loss or change in droppings. Deworming is prescribed against what is actually found rather than given routinely, because blanket dosing selects for resistance and some anthelmintics are hard on a debilitated animal.

Vitamin A deficiency, cloacoliths & beak overgrowth

Three conditions from three different corners that show up in the same tortoise.

Hypovitaminosis A

Vitamin A deficiency comes from an inappropriate diet, and the two that cause it are a diet based on iceberg lettuce and one containing meat. Its signs are easy to attribute elsewhere.

Signs Lack of appetite. Lethargy. Swelling of the eyelids, often with a pus-like discharge, so that the eyes are partly or fully closed. Swelling of the ear from an aural abscess, seen as a bulge on the side of the head behind the eye. Respiratory infections, which are frequently secondary to it. Kidney failure in later stages. Treatment is veterinary vitamin A, sometimes by injection, plus a corrected diet. Do not self-dose vitamin A: it is fat-soluble and an overdose causes skin sloughing.

Prevention is the diet on pages {{P:diet}} and {{P:weeds}}: dark leafy greens, weeds and small amounts of orange-fleshed vegetables all carry the carotenoids a tortoise converts to vitamin A. A tortoise eating dandelion and plantain will not be deficient.

Cloacoliths and urate blockage

Straining while vocalizing is a true emergency A cloacolith is a blockage formed by an accumulation of urates, the crystalline portion of a reptile's urine, which harden into a stone in the cloaca. A tortoise straining and making noise while doing it is a genuine emergency: this is one of very few sounds a tortoise makes and it means obstruction. Treatment is sedation, pain relief, and breaking down and flushing the blockage with forceps. Do not attempt to remove anything yourself.

The cause is nearly always chronic dehydration, which is why the weekly soak and the always-available water dish on page {{P:humidity}} matter more than they look. Thick, gritty urates are the warning that comes before the blockage. Page {{P:poop}}.

Beak and nail overgrowth

Beak and claws grow continuously and are worn down by biting tough food and walking on abrasive ground. On soft supermarket greens and a substrate of nothing but soil, both overgrow. An overgrown beak eventually stops the animal biting properly, at which point it loses weight, and a badly overgrown one forces the jaw out of alignment permanently.

  • Prevention: coarse fibrous weeds and hay rather than soft leaves, a permanent cuttlebone, and flat stones or paving to walk over.
  • Other causes exist, including metabolic bone disease and a past jaw injury, so a beak that keeps regrowing badly is a symptom rather than a grooming job.
  • Trimming is a veterinary procedure, done with a rotary tool. Do not use clippers, which crack the beak.
Shedding, which tortoises do differently A tortoise does not shed in one piece. Skin flakes from the neck and legs continuously, and scutes may shed thin translucent layers as the shell grows. Both are normal. Skin retained in tight bands around a limb means dehydration; scutes lifting to reveal soft damp tissue is page {{P:shell}}. Never peel a scute.
