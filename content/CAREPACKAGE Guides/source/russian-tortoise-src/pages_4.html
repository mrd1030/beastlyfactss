Health red flags & what to tell the vet

A hardy animal that hides decline for years, then declines quickly.

Almost every health problem here traces back to a handful of husbandry basics, and the pattern is worth learning as a pattern: weak UVB and low calcium cause metabolic bone disease; cold, damp or drafty conditions cause respiratory infection; overfeeding and poor hydration cause pyramiding; an unscreened new animal brings in parasites and viruses.

Within a few days

Metabolic bone disease & pyramiding

The two conditions that are written permanently into the shell.

Metabolic bone disease

MBD is among the most common conditions in pet tortoises, and it comes from an imbalance of calcium and phosphorus caused by an improper diet, a lack of adequate ultraviolet light, or inadequate protein intake. Without usable D3 the animal cannot absorb calcium regardless of how much is in the food, so it draws calcium out of its own skeleton and shell to keep blood levels workable.

Signs Misshapen shell. Deformed leg bones. Slow growth. A shell that stays flimsy beyond about six months of age. Weak limbs, an inability to lift the body clear of the ground when walking, or a wobbling gait. In severe cases, fractures that occur with no real trauma. Advanced cases can leave permanently misshapen bones or a permanent shell deformity even after treatment.

Prevention is the whole story: a T5 HO UVB tube at UVI 3.0 to 4.0 replaced yearly, calcium without D3 on most meals, a cuttlebone always available, and a high-calcium low-phosphorus plant diet. Treatment is veterinary, involving injectable calcium and often vitamin D alongside correcting the husbandry. Do not treat a suspected case with supplements alone: calcium given without addressing the D3 pathway is not absorbed.

Pyramiding

Pyramiding is abnormal raised growth of the scutes, so the carapace carries individual sections that look like small pyramids instead of lying flat. It is associated with suboptimal temperature and humidity and with imbalances of calcium, phosphorus and protein, and in practice the drivers are excess dietary protein, overfeeding, and insufficient hydration or humidity. It is entirely preventable and completely irreversible: once the scutes are raised they stay raised for the animal's life. A tortoise with mild pyramiding that is otherwise well cared for can live a normal lifespan, so it is a permanent record of past husbandry rather than an ongoing illness. Severe pyramiding does restrict movement and lung capacity.

Respiratory infection & herpesvirus

The urgent one on this list, and the one that can take a whole collection.

Respiratory tract infection

Caused by Mycoplasma, other bacteria or viruses, often secondary to vitamin A deficiency. The husbandry triggers are chronically low temperature, whole-enclosure humidity, and drafts, which is why the ventilation argument on page {{P:housing}} and the humidity argument on page {{P:humidity}} are health arguments rather than comfort ones.

Signs, and it does not resolve on its own Excess mucus in the mouth, bubbles at the mouth, nose or eyes, nasal discharge, lethargy, loss of appetite, wheezing, extending the neck to breathe, open-mouth breathing or gasping. Any of these is a veterinary appointment now, not a wait-and-see. Treatment is antibiotics orally, by injection or as nose drops; severe cases need hospitalization with fluids and assisted feeding.

While you arrange care, raise the warm end toward the top of the normal range, keep the animal hydrated, and remove any damp or draft. Do not raise the humidity, which is the common and counterproductive instinct.

Herpesvirus, and why quarantine is not optional

The reason a new tortoise never joins an existing one Testudo tortoises are susceptible to epidemics of viral disease and should be treated as potential high-risk carriers of herpes-type organisms. An infected animal is highly contagious and can look healthy for long periods. In the one published outbreak, 2018, the source tortoise had already done a year of quarantine and showed nothing for three years beyond an occasional wet nose; when it broke, 75% of the twenty tortoises exposed died. That is one outbreak, not a rate; the Tortoise Trust puts an unquarantined group near total. Signs: nasal and eye discharge, drooling, white or yellow mouth and tongue plaques, difficulty swallowing, regurgitation, lethargy, neurological signs, pneumonia in severe cases. A mild case can look like slight conjunctivitis, which is why symptom-watching is not screening. No cure, and a survivor carries it for life, shedding after stress and hibernation most of all.

What follows is simple and absolute. A new tortoise is quarantined in a separate room, on separate equipment, for months rather than weeks, and screened by a vet before it goes near another tortoise. Page {{P:parasites}} has the protocol. Never buy on impulse from an expo table where animals from many sources have sat together, and never accept a tortoise from a household whose other animals you have not asked about.

Ranavirus, and mycoplasma

Ranavirus also occurs in chelonians and, like herpesvirus, often proves fatal. Mycoplasma causes the chronic upper respiratory disease keepers know: a runny nose that comes and goes for years, worsens under stress, and is hard to clear. It has the same carrier problem, found in captive tortoises with no symptoms at all. All three argue for the same two habits: quarantine properly, never mix species or sources.

A runny nose is never just a runny nose in a tortoise Owners call it a cold and wait. There is no tortoise cold that resolves by itself: persistent nasal discharge is an infection, chronic mycoplasmal disease, or vitamin A deficiency. All three need a vet and none improve with time.

Shell rot, trauma & abscesses

The shell is living bone with a skin over it, and it gets infected like any other tissue.

Shell infection, or shell rot

Shell infections, also called shell rot and historically SCUD, or septicemic cutaneous ulcerative disease, are caused by bacteria, fungi or parasites entering through trauma: a scrape, a crack, a burn, or a damp patch that has softened the keratin. An overly damp enclosure is the commonest predisposing condition, which is one more reason for the dry surface on page {{P:humidity}}.

Signs, and why this is not cosmetic Soft, discolored or foul-smelling patches. Pitting, flaking or lifting scutes with damp tissue underneath. A discharge or an unpleasant smell. Deep ulcerations can become life-threatening if not treated aggressively, and shell rot that reaches the bloodstream causes septicemia, which is a genuine emergency. Diagnosis involves microscopic analysis and culture to identify the organism, because a bacterial and a fungal infection need different drugs.

Treatment is cleaning and debridement, medication chosen for the organism, and correcting the husbandry that allowed it. Do not treat a suspected shell infection with a household antiseptic and hope: superficial treatment over an established infection seals it in.

Shell trauma

The commonest cause of shell trauma in a pet tortoise is a dog in the same household. A dog does not need to be aggressive: chewing a tortoise like a toy produces punctures and crushing injuries, and a shell is bone with a blood and nerve supply, so it hurts and it bleeds. Any damage can lead to life-threatening bone infection, and severe injuries expose internal organs.

  • Never leave a tortoise and a dog in the same space unsupervised, including a garden, including a dog that has always ignored it.
  • Other sources: a fall from a height, a heavy object, being stepped on, a lamp burn, or a heat mat under the animal.
  • Any crack, puncture or bleeding is a same-day veterinary case. Keep the animal clean and dry, do not apply glue, epoxy, resin or tape, and do not submerge it.

Abscesses

Abscesses in tortoises present as hard, tumor-like swellings rather than the soft pockets a mammal forms, because the pus is thick and dry. The classic sites are the ear and the mouth: a large swelling on the side of the head just behind the eye is an aural abscess, and swellings in the roof of the mouth also occur. They are frequently related to vitamin A deficiency (page {{P:minor}}). Treatment is surgical drainage or removal, flushing with a medicated solution, culture to identify the organism, and antibiotics. They do not resolve without intervention and they do not drain on their own.

The monthly shell check Once a month, with the animal on your lap over a towel: look at the whole carapace under good light for soft spots, discoloration, lifting scutes and cracks; turn it over briefly and check the plastron the same way, especially where it meets the substrate; check both sides of the head behind the eyes for swelling; and press gently at the shell margins for give. It takes two minutes and it catches all three conditions on this page early, when they are all treatable.

Parasites, the fecal test & quarantine

Common, mostly invisible, and the reason every new tortoise is screened.

What they carry

Roundworms and protozoa are common gastrointestinal parasites in tortoises, especially wild-caught ones, and often cause no clinical signs at all. Severe infections cause diarrhea, weight loss and failure to thrive. Oxyurid (pinworm) burdens correlate with calcium deficiency, metabolic bone disease and kidney disease, mainly in juveniles, one more reason not to treat a parasite load as separate from husbandry.

Hexamita parva, the one specific to this species Hexamita parva is a flagellate protozoan, not a worm, attacking the kidneys and urinary tract rather than the gut. Identified frequently in Testudo horsfieldii, untreated it causes rapid weight loss, dehydration and renal failure. The sign to catch is the urine: abnormally watery or jelly-like, with soft droppings, means the kidneys are already under strain, and by the time the animal looks unwell it is well established. Diagnosis is a fresh urine or fecal sample under a microscope, fresh because the organism is motile. Treatment is metronidazole at a dose only a vet can set. Isolate immediately: it spreads by cysts through a shared enclosure or dish.

The fecal test

Every newly acquired tortoise gets a fecal test, whether or not it has symptoms. Collect a fresh dropping, bag it, refrigerate it, and get it to the vet within 24 hours; a sample from a soak is fine. Repeat annually, and immediately for unexplained weight loss or a change in droppings. Deworming is prescribed against what is found rather than given routinely: blanket dosing selects for resistance and some anthelmintics are hard on a weak animal.

Vitamin A deficiency, cloacoliths & beak overgrowth

Three conditions from three different corners that show up in the same tortoise.

Hypovitaminosis A

Vitamin A deficiency comes from an inappropriate diet, and the two that cause it are one based on iceberg lettuce and one containing meat. The signs are easy to attribute elsewhere.

Signs Lack of appetite, lethargy, swollen eyelids often with a pus-like discharge so the eyes are partly or fully closed, a swollen ear from an aural abscess seen as a bulge behind the eye, respiratory infections secondary to it, and kidney failure in later stages. Treatment is veterinary vitamin A, sometimes by injection, plus a corrected diet. Do not self-dose: it is fat-soluble and an overdose causes skin sloughing.

Prevention is the diet on pages {{P:diet}} and {{P:weeds}}: dark leafy greens, weeds and a little orange-fleshed vegetable carry the carotenoids a tortoise converts to vitamin A. One eating dandelion and plantain will not be deficient.

Cloacoliths and urate blockage

Straining while vocalizing is a true emergency A cloacolith is an accumulation of urates, the crystalline portion of a reptile's urine, hardened into a stone in the cloaca. A tortoise straining and making noise while it does is a genuine emergency: this is one of very few sounds a tortoise makes and it means obstruction. Treatment is sedation, pain relief, and breaking the stone down and flushing it out with forceps. Never try to remove anything yourself.

The cause is nearly always chronic dehydration, which is why the soaking routine and the water dish on page {{P:humidity}} matter more than they look. Thick, gritty urates are the warning before the blockage. Page {{P:poop}}. A bladder stone is the same chemistry further on, and this species is prone: a 2021 survey of 101 tortoises put Russian tortoises at 8.9% of urolithiasis cases, every stone analyzed pure ammonium acid urate. Straining and reduced appetite were commonest, and some showed nothing until the stone was large. X-ray to find, surgery to remove.

Beak and nail overgrowth

Beak and claws grow continuously and wear down on tough food and abrasive ground. On soft supermarket greens and bare soil, both overgrow. An overgrown beak eventually stops the animal biting properly and it loses weight; a badly overgrown one puts the jaw permanently out of alignment.

  • Prevention: coarse fibrous weeds and hay rather than soft leaves, a permanent cuttlebone, and flat stones or paving to walk over.
  • Other causes exist, including metabolic bone disease and an old jaw injury, so a beak that keeps regrowing badly is a symptom rather than a grooming job.
  • Trimming is a vet procedure, done with a rotary tool. Not clippers, which crack the beak.
Shedding, which tortoises do differently A tortoise does not shed in one piece. Skin flakes from the neck and legs continuously and scutes shed thin translucent layers as the shell grows; both are normal. Skin retained in tight bands around a limb means dehydration, and scutes lifting to reveal soft damp tissue is page {{P:shell}}. Never peel a scute.
