How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

If you haven't set up yet

Read pages 4 through 10 in order before buying anything, and read page 16 before you buy the rabbit. Two decisions cost the most to reverse: how much space the rabbit gets, and whether it is spayed or neutered. The pet aisle will sell you a cage that is a fraction of what a rabbit needs, and an unspayed female carries a cancer risk high enough that vets treat the surgery as close to routine. Get both right first, then use the Budget & Shopping List on page 28.

If your rabbit is already here

Go to the Setup Checklist on page 26 and audit what you have. If hay is not unlimited and not the bulk of what the rabbit eats, fix that this week: it is the single most protective habit available to you, and it prevents most of Section 03. If your rabbit is off its food right now, do not read further. Go to page 18, then call a vet today.

Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameOryctolagus cuniculus, the European rabbit, ancestor of 300-plus breeds
Adult sizeHighly variable by breed, from 2.5 lb (1.1 kg) dwarfs to 20 lb (9 kg) giants
Lifespan7 to 10 years indoors, some past 12. Outdoor rabbits average closer to 2
Native rangeIberian Peninsula, spread by the Romans and now worldwide
Diet typeHindgut-fermenting herbivore. Grass hay is 80 to 85%, unlimited (page 8)
Space minimum8 sq ft (0.75 m²) enclosure plus 24 sq ft (2.2 m²) exercise, 5+ hours a day
The one hard ruleNo food or droppings for 8 hours is an emergency, not a wait-and-see (page 18)
HousingIndoors. A bonded, neutered pair beats a single rabbit (page 15)
Handling difficultyBeginner to intermediate. Fragile spine, so support is not optional (page 11)
Conservation statusEndangered in the wild (IUCN), despite being invasive where introduced

Cost overview

First year

$1,000 to $1,500 all in: $150 to $600 of setup, $150 to $500 for a spay or neuter, then food and litter. Adoption fees of $25 to $100 often include the surgery already.

Every year after

$500 to $800. Food and litter run $60 to $100 a month, plus two wellness exams at $60 to $100 each.

The number that catches people out A GI stasis workup alone can exceed $1,500, and rabbits need an exotic vet whose rates are higher than a general clinic's. Keep $500 to $1,000 set aside separately from the routine budget. Rabbits hide illness well, and when something goes wrong it goes wrong within hours.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • They eat some of their droppings on purpose. Cecotropes are soft pellets produced overnight and taken directly from the source, carrying B vitamins the gut bacteria made on the first pass. A rabbit that stops eating them is telling you something (page 25).
  • Their teeth never stop. A rabbit's teeth grow as much as 4 to 5 in (10 to 13 cm) a year, which is why the diet has to do the filing. Hay is not a bedding material with a nutritional bonus, it is the dental equipment.
  • Rabbit milk is so rich that mothers nurse once, occasionally twice, a day for 2 to 10 minutes. Kits go roughly a day between feeds, which is why an unattended nest is usually normal rather than abandoned.

Housing & space

The House Rabbit Society standard is at least 8 sq ft (0.75 m²) of enclosure combined with at least 24 sq ft (2.2 m²) of exercise space for one or two rabbits, with a minimum of 5 hours a day to run in it. In practice that means a large foldable metal exercise pen, 4×4 ft (1.2×1.2 m) or bigger, and not a cage the rabbit is let out of occasionally. The wire hutch sold as a "rabbit cage" is a fraction of this and current welfare guidance considers it genuinely inadequate.

Permanent access beats scheduled access. A rabbit let out for an hour a day spends the other twenty-three confined, and the research on page 14 measured time-of-day effects for a reason: rabbits are crepuscular and want the room at dawn and dusk, not when it suits the household.

Never House a rabbit in an aquarium or any solid-walled container. Ventilation is the problem: ammonia from urine builds up in still air and irritates the respiratory tract over time, which is the slow road to the snuffles on page 22.

Flooring, litter training & rabbit-proofing

Flooring

Skip wire flooring entirely. It is the direct cause of sore hocks, which is on page 23 and is miserable to treat. Vinyl or linoleum laid over plywood, washable rugs, or fleece all give better traction and are easier on the feet. Put a waterproof tarp under the whole setup: accidents are not a training failure, they are a fact of the first few months.

Litter training, which is easier than it sounds

Rabbits eliminate almost reflexively while they eat, so the trick is not teaching a new behavior, it is routing an existing one. Put the hay directly in or immediately over the litter box and most of the training does itself.

Use a big box

A large cat-style box, not the small corner trays sold for rabbits. It needs room to sit and eat at once.

Right litter

Paper-based or compressed wood pellet. A shallow layer, with the hay on top or in a rack directly above it.

Spay or neuter

If training stalls with an intact rabbit, this is usually why. Hormonal marking overrides the habit, and the surgery resolves it (page 16).

Never use these litters Clumping cat litter, which causes serious problems if eaten, and pine or cedar shavings, whose aromatic oils irritate the respiratory system. Both are sold in the small-pet aisle and both are wrong here.

Rabbit-proofing the room

A rabbit will chew what it can reach, and the chewing is not naughtiness, it is a continuously growing set of teeth doing what they do. Proof the space rather than fighting the instinct.

Every accessible cable in split-loom tubing or lifted out of reach. This is the one that can kill
Houseplants moved up and out. Assume anything reachable will be tasted
Baseboards and furniture legs shielded with cardboard, plastic guards, or a strategically placed dig box
Carpet edges and corners covered, since these are the classic digging targets
Gaps behind and under furniture blocked, or you will spend an evening on your stomach with a torch
Anything toxic, cleaning products included, behind a door rather than on a low shelf
Give the behavior somewhere to go Proofing alone moves the problem. Pair every block with an outlet: a dig box by the carpet corner they favor, willow or cardboard where they chewed the skirting. A rabbit with nowhere to dig picks somewhere, and not the somewhere you would choose.

Indoors, outdoors & temperature

This is a lifespan decision, not a preference, and the gap is larger than almost any other choice in this book.

Indoors

7 to 10 years, and some past 12. You see the rabbit daily and closely, which is how illness gets caught in the hours that matter.

Outdoors

About 2 years on average. Predators, weather, and health problems that go unnoticed without daily close contact.

The second half of that is the part people underestimate. A rabbit outdoors is not just exposed to foxes and heat, it is out of sight. The window between a rabbit going off its food and a rabbit being in genuine danger is measured in hours (page 18), and nobody notices that window from indoors.

Temperature

Rabbits are far better at cold than at heat. They carry a dense coat, they cannot sweat, and they shed heat mainly through their ears.

RangeWhat it means
60 to 70°F (15 to 21°C)Comfortable. A normal indoor room, and no intervention needed
Above 77°F (25°C)Start actively cooling: airflow, a frozen water bottle wrapped in a towel to lie against, a ceramic tile in the shade
Above 85°F (29°C)Genuine heatstroke risk. Move the rabbit to the coolest room in the house and do not wait to see how it copes
Below 50°F (10°C)Tolerable for a healthy, acclimated rabbit with deep bedding and a windbreak, but see the lifespan figures above before deciding this is fine

Heatstroke signs are rapid shallow breathing, wet nose, red or very hot ears, lethargy, and a rabbit lying stretched flat and unwilling to move. Cool the ears with a damp cloth, move the rabbit somewhere cooler, and call the vet. Do not submerge a rabbit in cold water: the shock is its own emergency.

If a rabbit must live outside A shed or a large predator-proof structure rather than a hutch, with a permanently attached run, deep bedding, shade at every hour of the day, and daily hands-on checks including a look at the rear end for flystrike (page 22). Even then, plan to bring it in for heat waves, cold snaps, and any illness, and expect to catch problems later than you would indoors.
Vaccination is regional RHDV2 is a fatal viral disease and annual vaccination is recommended in the US. In the UK and much of Europe, the standard combined vaccine also covers myxomatosis and RHDV1. Ask your vet what is standard where you live rather than assuming this package's list is complete for your region.

Diet: hay first

If you take one thing from this package, take this page. Unlimited grass hay prevents most of Section 03.

Grass hay should be 80 to 85% of what an adult rabbit eats, available 24 hours a day, never rationed. Timothy, orchard, or meadow hay all work. It is doing two jobs at once, and both of them are the jobs that kill rabbits when they are not done.

Job one: the gut

Rabbits are hindgut fermenters and the gut has to keep moving. Long-strand fiber is what moves it. Low fiber is the biggest risk factor for GI stasis (page 18).

Job two: the teeth

Rabbit teeth grow 4 to 5 in (10 to 13 cm) a year. The side-to-side grinding of long hay is what wears them down. Pellets are chewed differently and do not do this (page 21).

Which hay

HayWhoNotes
TimothyAdultsThe default. First cut is coarser and better for teeth, second cut softer and more palatable
Orchard grassAdultsSofter and sweeter than timothy. A good option if a rabbit refuses timothy, or if you are allergic to it yourself
Meadow hayAdultsMixed grasses, more varied texture, often the most interesting to forage through
Oat hayMix inOffer alongside a grass hay rather than instead of it. Some rabbits eat only the seed heads and leave the stalks
AlfalfaUnder 6 monthsA legume, not a grass. Right for kits, juveniles, pregnant or nursing does, and underweight seniors. Too rich for a healthy adult: the calcium and calories drive obesity and bladder sludge

Making hay take time

Hay is also the cheapest enrichment here, and presentation matters. Stuff it into cardboard tubes, hang it in bundles, scatter greens through it, and keep a rack over the litter box so eating and toileting happen in the same corner, which is where the rabbit wants them anyway.

Buying and storing it Buy in bulk from a feed store rather than small pet-shop bags: cheaper per pound and fresher. Store it dry and airy, never sealed in plastic, which traps moisture and grows mold. Good hay smells sweet, is green rather than yellow-brown, and is not dusty.
A rabbit not eating hay is a problem, not a preference The two usual reasons are that pellets or treats are filling it up, in which case cut those back, or that eating hurts, which means dental disease and a vet visit (page 21). Both need acting on. A rabbit that eats everything but hay is on a trajectory toward the emergency on page 18.

Feeding by life stage

The diet changes twice: once off alfalfa, and once into old age.

StageFeeding
Nursing kits
birth to about 7 weeks		Mother's milk, and she nurses just once, occasionally twice, a day for 2 to 10 minutes. Rabbit milk is unusually rich, so kits go roughly a day between feeds. A nest left alone is normal, not abandoned. Weaned by around 8 weeks
Transition
3 to 7 weeks		Alfalfa hay and alfalfa-based pellets introduced free-choice alongside nursing. New greens usually start around 12 weeks, in small amounts
Juvenile
7 weeks to 7 months		Unlimited alfalfa hay and alfalfa-based pellets: the extra calories and calcium support growth. Vegetables added gradually from about 3 months, one new item at a time
Changeover
roughly 4 to 6 months		Alfalfa phased out for grass hays, and pellet portions reduced gradually over several weeks as unlimited grass hay takes over as the base of the diet. Do this slowly
Adult
7 months to about 5 years		Unlimited grass hay at 80 to 85% of the diet. Measured pellets, 1/8 to 1/4 cup per 5 to 6 lb (2.3 to 2.7 kg) daily. Greens at about 1 cup per 2 lb (1 kg), at least 3 varieties. Treats capped at 1 to 2 tbsp once or twice a week
Senior
6 years and up		Unlimited grass hay continues. Alfalfa may come back if the rabbit is losing weight, treats are allowed more freely, and feeding becomes individual and vet-guided rather than by the table

What changes with age, beyond the food

  • Weigh a senior rabbit monthly. Weight loss is the earliest signal of dental disease, kidney trouble, and most of Section 03.
  • Watch for a rabbit that stops eating its cecotropes or gets messy at the rear: arthritis or obesity can stop it reaching (page 25).
  • Move to twice-yearly vet exams if you were not already there, and expect the vet to want to see the back teeth.
  • Soften the setup: lower platforms, a ramp instead of a hop, thicker bedding for sore hocks.
Change food slowly, always There is no gut-loading or calcium dusting here, that is a reptile practice. The rabbit equivalent of getting it wrong is changing food too fast. A sudden switch of hay, pellets, or vegetables disrupts the cecal bacteria and produces soft stool or a stasis episode. Transition over a week or two.
The one figure to hold on to Sources put the alarm point for a rabbit not eating anywhere from 3 to 12 hours, and agree that fasting beyond about 24 hours risks fatty liver disease. Do not wait through a full day hoping it resolves. Same-day vet contact is the safe default the moment a rabbit skips a meal or stops producing normal droppings, at any age (page 18).

Handling: picking a rabbit up

Rabbits are more fragile than almost any first-time owner expects, and getting this wrong breaks bones.

Why support is the whole thing A rabbit that struggles against an unsupported hold can fracture its own lumbar spine. Its hind legs are powerful enough to do that, and without full body support that power works in exactly the wrong direction. This is not a handling preference. It is the single most important physical thing to get right with this species.

Picking one up

  1. One hand on the chest just behind the front legs, the other under the hindquarters. Both at once, never one then the other.
  2. Lift with both regions supported and bring the rabbit straight in against your body, not out at arm's length.
  3. Stay low to the ground for the whole session, so a struggle ends in a short drop rather than a long one.
  4. If it struggles, lower it back down immediately in one smooth motion, in a straight line. Do not try to keep your grip.

The "football hold" is worth learning: the rabbit's head tucked into the crook of your elbow, body along your forearm. Vets use it because it is secure and low-stress at once.

Never Lift a rabbit by the ears, ever. Never rely on the scruff alone to carry its weight: even where a grip at the neck is used to steady an animal, the body has to be supported underneath.

Building trust & reading a rabbit

Most rabbits never learn to love being picked up. A relationship built on the floor works better anyway.

Skip trancing

You will see advice about flipping a rabbit onto its back to calm it, sometimes called trancing. Don't. Research measuring rabbits in that position found significant increases in respiration, heart rate, and stress hormones. The stillness is a fear response the animal cannot escape, and it looks calm to the untrained eye, which is why the myth lasted.

Building trust instead

  • Most rabbits are calmest with all four feet down, and many never learn to enjoy being picked up even once they trust you. That is normal.
  • Sit or lie down at the rabbit's level and wait. Let it come to you rather than reaching in.
  • Hand-feed a green or a piece of herb at floor level. Repetition does the work.
  • Aim for a rabbit relaxed around you rather than one that tolerates being carried. Pick-ups can stay rare.

Stress signals to stop on

Thumping a hind foot · a tense, rigid body · wide eyes · fast, shallow breathing · teeth grinding, which is a pain signal here and not the soft contented tooth-purring · freezing in place · bolting or kicking.

Those are in-the-moment signals. When freezing or constant hiding becomes the baseline rather than a reaction to handling, that is a longer-running welfare problem, and the answer is on pages 5, 14 and 15, 13 and 14 rather than in your technique.

Enrichment: what the research says

The enrichment aisle is full of things to put in a hutch. The evidence points somewhere much less commercial.

Companionship

A study of social housing conditions in privately owned rabbits found that solitary rabbits expressed less behavioral diversity and more awake inactivity than socially housed ones. Socially housed rabbits were more active. The same work found that a larger age difference between paired rabbits was associated with a lower friendship index, which is a practical steer when choosing a companion.

Space, measured in a stress hormone

A study of pair-housed pet rabbits found that run access, hutch size, and time of day all affected welfare-relevant behavior and fecal corticosterone. Space is not a nicety here. It registers in a stress hormone metabolite, and the time-of-day finding is why permanent access beats a scheduled hour.

Structure

In group-housed rabbits, an elevated platform let animals move up and down, rest more comfortably, and increase exploratory behavior. Somewhere to get on top of, and somewhere to get underneath, is worth building in from the start.

Awake inactivity This is the uncomfortable one to learn about, because a still rabbit reads as a contented rabbit from the sofa. The measure exists precisely because those two states look identical from across a room. A rabbit sitting motionless and awake for long stretches in a small space is displaying the pattern the research associates with poorer welfare, not relaxing.

Priority order

  1. A neutered, bonded companion, close in age
  2. Permanent run access or a rabbit-proofed room
  3. Unlimited hay, presented so it takes time to eat
  4. A dig box
  1. Platforms to get on and cover to get under
  2. Chewing and shredding material: bamboo sticks, untreated willow, plain cardboard
  3. Novel objects, last, and the cheap part
What not to do Don't keep a rabbit alone and expect toys to close the gap: the research measured that gap and toys were not what changed it. Don't treat a hutch as housing. Don't schedule exercise around the household when the animal is crepuscular. Don't feed a muesli mix instead of hay. Don't pair rabbits without neutering both first. And don't treat digging as a behavior problem, because it is a burrowing animal doing what burrowing animals do.

Bonding & companionship

The highest-value thing you can do for a rabbit, and the one that takes the longest to do properly.

A neutered, bonded pair does things a single rabbit does not: mutual grooming, resting in contact, and simply more behavior of every kind. A single rabbit with an attentive owner is a compromise rather than an equivalent, and this is the finding the research most directly supports (page 14).

Choosing a companion

  • Neuter both first, and wait the vet's recommended interval before introducing. Hormones sabotage bonds that would otherwise work.
  • Closer in age is better: the friendship index dropped as the age gap widened.
  • Neutered male with spayed female is the pairing that works most often, though same-sex pairs are common and fine.
  • Many rescues run "bunny dating," where your rabbit meets candidates on neutral ground and picks. Use it if it exists near you.
  • Size and breed matter far less than temperament. A calm giant and a calm dwarf will usually do better than two pushy rabbits of matched size.

How bonding actually goes

StageWhat you do
Side by sideSeparate pens next to each other, swapped daily so each rabbit lives in the other's scent. Days to weeks
First meetingNeutral territory neither rabbit has claimed, cleared of anything to guard. Short, supervised, with a towel and a dustpan on hand to separate safely rather than with your hands
Stress bondingA mildly novel space or a car ride makes the rabbits allies against a shared oddity. Effective for some pairs, unnecessary for others
Building upSessions lengthen as long as they end well. Grooming, resting in contact, and eating side by side are the signs it is working
Full-timeOnly after they have slept touching through several long supervised sessions. Give them a cleaned, rearranged space so neither owns it
What is normal and what is not Chasing, mounting, and light nipping are normal early negotiation, and interrupting every one of them prevents the pair from settling a hierarchy. Fur pulled out in clumps, a rabbit cornered and unable to escape, or any biting that draws blood means separate immediately and go back a stage. Never leave a new pair alone together, and never break up a fight with bare hands.
It takes weeks Going too fast is how bonding fails. Weeks is normal, months happens. Keep a bonded pair together for vet visits too: separating them even for a day can break a bond, so ask the vet to see both in the same carrier.

Spay, neuter & why it isn't optional

The number this page exists for Up to 80% of unspayed does aged 5 and older develop uterine cancer, with risk climbing from around age two. That is one of the highest lifetime cancer risks documented in any commonly kept pet, which is why rabbit-savvy vets treat spaying as close to routine rather than as an elective extra.

What it changes

Females

  • Removes the uterine cancer risk almost entirely when done young
  • Ends false pregnancies, nest-building, and the territorial aggression that comes with them
  • Makes litter training work, since hormonal marking stops

Males

  • Stops urine spraying, which is the behavior most owners cannot live with
  • Reduces mounting, circling, and territorial aggression
  • Makes bonding possible at all: an intact rabbit is very hard to pair (page 15)

Timing and cost

WhenMales from around 4 months, females from 5 to 6, though your vet goes by size rather than the calendar. Earlier is better for the cancer risk, but not before the rabbit is grown enough for safe anaesthesia
NeuterTypically $200 to $300
SpayMore, since it is more invasive. Budget $150 to $500 or more overall, with exotic and metro clinics at the higher end
Low-cost programsNonprofit clinics sometimes bring it down to around $139 to $150. Rabbit rescues usually know which ones are near you
AdoptionFees of $25 to $100 frequently include the surgery and an initial vet check already. For many people, adopting an altered rabbit is the cheapest route overall, not the pet store

Choosing who does it

Ask directly how many rabbit spays or neuters the practice does in a year, and whether they withhold food beforehand. The answer you want is that they do not fast a rabbit before surgery, because rabbits cannot vomit and an empty gut is a stasis risk. A clinic that fasts rabbits the way it fasts dogs is telling you it does not do many rabbits.

Recovery Expect a rabbit to be eating within a few hours and back to normal in a couple of days. Bring hay and a favorite green to the clinic. The thing to watch for is the thing this whole package watches for: no eating and no droppings. Any rabbit that has not eaten by the evening of surgery day goes straight back to the vet. Pain relief matters here, so ask what is going home with you rather than assuming.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & the rabbit-savvy vet

Before anything else on this list Rabbits need a rabbit-savvy exotic vet, not a standard small-animal clinic. This is not snobbery about credentials. Rabbit physiology, anaesthetic protocols, and drug tolerances differ enough that a vet unfamiliar with the species can genuinely miss or mishandle everything in this section. Find one before you need one, ideally through a rabbit rescue or the House Rabbit Society, and write the number on page 27 today.

Go to a vet today

What you seeWhat it points at
No food and no droppings for 8 hours or moreGI stasis. Same-day, not overnight (page 18)

GI stasis: recognizing it

Ask a rabbit vet what kills more pet rabbits than anything else and this is the answer. Not an injury, not an exotic disease. The gut slowing down.

What it actually is

Rabbits are hindgut fermenters. The digestive system relies on continuous movement to process high-fiber food and to produce two kinds of droppings, including the nutrient-rich cecotropes the rabbit re-ingests directly (page 25). When gut motility slows or stops, gas builds up, painful bloating follows, and the balance of gut bacteria shifts dangerously. Untreated, that cascade becomes life-threatening quickly.

Why rabbits specifically

Most pets can comfortably skip a meal. A rabbit's gut needs near-constant input to keep moving, and going without food for even half a day can be enough to slow motility in a rabbit that is already stressed or unwell. That is the opposite of how most mammals work, and it is why a rabbit cannot tough out an off day the way a cat or dog can.

Triggers

TriggerWhy it matters
Low-fiber dietInsufficient hay slows motility over time. The single biggest risk factor, and the one entirely in your control
Dental painMakes eating uncomfortable, and reduced intake alone is enough to start the cascade
StressA loud environment, a new pet, a move, or a vet visit can be enough on its own
DehydrationReduces gut lubrication and motility directly
Pain from any other causeA rabbit in pain from anything often stops eating, which starts the same cascade
A heavy moltSwallowed fur plus dehydrated gut contents can form a mass that is hard to pass (page 24)
Some medicationsA few drugs used for other conditions slow gut motility as a side effect. Ask when anything new is prescribed

GI stasis: warning signs

The signs arrive in an order, and the first one you can see without touching the rabbit.

Early warning signs, in the order they usually appear

  1. Reduced or absent fecal output. The earliest and most reliable sign, and the reason a daily glance at the litter box is worth more than any other habit in this book.
  2. Smaller, oddly shaped, or fur-strung droppings in the hours before output stops entirely.
  3. Reduced appetite, picking at food without refusing it outright.
  4. Hunched posture with the belly pulled in, the classic sign of abdominal pain.
  5. Teeth grinding (bruxism) at a harsh, audible pitch. This is distinct from the soft tooth-purring a relaxed rabbit does, and once you have heard both you will not confuse them.
Know your out-of-hours option now Most rabbit vets are not open at 2am, and most emergency clinics do not see rabbits. Find out tonight which clinic near you will take a rabbit out of hours, how far it is, and what it costs. Write it on page 27. Working that out during the emergency costs the hours the emergency is made of.
Why wait and see is dangerous here With most illnesses, a day of monitoring at home is reasonable. With GI stasis, that same day is often the difference between a rabbit that recovers fully and one that does not. A rabbit that has produced no droppings in 8 to 12 hours, or has stopped eating entirely, needs a same-day visit to a rabbit-savvy or exotic vet. Not tomorrow morning.

GI stasis: treatment & prevention

What treatment looks like

Veterinary treatment typically means subcutaneous fluids, pain management, and motility medication, alongside identifying and treating whatever triggered the episode in the first place. That last part matters: a rabbit having a stasis episode usually needs a full workup rather than symptomatic treatment alone, because stasis is very often the visible end of something else, most commonly dental pain.

Syringe-feeding a critical care formula is often part of recovery, but it should be guided by a vet rather than attempted blind. Force-feeding a rabbit with a genuine blockage rather than a slowdown can make things worse, and the two look similar from the outside.

The number worth holding on to Roughly 70% of rabbits recover with prompt veterinary treatment. Delayed cases can need days of hospitalization and do considerably worse. Early intervention is not a nicety here, it is most of the outcome.

While you are getting to the vet

Do

  • Call first, and say the words "not eating, no droppings." That phrasing gets a rabbit seen faster than "seems a bit off."
  • Offer water, and offer the most tempting fresh greens you have. Wet greens add fluid too.
  • Keep the rabbit warm, quiet, and with its bonded partner if it has one.
  • Bring a sample of any droppings produced, and note the time of the last normal one.

Don't

  • Don't syringe-feed without veterinary direction.
  • Don't give simethicone, pineapple juice, or any home remedy in place of the appointment. Some of it is harmless, none of it is treatment.
  • Don't massage a hard, tense abdomen. If there is a blockage, this hurts and can do damage.
  • Don't wait for the morning to see if it passes.

Prevention, which is most of the answer

Unlimited grass hay at 80% or more of the diet. The single most protective habit available to any rabbit owner (page 8)
Fresh water always available, and greens that carry moisture with them
Diet changes made gradually over a week or two, never overnight
A daily glance at the litter box. Just a glance, to see that output looks normal in size and quantity
Brushing through heavy molts, so less fur is swallowed (page 24)
Twice-yearly dental checks, since dental pain is the most common hidden trigger

Dental disease

The most common hidden cause of everything else in this section, and the one that develops silently.

CauseRabbit teeth grow continuously, as much as 4 to 5 in (10 to 13 cm) a year, and they are worn down by the side-to-side grinding of long-strand fiber. A diet short on hay lets them overgrow. Molar spurs form on the outside edges and cut the tongue or cheek; incisors can grow past each other and stop meeting at all. Genetics play a part too, and some breeds, particularly flat-faced dwarfs and lops, are predisposed regardless of diet
SignsDrooling, or a permanently damp chin and dewlap. Dropping food while trying to eat, or taking a piece and putting it down again. A preference for soft food over hay. Weight loss. Pawing at the mouth. Watery eyes, because the roots of overgrown upper teeth press on the tear ducts. Reduced or absent cecotrope eating, since bending down to take them hurts
ResponseA vet exam, which for the back teeth means sedation and a proper look: molar spurs are not visible from the front and a torch through the incisors does not find them. Treatment is usually a sedated burr or trim. Overgrown incisors may need repeat trims, and in severe cases extraction, after which rabbits do surprisingly well on a modified diet
RecoveryPain relief and a return to eating within a day or two of the trim. Recurrence is common, so many rabbits settle into a schedule of trims every few months. The prevention is unchanged: unlimited hay, from day one and for life
Why this page sits before the others Dental pain is the most common hidden trigger for GI stasis. A rabbit whose mouth hurts eats less, and a rabbit that eats less has a gut that slows. If your rabbit has had a stasis episode with no obvious cause, the back teeth are the first place to look, which is why a full workup matters more than treating the stasis alone (page 20).

What you can check at home

  • Lift the lips and look at the incisors weekly. They should meet cleanly, top slightly overlapping bottom, with no spiralling or splaying.
  • Run a finger gently along the jawline. Lumps or bumps along the bone can mean root overgrowth or an abscess.
  • Watch the rabbit eat a piece of hay. It should take it and grind steadily, not pick it up and drop it.
  • Weigh weekly, and log it on page 35. Weight loss usually shows before anything visible does.
Never trim a rabbit's teeth yourself Nail clippers on incisors shatter and split the tooth down its length, which causes abscesses and enormous pain. This is a sedated veterinary procedure with a dental burr. There is no home version of it.

Flystrike & snuffles

Flystrike (myiasis)

CauseFlies lay eggs on soiled or damp fur, most often around the rear. The maggots hatch within hours and begin eating into the skin. The underlying reason is almost always something else: obesity or arthritis stopping a rabbit from cleaning itself, dental pain stopping it eating cecotropes, soft stool from a diet problem, or a wet, dirty enclosure
SignsVisible eggs like small grains of rice in the fur, or maggots. A patch of damp, matted, or smelling fur. Sudden lethargy or a rabbit sitting still and unwilling to move. It can go from eggs to a life-threatening emergency within 24 to 48 hours
ResponseEmergency vet, immediately, at any hour. On the way, pick off what maggots you can see with tweezers, but do not soak or drown the rabbit: wetting the fur drives maggots deeper and a chilled rabbit in shock is a second emergency. This is not a wait-until-morning condition
PreventionCheck the rear end daily in warm weather, twice daily for an outdoor rabbit. Keep the enclosure dry and cleaned. Fix the underlying cause: a rabbit with a permanently mucky bottom has a diet, dental, or mobility problem that needs solving, not just cleaning

Snuffles (upper respiratory infection)

CauseMost often Pasteurella multocida, though other bacteria do the same job. Frequently opportunistic: poor ventilation, ammonia buildup from a solid-walled enclosure, dusty bedding, or stress lets an organism the rabbit was already carrying take hold
SignsEye or nasal discharge, sneezing, and the giveaway that owners miss: crusty, matted fur on the insides of the front paws, from a rabbit wiping its own nose repeatedly. Later, noisy breathing, head tilt if it reaches the inner ear, or abscesses
ResponseVet, promptly rather than same-day-urgent unless breathing is affected. Treatment is usually a long antibiotic course, often weeks, chosen on a culture rather than a guess. Ask for the culture: rabbit-safe antibiotics are a narrow list and the wrong one can cause a fatal gut reaction on its own
RecoveryOften good, but chronic carriers are common and flare-ups recur under stress. Fix the ventilation and the bedding dust alongside the antibiotics, or you are treating the same infection again in three months
Open-mouth breathing is always an emergency Rabbits are obligate nose breathers. A rabbit breathing through its mouth has an airway that is genuinely obstructed or a respiratory system in real trouble, and it is a same-hour call whatever else is going on.

E. cuniculi, sore hocks, bladder sludge & RHDV2

Encephalitozoon cuniculi

A microsporidian parasite carried by a large share of pet rabbits, often with no signs at all. When it becomes active it affects the brain, kidneys, or eyes. The classic presentation is a head tilt, sometimes with rolling or loss of balance, and it also contributes to recurrent GI stasis episodes and to cataracts. Treatment is a course of fenbendazole plus supportive care, and the earlier it starts the better the outcome. A head tilt is not always E. cuniculi: an inner ear infection looks the same from outside, and telling them apart is the vet's job.

Sore hocks (pododermatitis)

Pressure sores on the underside of the hind feet, which rabbits lack the thick footpads to protect. Caused by wire flooring, hard or abrasive surfaces, inadequate bedding, damp, obesity, or simply too little movement. Starts as fur loss and reddened skin, progresses to open ulcers and, untreated, to bone infection. Prevention is soft, dry, non-abrasive flooring and a rabbit at a healthy weight. Once it is open it needs veterinary treatment and it is slow to heal, so catching the bald patch stage is worth the weekly look.

Bladder sludge and stones

Rabbits absorb dietary calcium unusually efficiently and excrete the surplus through the kidneys, so their urine is naturally chalky. When it thickens into a gritty sludge or forms stones, it causes straining, urine scald on the rear, blood in the urine, and pain. The drivers are a diet too high in calcium (alfalfa fed to an adult, or calcium-rich greens in excess), too little water, and too little exercise. Prevention is a grass-hay diet, water always available and made appealing, and enough room to move.

RHDV2

Rabbit Haemorrhagic Disease Virus type 2. Highly contagious, frequently fatal, and often kills with no warning signs at all. It survives on surfaces, clothing, shoes, and hay, so indoor-only rabbits are not automatically safe. Annual vaccination is recommended in the US and is genuinely worth discussing with your vet rather than treating as an optional extra. In the UK and much of Europe a combined vaccine also covers myxomatosis and RHDV1, on a schedule your vet will set.

The pattern in this section Three of these four are prevented by things already covered: grass hay instead of alfalfa, soft dry flooring, room to move, and water that gets drunk. The fourth is prevented by a vaccine. Very little on this page is treated better than it is avoided, which is the argument for the twice-yearly exam on page 17.

Grooming, nails & molting

Rabbits groom themselves thoroughly and cannot vomit, which is the whole reason this page exists.

Molting

Rabbits molt roughly every three months, and two of those a year are usually heavy. A heavy molt can come out in visible tufts, in a line moving down the body, or leave bald patches that regrow within days. It is normal. The risk is what happens to the fur the rabbit swallows while grooming: a cat coughs up a hairball, and a rabbit cannot. Swallowed fur combined with dehydrated gut contents forms a mass that is hard to pass, which is a direct route to the emergency on page 18.

Brush daily through a heavy molt, and weekly otherwise. Long-haired breeds need daily grooming year-round without exception
A rubber grooming mitt or a fine comb. Slicker brushes catch the skin, which is thin and tears easily
Pluck loose tufts out gently by hand as they lift. This gets more fur out than brushing alone
Push hay and water hard through a molt. Fiber and fluid are what move swallowed fur through

Nails

Trim every 4 to 8 weeks. Overgrown nails change how a rabbit stands, which loads the hocks wrongly and contributes to the sores on page 23, and they catch and tear. Shine a torch through a pale nail and the pink quick is easy to see; on dark nails, take a very small amount at a time and stop when the cut surface changes texture. Keep styptic powder or cornflour to hand for the one you will eventually catch. Two people is easier than one: one holds and supports the rabbit properly, the other trims.

Bathing, scent glands, and the rest

  • Never full-bath a rabbit. Wet fur is slow to dry, chills them fast, and the panic of being submerged can cause spinal injury. Spot-clean with a damp cloth instead, and dry thoroughly.
  • A mucky rear needs the cause fixed, not just cleaning. See flystrike on page 22.
  • The scent glands either side of the genitals collect a dark, strong-smelling wax. Clean them gently every month or two with a damp cotton bud.
  • Check ears for wax buildup, especially in lops, whose ear canals drain poorly.
Make grooming the health check Every session is a chance to run your hands over the whole rabbit: lumps, scabs, sore hocks, a damp chin, a matted rear, weight change. Doing both at once means the check actually happens weekly, rather than being a thing you meant to do.

Reading droppings & cecotropes

The litter box is the best diagnostic instrument you own, and checking it takes five seconds a day.

Rabbits make two kinds

Fecal pellets

Round, dry, uniform, straw-colored to dark brown, and roughly pea-sized in an average rabbit. Made of indigestible fiber. These are what you see in the litter box, in large quantities, all day.

Cecotropes

Soft, dark, shiny, clustered like a tiny blackberry, and strong-smelling. Produced mostly overnight and eaten directly from the source. You should rarely see one, because the rabbit takes it.

Cecotropes are not an accident and not a sign of anything wrong. They carry B vitamins and protein that the cecal bacteria produced but that were not absorbed on the first pass, and eating them is a genuine part of rabbit digestion. Do not stop the rabbit doing it, and do not clean them away before it can.

What the litter box is telling you

What you seeWhat it means
Plenty of round, even, pea-sized pelletsNormal. This is what a well-hayed rabbit produces, in quantity
Fewer pellets than yesterdayThe earliest sign of stasis. Check food intake now, don't wait for the count to reach zero (page 18)
Small, hard, dark, misshapen pelletsDehydration or slowing motility. Push water and greens, and watch closely for the next few hours
Pellets strung together with furMolt-related. Brush daily and increase hay and water (page 24)
No pellets at all for 8 hoursSame-day vet. This is the line (page 18)
Soft, unformed, or pudding-like stoolTrue diarrhea is rare in adults and is an emergency, particularly in a young rabbit. Vet today
Squashed cecotropes stuck to the fur or the floorNot diarrhea. Usually too many pellets or too much sugar in the diet, so the rabbit produces more than it wants. Cut pellets and treats first, then check for obesity or arthritis stopping it reaching

The five-second habit Look in the litter box every time you pass it, and once properly each morning. You are not counting, you are looking for a change in quantity or shape. Power outages & the blackout plan

A rabbit needs no equipment running to survive, which makes a blackout mostly a temperature problem.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Keep feeding normally. This is the one animal in this series where the blackout advice is to carry on: a rabbit that stops eating for a day is in more danger than one that is a bit cold.
  • In a cold outage, add deep bedding, a cardboard box stuffed with hay to sit inside, and a wrapped hot-water bottle placed beside the rabbit rather than under it.
  • In a hot outage, move to the coolest room in the house, add airflow if you have a battery fan, and give a frozen water bottle wrapped in a towel to lie against.
  • Keep water available and check it more often, since a rabbit drinking less is the start of the problem on page 18.

Don't

  • Don't use candles or an open flame anywhere a rabbit can reach or knock into.
  • Don't put a heat source in direct contact with the rabbit.
  • Don't move a bonded pair apart to make handling easier. Keep them together.
Outage kit A wrapped hot-water bottle, two freezer blocks, a battery fan, a torch, spare bedding, and the numbers from page 27.

1 nightPossible with a big hay pile, fresh water in two containers, and a full litter box, but only for a healthy adult and only if you accept that nobody will notice a stasis episode. A visit is better.
2 to 4 nightsA sitter every day without exception. The daily litter box check is the whole reason, and it cannot be skipped for a rabbit.
Over 4 nightsA daily sitter who has done a practice visit with you, or boarding with a rabbit-experienced rescue or vet. Leave page 34 filled in and the vet informed that someone else may bring the rabbit in.

Transport and vet trips

  • A hard carrier that opens from the top as well as the front, so a frightened rabbit can be lifted out with support rather than dragged. Line it with a towel over a non-slip mat.
  • Put hay and a couple of favorite greens in for every journey, including the trip to the vet. A rabbit that eats in the carrier is a rabbit whose gut kept moving.
  • Secure the carrier with a seatbelt in the footwell or on a seat, out of direct sun, and drive as smoothly as you can. Never in the boot of a car, and never left in a parked one.
  • Take a bonded partner along even when only one is ill. Separating a pair, even for an afternoon, can break the bond, and the companion calms the patient.
  • Moving house: set the pen up in a quiet room before anything else is unpacked, keep the diet identical for a fortnight, and watch output closely for a week. A move is a classic stasis trigger.

Glossary

Awake inactivitySitting still while awake for long stretches. A welfare measure rather than a mood, and associated in the research with solitary or confined housing.
Bonded pairTwo rabbits that have been properly introduced and now live together, groom each other, and rest in contact. Separating them, even briefly, can break the bond.
BruxismTooth grinding. Soft and quiet means a contented rabbit; harsh and audible means pain, and the two are easy to tell apart once you have heard both.
CecotropesSoft, dark, clustered droppings produced overnight and eaten directly from the source. A genuine part of digestion, not a problem.
CrepuscularMost active at dawn and dusk. It is why permanent run access beats an hour scheduled for the household's convenience.
DewlapThe fold of skin under the chin, larger in does. A permanently damp dewlap points at drooling, and so at dental disease.
Doe and buckFemale and male rabbit.
DystociaDifficulty giving birth. One more reason an unspayed doe carries risk a spayed one does not.
E. cuniculiEncephalitozoon cuniculi, a parasite many pet rabbits carry silently. When active it causes head tilt, kidney disease, or cataracts.
FlystrikeFly eggs laid in soiled fur hatching into maggots that eat into the skin. Can kill within 24 to 48 hours.
GI stasisThe gut slowing or stopping. The most common life-threatening emergency in pet rabbits, and the reason for the 8-hour rule.
Hindgut fermenterAn animal that digests fiber in the cecum, after the small intestine. It is why the gut must keep moving and why fiber is not optional.
KitA baby rabbit.
Molar spurA sharp point on an overgrown back tooth that cuts the tongue or cheek. Not visible from the front, which is why the dental exam needs sedation.
PododermatitisSore hocks: pressure sores on the underside of the hind feet, caused by wire or hard flooring, damp, or obesity.
RHDV2Rabbit Haemorrhagic Disease Virus type 2. Highly contagious, often fatal, and survives on shoes, clothing, and hay. Vaccinate.
SnufflesUpper respiratory infection, most often Pasteurella multocida. Look for crusted front paws as much as for a runny nose.
TrancingFlipping a rabbit onto its back until it goes still. Measured heart rate and stress hormones show this is fear, not relaxation. Don't.
X-penA foldable metal exercise pen. The current standard for rabbit housing, in place of a cage or hutch.
