Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameAgrionemys horsfieldii (also called Testudo horsfieldii)
Adult size5 to 10 in (13 to 25 cm), measured along the carapace
Captive lifespanCommonly 40 years or more with proper care; some individuals reach 50 or beyond
Native rangeSteppe and semi-desert of Central Asia: Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Iran, Pakistan, China
Diet typeHerbivore; weeds and leafy greens dominate, fruit under 5% of intake if given at all
Enclosure minimum (adult)4×2×2 ft indoor open-top tortoise table; preferred size closer to 7×3.5 ft
Handling difficultyIntermediate; comes with a genuine winter brumation decision to manage
Conservation statusVulnerable (IUCN)

Cost overview

Initial setup

$400 to $600 for a complete indoor setup: table, UVB fixture, heat, substrate, and furnishings. The open-topped tortoise table itself runs $125 to $200 of that.

Annual ongoing

$500 to $650 across the year for substrate refreshes, forage, supplements, and UVB bulb replacement.

Budget for the worst case too A routine vet checkup runs around $75; a workup for an illness commonly runs $150 to $300 or more. Neither is likely if you follow this package, but both are real enough to plan for.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Russian tortoises are serious diggers, capable of excavating burrows to escape both summer heat and winter cold, which is exactly why outdoor pen walls need to run at least 12 inches below grade.
  • They're one of the few pet tortoise species that genuinely offer their keeper a choice between brumating for winter or staying active year-round indoors, a flexibility most other species don't have built in as cleanly.
  • Wild collection for the pet trade is part of why this species is listed Vulnerable, which is one more reason captive-bred sourcing matters.

Housing & enclosure

4×2×2 ft is the indoor minimum for an adult, built as an open-top tortoise table rather than a glass tank. Floor space is what actually matters for a tortoise, not height, and the preferred size is closer to 7×3.5 ft once your budget and space allow it. An open top keeps airflow honest and cuts down on the fogging and stagnant air that a lidded glass tank traps.

Never Rely on a glass-walled tank as the full-time home. Poor top ventilation lets humidity and stale air build up, and the walls encourage pacing along reflections instead of using the floor space the way a tortoise actually needs to.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Outdoor pens are a strong warm-season option once conditions allow it. Wall depth needs to run at least 12 inches below grade, since Russian tortoises dig and will find any shortcut under a shallow wall.
  • Substrate depth matters as much as footprint: 6 to 12 or more inches of a 50/50 topsoil and coconut coir mix supports real digging and burrowing behavior, not just moisture retention.
  • Keep the tortoise table (or enclosure) away from cold drafts and out of a high-traffic path, even though the open top makes airflow easier to manage than a sealed tank.

Temperature, UVB & humidity

ZoneTarget
Basking surface95 to 100°F
Cool zone70s°F
NighttimeAround 60°F
Humidity30 to 50%

Measure basking surface temperature with an infrared thermometer aimed at the actual substrate or platform, not the air above it. Confirm cool-zone and nighttime readings the same way rather than guessing by feel.

Two fixtures, not one

UVB and heat are two separate jobs. A T5 HO UVB tube provides UV but little usable heat on its own, so pair it with a separate basking bulb over the same platform to reach the 95 to 100°F target, and confirm the surface temperature with an infrared thermometer rather than trusting the bulb's wattage.

UVB is not optional

Use a linear T5 HO tube (Arcadia 12% or Zoo Med ReptiSun 10.0 are commonly recommended), positioned over the basking end so it covers roughly a third to half of the enclosure. Never place it behind glass or plastic, both block UVB almost entirely. This fixture is for the indoor setup only: outdoors, natural sunlight is a superior UVB source and no fixture is needed.

UVI targets

Aim for a UV Index of 3.0 to 4.0 at the basking area, with a gradient down toward the cool zone so your tortoise can choose its own exposure level.

Bulb tracking

Linear fluorescent UVB output drops well before the bulb visibly dims. Track the install date on the Bulb & Equipment Log on page 19 and replace it on a schedule, not by appearance.

Nice to have A bright daylight light around 6500K alongside the UVB tube supports natural activity and grazing behavior through the day.

Signs your setup needs adjusting

You noticeLikely means
Substrate staying damp, glass or plastic panels foggingHumidity running too high for the space

Tortoise digs constantly at the coolest cornerEnclosure running too hot overall

Brumation & seasonal care

Russian tortoises are physiologically programmed to brumate: a period of dormancy lasting roughly 2 to 4 months over winter at cooler temperatures, around 40 to 55°F. In the wild this is not optional. In an indoor home, it is, and that's a genuine decision worth thinking through rather than defaulting into.

If you choose to brumate

A pre-brumation vet check is worth doing first, to confirm your tortoise is a healthy weight and free of parasites or illness before several months of reduced activity. Expect roughly 2 to 4 months of dormancy at 40 to 55°F, then a return to full basking temperatures, UVB, and feeding once it's over.

If you choose to stay active

Keep full basking heat, UVB, and normal feeding running straight through winter. A tortoise kept this way has no physiological need to slow down and can stay active and eating year-round. Neither choice is more correct than the other.

A genuine choice Many tortoise species don't offer this option cleanly. Russian tortoises can go either way as long as you commit fully: either maintain full heat, light, and feeding all winter, or let a properly cooled, vet-checked brumation run its course. Half measures, like letting temperatures drift down without the deliberate 40 to 55°F setup, are the outcome to avoid.

Warm-season outdoor time

When weather allows, supervised outdoor time is a genuine benefit: natural sunlight, varied terrain, and room to move all support the same basking and activity needs the indoor setup is built around. An outdoor pen needs walls at least 12 inches deep below grade, since a tortoise that wants out will dig rather than climb.

Diet & feeding

Russian tortoises are herbivores, and weeds and leafy greens should make up the large majority of every meal. This is not a species where insects, meat, or high-protein foods belong on the regular menu.

CategoryRoleNotes
Weeds & leafy greensStaple, bulk of every mealDandelion, plantain, clover, and similar forage; rotate varieties daily or every other day
FruitOccasional treat onlyUnder 5% of total intake if given at all
Calcium supplementShell & bone supportDust forage regularly as part of the normal feeding routine

Why rotation matters

Offering fresh forage and rotating the mix of weeds and greens daily or every other day keeps the diet varied and closer to what a tortoise would encounter grazing naturally, rather than settling on one or two convenient items long-term.

Never feed Beans, peas, or any high-protein food, dog or cat food, avocado, or anything sprayed with pesticide or fertilizer. A diet that runs too rich in protein and calcium relative to fiber is a leading driver of pyramiding, see page 11.

Hydration

Offer a shallow water dish at all times, and a regular soak supports hydration alongside the moisture already in fresh forage. Adequate hydration and activity also play a role in preventing pyramiding, alongside diet, see page 11.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Undersizing the enclosure "because it's small for now," then never upgrading toward the preferred 7×3.5 ft footprint.
  • Skipping UVB entirely, or using a coil or compact bulb instead of a proper linear T5 HO tube.
  • Feeding a protein- or fruit-heavy diet instead of weeds and greens, a leading cause of pyramiding.
  • Keeping substrate too shallow to allow real digging and burrowing behavior.
  • Letting winter temperatures drift down by accident instead of making a deliberate brumation decision (page 7).
  • Keeping substrate too damp and dirty, which invites shell rot (page 12).
  • Buying a cheap wild-caught adult without weighing the welfare and conservation cost against a captive-bred tortoise, see page 15.

Enrichment, by priority

Foraging

Scatter-feeding and rotating forage daily or every other day engages natural grazing behavior instead of routine bowl-feeding.

Digging area

Full-depth substrate (6 to 12+ in) lets a tortoise dig and burrow the way it would in the wild, not just walk on top of it.

Varied terrain

Rocks, logs, and gentle slopes encourage more natural movement and grazing paths than a flat, empty floor.

Outdoor time

Supervised outdoor access in warm weather, in a dig-proof pen, adds natural sunlight and terrain variety indoor setups can't fully match.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a vet promptly if you see Refusal to eat outside of a planned brumation, sunken eyes, discharge from eyes/nose/mouth, open-mouth breathing, soft or swollen limbs, lethargy with no clear trigger, straining without producing waste, or any soft, discolored, or foul-smelling patch on the shell.

What to tell the vet

Current basking & cool-zone temps, and UVB bulb age
Diet breakdown over the last two weeks
Whether brumation is planned, underway, or not happening this year
Any recent change in shell texture, color, or shape

Why UVB and diet come up first

Most of the conditions in this section, metabolic bone disease, pyramiding, and shell rot, trace back to a UVB, calcium, diet, or substrate problem more often than anything else. A vet will ask about your setup before anything else for exactly that reason.

Normal vs. concerning

NormalConcerning
Reduced appetite and activity during a vet-cleared, deliberate brumation periodLoss of appetite or lethargy outside of brumation, or dormancy running well beyond the typical 2 to 4 month window
Occasional light digging or shallow burrowing in substratePersistent straining without producing waste
Brief retreat into the shell when startled or handledStaying withdrawn for extended periods, paired with swelling or discharge

Metabolic bone disease & pyramiding

Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Cause: insufficient UVB or calcium relative to phosphorus over time, the same underlying mechanism seen across UVB-dependent reptiles. Signs: soft or misshapen shell, difficulty walking, tremors, general weakness. Diagnosis: physical exam plus an X-ray to check bone and shell density. Response: vet diagnosis and treatment promptly, this does not self-correct. An illness workup like this commonly runs $150 to $300 or more. Recovery: mild, early cases can improve over weeks to months with corrected UVB and vet-guided calcium; advanced shell or bone deformity often persists even once the disease itself is controlled.

Prevention beats treatment Correct UVB (page 6) and a diet built around weeds and greens with regular calcium (page 8) prevent nearly all MBD cases. This is the single highest-leverage thing in this whole package.

Pyramiding

Cause: a diet too rich in protein or calcium relative to fiber, or insufficient humidity and activity. Signs: abnormal, bumpy, raised growth on the shell scutes instead of smooth, even growth. Diagnosis: visual and physical exam.

Reality check Pyramiding cannot be reversed once it happens. This is a prevention-only issue: the response is correcting diet, humidity, and activity going forward, not undoing the existing shell shape. That's exactly why the diet guidance on page 8 matters so much.

Respiratory infection, shell rot & parasites

Three conditions worth knowing by name before you ever see them.

Respiratory Infection

Cause: most often temperatures running too cool for too long. Signs: open-mouth breathing, discharge from the nose or mouth, lethargy. Diagnosis: physical exam and listening to the lungs, the vet will ask about your temperature setup directly. Response: vet visit for treatment; correct the basking and cool-zone temperatures in parallel or it recurs.

Shell Rot

Cause: usually a too-damp, dirty substrate held against the shell over time. Signs: soft, discolored, or foul-smelling patches on the carapace or plastron. Diagnosis: physical exam. Response: vet treatment, and correcting substrate moisture and cleanliness so it doesn't return.

Parasites

Diagnosis: a fecal exam. Reality check: internal parasites are common in tortoises generally, especially wild-caught individuals or ones recently acquired from an unknown history. Response: vet-guided treatment, followed by a fecal recheck.

The common thread All three trace back to setup or sourcing: temperature for respiratory infection, substrate moisture and cleanliness for shell rot, and unknown history for parasites. A captive-bred tortoise from a known source, kept at correct temperatures on clean, appropriately moist substrate, avoids most of this.

First 30 days & days 31 to 90

Week 1

Confirm all temperatures and UVI with an actual thermometer/meter, not "feels warm"
Let your tortoise settle: minimal handling, focus on feeding and observing appetite
Schedule a wellness vet visit within the first couple of weeks

Week 2 to 3

Establish a daily or every-other-day forage rotation, weeds and greens dominant
Begin a consistent calcium supplement routine
Start the Owner Log on page 19

Week 4

Review weight and shell condition trends from the Owner Log
Mark your UVB bulb's install date on the Bulb & Equipment Log
Introduce first enrichment items from page 20

Days 31 to 90

Book the follow-up wellness check most vets recommend after initial setup
Re-measure UVI directly if you have access to a meter, don't rely on the bulb's age alone
Look back at the Owner Log for any slow drift in weight, appetite, or shell condition
If winter is approaching, work through the brumation decision guide on page 7 and book a pre-brumation vet check if you're choosing to brumate

Daily, weekly, monthly & seasonal routine

Daily

Check basking & cool-zone temps
Offer fresh weeds/greens forage, rotating varieties daily or every other day
Refresh water dish
Quick visual health check: eyes, shell, posture, appetite

Weekly

Spot-clean substrate and wipe down water dish
Weigh and log in the Owner Log
Rotate or refresh one enrichment item
Confirm UVB bulb is still within its tracked replacement window

Monthly

Check substrate depth and top up toward 6 to 12+ inches as needed
Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings
Check that hides and terrain still fit your tortoise's current size

Seasonal

Reassess indoor/outdoor time as the weather changes
Make and follow through on the brumation decision each winter (page 7); book a pre-brumation vet check if brumating

Glossary

BrumationA reptile's natural winter dormancy period. For Russian tortoises kept indoors with full heat, light, and feeding year-round, it's optional rather than required.
PyramidingAbnormal, bumpy shell growth instead of smooth growth, largely irreversible once it has occurred.
MBDMetabolic Bone Disease: weakened bones and shell from inadequate UVB or calcium.
UVIUV Index: the measurement scale used to describe basking-area UVB intensity.
CarapaceThe domed upper shell.
PlastronThe flat underside shell.
Fecal examA lab check of a stool sample, used to diagnose internal parasites.
Tortoise tableAn open-topped enclosure design that prioritizes floor space and ventilation over height.
Captive-bredBred in captivity rather than collected from the wild; preferred for both welfare and conservation reasons.
