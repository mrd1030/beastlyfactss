Enclosure size, type & the table question

Floor area, open top, and the reason a glass tank fails this species twice over.

Two credible sources give very different numbers and both are printed here rather than averaged. General care guidance, including our own, gives an indoor minimum of 4×2×2 ft (120×60×60 cm) and describes a functional size closer to 7 ft by 3.5 ft. The ReptiFiles species care sheet gives 8×4 ft (240×120 cm), about 32 sq ft of floor, with at least 10 sq ft (1 m²) more per additional tortoise. Treat 4×2 ft as the floor below which an adult is not adequately housed, and 8×4 ft as the target that a Russian tortoise will actually use, because this is one of the most active tortoises kept and it walks the whole of whatever it is given.

Not a glass tank An open-topped tortoise table is strongly preferred, for two separate reasons. Ventilation: a closed glass vivarium traps humidity against an arid-adapted animal, which is a direct cause of respiratory infection (page 19). Behavior: a tortoise sees through glass but cannot reach what is beyond it, and the result is restless pacing along the wall for hours a day. Neither problem is fixed by buying a bigger tank.

Building or buying

A tortoise table is a shallow open box with solid sides, usually wood sealed against moisture, and it is easy to build and cheap to enlarge. Length matters more than width, and both matter more than height. Do not add a second level: this is a walking animal, not a climbing one, though it climbs better than it looks, which is the subject of page 9.

  • Walls 12 in (30 cm) or more, solid rather than mesh, with nothing next to them a tortoise can use as a step. Corners are ladders; round them or block them.
  • A sealed, waterproof floor. Damp substrate against bare wood rots it and grows mold. Pond liner or sealed plywood, with a lip so substrate cannot spill.
  • A genuinely cool end. If the lamp warms the whole box, the box is too small, whatever the tape measure says.
  • Room for the lamps above it, at the mounting distances on page 7, without a shelf or a lid in the light path.
Hatchlings do not need a smaller enclosure A common piece of advice is to house a young tortoise small so it can find its food and water. It is not necessary and it wastes money on a second enclosure. Give a hatchling the adult footprint with more cover: extra hides, more plants, and food and water in the same place every day. A young tortoise with places to hide uses a large space happily.

Temperature, basking & night lows

A genuine gradient, measured on the surface, in an unusually cold-hardy species.

A tortoise cannot make its own heat, so the enclosure has to offer a range and let the animal choose. What matters is a real gradient across the length of the table: a hot basking spot at one end and a genuinely cool end at the other. If the whole enclosure reads the same temperature, the animal has no way to regulate itself, and that is true whether the reading is too high or exactly right.

ZoneTarget

This is one of the most cold-tolerant tortoises kept In the wild, Russian tortoises brumate in abandoned rodent burrows 20 to 50 cm (8 to 20 in) down, where the surrounding soil sits at 6 to 10°C (43 to 50°F) and the animal's own body temperature runs 5 to 11°C (41 to 52°F). Overnight temperatures in the +4 to +5°C range (39 to 41°F) are tolerated without harm. That hardiness is real, and it is exactly why owners get away with cold, damp setups for years before the animal shows what it has cost.

Getting the heat right

  • A halogen flood bulb is the right heat source for a basking spot. It puts out the short-wavelength infrared that penetrates and warms an animal properly, the way sunlight does. A ceramic emitter warms air rather than the animal and is a night or ambient tool, not a basking one.
  • Raise or lower the lamp, do not chase wattage. Distance is the adjustment. A dimming thermostat or a lamp dimmer on the basking bulb lets you hold a temperature through changing room conditions instead of guessing.
  • Never a heat mat under a tortoise. A tortoise's plastron is not built to take belly heat, it does not register overheating from below, and burns are a real outcome.
  • Two thermometers, permanently. A digital probe at the cool end and an infrared gun for the basking surface. Stick-on dial gauges are decorative.
  • The basking spot and the UVB tube go at the same end, so the animal gets heat and UV together, which is how it works outdoors. Page 7.
Heat is the emergency in summer, not winter A cold-adapted steppe animal in a conservatory or a sunny room in July is in far more danger than the same animal in an unheated room in January. Outdoors, a pen with no permanent shade is a lethal enclosure. Indoors, check the enclosure's afternoon temperature rather than its morning one, at the hottest point of the year.

UVB, lighting & the equipment that controls it

The one piece of kit you cannot substitute, and the one that fails invisibly.

UVB drives vitamin D3 synthesis, which drives calcium absorption, which builds and maintains a shell. Without it a tortoise develops metabolic bone disease however much calcium is in the food (page 18). What makes it dangerous is that output falls away months before the bulb looks dim, so a fixture that is visibly working can be delivering nothing useful.

RequirementDetail
Bulb typeA linear T5 HO tube in a reflective fixture. Arcadia 12% or Zoo Med ReptiSun 10.0 are the standard choices
LengthRoughly a third to half the enclosure length, mounted over the warm end alongside the basking lamp
TargetA UV index of 3.0 to 4.0 at the basking area, measured with a UV index meter
Mounting distanceWith mesh in the path, the tortoise's back sits 14 to 16 in (35 to 40 cm) below the tube. With no mesh, 17 to 20 in (43 to 50 cm)
ReplacementEvery 12 months, on the calendar, not when it stops lighting. Write the date on the bulb and on page 35
Visible lightA bright 6500K daylight lamp alongside. Tortoises are diurnal and a dim enclosure suppresses activity and appetite
Photoperiod12 hours on, 12 off, on a timer. Lengthen toward 14 in summer and shorten toward 10 in winter to track the seasons
Three ways to deliver no UVB while believing you are A coil or compact bulb. They concentrate output into a narrow cone, have been associated with eye and skin irritation, and are not adequate for a species that needs a UVI of 3 to 4 across a basking area.
Glass or plastic in the path. Glass removes essentially all of it, which is also why a sunny window is not sunlight to a tortoise. Mesh costs a substantial fraction, which is what the two mounting distances above are for.
A bulb over a year old. Output decays continuously from the day it is switched on.

The equipment that controls the rest

  • A timer for the lights, so the photoperiod holds whether or not you are home. The cheapest item on the list.
  • A dimming thermostat or lamp dimmer on the basking bulb, holding the surface temperature through changing room conditions. An on/off thermostat is the wrong controller for a basking lamp, because it flickers the light.
  • An infrared temperature gun for the basking surface. Under $25, and it settles what the other instruments cannot.
  • A digital probe thermometer at the cool end, plus a hygrometer. Page 8.
  • A UV index meter, optional and expensive, and the only way to know rather than assume. Borrow one if a local keeper has it.
Sunlight beats every bulb Unfiltered natural sunlight delivers UVB no lamp matches, so a few supervised hours outdoors in warm weather is worth more than any equipment upgrade. Page 9 covers doing it safely. It does not replace an indoor fixture: the weather does not cooperate for most of the year.

Humidity, substrate & the moist hide

An arid species that still needs somewhere damp, and an open disagreement about the number.
Two credible sources, two different figures General care guidance for this species, ours included, gives ambient humidity of 30 to 50% and warns that high ambient humidity across the whole enclosure is a real cause of respiratory infection. The ReptiFiles species care sheet gives 40 to 75%, lower during the day and higher at night, following what the animal experiences in a burrow. Both agree on the shape of the answer even where the numbers differ, and the shape is what matters: a dry, well-ventilated open surface with a genuinely humid microclimate available underneath and inside a hide. A tortoise in the wild spends most of its life in a burrow that is far damper than the steppe above it. Build both, and let the animal choose.

In practice: keep the open surface dry and the air moving, keep the deeper substrate slightly damp rather than bone dry, and provide at least one hide packed with moist sphagnum or peat moss on the cool side, remoistened every few days. That combination gives hydration and clean shedding without a permanently damp enclosure, which is what actually causes the respiratory and shell problems on pages 19 and 20.

Digging is not decoration Burrowing is how this species buffers temperature and humidity, and a substrate too shallow to dig into removes a behavior the animal actively needs rather than merely enjoys. If the main substrate cannot go deep enough, build a dedicated dig box: a plastic tub of damp soil mix sunk into the enclosure works, and it will be used constantly.

Soaking and water

Keep a shallow water dish available at all times, sunk level with the substrate so the animal can walk in and out and cannot tip. Change it daily; tortoises defecate in water routinely. Beyond that, soak the tortoise in shallow warm water for 20 to 30 minutes weekly, deeper for a young or newly acquired animal. The water should reach the bottom of the shell and no higher. Most tortoises drink and pass urates during a soak, which is also when you get the best look at both. Page 24.

Outdoor housing & escape-proofing

The best enclosure this species can have, and the one it will try hardest to leave.

Whenever climate and security allow, outdoor housing is the richest environment available to this species: unfiltered sunlight, real soil to dig, variable terrain, weather, and edible plants growing where the animal walks. Many long-term keepers treat an outdoor pen as the standard and the indoor table as the winter arrangement. A supervised hour on unsprayed grass beats any equipment purchase.

Five ways an outdoor tortoise dies Heat. A pen with no permanent shade at 2 pm in July.
A dog. Shell trauma from a household dog is one of the commonest serious injuries in pet tortoises and it is very often the family's own animal.
Escape. Digging under, or climbing a corner. Corners are ladders, so round them off or block them.
Drowning. A pond, a pool, a deep water bowl, or an upended tortoise in a shallow dish it cannot right itself out of.

When outdoors is not an option

Indoor keeping is genuinely viable for this species for its entire life, which is the main advantage it has over the larger tortoises. It requires the UVB fixture on page 7 to be right rather than approximately right, and it makes the enclosure size on page 5 matter more, because there is no garden making up the difference. A weekly supervised session outside in warm weather bridges the two arrangements and is worth arranging even for a committed indoor keeper.

Take the temperature out there too Pen surfaces reach temperatures a lawn does not. Check a paving slab, a stone and the shaded soil with the infrared gun on the hottest afternoon of the year before you trust the pen. If a surface is too hot for your hand, it is too hot for a plastron.

Handling, temperament & why males live alone

An observation animal with a personality, not a lap animal.

Russian tortoises are active, curious and genuinely responsive to their keepers: many approach confidently, recognize the person who brings food, and rearrange their enclosure overnight. None of that makes them a handling pet. Being picked up is a predator experience for an animal whose entire defense is staying on the ground inside its shell, and over-handling causes real, measurable stress. Keep physical contact purposeful, brief and infrequent, and get the relationship from watching and from feeding instead.

Picking one up, when you have to

  • Approach from the front or the side where it can see you, and pause. A hand descending from above is a bird.
  • Support the whole shell from underneath with both hands, keeping the animal level and low to the ground.
  • Never tip it vertically or onto its back. That compresses the lungs against the organs and is genuinely dangerous, not just unpleasant.
  • Never lift by a leg or the front of the shell, and never pull a retracted head or limb.
  • Sit on the floor. A dropped tortoise cracks, and a cracked shell is a page 20 problem.
  • Wash your hands before and after. Reptiles carry Salmonella as normal gut flora, and it is the reason a tortoise is a poor pet for a household with children under five or anyone immunocompromised.
Salmonella, plainly Healthy tortoises shed Salmonella without being ill themselves. The route to people is hands, then face or food. Wash hands after every contact with the animal, its enclosure, its water dish or its substrate. Do not clean tortoise equipment in a kitchen sink or a bath used by people. Do not kiss the tortoise. This is the reason behind the federal four-inch rule on page 25, and it is a real risk rather than a regulatory relic.

Housing more than one

House alone by default. This is a solitary species that does not benefit from company the way a social mammal does, and an owner adding a second tortoise for the first one's sake is usually creating a problem. If you do keep more than one:

  • Never two males. Males are genuinely territorial and will ram, bite legs and flip one another, sometimes fatally. This is not a personality question and it does not resolve with more space.
  • Two females can work, with caution, in a large enclosure with several hides, several feeding stations and sight breaks between them. Watch for one animal monopolizing the basking spot.
  • A male with a single female is a problem for the female, who gets pursued and mounted relentlessly. A group of several females to one male spreads that, which is a breeding arrangement rather than a pet one.
  • Never mix species. Different husbandry needs, and pathogens that one species carries harmlessly can be fatal to another. Page 21.
Better than they look, at escaping Despite the reputation, Russian tortoises climb and dig well and are persistent about both. They will scale a corner, push under a barrier, or work at the same weak point for weeks. Assume any enclosure is escapable until it has held for a season, and check the perimeter as a routine job rather than after the first escape.

Diet: what a steppe grazer actually eats

High fiber, low protein, low sugar, high calcium. And less of it than you think.

The dietary rule for Testudo species is short: low protein, low carbohydrate, low sugar, high calcium, high fiber, and that combination is the primary key to deformity-free growth. In the wild a Russian tortoise eats from a range of over 80 plant species across 27 families, almost entirely weeds, leaves and flowers. It is a grazer of tough, fibrous, low-nutrient plants, and every problem on page 18 comes from feeding it something richer.

The quantity nobody mentions In nature this species typically feeds on no more than about ninety days out of three hundred and sixty five, and even then spends roughly fifteen minutes grazing a day. Captive tortoises are fed every day, generously, on food far richer than steppe weeds, which is why overfeeding is the single most common husbandry error in this species and why pyramiding is so widespread. Feeding less, and feeding coarser, is closer to correct than most keepers are comfortable with.

How to feed

  • Scatter feed rather than using a bowl. Put greens in several places so the animal has to walk and search, which is closer to grazing and doubles as enrichment. Page 14.
  • Feed on a flat stone or a tile where you do scatter feed, so soil and substrate are not swallowed with every mouthful.
  • Adults: feed daily, in a portion roughly the size of the tortoise's shell, and expect it to be eaten within a few hours rather than picked at all day. A fasting day once a week does an adult no harm at all and reflects how the animal eats in the wild.
  • Hatchlings and juveniles eat daily and more, with no fasting days, because they are building shell.
  • Remove uneaten fresh food the same day. Hay can stay.

Weeds, grazing & growing your own

The cheapest and best food for this species grows in the cracks of a pavement.

Supermarket greens are bred to be tender, watery and mild, close to the opposite of what a steppe grazer evolved to eat. Weeds are tougher, higher in fiber and minerals, more varied, and free. A keeper who learns six weeds by sight has better food than one spending $200 a year on salad, and the variety matters: the wild diet runs to over 80 plant species.

WeedHow to recognize it, and notes
DandelionThe staple. Deeply toothed leaves in a flat rosette, hollow stem with milky sap, single yellow flower. Leaves and flowers both, in quantity. High in calcium

Where you must not pick Anywhere sprayed with herbicide, pesticide or fertilizer, including a neighbor's overspray. Within a few meters of a road, where exhaust residue and salt accumulate. Anywhere dogs are walked. Any verge a council or contractor maintains. Farmland margins. And never pick something you cannot name: buttercup, ragwort, foxglove, hemlock, ivy, deadly nightshade and daffodil all grow alongside the good weeds and several of them are lethal. If you are not certain, leave it.

Growing your own

A seed tray or two of a tortoise forage mix, sown in rotation, turns cut greens into living plant the animal crops itself, which is as close as a captive setup gets to grazing. Three trays on a windowsill on a two-week rotation keeps a single tortoise supplied through most of the year. Outdoors, sow forage mix straight into the pen and let the animal graze as it walks; that is the single biggest quality-of-life change available for this species. A pot of dandelion and plantain in a sunny corner covers the winter.

Drying weeds for winter Dandelion, plantain and clover all dry well. Cut on a dry day, spread in a single layer somewhere warm and airy out of direct sun until crisp, then store in a paper bag rather than plastic. Crumbled into hay it turns a dull winter diet into a varied one, and it costs an afternoon in June.

Fruit, protein, supplements & the never-feed list

The short page that prevents most of the health section.
Never feed a Russian tortoise Any animal protein. Dog or cat food, boiled egg, cheese, meat, insects, mealworms. Excess protein drives pyramiding and burdens the kidneys, and none of it belongs in a steppe herbivore.
Fruit. Fruits are inappropriate for arid-habitat species: the sugar disrupts gut flora, causes loose droppings and encourages the wrong fermentation. If you feed any at all it belongs under 5% of the diet, and zero is a defensible answer for this species.
Bread, pasta, grain, cereal, cat treats, "tortoise" biscuits.
Iceberg lettuce. Almost no nutrition, and linked to vitamin A deficiency in tortoises fed on it. Page 22.
Toxic plants: avocado, rhubarb leaves, buttercup, ragwort, foxglove, daffodil and other bulbs, ivy, hemlock, nightshade, azalea, oleander, yew.
Anything from treated ground. Page 12.

Why protein and fruit matter so much here

A tortoise's shell grows continuously, in layers, at whatever rate the diet allows. Fed richly, it grows fast and unevenly, and the scutes lift into the raised cone shapes called pyramiding, which is permanent (page 18). Fed on tough, fibrous, low-nutrient plants, it grows slowly and smoothly, which is what it does in the wild. The commonest owner instinct, that a growing animal should be fed generously and well, is the exact instinct that deforms this one.

Females, eggs & egg binding

A female with no male in the house can still produce eggs, and she still has to pass them.

A mature female develops and lays eggs on her own hormonal schedule, whether or not she has ever met a male. Those eggs are infertile, but the physical process is identical and so is the risk if she cannot complete it. Every owner of a female should know what a gravid female looks like and what to do when the laying does not happen.

Signs a female is gravid

  • Restlessness and pacing, often out of character, and walking the perimeter repeatedly.
  • Digging test nests. Repeated shallow excavations, often abandoned, usually in a warm spot.
  • Reduced appetite as the eggs take up room.
  • A firm, rounded feel if a vet palpates the soft area in front of the hind legs. Do not attempt this yourself; pressing on eggs can break them.
Provide a nest site before she needs one A female with nowhere acceptable to dig will hold eggs, and holding eggs is how a straightforward event becomes an emergency. Give her a deep area of slightly damp substrate, at least as deep as the shell is long and ideally 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm), in a warm and undisturbed corner. Outdoors, a mound of soil in a sunny spot works. She may reject several sites before choosing one, and being watched puts many females off entirely.
Egg binding, or dystocia A female straining, restless for days, digging obsessively and then stopping, going off food, becoming lethargic, or producing fluid or blood from the vent is a same-day veterinary case. So is a female you know is gravid who has been trying to nest for more than a couple of days without laying. Left alone this ends in a ruptured oviduct, peritonitis or death. Treatment ranges from correcting the nest site and giving fluids and calcium, through hormonal induction, to surgical removal of the eggs. It is not something to wait out.

What to do with the eggs

With no male, they are infertile and can be discarded once she has finished and covered the nest. Let her finish: a female interrupted mid-clutch may retain the remainder. If a male has had access they may be fertile, and incubating them is a decision to produce more forty-year animals into a market where rehoming is already hard.

Calcium is what this costs her Producing a clutch draws heavily on a female's calcium reserves, which is the practical link between this page and page 18. A laying female needs her calcium supplementation kept up, a cuttlebone permanently available, and her UVB fixture in date. A female laying repeatedly on a marginal setup is the classic route to metabolic bone disease in an adult that was fine for years.

Metabolic bone disease & pyramiding

The two conditions that are written permanently into the shell.

Metabolic bone disease

MBD is among the most common conditions in pet tortoises, and it comes from an imbalance of calcium and phosphorus caused by an improper diet, a lack of adequate ultraviolet light, or inadequate protein intake. Without usable D3 the animal cannot absorb calcium regardless of how much is in the food, so it draws calcium out of its own skeleton and shell to keep blood levels workable.

Signs Misshapen shell. Deformed leg bones. Slow growth. A shell that stays flimsy beyond about six months of age. Weak limbs, an inability to lift the body clear of the ground when walking, or a wobbling gait. In severe cases, fractures that occur with no real trauma. Advanced cases can leave permanently misshapen bones or a permanent shell deformity even after treatment.

Prevention is the whole story: a T5 HO UVB tube at UVI 3.0 to 4.0 replaced yearly, calcium without D3 on most meals, a cuttlebone always available, and a high-calcium low-phosphorus plant diet. Treatment is veterinary, involving injectable calcium and often vitamin D alongside correcting the husbandry. Do not treat a suspected case with supplements alone: calcium given without addressing the D3 pathway is not absorbed.

Pyramiding

Pyramiding is abnormal raised growth of the scutes, so the carapace carries individual sections that look like small pyramids instead of lying flat. It is associated with suboptimal temperature and humidity and with imbalances of calcium, phosphorus and protein, and in practice the drivers are excess dietary protein, overfeeding, and insufficient hydration or humidity. It is entirely preventable and completely irreversible: once the scutes are raised they stay raised for the animal's life. A tortoise with mild pyramiding that is otherwise well cared for can live a normal lifespan, so it is a permanent record of past husbandry rather than an ongoing illness. Severe pyramiding does restrict movement and lung capacity.

Respiratory infection & herpesvirus

The urgent one on this list, and the one that can take a whole collection.

Respiratory tract infection

Caused by Mycoplasma, other bacteria, or viruses, and often secondary to vitamin A deficiency. The husbandry triggers are chronically low temperature, excess humidity across the whole enclosure, and drafts, which is why the ventilation argument on page 5 and the humidity argument on page 8 are health arguments rather than comfort ones.

Signs, and this does not resolve on its own Excess mucus in the mouth, seen as bubbles at the mouth, nose or eyes. Nasal discharge. Lethargy. Loss of appetite. Wheezing. Extending the neck to breathe. Open-mouth breathing or gasping. Any of these is a veterinary appointment now, not a wait-and-see, and treatment is antibiotics given orally, by injection or as nose drops. Severe cases need hospitalization with fluid therapy and assisted feeding.

While you arrange care, raise the warm end toward the top of the normal range, make sure the animal is hydrated, and remove any source of damp or draft. Do not raise the humidity, which is a common and counterproductive instinct.

Herpesvirus, and why quarantine is not optional

The reason a new tortoise never joins an existing one Testudo tortoises, this species included, are susceptible to epidemics of viral disease and should be treated as potential high-risk carriers of herpes-type organisms. An infected animal is highly contagious and can appear healthy for long periods; in a group with no quarantine, mortality can approach 100%. Signs when they appear include a runny nose, swelling, a white or yellow plaque in the mouth and on the tongue, difficulty swallowing, lethargy and neurological signs. There is no cure. Treatment is supportive and a survivor remains a carrier for life.

What follows from that is simple and absolute. A new tortoise is quarantined in a separate room, on separate equipment, for months rather than weeks, and is screened by a vet before it goes anywhere near another tortoise. Page 21 sets out the full protocol. Never buy a tortoise on impulse from a table at an expo where animals from many sources have been sitting together, and never accept a tortoise from a household whose other animals you have not asked about.

Ranavirus, and mycoplasma

Ranavirus infections also occur in chelonians and, like herpesvirus, often prove fatal. Mycoplasma causes the chronic upper respiratory tract disease familiar to tortoise keepers: a persistent runny nose that comes and goes for years, worsens under stress, and is very difficult to clear. All three are arguments for the same two habits: quarantine properly, and never mix species or sources.

A runny nose is never just a runny nose in a tortoise Owners describe it as a cold and wait. There is no tortoise equivalent of a cold that resolves by itself. Persistent nasal discharge is either an infection, a chronic mycoplasmal disease, or vitamin A deficiency, and all three need a vet and none improve with time.

Shell rot, trauma & abscesses

The shell is living bone with a skin over it, and it gets infected like any other tissue.

Shell infection, or shell rot

Shell infections, also called shell rot and historically SCUD, or septicemic cutaneous ulcerative disease, are caused by bacteria, fungi or parasites entering through trauma: a scrape, a crack, a burn, or a damp patch that has softened the keratin. An overly damp enclosure is the commonest predisposing condition, which is one more reason for the dry surface on page 8.

Signs, and why this is not cosmetic Soft, discolored or foul-smelling patches. Pitting, flaking or lifting scutes with damp tissue underneath. A discharge or an unpleasant smell. Deep ulcerations can become life-threatening if not treated aggressively, and shell rot that reaches the bloodstream causes septicemia, which is a genuine emergency. Diagnosis involves microscopic analysis and culture to identify the organism, because a bacterial and a fungal infection need different drugs.

Treatment is cleaning and debridement, medication chosen for the organism, and correcting the husbandry that allowed it. Do not treat a suspected shell infection with a household antiseptic and hope: superficial treatment over an established infection seals it in.

Shell trauma

The commonest cause of shell trauma in a pet tortoise is a dog in the same household. A dog does not need to be aggressive: chewing a tortoise like a toy produces punctures and crushing injuries, and a shell is bone with a blood and nerve supply, so it hurts and it bleeds. Any damage can lead to life-threatening bone infection, and severe injuries expose internal organs.

  • Never leave a tortoise and a dog in the same space unsupervised, including a garden, including a dog that has always ignored it.
  • Other sources: a fall from a height, a heavy object, being stepped on, a lamp burn, or a heat mat under the animal.
  • Any crack, puncture or bleeding is a same-day veterinary case. Keep the animal clean and dry, do not apply glue, epoxy, resin or tape, and do not submerge it.

Abscesses

Abscesses in tortoises present as hard, tumor-like swellings rather than the soft pockets a mammal forms, because the pus is thick and dry. The classic sites are the ear and the mouth: a large swelling on the side of the head just behind the eye is an aural abscess, and swellings in the roof of the mouth also occur. They are frequently related to vitamin A deficiency (page 22). Treatment is surgical drainage or removal, flushing with a medicated solution, culture to identify the organism, and antibiotics. They do not resolve without intervention and they do not drain on their own.

The monthly shell check Once a month, with the animal on your lap over a towel: look at the whole carapace under good light for soft spots, discoloration, lifting scutes and cracks; turn it over briefly and check the plastron the same way, especially where it meets the substrate; check both sides of the head behind the eyes for swelling; and press gently at the shell margins for give. It takes two minutes and it catches all three conditions on this page early, when they are all treatable.

Parasites, the fecal test & quarantine

Fairly common, mostly invisible, and the reason every new tortoise is screened.

What they carry

Roundworms and protozoa are common gastrointestinal parasites in tortoises, particularly in wild-caught animals, and in many cases a parasitic infection causes no clinical signs at all. Severe infections cause diarrhea, weight loss and failure to thrive. Oxyurid (pinworm) burdens have been found to correlate with calcium deficiency and metabolic bone disease as well as with kidney disease, mainly in juveniles, which is one more reason not to treat a parasite load as a separate issue from husbandry.

Hexamita parva, the one specific to this species Hexamita parva is a highly damaging parasite of the renal and urinary system and has been identified frequently in Testudo horsfieldii. Untreated it causes rapid weight loss, dehydration and terminal renal failure. Treatment is oral metronidazole, reported at 260 mg/kg, and it needs a vet: the dose is calculated on body weight and the diagnosis needs a urine or fecal examination looking for the right organism. A Russian tortoise losing weight steadily with no obvious cause deserves this specifically ruled out.

The fecal test

Every newly acquired tortoise gets a fecal test, whether or not it has symptoms. Collect a fresh dropping, keep it in a sealed bag in the fridge, and take it to the vet within 24 hours; a sample from a soak is fine. Repeat annually thereafter, and repeat immediately for any unexplained weight loss or change in droppings. Deworming is prescribed against what is actually found rather than given routinely, because blanket dosing selects for resistance and some anthelmintics are hard on a debilitated animal.

Vitamin A deficiency, cloacoliths & beak overgrowth

Three conditions from three different corners that show up in the same tortoise.

Hypovitaminosis A

Vitamin A deficiency comes from an inappropriate diet, and the two that cause it are a diet based on iceberg lettuce and one containing meat. Its signs are easy to attribute elsewhere.

Signs Lack of appetite. Lethargy. Swelling of the eyelids, often with a pus-like discharge, so that the eyes are partly or fully closed. Swelling of the ear from an aural abscess, seen as a bulge on the side of the head behind the eye. Respiratory infections, which are frequently secondary to it. Kidney failure in later stages. Treatment is veterinary vitamin A, sometimes by injection, plus a corrected diet. Do not self-dose vitamin A: it is fat-soluble and an overdose causes skin sloughing.

Prevention is the diet on pages 11 and 12: dark leafy greens, weeds and small amounts of orange-fleshed vegetables all carry the carotenoids a tortoise converts to vitamin A. A tortoise eating dandelion and plantain will not be deficient.

Cloacoliths and urate blockage

Straining while vocalizing is a true emergency A cloacolith is a blockage formed by an accumulation of urates, the crystalline portion of a reptile's urine, which harden into a stone in the cloaca. A tortoise straining and making noise while doing it is a genuine emergency: this is one of very few sounds a tortoise makes and it means obstruction. Treatment is sedation, pain relief, and breaking down and flushing the blockage with forceps. Do not attempt to remove anything yourself.

The cause is nearly always chronic dehydration, which is why the weekly soak and the always-available water dish on page 8 matter more than they look. Thick, gritty urates are the warning that comes before the blockage. Page 24.

Beak and nail overgrowth

Beak and claws grow continuously and are worn down by biting tough food and walking on abrasive ground. On soft supermarket greens and a substrate of nothing but soil, both overgrow. An overgrown beak eventually stops the animal biting properly, at which point it loses weight, and a badly overgrown one forces the jaw out of alignment permanently.

  • Prevention: coarse fibrous weeds and hay rather than soft leaves, a permanent cuttlebone, and flat stones or paving to walk over.
  • Other causes exist, including metabolic bone disease and a past jaw injury, so a beak that keeps regrowing badly is a symptom rather than a grooming job.
  • Trimming is a veterinary procedure, done with a rotary tool. Do not use clippers, which crack the beak.
Shedding, which tortoises do differently A tortoise does not shed in one piece. Skin flakes from the neck and legs continuously, and scutes may shed thin translucent layers as the shell grows. Both are normal. Skin retained in tight bands around a limb means dehydration; scutes lifting to reveal soft damp tissue is page 20. Never peel a scute.

Brumation: the decision and the protocol

Programmed into the species, optional indoors, and dangerous done casually.

Russian tortoises are physiologically programmed to brumate. In their native range they are active for only about three months of the year, spending the rest underground in burrows 20 to 50 cm (8 to 20 in) deep where the soil sits at 6 to 10°C (43 to 50°F). Captive tortoises keep the drive, and many slow down, refuse food and dig in as autumn arrives whatever the enclosure is doing.

The genuine choice indoor keeping gives you By maintaining full lighting, heat and feeding year-round, an indoor keeper can keep a Russian tortoise active through winter rather than brumating it. This is a real option that many species do not offer, and it is a perfectly legitimate way to keep this animal. Wild-caught adults in particular may refuse food or show real stress if prevented from brumating, so the choice is easier with a captive-bred animal, and the honest position is that both routes are defensible and that the wrong route is a half-hearted one.

Do not brumate a tortoise that is

  • Underweight, or has lost weight this year
  • Under about two years old, or newly acquired
  • Showing any sign of respiratory infection, however mild
  • Carrying an untreated parasite load
  • Recovering from any illness or injury
  • Not health-checked by a vet in the last few months

A pre-brumation veterinary health check is worth doing before a first brumation and after any year with a health problem in it. A tortoise that goes into brumation sick does not come out.

The protocol, if you say yes

  1. Weigh and record, and check the shell, eyes, nose and mouth. This is the baseline every later weighing is measured against.
  2. Stop feeding, two to four weeks before the temperature comes down, while heat and light stay on. The gut has to be completely empty: food left fermenting in a cold tortoise is a route to serious illness. Keep offering water and soaking through this period.
  3. Wind the temperature and photoperiod down gradually over a week or two, rather than moving the animal straight into the cold.
  4. Hold it at 40 to 55°F (4 to 13°C) for 2 to 4 months, in a ventilated box of dry substrate in a place that stays reliably in that band. A dedicated refrigerator with a thermometer and a daily door-opening for air exchange is the method most experienced keepers now use, because a garage or a shed swings with the weather.
  5. Check weekly. Weigh the animal. A loss of more than roughly 1% of body weight a month, or any waking, urination, discharge or discomfort, ends the brumation early.
  6. Warm up gradually, over a few days, then straight back to full basking temperature and UVB.
  7. Soak immediately on waking, and daily for the first week. Rehydration comes before food. Expect the first meal to be refused and the first droppings to take several days.
Two things that kill brumating tortoises Food left in the gut. The fasting period is not optional and it is not a suggestion. Temperature that drifts. Too warm and the animal burns reserves without being properly dormant; below freezing and it suffers permanent eye and tissue damage or dies. A brumation with no thermometer on it is a gamble.

The law & the four-inch rule

The species is legal almost everywhere. The rule everyone quotes is not what they think it is.

Of the species we have checked state by state, the Russian tortoise has the fewest restrictions in the United States. No state bans it. One state requires a permit. Hawaii, which bans almost everything, expressly allows it. The rule that actually shapes how these animals are bought and sold is federal, says nothing about the species, and is misquoted more often than any other rule in the pet trade.

What the four-inch rule actually says It is 21 CFR 1240.62, an FDA regulation from 1975 written to reduce Salmonella transmission to small children. Its operative sentence is that viable turtle eggs and live turtles with a carapace length of less than 4 inches shall not be sold, held for sale, or offered for any other type of commercial or public distribution. Sold, held for sale, offered for distribution. Nothing about owning one, and nothing about the species: it catches a hatchling Russian tortoise for the same reason it catches a hatchling red-eared slider.

Then comes the part almost nobody quotes. Subsection (c) lists exceptions, and the second covers the sale, holding for sale and distribution of live turtles and viable turtle eggs not in connection with a business. A private keeper rehoming a hatchling is not a business; a breeder selling them is. That clause is why hatchlings move hand to hand through private keepers while shops stock only animals past the four-inch line, and it is the real reason a Russian tortoise you buy is usually already a few years old.

Power outages, travel & transport

A reptile depends on an outlet for the one thing it cannot make: body heat.

A healthy adult Russian tortoise tolerates one ordinary cool night without harm. The risk is duration, not the single low reading, and this species has more margin than almost any other reptile kept: it tolerates 60°F (16°C) comfortably, and the point to act is a sustained period below 60°F outside an intentional brumation. Pushed meaningfully below its preferred range for more than a day or two, a reptile becomes more susceptible to bacterial infection and stops eating, which is the textbook setup for the respiratory infection on page 19.

During an outage

  • Move the enclosure to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold in the heat that is left.
  • A wrapped hot water bottle or a commercial hand warmer beside the animal, never touching it directly and never underneath it.
  • Stop feeding if the enclosure is genuinely cold. A reptile cannot digest below its working temperature, and food sitting undigested in a cold gut is a real problem. This is the opposite of the rule for a mammal or a bird.
  • Keep water available, and keep soaking on the normal schedule with warm water heated another way.
  • No candles, gas stoves or open flames near the enclosure. The fire and carbon monoxide risk outweighs the cold, which this species handles well.

Outage kit, packed before you need it: two or three hand warmers or a hot water bottle, two thick towels, a battery or wind-up thermometer, a ventilated tub sized for the animal, and your reptile vet and 24-hour exotics numbers written on paper rather than saved on a phone that may die.
