Cost overview

Getting started

The bird is $700 to $3,000 for a common species, with umbrellas at $1,000 to $3,000 and Moluccans $1,400 to $3,500. A rescue fee is $300 to $800 and adoptable birds are not rare. Add cage, stand, toys, air purifier and a first vet visit and a realistic total is $2,100 to $6,900. Page 34.

Every year

Food is the small line. Destructible toys are the big one, because a cockatoo destroying them is the system working. Budget $1,160 to $2,380 a year including an annual exam. Across a 40-year life that is $46,000 to $95,000, before a single emergency.

The reputation is earned, and it is on the record A parrot behavior consultant who specializes in this problem reports that clients with cockatoos make up more than half of her entire consulting practice. Rescues routinely describe cockatoos as the bird they take in most. That is not a rumour about a difficult species, it is a measured pattern, and page 5 explains what causes it.

Section 01

Is a cockatoo right for you?

The only page in this series written to change some readers' minds. Answer it before you buy, not after.

Cockatoos are extremely social, extremely intelligent, extremely loud, extremely destructive and extremely long-lived, and the combination produces more behavioral problems than any other commonly kept parrot. The bird is not defective. Screaming and plucking are, in large part, a direct readout of unmet needs. But it means the difference between a wonderful cockatoo and a tragic one is almost entirely what the household can provide, every day, for decades.

Nine questions. Answer them out loud.

QuestionWhy it decides
Can I give this bird 4 to 6 hours of real engagement most days?Not company in the same room. Interaction. This is the figure behind the surrenders
Will that still be true in ten years, through a job change, a baby, or an illness?The commonest surrender story is a life that changed, not an owner who stopped caring
Can my household tolerate screaming that carries through walls?Dawn and dusk contact calling is normal behavior, not a fault to be trained out. Page 19
Do I own the building, or will a neighbor complain?Noise is the second commonest reason a cockatoo is rehomed. Renting with one is genuinely hard
Is anyone here asthmatic or immunocompromised?Cockatoos produce very heavy feather dust. Page 10 is not optional reading
Can I afford $1,100 to $2,400 a year, plus emergencies, indefinitely?Chronic conditions in this species are expensive and common. Page 34
Am I willing to have my woodwork, furniture and skirting boards destroyed?Destruction is normal behavior. A bird with nothing sanctioned to destroy picks your house, or itself
Do I have a plan for the bird if it outlives me?A 40-year animal needs a named person and money set aside. Page 31
Have I met an adult cockatoo, not a baby?Babies are uniformly sweet. The animal you are buying is the one at eight years old
If more than two answers are "probably" Then the honest recommendation is a different bird. That is not a judgement about you. A cockatoo whose needs go unmet does not become a slightly worse pet, it becomes a screaming, self-mutilating animal in a spare room, and then it becomes a rescue's problem. The cockatiel, which is a small cockatoo and shares much of the charm, is a genuinely realistic alternative for a normal household. So is a rescue cockatoo you visit weekly rather than one you own.

Two things that make it work, and one that does not

  • A household, not a person. A bird that accepts several people has several sources of company and does not collapse when one of them goes to work. Building this deliberately from day one is page 18, and it is the highest-leverage thing a new owner does
  • Structured time, not just presence. Ten or fifteen minutes of training twice a day beats an afternoon on your shoulder while you work. Page 16
  • Not more toys. They matter, and they are on page 15, but the tested winner for plucking here was attention. No product substitutes for it
Consider adoption seriously Rescue fees run $300 to $800 against $1,000 to $3,500 for a baby, and adoptable cockatoos are not rare precisely because of everything on this page. An adult bird also shows you who it actually is, which a hand-raised baby cannot. A good rescue is honest about a bird's history, takes it back if it does not work, and is far more useful to you at year three than a shop will be.

Cage size, bar gauge, locks & placement

The most expensive object you will buy, and the one most often bought twice.

The commonly cited minimum for a large cockatoo is 36 in wide by 24 in deep by 48 in tall (91×61×122 cm), with many avian vets recommending closer to 48×48×60 in (122×122×152 cm) as genuinely comfortable. Bigger is always better here, and a cage top that opens into a play stand is the best arrangement there is, because it turns the cage into a base rather than a container.

A cage is a baseline, not the answer. No cage you can fit in a house is large enough for a bird this size to exercise properly, which is why the out-of-cage hours on page 8 are the part that actually matters.

Bar gauge, spacing and locks Bar spacing 3/4 to 1 in (19 to 25 mm) for a large cockatoo, on genuinely heavy-gauge bar. A cockatoo's beak bends thin cage wire, and a bird that opens the cage while you are at work is loose in a house of hazards for eight hours.

Assume the latch will be defeated. Cockatoos are notorious escape artists with both the intelligence and the strength to work out standard fastenings, and cage-specific padlocks or spring clips on every door and feeder hatch are standard practice rather than paranoia.

Never galvanized or zinc-coated. A cockatoo chews the bars, and that coating is a zinc source. Stainless steel is the gold standard, rust-proof, non-toxic and able to survive this beak for decades. Heavy-gauge powder-coated steel is the affordable alternative and is acceptable. Page 27 on what metal poisoning does.

Perches, play stands & out-of-cage space

The hours outside the cage are the ones that decide how this bird turns out.

Perches

Varied diameters from 3/4 to 2 in (19 to 51 mm), in several materials, at several heights. A cage of identical dowels loads the same points of a large bird's foot continuously, and a heavy bird develops pressure sores faster than a small one does. Natural hardwood branch is the best surface there is and it is free: apple, willow, eucalyptus and manzanita, unsprayed, scrubbed and dried.

Expect to replace them. A cockatoo reduces a perch to splinters, and that is the perch working as intended rather than a waste of money. Budget perches as a consumable on page 34.

Out-of-cage space, which is the real requirement

A cockatoo needs hours out of the cage daily, in a proofed room, with things to climb and chew and somewhere to be that is not your shoulder. Three arrangements, and most households end up with all three:

SetupWhat it does
A cage-top play areaThe cheapest useful hour of the day. The bird is out, in the room, occupied, and it goes back down without a chase
A free-standing play standPuts the bird somewhere in the room that is not the cage and not you. Essential for the independence work on page 18
A proofed roomWhere actual exercise happens. Windows covered, fans off, cables and skirting protected, other pets out. Page 11
Destruction is the point, not the problem Cockatoos destroy things. It is normal behavior, not damage, and a bird with nothing sanctioned to destroy will pick your furniture, the cage, or itself. Provide untreated softwood, cardboard, palm and yucca, paper, and cheap consumable toys in constant supply, and treat the whole category as an ongoing running cost. The good outcome is that everything you provide gets wrecked. A toy that survives a month has failed.

Dishes, water and the floor

  • Stainless steel dishes, ideally on swing-out feeders you can service without opening the main door. None of them under a perch
  • Water changed at least daily, and again whenever it is fouled, which with a bird that dunks food is often twice
  • Plain paper on the tray, changed daily, so you can read the droppings. Page 28
  • Hard floor under the cage, or a washable mat. Dust, shredded wood, dropped food and droppings go a surprising distance
What to leave out No grit: cockatoos hull seed before swallowing and grit causes obstruction. No happy huts, tents, nest boxes or any dark enclosed cavity, which are a direct hormonal trigger: page 22. Nothing galvanized, and nothing with zinc-plated clips or quick links. No frayed rope or loose fabric. And no toy a beak this strong can reduce to a swallowable fragment of metal or hard plastic.
Buy the stand at the same time as the cage Owners routinely buy the cage first and the stand a year later, after the bird has decided the only acceptable place to be is on a person. Having somewhere else that is genuinely good to stand, from week one, is most of the independence problem solved before it starts.

Light, sleep & the 10 to 12 hour rule

In this species, sleep is not a comfort question. It is the cheapest treatment for screaming there is.

A cockatoo needs 10 to 12 hours of genuine darkness and quiet every night. Not a dim room with the television on in the corner. Sleep deprivation in this species measurably worsens screaming and other behavioral issues, and it is one of the more common invisible causes of behavioral problems in pet parrots generally. It is also the first thing to check and the cheapest thing to fix.

Before you spend money on a behavior problem, count the hours A bird living in a family room that stays lit until midnight and wakes with the house at six is getting six hours. That bird will be irritable, loud and prone to plucking, and no toy, no supplement and no consultation fixes it while the sleep debt stands. If the main room cannot go dark and quiet on schedule, the answer is a separate sleep cage in a quiet room, moved to at the same time every night. This is standard practice with large parrots, not an extravagance.

Day length is also the hormone lever

Long days signal a breeding season. In a hen that means the laying cycle on page 22; in a cock it means territorial aggression, displaying, and a sharp rise in biting. The routine figure is 10 to 12 hours of darkness. The intervention figure, used under veterinary advice to break a laying cycle, is to limit light to 8 hours a day, which is 16 hours of darkness. Both are on page 22.

The routine, not just the hours

Cockatoos do better on a predictable day than an unpredictable one, and this is doubly true for the anxiety-driven behavior on page 18. A bird that knows when the lights go out, when you leave, when you come back and when the training session happens has far less to be anxious about than one for whom attention arrives at random. Fix the times before you fix anything else.

Cover partially, not completely A cover on three sides and part of the top gives security while leaving the bird able to see where it is. Blacking a cage out entirely suits some birds and panics others, and there is no way to know which yours is except by trying it and watching. If a bird thrashes in the dark, uncover a side and add a dim night light.

Feather dust, air quality & your own lungs

The page nobody mentions in the shop, and the one that decides whether the household copes.

Cockatoos are powder-down birds. Specialized feathers break down continuously into a fine keratin dust that the bird uses to condition its plumage, and a cockatoo produces a great deal of it. In practice that means a visible film on every horizontal surface in the room within a day, dust in electronics, and a haze in a sunbeam. It is not a sign of poor husbandry. It is what the bird is.

Bird fancier's lung Prolonged exposure to avian proteins in feather dust and dried droppings can cause hypersensitivity pneumonitis, known as bird fancier's lung. The NHLBI lists having pet birds in the home as a risk factor in its own right, not just occupational exposure, and puts most diagnoses between the ages of 50 and 70. It presents as breathlessness, a dry cough, and a flu-like illness with fever, chills and aching muscles or joints, with persistent bronchitis and weight loss over a longer course. The serious form is progressive lung fibrosis, and the treatment is steroids plus removing the exposure, which for a pet owner can mean rehoming the bird.

This is rare, and it is not a reason not to keep a cockatoo. It is a reason to control the dust properly from day one rather than after symptoms start, and a reason to tell your doctor you keep a parrot if you develop an unexplained cough. Anyone in the house who is asthmatic, immunocompromised, or has existing lung disease should weigh this species specifically.

The dust is also a disease route

PBFD is shed in high concentration in feather dander and dust, which is precisely why a quarantine has to be a separate room, ideally not on the same air, and not a corner of the same one. Page 29. It is also why a glossy, flaking or unusually shiny beak matters as a sign in this family: the beak looks that way because the powder down that normally dulls it has failed. Page 25.

Buy the purifier with the cage Not after the first month, when the dust has already been through the house. It belongs in the setup budget on page 34 alongside the cage and the stand, and it is the piece of equipment cockatoo owners most consistently say they should have bought sooner.

Household hazards & bird-proofing

A bird's lungs make ordinary household air lethal, and this beak reaches things a small parrot cannot.
PTFE: the one that kills with no warning Nonstick cookware releases polytetrafluoroethylene gas when overheated to about 536°F (280°C), which an empty or forgotten pan reaches in minutes. The gas causes direct caustic damage to the lung, and the Merck Veterinary Manual notes that acute death is often the only clinical sign. The fumes travel, so a bird in another room is not safe.

Cookware is not the only source. Merck also lists irons and ironing board covers, some self-cleaning ovens, heating elements in some reverse-cycle heat pumps, and some heat lamps. If a bird lives in the house, replace nonstick pans with stainless steel or cast iron, never run an oven's self-clean cycle, and keep the cage out of the kitchen entirely.

And the hazard that runs the other way

A large cockatoo can inflict a bite that needs stitches, and it can destroy woodwork, a laptop or a set of blinds in the time it takes to answer the door. Bird-proofing for this species means protecting the room from the bird as much as the bird from the room. Page 17.

The pre-flight check Windows and doors shut. Blinds partly drawn. Ceiling fan off. Hob off and pans away. Toilet lids down and sinks empty. Other pets out of the room. Candles out. Cables covered. Do it in the same order every time until it is automatic, and do it even on the day you are only letting the bird out for five minutes.

Section 03 · Diet

Diet: pellets, seed & converting a seed eater

One ratio worth memorizing, and the disease list that follows from getting it wrong.

VCA gives the target as approximately 75 to 80% formulated pellets, with fresh fruit and vegetables making up no more than 20 to 40% of the daily diet, and seed as a small minority rather than the base of it. That is the whole prescription, and it is the same figure across every large parrot.

A seed-heavy diet is the central feeding mistake in this species It leads directly to obesity, fatty liver disease and vitamin A deficiency, and high-fat diets are specifically linked to the lipomas that show up so often in overweight cockatoos. Page 26 is the consequence page. Sunflower seeds and peanuts are the worst offenders, and a bird left to choose eats them and leaves everything else, so the diet the bird actually consumes is worse than the one in the bowl.

Portions, which are smaller than they look

VCA offers a mental picture worth keeping: a small handful of food for a cockatoo is roughly a dinner-plate portion for a person. Over-portioning is easy and invisible, and it is how a bird on a correct diet still gets fat. Offer fresh food daily, take the fresh part out after about two hours before it spoils, and clean both dishes every day. Fresh water is always available.

Converting a seed eater

Do it gradually, and expect it to take days, weeks or months. Nothing here is a hunger strike. Pick one method or run several together:

MethodHow
Morning firstPellets alone first thing, when the bird is hungriest. Seed goes back in later
Mix and shiftMix pellets into the seed and reduce the seed share over weeks
Grind it inGrind pellets to powder over a moist food, then increase the chunk size gradually
Foraged, not offeredThis species' particular advantage. A pellet hidden inside a wrapped parcel is worth investigating in a way a pellet in a bowl is not. Page 15
Eat it in front of the birdCockatoos are intensely social feeders. Visibly eating the same food yourself converts more birds than any product

Withdraw seed only once the bird is reliably eating pellets and produce, and weigh it through the change, on the scale from page 21. A bird losing weight is not converting, it is starving, and that is the only failure mode that matters here.

Why this species can convert more easily than most Cockatoos are exceptionally curious and exceptionally food-motivated, which is normally a nuisance and here is an asset. A new pellet presented as a puzzle, wrapped in paper or buried in a foraging box, gets investigated. The same pellet in a dish gets ignored. Use the intelligence rather than fighting it.

Foraging: filling the six-hour gap

Wild parrots spend up to six hours a day getting food. A full bowl takes minutes. That gap is the problem.

Wild parrots spend up to six hours a day searching for, selecting and manipulating food. A captive bird with a full dish is finished in minutes. That is not a small difference in daily routine, it is most of the bird's waking behaviour missing, and something fills the space: in this species it is screaming, destruction and plucking.

The evidence points the same way it does for the training on page 16. Increased foraging opportunity has repeatedly been found to improve feather condition in parrots, and in young Amazon parrots foraging opportunity and increased physical complexity both prevented and reduced psychogenic feather picking. It is one of the stronger results in bird welfare.

The rule: nothing in a bowl Not "some food in toys". Aim for the whole daily ration delivered through work, across multiple sites in the cage and the room, so the bird has to search as well as solve. A bowl of pellets in the corner undoes everything else on this page, because a bird that can eat for free will.

How to start, and how to progress

  1. Stage one: visible. A bird that has only ever been bowl-fed does not know food can be inside a device. Start with food sitting openly on top of a foraging toy, or lightly covered by a single sheet of paper
  2. Stage two: wrapped. Food in paper parcels, in cardboard tubes, in a paper cup taped shut. Cheap, endless, and destroyed in the process, which is the point
  3. Stage three: contained. Purpose-made large-parrot foraging toys, rated for a beak this strong, and a foraging box the bird has to dig through
  4. Stage four: distributed. Several sites, some empty, so the bird searches rather than goes straight to the one place food lives
  5. Then rotate the presentation constantly. A cockatoo solves a puzzle once and after that it is a container
Watch intake while you make it harder Do not make the first foraging toy hard. A bird that cannot solve a device eats less rather than working harder, and in a species that is easy to over-condition it is also easy to under-feed by accident. Weigh weekly through any change to how food is delivered, and step the difficulty back if the weight moves. Page 21.

Improvised, which is most of it

  • Pellets and vegetables inside paper parcels, twisted shut, hung from the cage roof
  • A cardboard box of shredded paper with food buried through it
  • Food threaded on a stainless skewer between blocks of untreated wood
  • Whole vegetables woven through the bars so they have to be worked loose
  • A paper cup taped shut with a nut inside. This costs nothing and occupies a cockatoo for a genuinely useful stretch
  • Nuts in the shell, which are the original foraging toy
Budget destruction as a running cost Everything in the list above is consumed. That is the system working, not waste, and it is why the toy line on page 34 is bigger than the food line. Cardboard, paper, untreated softwood and palm are all effectively free, which is what makes a proper foraging routine affordable at this scale.

Training, and why it beat everything else

The most useful research result in this book, and it is quietly uncomfortable.
What the study found An assessment and treatment programme for feather plucking in sulphur-crested cockatoos compared medication, socialization, training sessions and feeding enrichment, and checked the results against behavioural observation, feather condition scoring and corticosterone levels. Training sessions were the most successful treatment. The authors attributed that to the social attention and mental stimulation the sessions provided, rather than to anything about the specific behaviours being taught.

That is not a result about tricks. It says the most effective intervention available for the signature problem of this species is structured time you personally spend with the bird, which is exactly the thing most owners have least of and no product can replace. So protect the sessions when the week gets busy, ahead of almost anything else in the routine.

Handling, step-up, bites & sexual maturity

Including the change at five to seven years old that catches owners of a sweet bird completely off guard.

The mechanics

  • Offer a forearm or a flat hand, not a single finger, and let the bird choose to step
  • A handheld perch is standard practice with a bird that bites, and using one is not a failure. Target training on page 16 keeps hands away from the beak
  • Never force it. A hand pushed at a cockatoo's chest is a hand it learns to bite. If the bird will not step up, something is wrong with the moment, not with the bird
  • Never chase a loose bird, and never grab one. Both cost trust that takes months to rebuild
Head and neck only Stroking the back, under the wings, or anywhere near the vent mimics a mate. In a cockatoo it triggers hormonal behaviour, which drives both the aggression on this page and the egg problems on page 22, and it is one of the classic first-timer mistakes. This applies to cocks as well as hens, and it applies to the cuddling this species is bought for. Scratch the head. Leave the body alone.

Bites

Large cockatoos bite more readily and with less restraint than many other parrots, and a bite from a bird this size can require stitches. That is a reason to read the bird, not to fear it. Almost every bite follows a warning that was missed.

Over-bonding, separation anxiety & independence

The defining problem of this species, and it is created in the first few weeks by owners doing what feels loving.

Cockatoos form unusually intense bonds with their people. In the wild they maintain near-constant physical contact with a mate and a flock, and that trait carries straight into a house as an expectation of the same from you. This is why they are described as velcro birds, and why a cockatoo that gets what it needs can be an extraordinary companion. It is also why the failure mode is so severe: an over-bonded cockatoo develops genuine separation anxiety when left alone, even briefly, and screams and plucks when the person it has chosen leaves the room.

The biggest first-timer mistake Over-bonding early on. Spending intense one-on-one time in the first weeks, because the bird is new and adorable and available, cements an anxious dependency rather than a healthy attachment. The bird learns that constant contact with one person is the normal state of the world, and then everything that is not that becomes distress. What feels like the most loving possible start is the thing that produces the screaming at year two.

Screaming, noise & the household reality

Some of it is normal and permanent. The rest is a message, and there is a specific way to answer it.

In the wild, cockatoos use loud, far-carrying contact calls to locate flock members across dense canopy. It is a functional communication tool, not noise for its own sake, and it does not switch off because the bird lives indoors. A cockatoo separated from its flock, which is your household, in another room, or one whose social needs are not being met through the day, escalates contact calling because that is precisely what it evolved to do when isolated.

What is normal and will not change Brief bouts of loud contact calling around dawn and dusk are normal cockatoo behaviour, mirroring when wild flocks are most vocal. Accept it. Cockatoos are loud by design and quieting one is not an achievable goal, only a source of frustration for both of you. What is not normal is prolonged, escalating screaming through the day, and that usually signals unmet social or enrichment needs rather than a fixed habit.

Wing clipping & flight

You will be asked in week one, usually by whoever is selling the trim. Here is both sides, honestly.

Wing clipping means shortening some primary flight feathers so the bird cannot gain height. It is not permanent: cut feathers are dead structures and are replaced at the next molt, so a clip lasts one molt cycle. It is also not a behavior tool. A clipped cockatoo is not tamer, calmer, quieter or less hormonal than a flighted one, and clipping does not reduce screaming, plucking or biting. It is a bird that cannot fly.

The case for clippingThe case against
Removes the escape risk from an open door in a house of several peopleThe same risk is removed by closing the door. A clipped bird caught by wind outdoors can still travel, and it cannot fly back
Removes the collision risk with windows and mirrors in a new homeFlight is this bird's main exercise, and a heavy species that cannot fly gains weight. Obesity, lipomas and fatty liver are all on page 26

Blood feathers, and why this is not a haircut A growing feather has a live blood supply in its shaft and looks dark and full at the base. Cut one and it bleeds, and on a large parrot it can bleed a great deal. Only fully mature feathers are ever trimmed, and knowing the difference is the entire skill. Merck's method is four to seven of the outermost primaries on both wings, cut below the coverts that overlay them, with the secondaries closer to the body left alone, because those are what let a bird slow down and land. If a bird is clipped, an avian vet or an experienced groomer does it and you watch. Never attempt a first clip yourself from a video, and never clip a bird that is molting.

If it is done, what a correct clip leaves

A correct trim leaves the bird able to glide down to the floor under control from a standing perch, symmetrically on both wings so it does not spiral into a wall. It does not leave a bird that drops like a stone, and it never removes every primary. On a bird this heavy, an over-clip is genuinely dangerous. Ask for a light, symmetrical trim, and watch the bird land before you leave the clinic.

The honest recommendation

For a household that can properly proof a room, a flighted cockatoo is the better outcome, and the work goes into the pre-flight check on page 11 rather than into the bird. The exercise matters more in a large heavy parrot than in a small one, and a recall trained per page 16 solves most of what clipping is meant to solve. Clipping is defensible in a specific, temporary situation, and in those cases it is a decision to revisit at the next molt rather than a standing policy.

Train the recall either way A cockatoo that comes to your hand on cue is safer than a clipped one, because you can retrieve it from anywhere in the room without a chase. A nut, a short distance, a consistent cue, twice a day. Alongside the station cue, it is the most useful pair of behaviours in this book.

Sexing, weight & body condition

The iris trick that works in most white cockatoos, and the number to track weekly.

Sexing

In most of the white Cacatua species, including the umbrella and the Moluccan, mature birds can often be told apart by iris colour: the male's is very dark brown to black, the female's a reddish or chestnut brown, most visible in good light. It is a useful field feature, and it is not reliable in every bird, species or lighting condition. LafeberVet gives the reason it fails in a young bird: immature cockatoos have a pale grey iris, so the cue simply is not there yet.

Where it matters, and it matters for everything on page 22, a DNA test on a blood spot or a plucked feather is the actual answer, and it costs a fraction of one vet visit. Do not make a breeding, pairing or hormone-management decision on iris colour alone.

It matters because a hen can lay without a mate, and chronic laying is serious, expensive and preventable. An owner who assumes their bird is a cock is an owner who does not recognise a laying cycle starting. Page 22.

Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

A hen lays because the environment told her to. Every lever on this page is one you control.

A cockatoo hen will lay with no male present. She does it because something in her environment reads as a breeding season, and every clutch costs her calcium she draws out of her own skeleton. Chronic egg laying, clutch after clutch out of season, ends in depletion, weakness and egg binding. Hormonal behaviour in a cock is the same physiology producing aggression, displaying and biting instead, and it responds to the same levers.

Egg binding, which is the emergency

A hen unable to pass an egg is a same-day emergency, not something to monitor. Signs, roughly in order: weakness and sitting low rather than perching; straining as if to pass a dropping, repeatedly and with nothing produced; a swollen or firm lower abdomen; fluffed feathers, wide stance and laboured breathing; sometimes an egg visible at the vent or tissue prolapsing; and in late cases leg weakness or paralysis from the egg pressing on the nerves. VCA adds a marker that stands on its own before the rest of that list appears: no egg passed in 48 hours by a hen you know is laying.

The causes are low calcium above everything else, because calcium is what makes the oviduct contract, plus obesity and inactivity, chronic laying, a first or an elderly clutch, and an oversized or misshapen egg. Seed diets are deficient in calcium, selenium, and vitamins D and E, which is why this page runs back to page 12. Treatment is supplemental heat, fluids, calcium and vitamin D3, then oxytocin, then manual or surgical removal.

Meanwhile: carrier, dark and quiet, warmed to 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C), inside LafeberVet's 80 to 90°F range under supplemental heat where VCA puts a recovering bird at 75 to 80°F. Back off at flat sleek feathers, wings held out or open-mouth breathing, which mean too hot. Never massage the abdomen or pull at a visible egg.

Support a laying hen, and know when to ask for more Cuttlebone and a mineral block permanently available; a pellet diet carrying vitamin D3 so the calcium can be used; extra protein while laying; daily weighing. If she keeps laying after every environmental lever has been pulled, that is a veterinary conversation, not another month of adjustments. Hormone therapy by injection or implant exists for exactly this, and it is better used before she is depleted than after the first emergency.

Feather-destructive behavior, molt & the differential

Particularly prevalent in this species, more so than in most other parrots. And it is a medical question first.
A vet visit comes first, every time Medical triggers including Giardia and PBFD can cause plucking, and a bird in pain from an internal problem plucks over the site. Assuming a behavioural cause before an avian vet has ruled out a medical one is the commonest and costliest mistake made with this species. Fix a real medical cause and the behaviour often stops; rearrange the cage instead and you lose months.

The three-way differential owners get wrong

What it isWhat you see
Normal moltSymmetrical, staggered, matching feathers on each wing, new feathers pushing up before the old drop, whole feathers found intact on the floor, head included, and the bird otherwise well. Merck's picture is a full molt at least once a year, with many birds also running a smaller partial molt about six months after. A molting cockatoo is quieter, hungrier, touchier, and covered in sore pin feathers. Bathe more, handle less, offer more protein
Feather-destructive behaviorAsymmetrical, confined to where the bird can reach, head feathers untouched, chewed or snapped shafts left behind. Over-preening to bare skin, and in serious cases self-mutilation of the skin itself
PBFDFeathers coming in wrong rather than falling out: stunted, club-shaped or pinched off, worsening at each successive molt, often with a glossy or flaking beak. Page 25

A bird that has lost head feathers has not plucked them, because it cannot reach. That single observation separates disease from behavior faster than anything else, and it is the first thing to check.

Once medical causes are excluded

The behavioural drivers are stress, boredom, loneliness, separation anxiety, and poor early socialization, and management means addressing the underlying social and emotional needs rather than treating the symptom. In rough order of value:

  1. Daily training sessions. The tested winner in this species. Page 16
  2. All food through foraging, across multiple sites. Increased foraging opportunity repeatedly improves feather condition in parrots. Page 15
  3. Constant destructible material. A bird with nothing sanctioned to destroy destroys itself. Page 8
  4. Sleep. Count the actual hours of dark. Page 9
  5. Independence work, if the plucking follows the primary person leaving the room. Page 18
  6. A predictable routine, because unpredictable attention is itself a stressor
  7. Bathing, two or three times a week, which also helps the dust on page 10
Catch it early, because established plucking is different Once established, plucking becomes self-soothing and habitual and can continue after the original cause is resolved, which is why prevention is disproportionately more effective than treatment. Watch for time spent preening one area beyond normal grooming, small bald patches on the chest or under the wings developing gradually, and any increase in agitation that coincides with a change in routine, a move, or reduced attention. Act at those, not at the bald patch.
When to bring in a behaviorist If it escalates after several weeks of consistent change, or if the bird has broken skin or drawn blood, do two things in parallel: an avian vet visit to rule out a medical cause, and a referral to a certified avian behaviorist once the bird is cleared. Behaviorists work with entrenched cases far more effectively than general advice can, and waiting makes the behaviour more deeply ingrained.

PBFD & viral disease

First described in Australian cockatoos, incurable, and the reason page 29 exists.

Psittacine beak and feather disease is caused by a circovirus and affects over 50 species of bird, concentrated in parrots from Australia, Africa, New Zealand and Indonesia, which puts every cockatoo squarely in the susceptible group. It was first described in Australian cockatoos, and white cockatoo species are specifically noted for the beak malformations it causes. It is rare in New World parrots such as macaws, amazons and conures. Infection can occur at any age but is most common in birds under three years old.

FormWhoWhat happens
PeracuteNewly hatched chicksDeath, often before any feather sign
AcuteYoung birds growing their first feathersRapid, severe feather abnormality during that development
ChronicOlder birdsProgressive abnormality worsening at each successive molt. This is how most owners meet it

Signs

  • Feathers that come in stunted, club-shaped or pinched off at the shaft, with premature molting and eventually bald areas
  • Loss of the normal powder down, which is why a glossy-looking beak is a sign. In a healthy cockatoo the powder down dusts and dulls the beak; when the virus destroys the cells producing it, the beak shines. Page 10
  • Beak deformity: in cockatoos, elongated, excessively flaky or shiny, and breaking easily
  • In African greys, by contrast, generalized sickness and anemia with no feather or beak signs at all, which is worth knowing if you keep both
There is no treatment and no cure Most affected birds die of secondary infections within months to years. Transmission is through the mouth, nose or cloaca, and the virus is shed heavily in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust, which is exactly why quarantine has to mean separate air rather than a separate corner. Incubation runs from weeks to years, so a bird can look perfectly well and be shedding. Diagnosis is a blood test or an oral swab for the viral DNA, or a skin and feather biopsy.

Avian polyomavirus

The second virus a quarantine panel is designed to catch. It is most associated with budgerigars, where it is called budgerigar fledgling disease, but it infects other psittacines including cockatoos. The dangerous form is in unweaned chicks, causing crop stasis, haemorrhage under the skin and death within a day or two, so it is largely a breeder and shop problem rather than a threat to a settled adult. A vaccine exists and is used in aviaries. Its relevance to a pet buyer is the same as PBFD's: it is on the panel, and it is a reason not to buy from a place with sick birds in the next cage.

What prevention actually is Quarantine any new bird before it meets an established one, and test rather than merely wait. That is genuinely important prevention rather than excessive caution, and it is the only control available for a disease with no treatment. Page 29 is the procedure.

Obesity, lipomas & fatty liver

One cause, three diagnoses, and all of them run back to the food bowl.

Obesity

Merck's working figure for obesity in a bird is roughly 20% over ideal weight, and Merck names galahs, alongside macaws, Amazons and Quaker parrots, as species prone to it. That is a cockatoo on the list. Fat accumulating in the abdomen presses on the air sacs, so the presenting complaint is often breathlessness after mild exertion, or lameness, rather than anything an owner would call a weight problem. Under a thick white plumage you will not see it. You will see it on the scale. Page 21.

Lipomas

Benign fatty tumors, common in overweight cockatoos and tied directly to high-fat, seed-heavy diets. They typically appear as soft masses on the chest or abdomen. Birds actually eating their pellets develop them far less often. Management is veterinary guidance plus genuine diet correction, and surgical removal is sometimes needed when a mass interferes with movement, but the diet is the actual treatment.

Hepatic lipidosis

Fatty liver disease, from the same seed-heavy overfeeding pattern. It is quiet for a long time and then it is not.

SignDetail

Do not crash-diet a fat bird Rapid weight loss mobilizes fat into a liver that is already struggling and can kill the bird. Weight reduction in a parrot is a supervised project run over months with an avian vet: correct the pellet share, increase vegetables, cut nuts and seed to earned training rewards, and add real exercise through flight and out-of-cage time. Vitamin A deficiency belongs on the same page The other consequence of a seed diet, and the commonest nutritional disease in pet birds. Psittacosis, respiratory disease, metals & injuries

The rest of the health section, including the one that passes to people.

Breathing trouble is always urgent

A bird's respiratory system has very little reserve, and there is no mild version of avian breathing difficulty. Learn what normal looks like while the bird is well: 15 to 40 breaths a minute at rest, with the tail perfectly still. Tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, a clicking or rasping sound, a change or loss of voice, discharge at the nostrils, or sitting fluffed with the neck stretched up are all same-day signs.

Psittacosis

Avian chlamydiosis, caused by Chlamydia psittaci, and it is zoonotic. A bird can carry it with no signs at all, shedding it in droppings and respiratory secretions, which is why a new bird is tested rather than trusted. When it shows, expect respiratory signs, diarrhea, weakness, an enlarged liver, and lime-green droppings as the classic pointer. Treatment is doxycycline: 45 days has historically been the recommendation, with shorter courses of 21 to 30 days also potentially effective. Finish the course, because a partial one produces a carrier.

In people Illness typically begins 5 to 14 days after exposure, with an abrupt fever, chills, headache, malaise and muscle aches, and a dry cough that can come with breathlessness or chest tightness. It responds to antibiotics. If anyone in the house develops a flu-like illness with a dry cough and there is a sick bird at home, tell the doctor there is a bird. They will not think of it. Page 10 covers the separate, non-infectious lung problem this species can cause.

Reading droppings & hydration

The cheapest daily health check you own, and the reason the tray is lined with plain paper.

A bird dropping has three components, and telling them apart is the whole skill. The feces is the solid, formed, coiled part. The urates are the chalky white part, uric acid crystals that let a bird excrete nitrogen using almost no water. The urine is the clear liquid halo around the outside. Each can change independently, and which one changed tells you where the problem is.

What you seeWhat it usually means

Brown feces, formed, in a pellet-fed birdNormal. Expect the colour to change during a diet conversion and do not panic at it

Unformed feces, no solid partTrue diarrhea, much less common than owners think and much more serious. Vet

The 24-hour rule Learn your own bird's normal droppings, because once you know them anything else is abnormal by definition. A single odd dropping is not an emergency; you changed the food, or it ate a blueberry. Droppings that stay abnormal for more than about 24 hours are a vet visit. Photograph the paper before you throw it away and take the photo with you.

Hydration

A dehydrated bird has dry, tacky mouth tissue, dull sunken eyes, skin over the keel that stays tented when gently lifted, and urates that go thick and yellow. A large parrot on a pellet diet drinks noticeably more than one eating a lot of fresh food, so a change in water consumption is only meaningful against what that bird normally does. Change the water daily as a minimum, and again whenever it is fouled, which with a bird that dunks food is often twice.

Line the tray so you can read it Plain paper, changed daily, with the grate out if the bird will not stand on the tray. Corncob, sand and wood chip all hide droppings, absorb them, and grow mold underneath, which turns the most informative surface in the cage into the least. It also makes it much harder to notice the day the droppings stop.

Quarantine & adding a second bird

Skipping quarantine is how PBFD spreads. It is also the only control there is.

A new bird can carry PBFD, polyomavirus or chlamydia with no visible sign at all, shedding all three in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust. Quarantine is not about whether the new bird looks well. It looks well precisely because that is how these diseases work, and PBFD's incubation runs from weeks to years.

30 days minimum, and test Public health guidance for birds exposed to other birds at shows, sales or events is to quarantine at least 30 days and test before returning or adding them to a group. Take that as the floor. In a household with an existing bird, Merck's figure for introducing a bird to an aviary or an established group is 90 days with testing, because an infected adult can shed polyomavirus intermittently for up to 90 days before clearing it. There is no version of this that takes a weekend.

How to actually run it

Then the introduction

  1. Cages in sight of each other, several feet apart, for at least a week. Let them see and call with no contact
  2. Move them closer gradually, watching for interest rather than lunging at the bars
  3. Neutral ground first. The first physical meeting happens on a play stand or a table, never inside either cage. A cockatoo defends its cage seriously and a bite from this beak lands differently between birds than between budgies
  4. Short supervised sessions, extended as they get boring. Normal negotiation is beak fencing, chasing and noise; a real fight is one bird pinning another or gripping a foot. Separate for that, not the noise
  5. Separate cages permanently is the default with large cockatoos. Two birds who live beside each other and call back and forth get most of the social benefit with none of the risk
A second cockatoo is not a solution to the first one's needs Owners of an over-bonded, screaming cockatoo frequently consider a second bird as a fix. It rarely is. You now have two birds needing 4 to 6 hours each, two cages, two vet bills, and a real possibility that they dislike each other or that one is injured. The interventions on pages 16, 15 and 18 address the actual problem. Get a second cockatoo because you want one and can serve one, not as a treatment.
Quarantine even if it is your only bird A 30-day settling period in a quiet room, with a vet exam at the start of it, is good for a single new cockatoo too. It catches problems while your expectations are still forming, gives the bird the low-pressure start page 18 asks for, and stops the intense first-weeks over-bonding that causes so much trouble later.

Legal status, CITES & state rules

Broadly legal, with real exceptions that depend on which cockatoo you own.

In the great majority of US states a pet cockatoo raises no legal question at all. Idaho and Colorado clear pet birds as a category outright; Oklahoma's noncontrolled exotic-species exemption names cockatoos specifically; New Hampshire and Montana name the parrot order or family as unregulated. Where a state does restrict a parrot, it is almost always the monk parakeet, named in California, Connecticut, New Jersey and Virginia among others, and every cockatoo is outside those lists.

CITES does not make owning a cockatoo illegal CITES governs international trade, not domestic ownership, so an Appendix I listing does not by itself make a captive-bred bird illegal to keep. It matters here for two reasons: a state can write its own rule that keys off the listing, and moving a bird across an international border is a serious permitting exercise rather than a formality.

The sixty-year bird

Succession and rehoming, written down now, while nothing has gone wrong.

The veterinary figure for cockatoo life span is 30 to 45 years, with Moluccans documented up to about 70. A cockatoo bought by a forty-year-old will very plausibly outlive them, and one bought by a sixty-year-old almost certainly will. That is a planning problem, and it is the most neglected part of owning this species.

The default outcome, if you do nothing The bird goes to whoever is sorting out your affairs, at the worst possible week, having just lost the only person it was bonded to. Rescues are full, and cockatoos are the bird they take in most. An unplanned cockatoo gets passed through several homes, and each move makes the next one harder.

Power outages, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in an outage is not the cold. It is what people light to fix the cold.
The rule that inverts from every other pet A reptile in a power cut is a temperature problem. A cockatoo in a power cut is a fumes problem. Candles, kerosene and propane heaters, a gas hob left on for warmth, and a wood stove that smokes are all far more dangerous to a bird than a cold night is. A generator runs outside and at least 20 feet from any door, window or vent, the CDC's figure, and the CPSC rules out running one in or near an enclosed space, so an open garage door does not qualify. Keep a working carbon monoxide detector in the house. If the power goes out, the first thing you decide is what you are not going to light in the bird's air.

If the power goes out

  • Keep feeding. Food and water stay available whatever else is happening
  • Move the cage to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors, and cover three sides with blankets to hold warmth while leaving airflow at the front
  • Body heat beats fire. A carrier under a blanket in an occupied room is safer than any flame
  • Ventilate anything you do burn. If a heater has to run, it runs in a different room with the door closed and a window cracked, and the bird is not in that room
  • The purifier being off is not an emergency, but the dust builds fast. Open a window in another room and damp-wipe more often until the power is back. Page 10
