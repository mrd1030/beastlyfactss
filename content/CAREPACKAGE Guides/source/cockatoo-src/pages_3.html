Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Wing clipping & flight

You will be asked in week one, usually by whoever is selling the trim. Here is both sides, honestly.

Wing clipping means shortening some primary flight feathers so the bird cannot gain height. It is not permanent: cut feathers are dead structures and are replaced at the next molt, so a clip lasts one molt cycle. It is also not a behavior tool. A clipped cockatoo is not tamer, calmer, quieter or less hormonal than a flighted one, and clipping does not reduce screaming, plucking or biting. It is a bird that cannot fly.

The case for clippingThe case against
Removes the escape risk from an open door in a house of several peopleThe same risk is removed by closing the door. A clipped bird caught by wind outdoors can still travel, and it cannot fly back
Removes the collision risk with windows and mirrors in a new homeFlight is this bird's main exercise, and a heavy species that cannot fly gains weight. Obesity, lipomas and fatty liver are all on page {{P:liver}}
Makes early handling of an unfamiliar adult bird more manageableHandling built on a bird that cannot leave is compliance, not trust. Page {{P:training}} builds the real thing, and it works on a flighted bird
Reduces the risk in a house with a hob, a dog or small childrenA clipped cockatoo lands hard. Keel and leg injuries from bad landings are common, especially on hard floors, and this is a heavy bird
Blood feathers, and why this is not a haircut A growing feather has a live blood supply in its shaft and looks dark and full at the base. Cut one and it bleeds, and on a large parrot it can bleed a great deal. Only fully mature feathers are ever trimmed, and knowing the difference is the entire skill. If a bird is clipped, an avian vet or an experienced groomer does it and you watch. Never attempt a first clip yourself from a video, and never clip a bird that is molting.

If it is done, what a correct clip leaves

A correct trim leaves the bird able to glide down to the floor under control from a standing perch, symmetrically on both wings so it does not spiral into a wall. It does not leave a bird that drops like a stone, and it never removes every primary. On a bird this heavy, an over-clip is genuinely dangerous. Ask for a light, symmetrical trim, and watch the bird land before you leave the clinic.

The honest recommendation

For a household that can properly proof a room, a flighted cockatoo is the better outcome, and the work goes into the pre-flight check on page {{P:hazards}} rather than into the bird. The exercise matters more in a large heavy parrot than in a small one, and a recall trained per page {{P:training}} solves most of what clipping is meant to solve. Clipping is defensible in a specific, temporary situation, and in those cases it is a decision to revisit at the next molt rather than a standing policy.

Train the recall either way A cockatoo that comes to your hand on cue is safer than a clipped one, because you can retrieve it from anywhere in the room without a chase. A nut, a short distance, a consistent cue, twice a day. Alongside the station cue, it is the most useful pair of behaviours in this book.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Sexing, weight & body condition

The iris trick that works in most white cockatoos, and the number to track weekly.

Sexing

In most of the white Cacatua species, including the umbrella and the Moluccan, mature birds can often be told apart by iris colour: the male's is very dark brown to black, the female's a reddish or chestnut brown, most visible in good light. It is a useful field feature, and it is not reliable in every bird, species or lighting condition, and it does not appear until the bird matures.

Where it matters, and it matters for everything on page {{P:eggs}}, a DNA test on a blood spot or a plucked feather is the actual answer, and it costs a fraction of one vet visit. Do not make a breeding, pairing or hormone-management decision on iris colour alone.

It matters because a hen can lay without a mate, and chronic laying is serious, expensive and preventable. An owner who assumes their bird is a cock is an owner who does not recognise a laying cycle starting. Page {{P:eggs}}.

Weight is the vital sign

A bird hides illness until it cannot, but it cannot hide a falling weight, and a heavily feathered white cockatoo hides body condition better than almost any bird kept. A digital scale reading in 1 g steps, with a perch fixed to it, is the most useful equipment in the house. Weigh at the same time each week, before the first feed, and log it on page {{P:ownerlog}}.

ReadingWhat it means
Goffin's about 350 g; umbrella 400 to 700 g; Moluccan and greater sulphur-crested up to about 880 gThe published ranges. Your own bird's stable number matters far more than where it falls in them
Week-to-week variation of a few gramsNormal
A steady fall across three weighingsInvestigate. In a converting bird, the conversion is failing. In a settled bird, it is a vet call
Roughly 10% below baselineCall the vet. In a 600 g bird that is 60 g, which is a great deal of muscle
Roughly 20% above idealMerck's working definition of obesity in a bird. Page {{P:liver}}

Feeling the keel

The keel is the ridge of breastbone running down the front of the bird. Rest a finger either side of it during a head scratch, weekly, so you know what your own bird feels like.

  • Sharp and prominent, muscle falling away either side: underweight, and in this species usually a sick bird rather than an underfed one
  • Ridge easily felt, muscle rounded and full to each side: what you want
  • Keel hard to find, chest convex, a groove down the centre: overweight, and you may also feel or see fat pads over the abdomen. Diet and flight, never a crash diet
Do not crash-diet a fat cockatoo Rapid weight loss mobilizes fat into a liver already under strain and can kill the bird. Weight reduction is a supervised, months-long project run with an avian vet: the correct pellet share, more vegetables, fewer nuts, far more exercise. Page {{P:liver}}.
Establish the baseline in the first month Weigh daily for the first two weeks and record it. That fortnight of numbers makes every later reading readable, and it is what owners wish they had when something looks slightly off in year six.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

A hen lays because the environment told her to. Every lever on this page is one you control.

A cockatoo hen will lay with no male present. She does it because something in her environment reads as a breeding season, and every clutch costs her calcium she draws out of her own skeleton. Chronic egg laying, clutch after clutch out of season, ends in depletion, weakness and egg binding. Hormonal behaviour in a cock is the same physiology producing aggression, displaying and biting instead, and it responds to the same levers.

The trigger list, and what to do about each

TriggerRemove it
Long daysThe main lever. 10 to 12 hours of darkness normally; to break a cycle, limit light to 8 hours a day under vet advice. Page {{P:light}}
Any dark enclosed cavityHappy hut, tent, nest box, a cardboard box, a cupboard she found, the space behind the sofa. All of it goes, and for this species that includes the snuggly things sold specifically for cockatoos
Petting the back, wings or ventHead and neck only, by everyone in the house. This is the hardest change to make in a species bought for cuddling, and it is the most important. Page {{P:handling}}
A mirror, or a bonded toyOut. To a lone bird a mirror is a mate
Soft nesting material and paper shreddingRestrict shredding during a hormonal period, and keep the tray liner out of reach
Rich, abundant, constant foodPlenty reads as a good season. Measure portions, keep nuts rare, and move food into foraging. Page {{P:foraging}}
A static territoryMoving the cage and rearranging perches disrupts a site she has decided is a nest
Do not remove the eggs as she lays them Pulling each egg tells the hen the clutch has failed and she lays a replacement, which is exactly how chronic laying starts. Leave the clutch and let her sit. A cockatoo clutch is typically 3 to 4 eggs and incubation runs 23 to 30 days, so a hen allowed to sit usually gives up after a few weeks and stops. Swap real eggs for dummy eggs if you are worried about them spoiling. Sitting on a clutch costs her far less than laying three more.

Egg binding, which is the emergency

A hen unable to pass an egg is a same-day emergency, not something to monitor. Signs, roughly in order: weakness and sitting low rather than perching; straining as if to pass a dropping, repeatedly and with nothing produced; a swollen or firm lower abdomen; fluffed feathers, wide stance and laboured breathing; sometimes an egg visible at the vent or tissue prolapsing; and in late cases leg weakness or paralysis from the egg pressing on the nerves.

The causes are low calcium above everything else, because calcium is what makes the oviduct contract, plus obesity and inactivity, chronic laying, a first or an elderly clutch, and an oversized or misshapen egg. Seed diets are deficient in calcium, selenium, and vitamins D and E, which is why this page runs back to page {{P:diet}}. Treatment is supplemental heat, fluids, calcium and vitamin D3, then oxytocin, then manual or surgical removal.

Support a laying hen, and know when to ask for more Cuttlebone and a mineral block permanently available; a pellet diet carrying vitamin D3 so the calcium can be used; extra protein while she is laying; and daily weighing. If she keeps laying after every environmental lever has been pulled, that is a veterinary conversation, not another month of adjustments. Hormone therapy by injection or implant exists for exactly this, and it is better used before she is depleted than after the first emergency.
Section 05 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & what to tell the vet

A bird that looks sick has been sick for a while. Under all that white feather, longer than usual.

Birds mask illness because in a flock a predator picks the weakest-looking animal. A large, densely feathered white cockatoo hides it particularly well, which is why the weekly weight on page {{P:sexing}} and the droppings check on page {{P:droppings}} matter more here than looking at the bird does.

Same-day call, no waiting Any difficulty breathing: tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, clicking or wheezing, or a change in voice. Bleeding from anywhere, including a broken blood feather, which on a bird this size can lose real volume. Sitting on the cage floor instead of perching. Straining in a hen: page {{P:eggs}}. Discharge from eyes, nostrils or mouth. Fluffed and unresponsive with both feet down. Seizures, one-sided weakness, or sudden collapse. A fall, a burn, a cat contact of any kind, or a suspected PTFE exposure.

Call within 24 hours

  • A sustained drop in appetite, especially alongside anything else here
  • Any change in droppings that persists beyond about 24 hours. Page {{P:droppings}}
  • Weight down roughly 10% from the bird's own baseline
  • New feather damage, bald patches, or obsessive preening of one area. Page {{P:plucking}}
  • Feathers coming in stunted, clubbed or pinched, or a beak that has gone glossy, flaky or overgrown. Page {{P:pbfd}}
  • Lameness, or unwillingness to bear weight on one leg
  • A sudden change in vocal or behavioural pattern in a previously stable bird, which can signal pain or illness rather than an emotional cause
  • Vomiting, which sticks to the face and chest feathers, as distinct from deliberate courtship regurgitation onto a toy or a hand

The normal numbers, for reference

Resting heart rateScales with size: about 147 bpm in a 500 g bird, about 127 in a 1,000 g bird

Find the vet before you need one, and check they take this species Avian practice is a specialty, and not every avian vet is set up for a bird that can take a finger off. Call in week one, ask specifically about large cockatoos, ask what their after-hours arrangement is, and write both numbers on page {{P:emergency}}. Add a certified avian behaviour consultant to the same card while you are at it.

Feather-destructive behavior, molt & the differential

Particularly prevalent in this species, more so than in most other parrots. And it is a medical question first.
A vet visit comes first, every time Medical triggers including Giardia and PBFD can cause plucking, and a bird in pain from an internal problem plucks over the site. Assuming a behavioural cause before an avian vet has ruled out a medical one is the commonest and costliest mistake made with this species. Fix a real medical cause and the behaviour often stops; rearrange the cage instead and you lose months.

The three-way differential owners get wrong

What it isWhat you see
Normal moltSymmetrical, staggered, matching feathers on each wing, new feathers pushing up before the old drop, whole feathers found intact on the floor, head included, and the bird otherwise well. A molting cockatoo is quieter, hungrier, touchier, and covered in sore pin feathers. Bathe more, handle less, offer more protein
Feather-destructive behaviorAsymmetrical, confined to where the bird can reach, head feathers untouched, chewed or snapped shafts left behind. Over-preening to bare skin, and in serious cases self-mutilation of the skin itself
PBFDFeathers coming in wrong rather than falling out: stunted, club-shaped or pinched off, worsening at each successive molt, often with a glossy or flaking beak. Page {{P:pbfd}}

A bird that has lost head feathers has not plucked them, because it cannot reach. That single observation separates disease from behavior faster than anything else, and it is the first thing to check.

Once medical causes are excluded

The behavioural drivers are stress, boredom, loneliness, separation anxiety, and poor early socialization, and management means addressing the underlying social and emotional needs rather than treating the symptom. In rough order of value:

  1. Daily training sessions. The tested winner in this species. Page {{P:training}}
  2. All food through foraging, across multiple sites. Increased foraging opportunity repeatedly improves feather condition in parrots. Page {{P:foraging}}
  3. Constant destructible material. A bird with nothing sanctioned to destroy destroys itself. Page {{P:furnishings}}
  4. Sleep. Count the actual hours of dark. Page {{P:light}}
  5. Independence work, if the plucking follows the primary person leaving the room. Page {{P:bonding}}
  6. A predictable routine, because unpredictable attention is itself a stressor
  7. Bathing, two or three times a week, which also helps the dust on page {{P:dust}}
Catch it early, because established plucking is different Once established, plucking becomes self-soothing and habitual and can continue after the original cause is resolved, which is why prevention is disproportionately more effective than treatment. Watch for time spent preening one area beyond normal grooming, small bald patches on the chest or under the wings developing gradually, and any increase in agitation that coincides with a change in routine, a move, or reduced attention. Act at those, not at the bald patch.
When to bring in a behaviorist If it escalates after several weeks of consistent change, or if the bird has broken skin or drawn blood, do two things in parallel: an avian vet visit to rule out a medical cause, and a referral to a certified avian behaviorist once the bird is cleared. Behaviorists work with entrenched cases far more effectively than general advice can, and waiting makes the behaviour more deeply ingrained.

PBFD & viral disease

First described in Australian cockatoos, incurable, and the reason page {{P:quarantine}} exists.

Psittacine beak and feather disease is caused by a circovirus and affects over 50 species of bird, concentrated in parrots from Australia, Africa, New Zealand and Indonesia, which puts every cockatoo squarely in the susceptible group. It was first described in Australian cockatoos, and white cockatoo species are specifically noted for the beak malformations it causes. It is rare in New World parrots such as macaws, amazons and conures. Infection can occur at any age but is most common in birds under three years old.

FormWhoWhat happens
PeracuteNewly hatched chicksDeath, often before any feather sign
AcuteYoung birds growing their first feathersRapid, severe feather abnormality during that development
ChronicOlder birdsProgressive abnormality worsening at each successive molt. This is how most owners meet it

Signs

  • Feathers that come in stunted, club-shaped or pinched off at the shaft, with premature molting and eventually bald areas
  • Loss of the normal powder down, which is why a glossy-looking beak is a sign. In a healthy cockatoo the powder down dusts and dulls the beak; when the virus destroys the cells producing it, the beak shines. Page {{P:dust}}
  • Beak deformity: in cockatoos, elongated, excessively flaky or shiny, and breaking easily
  • In African greys, by contrast, generalized sickness and anemia with no feather or beak signs at all, which is worth knowing if you keep both
There is no treatment and no cure Most affected birds die of secondary infections within months to years. Transmission is through the mouth, nose or cloaca, and the virus is shed heavily in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust, which is exactly why quarantine has to mean separate air rather than a separate corner. Incubation runs from weeks to years, so a bird can look perfectly well and be shedding. Diagnosis is a blood test or an oral swab for the viral DNA, or a skin and feather biopsy.

Avian polyomavirus

The second virus a quarantine panel is designed to catch. It is most associated with budgerigars, where it is called budgerigar fledgling disease, but it infects other psittacines including cockatoos. The dangerous form is in unweaned chicks, causing crop stasis, haemorrhage under the skin and death within a day or two, so it is largely a breeder and shop problem rather than a threat to a settled adult. A vaccine exists and is used in aviaries. Its relevance to a pet buyer is the same as PBFD's: it is on the panel, and it is a reason not to buy from a place with sick birds in the next cage.

What prevention actually is Quarantine any new bird before it meets an established one, and test rather than merely wait. That is genuinely important prevention rather than excessive caution, and it is the only control available for a disease with no treatment. Page {{P:quarantine}} is the procedure.

Obesity, lipomas & fatty liver

One cause, three diagnoses, and all of them run back to the food bowl.

Obesity

Merck's working figure for obesity in a bird is roughly 20% over ideal weight, and Merck names galahs, alongside macaws, Amazons and Quaker parrots, as species prone to it. That is a cockatoo on the list. Fat accumulating in the abdomen presses on the air sacs, so the presenting complaint is often breathlessness after mild exertion, or lameness, rather than anything an owner would call a weight problem. Under a thick white plumage you will not see it. You will see it on the scale. Page {{P:sexing}}.

Lipomas

Benign fatty tumors, common in overweight cockatoos and tied directly to high-fat, seed-heavy diets. They typically appear as soft masses on the chest or abdomen. Birds actually eating their pellets develop them far less often. Management is veterinary guidance plus genuine diet correction, and surgical removal is sometimes needed when a mass interferes with movement, but the diet is the actual treatment.

Hepatic lipidosis

Fatty liver disease, from the same seed-heavy overfeeding pattern. It is quiet for a long time and then it is not.

Do not crash-diet a fat bird Rapid weight loss mobilizes fat into a liver that is already struggling and can kill the bird. Weight reduction in a parrot is a supervised project run over months with an avian vet: correct the pellet share, increase vegetables, cut nuts and seed to earned training rewards, and add real exercise through flight and out-of-cage time. It is slow on purpose.
Vitamin A deficiency belongs on the same page The other consequence of a seed diet, and the commonest nutritional disease in pet birds. Merck notes that even a diet of half seed and half pellets leaves a bird deficient. It shows as nasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eye, white plaques in the mouth, poor feather quality, feather picking and excessive drinking, and it weakens the airway lining, which is why so many respiratory cases on page {{P:minor}} are nutritional underneath.
The whole prevention plan, in one line Pellets at 75 to 80%, dark vegetables daily, nuts as earned training rewards rather than a bowl item, portions measured against a small handful, hours of flight and climbing, and a weight written down every week. Everything on this page is downstream of that.

Psittacosis, respiratory disease, metals & injuries

The rest of the health section, including the one that passes to people.

Breathing trouble is always urgent

A bird's respiratory system has very little reserve, and there is no mild version of avian breathing difficulty. Learn what normal looks like while the bird is well: 15 to 40 breaths a minute at rest, with the tail perfectly still. Tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, a clicking or rasping sound, a change or loss of voice, discharge at the nostrils, or sitting fluffed with the neck stretched up are all same-day signs.

Psittacosis

Avian chlamydiosis, caused by Chlamydia psittaci, and it is zoonotic. A bird can carry it with no signs at all, shedding it in droppings and respiratory secretions, which is why a new bird is tested rather than trusted. When it does show, expect respiratory signs, diarrhea, weakness and an enlarged liver, and lime-green droppings as a classic pointer. Treatment is doxycycline: 45 days has historically been the recommendation, with shorter courses of 21 to 30 days also potentially effective. Finish the course, because a partial one produces a carrier.

In people Illness typically begins 5 to 14 days after exposure, with an abrupt fever, chills, headache, malaise and muscle aches, and a dry cough that can come with breathlessness or chest tightness. It responds to antibiotics. If anyone in the house develops a flu-like illness with a dry cough and there is a sick bird at home, tell the doctor there is a bird. They will not think of it. Page {{P:dust}} covers the separate, non-infectious lung problem this species can cause.
