Section 03 · Diet

Diet: pellets, seed & converting a seed eater

One ratio worth memorizing, and the disease list that follows from getting it wrong.

VCA gives the target as approximately 75 to 80% formulated pellets, with fresh fruit and vegetables making up no more than 20 to 40% of the daily diet, and seed as a small minority rather than the base of it. That is the whole prescription, and it is the same figure across every large parrot.

A seed-heavy diet is the central feeding mistake in this species It leads directly to obesity, fatty liver disease and vitamin A deficiency, and high-fat diets are specifically linked to the lipomas that show up so often in overweight cockatoos. Page {{P:liver}} is the consequence page. Sunflower seeds and peanuts are the worst offenders, and a bird left to choose eats them and leaves everything else, so the diet the bird actually consumes is worse than the one in the bowl.

Portions, which are smaller than they look

VCA offers a mental picture worth keeping: a small handful of food for a cockatoo is roughly a dinner-plate portion for a person. Over-portioning is easy and invisible, and it is how a bird on a correct diet still gets fat. Offer fresh food daily, take the fresh part out after about two hours before it spoils, and clean both dishes every day. Fresh water is always available.

Converting a seed eater

Do it gradually, and expect it to take days, weeks or months. Nothing here is a hunger strike. Pick one method or run several together:

MethodHow
Morning firstPellets alone first thing, when the bird is hungriest. Seed goes back in later
Mix and shiftMix pellets into the seed and reduce the seed share over weeks
Grind it inGrind pellets to powder over a moist food, then increase the chunk size gradually
Foraged, not offeredThis species' particular advantage. A pellet hidden inside a wrapped parcel is worth investigating in a way a pellet in a bowl is not. Page {{P:foraging}}
Eat it in front of the birdCockatoos are intensely social feeders. Visibly eating the same food yourself converts more birds than any product

Withdraw seed only once the bird is reliably eating pellets and produce, and weigh it through the change, on the scale from page {{P:sexing}}. A bird losing weight is not converting, it is starving, and that is the only failure mode that matters here.

No grit, and no supplements on a good diet Grit is not needed: cockatoos hull seed before swallowing the kernel, and grit can cause a dangerous digestive blockage. And a bird eating 75 to 80% pellets does not need added vitamins or minerals, because the pellet is already fortified; stacking supplements on top can do harm. The exceptions are a bird still transitioning off seed and a laying hen needing calcium, and both are a conversation with your avian vet rather than a jar off a shelf.
Why this species can convert more easily than most Cockatoos are exceptionally curious and exceptionally food-motivated, which is normally a nuisance and here is an asset. A new pellet presented as a puzzle, wrapped in paper or buried in a foraging box, gets investigated. The same pellet in a dish gets ignored. Use the intelligence rather than fighting it.
Section 03 · Diet

Safe vegetables, greens & fruit

The 20 to 40% that is not pellets. Vegetables should be most of it and fruit the smaller part.

Vegetables carry the vitamin A that a seed diet lacks, and the rule that does the work is dark green and dark orange. Fruit belongs inside the same share rather than on top of it: it is mostly sugar and water, cockatoos adore it, and a bird filling up on mango is a bird not eating kale. Wash everything, cut it to a size a large beak can hold, and remove pits and apple seeds.

The chop, which is how most large-parrot owners actually do this

Batch-prepare a mixed vegetable chop, portion it into containers and freeze it. A week's worth takes half an hour on a Sunday, it solves the "I have no time to chop kale on a Tuesday" problem that quietly ends most fresh-food regimes, and it lets you hide the vegetable a bird refuses among five it accepts. Thaw a portion overnight, serve it in the morning, take it out after two hours.

Getting a refuser to eat vegetables Eat it in front of the bird, obviously and with enthusiasm, from your own plate; this species watches you decide what counts as food. Change the format rather than the food, so a refused chunk gets accepted grated, threaded on a skewer, or woven through the cage bars. Offer it first thing, before the pellet bowl. And keep offering: twenty refusals then acceptance is a normal pattern, not a failure.
Section 03 · Diet

Nuts, treats & the never-feed list

The best training currency you have, and the fastest route to a fat bird.

Nuts

A couple of tree nuts a day, almonds, walnuts or Brazil nuts, are a fine daily treat and double as genuine enrichment for a bird this intelligent: an in-shell nut is a puzzle, a shelled one is a snack. Cracking it is most of the value.

The limit is fat. Nuts and sunflower seed are the highest-fat things in the cage, and this species is prone to obesity and to fatty liver. Keep nuts as the paid reward in a training session, on page {{P:training}}, rather than as something in a dish, and you get the training for free and the calories under control. A bird earning three almonds across two sessions is a bird working for its treats.

TreatTierUse it for
In-shell almond, walnut, Brazil nutDaily, countedThe high-value training reward, and a foraging puzzle in itself
Sunflower seed, peanutSparingThe highest-fat, lowest-value items in the cage. Fine as an occasional jackpot, never as a diet
Cooked egg, lean meat, a little cheeseSparingUseful during a molt or for a laying hen. Nothing processed, salted or seasoned
Whole raw vegetables, corn cobs, palm nutsAnytimeFood that is also work. The best category there is for this species
Never, at any quantity Avocado. Chocolate. Caffeine, including coffee, tea and soft drinks. Alcohol. VCA names all four as toxic and potentially fatal. Onion and garlic, which can cause a serious blood disorder in birds. Fruit pits and apple seeds, which release cyanide-related compounds. Salt and salted human snacks. Grit or gravel, which this species has no use for and which can obstruct. And anything sugar-free containing xylitol, which has no established safe dose in birds.
The overfed hand-raised baby Even on a correct base diet, a cockatoo can be tipped toward obesity by regular calorie-dense treats or by having been overfed as a hand-raised chick. Portion awareness matters even when the food itself is right, and the way to know is the weekly weight on page {{P:sexing}}, not how the bird looks. A heavily feathered white bird hides a great deal.
Treats belong in your hand, not in a dish Every treat handed over is a chance to practise a step-up, a target touch, a station, or simply calm behavior being rewarded. Every treat left in a bowl is calories with nothing bought. Over a decade that difference compounds into either a trained, handleable cockatoo or an overweight one that has never had to do anything.
Section 03 · Diet

Foraging: filling the six-hour gap

Wild parrots spend up to six hours a day getting food. A full bowl takes minutes. That gap is the problem.

Wild parrots spend up to six hours a day searching for, selecting and manipulating food. A captive bird with a full dish is finished in minutes. That is not a small difference in daily routine, it is most of the bird's waking behaviour missing, and something fills the space: in this species it is screaming, destruction and plucking.

The evidence points the same way it does for the training on page {{P:training}}. Increased foraging opportunity has repeatedly been found to improve feather condition in parrots, and in young Amazon parrots foraging opportunity and increased physical complexity both prevented and reduced psychogenic feather picking. It is one of the stronger results in bird welfare.

The rule: nothing in a bowl Not "some food in toys". Aim for the whole daily ration delivered through work, across multiple sites in the cage and the room, so the bird has to search as well as solve. A bowl of pellets in the corner undoes everything else on this page, because a bird that can eat for free will.

How to start, and how to progress

  1. Stage one: visible. A bird that has only ever been bowl-fed does not know food can be inside a device. Start with food sitting openly on top of a foraging toy, or lightly covered by a single sheet of paper
  2. Stage two: wrapped. Food in paper parcels, in cardboard tubes, in a paper cup taped shut. Cheap, endless, and destroyed in the process, which is the point
  3. Stage three: contained. Purpose-made large-parrot foraging toys, rated for a beak this strong, and a foraging box the bird has to dig through
  4. Stage four: distributed. Several sites, some empty, so the bird searches rather than goes straight to the one place food lives
  5. Then rotate the presentation constantly. A cockatoo solves a puzzle once and after that it is a container
Watch intake while you make it harder Do not make the first foraging toy hard. A bird that cannot solve a device eats less rather than working harder, and in a species that is easy to over-condition it is also easy to under-feed by accident. Weigh weekly through any change to how food is delivered, and step the difficulty back if the weight moves. Page {{P:sexing}}.

Improvised, which is most of it

  • Pellets and vegetables inside paper parcels, twisted shut, hung from the cage roof
  • A cardboard box of shredded paper with food buried through it
  • Food threaded on a stainless skewer between blocks of untreated wood
  • Whole vegetables woven through the bars so they have to be worked loose
  • A paper cup taped shut with a nut inside. This costs nothing and occupies a cockatoo for a genuinely useful stretch
  • Nuts in the shell, which are the original foraging toy
Budget destruction as a running cost Everything in the list above is consumed. That is the system working, not waste, and it is why the toy line on page {{P:budget}} is bigger than the food line. Cardboard, paper, untreated softwood and palm are all effectively free, which is what makes a proper foraging routine affordable at this scale.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Training, and why it beat everything else

The most useful research result in this book, and it is quietly uncomfortable.
What the study found An assessment and treatment programme for feather plucking in sulphur-crested cockatoos compared medication, socialization, training sessions and feeding enrichment, and checked the results against behavioural observation, feather condition scoring and corticosterone levels. Training sessions were the most successful treatment. The authors attributed that to the social attention and mental stimulation the sessions provided, rather than to anything about the specific behaviours being taught.

That is not a result about tricks. It says the most effective intervention available for the signature problem of this species is structured time you personally spend with the bird, which is exactly the thing most owners have least of and no product can replace. So protect the sessions when the week gets busy, ahead of almost anything else in the routine.

What a session actually looks like

ElementDetail
Length and frequencyTen to fifteen minutes, twice a day. Short and often beats long and occasional
MethodPositive reinforcement only: a clicker or marker word and a high-value reward from page {{P:nuts}}. Never punishment, restraint, or forcing a bird onto a hand
Stop pointEnd while the bird still wants more. A session that runs until the bird is bored teaches it that sessions get boring
WhereOn a play stand, away from the cage, so the behaviour is not tied to territory and the stand becomes a good place to be
WhoEveryone in the household, in rotation. This is how you build a bird with several people rather than one

The behaviours worth teaching, in order

  1. Target touch. The bird touches its beak to a stick. Everything else is built from it, and it lets you move a large parrot without a hand near its beak
  2. Step up, and step down. Step down is the one people skip and then need at 10pm
  3. Station. Go to a named perch and stay there. This is the behaviour that gets a bird off your shoulder, out of the kitchen, or away from a visitor
  4. Recall, for a flighted bird. A safety behaviour, and the alternative to a chase
  5. Voluntary crate entry. A bird that walks into its carrier for a nut is a bird you can take to a vet on a bad night. Page {{P:outage}}
  6. Towel acceptance, taught slowly with rewards, so restraint at the vet is not a fight
  7. Simple problem solving and novel objects, once the basics are solid
Reward calm, deliberately, every day The single most useful thing to reinforce is not a trick. It is the bird sitting quietly on its stand doing something of its own. Walk past, drop a reward, say nothing, walk on. Do that fifty times and you have paid for the behaviour you actually want, which is a cockatoo that can be near you without needing to be on you. Page {{P:bonding}}.
What training is not It is not a substitute for the hours out of the cage, the foraging, or the sleep. And it is not a fix applied after a problem starts: the study treated established plucking, but every practitioner in this field says the same thing, which is that prevention in this species is disproportionately more effective than treatment. Start the sessions in week one, on a bird with no problems at all.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Handling, step-up, bites & sexual maturity

Including the change at five to seven years old that catches owners of a sweet bird completely off guard.

The mechanics

  • Offer a forearm or a flat hand, not a single finger, and let the bird choose to step
  • A handheld perch is standard practice with a bird that bites, and using one is not a failure. Target training on page {{P:training}} keeps hands away from the beak
  • Never force it. A hand pushed at a cockatoo's chest is a hand it learns to bite. If the bird will not step up, something is wrong with the moment, not with the bird
  • Never chase a loose bird, and never grab one. Both cost trust that takes months to rebuild
Head and neck only Stroking the back, under the wings, or anywhere near the vent mimics a mate. In a cockatoo it triggers hormonal behaviour, which drives both the aggression on this page and the egg problems on page {{P:eggs}}, and it is one of the classic first-timer mistakes. This applies to cocks as well as hens, and it applies to the cuddling this species is bought for. Scratch the head. Leave the body alone.

Bites

Large cockatoos bite more readily and with less restraint than many other parrots, and a bite from a bird this size can require stitches. That is a reason to read the bird, not to fear it. Almost every bite follows a warning that was missed.

SignalWhat it means
Crest raised sharply and held forward, body rigidAlarm or threat. Stop moving toward the bird
Eye pinning, pupils flashing wide and narrowHigh arousal. It can be excitement or aggression, and next to a raised crest it is a warning
Beak open, head lowered, feathers up on the neckCommitted threat. Back away
Lunging at the bars as you approach the cageCage territoriality. Work with the bird outside the cage instead
Crest relaxed, feathers smooth, body looseCalm. Carry on
Beak grinding, one foot up, half-closed eyesContentment, usually before sleep
Sexual maturity: 3 to 4 years, or 5 to 6 in the large species Aggression often increases sharply at sexual maturity, and it catches owners of a previously sweet, cuddly bird completely off guard. A hand-raised cockatoo that has been a lap animal for five years can become territorial, hormonal and genuinely dangerous to the person it is not bonded to. Nothing has gone wrong and the bird is not broken; it has grown up.

What helps: hold the photoperiod at 10 to 12 hours of darkness (page {{P:light}}), stop all body petting, remove every cavity, keep training sessions running through it, and make sure the bird has independent occupation. What does not help is punishment, or withdrawing from the bird, which makes the following year worse.
If you are bitten Do not shout, fling the bird off, or react dramatically. Any big reaction is interesting, and interesting is a reward. Put the bird calmly on a perch, walk away, and work out afterwards which warning you missed. Then clean the wound and see a doctor if it is deep, because this is a puncture wound.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Over-bonding, separation anxiety & independence

The defining problem of this species, and it is created in the first few weeks by owners doing what feels loving.

Cockatoos form unusually intense bonds with their people. In the wild they maintain near-constant physical contact with a mate and a flock, and that trait carries straight into a house as an expectation of the same from you. This is why they are described as velcro birds, and why a cockatoo that gets what it needs can be an extraordinary companion. It is also why the failure mode is so severe: an over-bonded cockatoo develops genuine separation anxiety when left alone, even briefly, and screams and plucks when the person it has chosen leaves the room.

The biggest first-timer mistake Over-bonding early on. Spending intense one-on-one time in the first weeks, because the bird is new and adorable and available, cements an anxious dependency rather than a healthy attachment. The bird learns that constant contact with one person is the normal state of the world, and then everything that is not that becomes distress. What feels like the most loving possible start is the thing that produces the screaming at year two.

Building independence, from day one

PracticeWhy
Several people, in rotationFeeding, training and out-of-cage time shared across the household. A bird with four people has four sources of company and does not collapse when one goes to work. This is the highest-leverage habit on the page
Reward self-directed activityWalk past, drop a reward when the bird is playing on its own, say nothing, walk on. You are paying for exactly the behaviour you want
Leave the room constantly, brieflyOut for ten seconds, back. Out for a minute, back. Departures become unremarkable because they happen forty times a day and nothing bad follows
A stand that is genuinely good to be onFood, foraging and things to destroy live there. Somewhere to be that is not your shoulder. Page {{P:furnishings}}
Do not make yourself the only stimulationRadio, a view, foraging, a routine. A bird whose entire day is you has one point of failure
Keep departures and returns low-keyAn emotional goodbye teaches the bird that your leaving is a significant event. It should be the least interesting thing that happens all day

The shoulder question

A cockatoo on a shoulder is next to your face and out of your sight, and this is a beak that can take a stitch-requiring bite without meaning much by it. It also puts the bird physically level with you, which for a species this hormonal is not a neutral position. Many experienced keepers of large parrots simply do not do shoulders, and work from the hand, the forearm and a stand instead. If yours already rides on your shoulder, teach the station cue on page {{P:training}} so you can end it politely.

If the bird is already over-bonded It is retrievable and it is slow. Widen the circle gradually: have the second person do the feeding and the training while the primary person is out of the room, reward calm separation in very small increments, and keep the schedule predictable so attention is not something the bird has to demand. If it escalates over several weeks of consistent effort, get a certified avian behaviour consultant involved rather than trying harder. This is the exact problem they exist for.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Screaming, noise & the household reality

Some of it is normal and permanent. The rest is a message, and there is a specific way to answer it.

In the wild, cockatoos use loud, far-carrying contact calls to locate flock members across dense canopy. It is a functional communication tool, not noise for its own sake, and it does not switch off because the bird lives indoors. A cockatoo separated from its flock, which is your household, in another room, or one whose social needs are not being met through the day, escalates contact calling because that is precisely what it evolved to do when isolated.

What is normal and will not change Brief bouts of loud contact calling around dawn and dusk are normal cockatoo behaviour, mirroring when wild flocks are most vocal. Accept it. Cockatoos are loud by design and quieting one is not an achievable goal, only a source of frustration for both of you. What is not normal is prolonged, escalating screaming through the day, and that usually signals unmet social or enrichment needs rather than a fixed habit.

What actually works, and what makes it worse

ApproachEffect
Ignoring the bird when it is calm, responding when it screamsMakes it worse. The bird has no reliable way to get attention except escalating
Yelling back, or reacting loudlyMakes it worse. Any reliable response, including an annoyed one, registers as the call working
Covering the cage as a punishmentMakes it worse, and it damages the sleep routine that is helping. Page {{P:light}}
Rewarding quiet or calmer vocalizations with attentionWorks. Teaches the bird that softer sounds also get a response. Answer the whistles and the talk, not the scream
Consistent daily interaction, scheduled in advanceWorks. Reduces the anxiety-driven escalation that comes from unpredictable attention
A contact call of your own, answered from another roomWorks. Whistle back. The bird wants to know where the flock is, and a two-second answer often ends it

The pattern across almost every case is proactive engagement rather than reactive discipline. Predictable, generous attention on a schedule removes the pressure that builds into screaming, and it is much easier than trying to extinguish it afterwards.

Before you treat it as behavioural

A sudden change in a previously stable bird's vocal or behavioural pattern can signal underlying pain or illness. Any real change in behavior is worth a vet visit to rule that out, and that is true here as much as it is for plucking on page {{P:plucking}}.

Noise is a housing decision, and it is worth being honest about A cockatoo scream carries through walls. Renting with one is difficult, an attached home means your neighbours are part of this decision, and noise is one of the commonest reasons a cockatoo is rehomed. There is no soundproofing that solves it and no training that removes it. If a noise complaint would cost you your home, this is the wrong bird, and page {{P:decision}} would rather you found that out now.
