Section 03 · Feeding

What African fat-tailed geckos eat

The short answer, then the four pages that make it work.
The short answer Live insects, and nothing else. An African fat-tailed gecko is an obligate insectivore: it must eat insects and cannot digest fruit, vegetables or plant-based gecko diets. The staple feeders are crickets, dubia roaches, discoid roaches, mealworms, superworms and black soldier fly larvae, captive-bred and gut-loaded (fed a calcium-rich diet before they are offered), dusted with plain calcium at most feedings, and offered in the evening. Fresh water in a shallow dish, always.

One way to lay out an adult fed 3 times a week. Every feeding gets calcium; vitamin D3 comes once or twice; the multivitamin once.

If your multivitamin already contains D3, that day counts as one of the D3 days. Without UVB, a second calcium with D3 day is the usual choice. With UVB, one is enough.

Three things people get wrong

  • Feeding fruit, baby food or crested gecko powder. Not food for this animal, whatever a video shows.
  • Catching insects in the yard. Pesticides and parasites, and some common insects are toxic outright.
  • Counting days when it stops eating. A healthy adult can fast for weeks to months on its tail. Watch the tail, not the calendar (page {{P:refusal}}).

Feeding schedule by age & how much

Daily while it grows, then less often. The tail tells you whether the schedule is right.

Hatchling, under about 4 monthsDailyAbout 5 crickets, each around 1/4 in (6 mm) long, sizing up as the gecko grows
JuvenileDaily for a still-growing animal, easing toward 3 times a weekAbout 9 crickets or mealworms a feeding at the 3-times-a-week end. Move toward the adult schedule as the tail fills out

The adult interval is a range rather than one number, so treat it as a range and let the tail decide. Fat, as thick as the neck or thicker, and holding steady: the schedule is right. Thinning: feed more often, and see page {{P:refusal}} if it is refusing.

Sizing the prey

No insect wider than the space between the gecko's eyes. Oversized prey is an impaction risk, and so is a mouthful of substrate taken with a cricket that ran into it. Measure against the gecko, not the label on the cup: hatchlings take insects about 1/4 in (6 mm) long, and the size goes up with the gecko.

How to offer it

  • In the evening. A nocturnal gecko responds to food offered at dusk far better than to food offered while it should be asleep.
  • From tongs, or in a smooth-sided dish. Feeding tongs control the portion and keep insects from burrowing into the substrate, which is where they get lost and where substrate gets swallowed.
  • Remove what is not eaten after about 15 minutes, or by the next morning at the latest.
  • Fresh water in a shallow dish at all times, changed daily.
  • An elderly gecko can lose teeth and struggle with hard-bodied prey such as crickets. Softer prey keeps it eating.
A tail-raise is a good sign A fat-tail stalking prey often lifts its tail and waves it slowly. That is hunting behavior, not distress, and it means the feeding is doing its job. Varying where and when prey appears keeps it that way (page {{P:enrichment}}).

Section 03 · Feeding

Gut-loading & calcium dusting

A gecko's nutrition is mostly decided before the insect is ever offered.

Why insects need help

A gecko needs more calcium than phosphorus. The target for insect-eating reptiles is roughly 2 parts calcium to 1 part phosphorus, because phosphorus competes with calcium for absorption. Feeder insects come nowhere close: crickets sit near 1 to 9, mealworms near 1 to 14. Two habits close the gap. Gut-loading fills the insect with calcium-rich food before it is eaten, and dusting coats it in calcium powder. Do both: you cannot see how much a given cricket actually ate.

Gut-loading

Feed the insects 24 to 48 hours before offering them, inside the 24 to 72 hour window where gut-loading works. Gut-loading is not cumulative: an insect carries only what is in its gut right now, so offer insects soon after they come off the food.

To dust, put the insects in a cup or bag with a pinch of powder and shake gently until they look lightly floured, then offer them straight away.

Never dust D3 at every feeding Vitamin D3 is fat-soluble and builds up in the body, so more is not safer. Plain calcium is the routine; calcium with D3 is once or twice a week. Write your dusting days on page {{P:emergency}} so a sitter does not double them.

Why it stops eating & when to worry

This gecko can go months without eating and be fine. That makes the tail the question, not the day count.

The common reasons

  1. The season. As days shorten in fall and winter, adults commonly eat less or stop for a while. Juveniles usually keep eating through it.
  2. Breeding season. Females may go off food for a few days before laying, within a breeding season of roughly 4 to 5 months (page {{P:eggs}}).
  3. A shed. Shedding takes energy and often comes with a short appetite drop. If the shed sticks, including retained eye caps, the gecko can turn lethargic and stop eating until it is dealt with (page {{P:shed}}).
  4. Stress. A new home, or recent handling. Leave it alone until it is eating and passing stool reliably.
  5. An enclosure that is too cool. A cold gecko can act as if a winter slowdown has started, out of season. A husbandry fix, not an emergency by itself: measure the warm hide floor first.
  6. Dehydration. Too little humidity or water access suppresses appetite.
  7. Illness. Internal parasites, bacterial infection or, rarely, tumors, usually with weight loss and lethargy.
  8. Old age. Lost teeth make hard-bodied prey difficult. Softer prey helps.
  9. A long picky phase. Otherwise healthy geckos have been recorded refusing food for months while holding a stable weight, the tail buffering the gap.

What to check first

Warm hide floor with the infrared gun: 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C)
Humid hide still damp, and no skin stuck on toes, tail tip or eyes
Weight in grams against the log on page {{P:ownerlog}}
Tail: as thick as the neck, or thinner?
A female digging or restless: is she about to lay?
Stool and urates, the white part of a dropping, in the last week (page {{P:poop}})
When a refusal becomes a vet visit There is no universal day count. What matters is real weight loss, as opposed to a tail that is a little less plump: a tail thinner than the neck is the warning sign. Go to the vet for weight loss, a bloated belly, lethargy or weakness beyond a normal seasonal slowdown, retained shed that does not clear with humidity and soaking, or signs of dehydration alongside not eating. Dehydration alongside a stuck shed that runs past about a week can become fatal.
Juveniles have less margin Juveniles usually keep eating through the fall and winter drop, so a juvenile that stops is not taking a seasonal break. It gets the checks above the same day, and a vet call if it does not resolve.
