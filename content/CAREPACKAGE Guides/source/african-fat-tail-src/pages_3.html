Section 03 · Feeding

Gut-loading & calcium dusting

A gecko's nutrition is mostly decided before the insect is ever offered.

Why insects need help

A gecko needs more calcium than phosphorus. The accepted target for insect-eating reptiles is roughly 2 parts calcium to 1 part phosphorus, because phosphorus competes with calcium for absorption. Feeder insects come nowhere close: crickets sit near 1 to 9, mealworms near 1 to 14. Two habits close the gap. Gut-loading fills the insect with calcium-rich food before it is eaten, and dusting coats it in calcium powder. Do both: you cannot see how much a given cricket actually ate.

Gut-loading

Feed the insects 24 to 48 hours before offering them, inside the 24 to 72 hour window where gut-loading works. Gut-loading is not cumulative: an insect carries only what is in its gut right now, so offer insects soon after they come off the food.

To dust, put the insects in a cup or bag with a pinch of powder and shake gently until they look lightly floured, then offer them straight away.

Never dust D3 at every feeding Vitamin D3 is fat-soluble and builds up in the body, so more is not safer. Plain calcium is the routine; calcium with D3 is once or twice a week. Write your dusting days on page {{P:emergency}} so a sitter does not double them.
Section 03 · Feeding

Why it stops eating & when to worry

This gecko can go months without eating and be fine. That makes the tail the question, not the day count.

The common reasons

  1. The season. As days shorten in fall and winter, adults commonly eat less or stop for a while. Juveniles usually keep eating through it.
  2. Breeding season. Females may go off food for a few days before laying, within a breeding season of roughly 4 to 5 months (page {{P:eggs}}).
  3. A shed. Shedding takes energy and often comes with a short appetite drop. If the shed sticks, including retained eye caps, the gecko can turn lethargic and stop eating until it is dealt with (page {{P:shed}}).
  4. Stress. A new home, or recent handling. Leave it alone until it is eating and passing stool reliably.
  5. An enclosure that is too cool. A cold gecko can act as if a winter slowdown has started, out of season. A husbandry fix, not an emergency by itself: measure the warm hide floor first.
  6. Dehydration. Too little humidity or water access suppresses appetite.
  7. Illness. Internal parasites, bacterial infection or, rarely, tumors, usually with weight loss and lethargy.
  8. Old age. Lost teeth make hard-bodied prey difficult. Softer prey helps.
  9. A long picky phase. Otherwise healthy geckos have been recorded refusing food for months while holding a stable weight, the tail buffering the gap.

What to check first

Warm hide floor with the infrared gun: 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C)
Humid hide still damp, and no skin stuck on toes, tail tip or eyes
Weight in grams against the log on page {{P:ownerlog}}
Tail: as thick as the neck, or thinner?
A female digging or restless: is she about to lay?
Stool and urates, the white part of a dropping, in the last week (page {{P:poop}})
When a refusal becomes a vet visit There is no universal day count. What matters is real weight loss, as opposed to a tail that is a little less plump: a tail thinner than the neck is the warning sign. Go to the vet for weight loss, a bloated belly, lethargy or weakness beyond a normal seasonal slowdown, retained shed that does not clear with humidity and soaking, or signs of dehydration alongside not eating. Shedding-related dehydration that runs past about a week can become fatal.
Juveniles have less margin Juveniles usually keep eating through the fall and winter drop, so a juvenile that stops is not taking a seasonal break. It gets the checks above the same day, and a vet call if it does not resolve.
Section 04 · Handling, Females & Behavior

Handling a calm gecko

Among the calmest, most handleable pet geckos there are. The rules are few and they matter.

Adults tolerate handling and usually stay calm through a session. Juveniles tend to be shyer and take longer to settle. Any gecko can bite if it is sick, scared or provoked, but for this species a bite is the exception. The calm has a context: in the wild this is a nocturnal savanna gecko that spends the day asleep under cover, so handling goes best in the evening, when it is already awake and moving.

Getting started

  • Wait about two weeks after a new gecko comes home, and do not start until it is eating regularly.
  • Approach from the side. A hand that comes in from the side is easier on it than one that scoops from above.
  • Support the whole body and let it walk across your hands rather than gripping it. Keep early sessions short.
  • Stay low, over a bed, couch or the floor. A gecko that walks off a hand at chest height is a fall risk.
  • Not right after a meal, and not during a shed.

Reading it in the hand

This species is more vocal than most pet geckos, and the sounds mean something. In the hand, a chirp or a squeak signals discomfort: the cue to ease off and put it down. A slow tail-swish combined with an arched back is a clearer "leave me alone". A slow tail-wave while stalking an insect is something else: hunting.

Never by the tail Like most geckos, a fat-tail drops its tail if grabbed roughly or badly frightened. This is called autotomy. The tail regrows within weeks, but comes back smoother and more bulbous, and the fat reserve it carried is gone. Never grab or restrain the tail, and tell every visitor the same.

Handling as a health check

Each session is the time to look the gecko over: a plump tail, no old skin on the toes or tail tip, clear eyes. Weigh it on a kitchen scale that reads in single grams and log the number once a month (page {{P:ownerlog}}).

Salmonella Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella bacteria in their droppings without looking ill. A CDC (United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) investigation into infections linked to pet geckos, closed in January 2026, counted 113 cases and 31 hospitalizations across 36 states. Wash your hands with soap after touching the gecko, its food or anything in its enclosure. Keep it and its gear out of the kitchen, and never clean gear in a kitchen sink or a bathtub people use: keep a plastic tub for it. Do not kiss the gecko. Children under 5 should not handle reptiles at all, and adults over 65, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system are at higher risk.
Section 04 · Handling, Females & Behavior

Sexing, weight & body condition

Telling male from female

Sexing is done by looking at the underside near the vent, the single rear opening that waste and eggs pass through, and it only works on maturing animals. Females mature at roughly 250 to 300 days after hatching, males at 300 to 350 days, and males grow larger than females from around then. A mature male shows:

  • A chevron-shaped row of enlarged preanal pores just in front of the vent: small openings that produce a waxy scent secretion.
  • A proportionately more bulbous tail base behind the vent, where the hemipenes, the paired male reproductive organs, sit.

Females lack the enlarged pore row and have a flatter tail base. Never hold the gecko by the tail to look.

Hatchlings: the incubation clue

Sex in this species is set by the temperature the egg was incubated at, which is called temperature-dependent sex determination. A breeder who records it can tell you which way a clutch was likely to go:

Incubated atMostly
24 to 28°C (75 to 82°F)Female
29 to 30.5°C (84 to 87°F)A roughly even split
31 to 32°C (88 to 90°F)Male

A temperature is a probability. A young gecko sold as "probable female" gets checked again once the pores appear, and it matters: two males must never share an enclosure.

Weight

Adults weigh 40 to 90 g, averaging about 50 g. No veterinary growth table by age has been published for this species, so the useful number is your own gecko's trend. A loss of 5 g is a tenth of an average adult and invisible by eye until the tail has already thinned. Weigh monthly, more often if anything looks off, and log it.

Body condition at a glance

Too thin

A tail thinner than the neck. A tail that thins to skin over bone, sometimes called stick tail, means underweight and a vet check for parasites. Rule out a cool warm side first.

Right

A plump tail, as thick as the neck or thicker; in a well-fed adult it can run about 1.25 in (3 cm) thick. Weight steady in an adult, rising in a juvenile.

Too heavy

Obesity in reptiles comes from too many calories and too little activity. Bring it down slowly over months, with the vet, by cutting fatty treats and spacing feedings. Not a crash diet.

Section 04 · Handling, Females & Behavior

Females, eggs & the laying box

A female kept entirely alone can still produce eggs. This page is for every female owner, not just breeders.

Eggs without a male

Female egg-laying reptiles can form eggs with no male present, so every adult female is at risk of egg binding, a female unable to pass the eggs she has formed. Those eggs are infertile and will not hatch, but the physical work of forming and laying them is the same. Females can also store sperm from an earlier mating, so a female who once lived with a male can lay fertile eggs later.

The laying season

WhatTypical
Breeding seasonRoughly 4 to 5 months a year
Eggs per clutch1 to 3
ClutchesUp to 5 in a season, many females fewer, often 15 to 22 days apart
AppetiteMay drop for a few days before laying
If fertile: incubation6 to 12 weeks, shorter when warmer

Signs she is about to lay

  • Restlessness and repeated digging, in corners, in the substrate or in the humid hide.
  • Going off food for a few days.

Give her somewhere to lay

A missing or unsuitable nesting site is one of the recognized causes of egg binding, and it is the easiest to remove. Every adult female gets a laying box: a covered box of moist substrate, such as damp sphagnum moss or a damp soil mix, deep enough for her to dig into, set over the warm end. Fat-tail females are particular about temperature when they lay: in a gradient study they chose nest sites averaging 32.4°C (about 90°F), warmer than leopard geckos chose, so the box belongs where the warm side reaches. Leave her alone with it.

After she lays Remove infertile eggs, which will not hatch. Offer food and water. Weigh her, so the next clutch is readable against this one. Poor nutrition is among the causes of egg binding, so a laying female's calcium dusting is not the place to cut corners.
Egg binding is a same-day vet call Straining, a swollen vent, restlessness that does not settle, repeated digging with nothing produced, or a gravid female, one carrying eggs, who stops eating and turns dull. Page {{P:eggbinding}} has the full entry.
