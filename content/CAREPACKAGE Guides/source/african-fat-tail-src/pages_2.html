Section 02 · Housing & Environment

A floor-space enclosure & where it goes

A ground-dweller and a burrower. It lives on the floor and under it, so buy floor.

A 20-gallon long tank is the minimum for one adult, about 30×12×12 in (76×30×30 cm), and a 40-gallon breeder, 36×18×16 in (91×46×41 cm), is meaningfully better. Height buys almost nothing for a terrestrial species. The extra length is what lets one end run warm and the other cool, and the extra depth is room for the deep digging substrate on page {{P:substrate}}. Skip the tall branches and climbing walls that arboreal geckos need.

One gecko per enclosure. Both sexes are territorial, males fight over space and females, and two males must never share. A second fat-tail means a second full setup. A single gecko loses nothing: in the wild this species is solitary and rarely seen in groups.

Where the enclosure goes

PlaceWhy
Out of direct sunSun through glass heats a closed box far past the warm-side target, and the gecko cannot leave. Heat comes from the thermostat-controlled mat on page {{P:heat}}, never the window
Off radiators, vents and draftsEach one swings the temperature and dries the air, which fights the humidity on page {{P:humidity}}
Not in the kitchenReptiles carry Salmonella bacteria with no sign of illness, and the hygiene rules on page {{P:handling}} start with keeping the gecko and its gear away from food
Where you will see itThis gecko sleeps through the day and comes out at dusk. Put it where you will be in the evening, because that is when the daily health check on page {{P:routine}} happens

A front-opening enclosure, or a tank with a secure mesh lid, both work. The mesh lid matters because glass blocks UVB (page {{P:lighting}}).

The heat source

An under-tank heat mat covering about a third of the floor, always on a thermostat, with the warm hide sitting directly over it. A mat under a thick layer of substrate heats the surface poorly, so put the warm hide on the mat and keep the deep digging area off it. Skip heat rocks and strong overhead basking bulbs entirely: both carry real burn risk for an animal that presses its belly to the warmest spot it can find.

The thermostat is the safety device

A heat mat has no idea how hot it is. Left unregulated it climbs past the target on a warm afternoon, and a gecko seeking belly heat stays on the hot spot rather than moving off. That is how thermal burns happen. A basic on/off thermostat suits a mat, since a mat changes temperature slowly enough that a full on and off cycle stays gentle. A faster heat source such as a ceramic heat emitter, a screw-in heater that gives heat and no light, is better on a pulse proportional or dimming unit, which eases power down before it overshoots.

Where the probe goes

The probe goes flat on the floor of the warm hide, the surface the gecko actually lies on, held down with tape so it cannot drift. Never taped to the back of the mat, which reads the mat's own temperature, and never hanging in the air. A probe that slips off its spot fails silently: the display keeps showing a correct number for the wrong place. Check its position at every water change.

Measure it two ways

An infrared temperature gun, which reads a surface from a distance, aimed at the warm hide floor: the number that matters
A second digital thermometer, apart from the thermostat probe, read daily. A thermostat stuck on, or a probe the gecko has shifted, gives no other warning
A digital probe thermometer at the cool end. One reading tells you nothing about a gradient
Cold nights The mat keeps working at night with the lights off. If the room itself takes the enclosure below 70°F (21°C), add a heat source that gives no light, such as a ceramic heat emitter on its own thermostat, set well clear of anything the gecko can reach. A gecko kept too cool can stop eating as if a winter slowdown had started, and a cool, damp enclosure is the classic setup for respiratory infection.

Humidity & the three hides

Higher than a leopard gecko needs, but this is still a mostly dry enclosure. The moisture lives in one box.
WhereTarget
The enclosure air, read mid-height50 to 70%
Inside the humid hide70 to 80%, permanently

The humid hide is not optional here

A fat-tail needs a persistently damp retreat as a standing feature, not a shedding aid set out now and then. A closed gecko cave packed with damp sphagnum moss, or damp paper towel, stays humid inside while the rest of the enclosure runs drier. Damp means it holds together when squeezed and does not drip. This one hide is what prevents almost all of the stuck shed on page {{P:shed}}, and it helps keep the gecko hydrated. A humid hide that has dried out is only a hide, so check it every day.

Three hides, minimum

Warm hide

Dry, snug, directly over the heat mat, with the thermostat probe on its floor. Where the gecko digests.

Humid hide

Damp moss inside, at the cool end or the middle. Directly over the mat it bakes dry in hours.

Cool hide

Dry, snug, at the far end. Where the gecko goes to cool down without giving up cover.

The point of three is that the gecko can be hidden and at the temperature and moisture it wants, at the same time. One hide forces it to choose between feeling safe and being comfortable, and it will choose safe.

Holding the numbers

  • Read a digital hygrometer (a humidity meter) mid-enclosure, away from the humid hide, a few times a week, and at the same hour each time.
  • Too dry in a dry house? A light misting a few times a week raises the air for a while. A lightly moist lower layer of substrate helps too. Let the hygrometer decide, not the calendar.
  • Too wet? Condensation that sits on the glass, a damp or sour smell, or mold. Mist less and let air move. A damp enclosure that is also too cool is the respiratory infection setup.
  • Refresh the moss whenever it smells sour or looks soiled. A warm, damp hide is where waste and mold build up fastest.
Never Raise the whole enclosure to 70 to 80% to make up for a missing humid hide. That is the leopard gecko mistake in reverse, and it trades stuck shed for a respiratory risk. Some keepers run a seasonal humidity cycle; a humid hide that never dries matters more than chasing an exact seasonal number.

Substrate for a burrower & furnishings

The detail that makes this species different from most pet geckos: it digs.
WhoSubstrate
Healthy adultsA naturalistic sandy-soil mix, at least 4 in (10 cm) deep so the gecko can burrow. A common blend is roughly 70% topsoil to 30% play sand, or coconut fiber mixed with reptile sand. Use untreated topsoil, with no fertilizer or added chemicals. Keep it lightly moist rather than dust-dry, which also helps hold a burrow
JuvenilesPaper towel
Any gecko in quarantinePaper towel, for the whole quarantine. It shows stool, mites (tiny parasites that live on the skin) and a regurgitated meal that loose substrate hides (page {{P:arrival}})
An unwell geckoPaper towel until it is well again

The sand question, settled

Never keep one on pure sand: it dries the gecko out and interferes with shedding, which is reason enough on its own. Avoid loose calcium sand too. The impaction half of the sand argument, the risk of a gut blockage, is narrower than it sounds. Sand swallowed with prey tends to cause trouble in a gecko that is already unhealthy, dehydrated or kept too cool, not in a healthy one on a soil mix. That is why juveniles, unwell animals and quarantine geckos go on paper, and why prey is sized and offered from tongs (page {{P:schedule}}).

Furnishings checklist

Warm dry hide, snug, over the mat
Cool dry hide at the far end
Humid hide with damp sphagnum moss
Shallow water dish, refreshed daily
Cork flats and stacked slate, low and stable
A deep patch of diggable substrate off the mat
For any female: a laying box (page {{P:eggs}})
Low branches only if wanted; height is secondary

Every piece of decoration has to be stable and able to take the gecko's full weight. A burrower digs under things, so set slate and rock on the tank floor before the substrate goes in, not on top of it.

Light cycle & UVB (ultraviolet B) light

A nocturnal gecko still needs a day. Whether it needs UVB is a choice with one rule attached.

The day length

Run lights for 10 to 12 hours a day on a plug-in timer, so the cycle never depends on someone remembering. At night the lights go off. This gecko is awake in the dark, and the heat mat keeps the warm side warm with no light at all.

UVB: optional, and increasingly recommended

UVB is the part of ultraviolet light that lets a reptile make vitamin D3 in its own skin, and vitamin D3 is what lets it use the calcium it eats. Fat-tailed geckos are nocturnal and can live without UVB if they get vitamin D3 from a calcium powder with D3 instead. Low-output UVB in the 2 to 5% range is increasingly recommended anyway. Either path is acceptable. No path is acceptable that gives the gecko neither, because that is how metabolic bone disease starts (page {{P:mbd}}).

If you run UVBHow
StrengthA low-output tube rated 2 to 5%. Researchers sort reptiles into four Ferguson Zones by the ultraviolet exposure they seek in the wild, and this gecko sits in Zone 1, the shade dwellers and the crepuscular species, those most active at dusk and dawn. So the target UVI (ultraviolet index, the measured strength of UVB where the gecko sits) is 0 to 0.7 across the enclosure and 0.6 to 1.4 at the closest point
PlacementAlong the warm side, inside the enclosure or over a mesh top. Never over glass, which blocks most UVB. A mesh lid costs some output; a glass lid costs most of it
ShadeThe hides are the shade. A gecko that wants no UVB goes under cover, which is exactly how a shade-dwelling species uses it
ReplacementAbout every 12 months, long before the tube stops looking bright. UVB output fades well before visible light does. Some models need it sooner; follow the maker's interval
TimerOn the same timer as the day cycle

If you do not run UVB

Then the calcium with D3 days on page {{P:supplements}} are not optional, and the dusting tally on page {{P:equiplog}} is worth keeping. A gecko without UVB depends on the powder for all of its vitamin D3.

Do not stack both and then add more Vitamin D3 is fat-soluble and builds up in the body, so more is not safer. A gecko under a working UVB tube is already making its own; keep its calcium with D3 at the low end of the schedule, and never dust D3 at every feeding.
What a fat-tail does with light It sleeps through most of it. Expect to see your gecko at dusk and after the lights go out, not under the lamp. A gecko that is out and moving in its evening window is a healthy sign in itself (page {{P:behavior}}).
Section 03 · Feeding

What African fat-tailed geckos eat

The short answer, then the four pages that make it work.
The short answer Live insects, and nothing else. An African fat-tailed gecko is an obligate insectivore: it must eat insects and cannot digest fruit, vegetables or plant-based gecko diets. The staple feeders are crickets, dubia roaches, discoid roaches, mealworms, superworms and black soldier fly larvae, captive-bred and gut-loaded (fed a calcium-rich diet before they are offered), dusted with plain calcium at most feedings, and offered in the evening. Fresh water in a shallow dish, always.

The four parts of a fat-tail diet

A sample week for an adult

One way to lay out an adult fed 3 times a week. Every feeding gets calcium; vitamin D3 comes once or twice; the multivitamin once.

If your multivitamin already contains D3, that day counts as one of the D3 days. Without UVB, a second calcium with D3 day is the usual choice. With UVB, one is enough.

Three things people get wrong

  • Feeding fruit, baby food or crested gecko powder. Not food for this animal, whatever a video shows.
  • Catching insects in the yard. Pesticides and parasites, and some common insects are toxic outright (page {{P:feeders}}).
  • Counting days when it stops eating. A healthy adult can fast for weeks on its tail. Watch the tail, not the calendar (page {{P:refusal}}).

Feeding schedule by age & how much

Daily while it grows, then less often. The tail tells you whether the schedule is right.
AgeHow oftenHow much
Hatchling, under about 4 monthsDailyAbout 5 crickets, each around 1/4 in (6 mm) long, sizing up as the gecko grows
JuvenileDaily for a still-growing animal, easing toward 3 times a weekAbout 9 crickets or mealworms a feeding at the 3-times-a-week end. Move toward the adult schedule as the tail fills out
Young adultEvery 2 to 3 daysAbout 2 insects per inch of body length, or what it eats in about 15 minutes
Adult3 times a week, or 3 to 4 timesAs above
Mature adult with a visibly fat tailEvery 5 daysAs above

The sources give these adult intervals as a range rather than one number, so treat them as a range and let the tail decide. Fat, as thick as the neck or thicker, and holding steady: the schedule is right. Thinning: feed more often, and see page {{P:refusal}} if it is refusing.

Sizing the prey

No insect wider than the space between the gecko's eyes. Oversized prey is an impaction risk, and so is a mouthful of substrate taken with a cricket that ran into it. Measure against the gecko, not the label on the cup: hatchlings take insects about 1/4 in (6 mm) long, and the size goes up with the gecko.

How to offer it

  • In the evening. A nocturnal gecko responds to food offered at dusk far better than to food offered while it should be asleep.
  • From tongs, or in a smooth-sided dish. Feeding tongs control the portion and keep insects from burrowing into the substrate, which is where they get lost and where substrate gets swallowed.
  • Remove what is not eaten after about 15 minutes, or by the next morning at the latest.
  • Fresh water in a shallow dish at all times, changed daily.
  • An elderly gecko can lose teeth and struggle with hard-bodied prey such as crickets. Softer prey keeps it eating.
A tail-raise is a good sign A fat-tail stalking prey often lifts its tail and waves it slowly. That is hunting behavior, not distress, and it means the feeding is doing its job. Varying where and when prey appears keeps it that way (page {{P:enrichment}}).
