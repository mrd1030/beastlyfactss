Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & finding a vet

Fat-tails are hardy when their two biggest needs, heat and humidity, are met. Nearly every common problem traces back to one of them.

See a reptile vet Today: open-mouth breathing, wheezing or mucus at the nose or mouth. A female straining or digging with nothing produced. Tissue pushed out of the vent. Bloating, straining and appetite loss together. An injury. Within a few days: lethargy, difficulty standing or walking, swollen joints, muscle twitches or a curving spine. Retained shed tightening around a toe or the tail tip that home care has not cleared. Cloudiness, swelling or discharge in an eye outside a normal shed. Real weight loss, or a tail thinner than the neck. Sunken eyes or diarrhea.

What to tell the vet

Warm hide floor reading from the infrared gun, not the thermostat setting
Enclosure humidity, and whether the humid hide was damp
Substrate, and the dusting routine: which powders, which days, UVB or not
When it started and whether it is getting worse
Last meal, last stool, and what the stool looked like
The weight log, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag

Finding a vet before you need one

Not every practice treats reptiles. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory, and when you call a clinic, ask how many reptiles it sees. Book an annual wellness exam with a fecal test even when nothing is wrong: it covers body condition, hydration and nutrition, and the weight log is the most useful thing to bring. A routine exam commonly runs $35 to $105 and a fecal test $65 to $110; a sick visit with tests is typically $150 to $300 or more.

Why weight comes up first A reptile hides illness until it is advanced, and a 50 g gecko hides weight loss until the tail has already thinned. A monthly number on a gram scale turns "seems thinner" into a fact a vet can use.

Retained shed & eye problems

The most common fat-tail-specific problem, and one of the most common reasons it sees a vet.

Retained shed (dysecdysis)

CauseHumidity too low, or no humid hide, or one that has dried out. Less often parasites, poor nutrition, infection, or nothing rough to rub against. Dysecdysis means an abnormal or incomplete shed
SignsOld skin left on the toes and the tail tip, and sometimes over the eyes. A ring of old skin left around a toe after the rest of the shed has come off
ResponseRaise humidity with a properly damp humid hide. Soak the gecko for about 30 minutes in chin-deep water at enclosure temperature, never left alone, then ease the softened skin off a toe with a damp cotton swab. Repeat over a couple of days rather than forcing it. Never peel dry skin. See a vet if a ring has already tightened, the toe is swollen or darkened, or tissue is dying. A vet may soften it over hours and remove it under magnification
RecoveryCaught early, home care and a corrected hide usually clear it. Skin left to constrict cuts off circulation and can cost the tip of a toe or tail, and that loss is permanent
Prevention is one box A proper moist hide and correct humidity prevent almost all of this. Check toes, tail tip and eyes the day after every shed, and keep the moss damp all year, not only when a shed is due.

Eye problems

CauseReported relatively often in this species, and one of the problems vets see most. Retained eye caps from a bad shed are the usual cause. Vitamin A deficiency can swell the eyelids, and infection can follow either
SignsCloudiness, swelling or discharge outside a normal shed cycle, or an eye held closed
ResponseA vet, promptly. Retained eye caps need veterinary attention rather than home removal. Never try to take anything off an eye yourself. Raise the humid hide's moisture in the meantime
RecoveryFollows the cause. The vet removes a retained cap safely, sometimes after softening it first. Fix the humidity, or it comes back with the next shed

Metabolic bone disease & vitamin problems

The most serious common problem in this species, and entirely a diet and setup disease.

MBD (metabolic bone disease)

CauseA shortage of calcium or vitamin D3, often with no UVB. A poor calcium to phosphorus balance in the diet, or a warm side that runs too cool, adds to it. Veterinarians call it nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism
SignsLethargy, difficulty standing or walking, swollen joints, muscle twitches, bones that feel unusually flexible, a curving spine. Later, a swollen or misshapen jaw, fractures from small falls, and loss of appetite
DiagnosisA vet exam with x-rays, which show thin, poorly mineralized bone and any fractures, and blood tests
TreatmentThe vet may give fluids, feeding support and calcium by injection if blood calcium is low. Correcting the diet and the setup is the core of treatment. The vet sets any dose; do not add extra D3 at home
RecoveryThe outlook is favorable, especially if the gecko is still eating. Caught early it can be managed, but deformities already present are permanent, and advanced cases can be fatal
In the meantime Any tremor, any softness in the jaw or new trouble walking is a vet appointment this week. Handle the gecko as little as possible, and take out anything it can climb and fall from: weak bone breaks on a drop that would normally mean nothing.

Vitamin A deficiency

CauseA diet short of preformed vitamin A, the ready-made form. Many reptiles cannot convert the plant form, beta carotene, so a multivitamin with preformed vitamin A is the prevention
SignsPoor growth, poor appetite, swollen eyelids, mouth inflammation, and respiratory infections that follow
DiagnosisFrom the diet history and the exam
Treatment and recoveryDiet correction and a supplement with preformed vitamin A, as the vet directs. Recovery follows the secondary problems, so the earlier the better

Gout

CauseA buildup of uric acid that deposits in joints or organs. Too much protein, or dehydration and kidney trouble. A calcium powder that contains phosphorus can damage the kidneys and lead to it
SignsPain severe enough that the gecko will not move, eat or drink. Gritty or discolored urates are the early warning of the same uric acid chemistry
Diagnosis and treatmentX-rays show the deposits. The vet corrects the diet or the underlying problem and may prescribe long-term medication
RecoveryTypically poor once advanced. Prevention is water, the humid hide, and phosphorus-free calcium

Impaction & respiratory infection

One comes from what goes into the mouth, the other from the air the gecko sits in.

Impaction

CauseA blockage of the gut. A ground gecko feeding on loose substrate can swallow some with its prey, and prey wider than the space between the eyes can lodge. It mostly happens in a gecko that is already unhealthy, dehydrated or kept too cool, because a cold gecko cannot move food through
SignsBloating, lethargy, appetite loss and straining, often with no normal stool
DiagnosisA vet exam and x-rays, which show the blockage or swallowed material
ResponseA vet visit, not a home remedy. Check the warm hide floor reads 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C) the same day, and move the gecko onto paper towel
RecoveryDepends on what was blocking and how it had to be removed. Afterward, paper towel until the vet clears it, correctly sized prey from tongs, and a warm side checked weekly
Prevention Paper towel for juveniles, quarantine and unwell geckos. A soil and sand mix rather than pure sand for adults. Prey no wider than the space between the eyes, offered from tongs or a dish. A warm side that is actually warm.

Respiratory infection

CauseAn enclosure kept too cool, too damp, or both. Poor hygiene, poor nutrition and vitamin A deficiency add to the risk
SignsWheezing, mucus, open-mouth breathing and lethargy. Discharge from the nose or eyes, and fast or labored breathing
DiagnosisA vet exam, often with x-rays of the chest
TreatmentA vet and antibiotics. It will not clear on its own. The vet sets the drug and the dose. At home: clean conditions, the warm side at target, and the gecko kept toward the middle to upper end of its temperature range, which helps the immune system and thins the mucus
RecoveryTreatment works best started early, with the setup fixed at the same time. A severe or drawn-out infection can spread into the bloodstream
The common thread Any gecko with either of these gets its warm hide floor, cool side and enclosure humidity measured the same day. The fix is usually in those three numbers, and the vet will ask for them.

Egg binding, prolapse & male problems

Reproductive emergencies. Every one of these is a vet visit, not a wait-and-see.

Egg binding (dystocia)

CauseA female cannot pass eggs she has formed, with or without a male ever present. Causes include a missing or unsuitable nesting site, the wrong temperature or humidity, poor nutrition, weak muscles from too little exercise, oversized or misshapen eggs, an old pelvic injury, and illness
SignsRestlessness, repeated digging in different spots, straining, a swollen vent, and a female who stops eating. As it goes on she turns dull and lethargic, and tissue may push out of the vent
DiagnosisA vet exam with x-rays or ultrasound to find the eggs, and often blood work
TreatmentRanges from a proper nesting site at the right conditions, through hormone injections or gently massaging the eggs out, to surgery. Medical treatment often fails, and surgery, which may remove the reproductive organs, is common. The vet decides the route and any doses
RecoveryLikely when she is in good overall condition and treated quickly. A female who has been egg-bound once can do it again, which is a conversation to have with the vet
Not always sudden In reptiles, egg binding is often not a sudden event. A female can hold eggs for weeks or months past the normal time to lay, which is exactly why a dull, digging, non-eating female is a same-day call rather than one to watch for a week.

Prolapse

CauseAn organ pushed out through the vent and stuck outside the body: the cloaca, the chamber just inside the vent, or the colon, the oviduct (the tube eggs pass down) in a female, or a hemipenis in a male. Parasites commonly cause it; so can a gut blockage, gut inflammation, bladder stones, straining with eggs, and injury
SignsPink, red or dark tissue outside the vent that does not go back in
ResponseThe same day. Do not push it back. Move the gecko onto clean, damp paper towel so the tissue does not dry out or pick up substrate
Diagnosis and recoveryThe vet works out which organ it is and why it happened, and treats the cause as well as the tissue. The outlook depends on both, and on how quickly it was seen

Male problems

Impacted poresThe preanal pores of a male secrete a waxy scent. If they block, the area swells and may discharge. Signs: swelling or matter in the pore row in front of the vent. Response and recovery: a vet clears and treats them; do not squeeze them at home
Hemipenis prolapseA male can prolapse a hemipenis. Treat it exactly as the prolapse above: damp paper towel, same-day vet

Parasites, mouth rot, tail loss & burns

Less dramatic than the last few pages. Each one still has a cause you can find and fix.

Internal parasites

CauseGut parasites, most of all in wild-caught animals, and spread between reptiles in a shared room or on shared tools
SignsWeight loss, a tail thinning toward stick tail, repeated watery or foul-smelling stool, lethargy and appetite loss. A parasite load shows up in a fecal test long before it shows as weight loss
DiagnosisA fecal exam: a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag, checked by the vet. Fecal exams at purchase and then on a regular schedule
Treatment and recoveryThe vet treats when parasites are found and sets the drug and dose. Clean the enclosure thoroughly during treatment, and keep the gecko on paper towel so reinfection from droppings is easy to stop

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

CauseInfection and inflammation of the lining of the mouth, after an injury, with a nutritional deficiency, or in a poor environment
SignsTiny purplish red spots in the mouth are the early sign. Look whenever the gecko opens its mouth to eat
Treatment and recoveryThe vet removes dead tissue, cleans the mouth with an antiseptic, and gives antibiotics and supportive care. Recovery follows how far it had spread, so the earliest spots are the time to go. Fix the underlying setup or diet problem at the same time

Tail loss

CauseRough handling, grabbing the tail, or a bad fright. A gecko that drops its tail again and again may have an underlying health problem
SignsThe tail is off. The gecko is otherwise usually fine
ResponseMove it onto clean paper towel, keep the enclosure clean while the stump heals, and skip handling. A stump that swells, oozes or smells is a vet visit
RecoveryThe tail regrows within weeks, smoother and more bulbous than the original. The real loss is the fat reserve it held, so watch the weight closely for the next few months

Burns

CauseA heat source the gecko can reach or one that is not regulated: a heat rock, an unscreened bulb, a mat with no thermostat or a failed one
SignsDamaged, discolored or blistered skin, usually on the belly, and a gecko that may lose fluid and become dehydrated
ResponseTurn the heat source off, find out why the regulation failed, and see the vet. The vet cleans the burn, treats it against infection, and gives pain relief and fluids
RecoveryMany burns respond well to treatment. Severe ones have a guarded outlook. Keep the gecko on clean, dry paper towel while it heals

Reading poop & hydration

Stool and urates are a free daily health check. Look before you scoop.

A reptile passes nitrogen waste as urates: a white to cream, chalky or paste-like portion that comes out alongside the solid stool, a reptile's version of urine. The urate is the fastest daily read on hydration a keeper has. Adult geckos usually pass stool every few days to about weekly, and juveniles more often; learn your own gecko's pattern.

It looks likeUsually meansDo
A firm, defined log with a soft white urateNormal. Small bits of insect shell in it are normal tooRemove it, note it in the log
Watery or unformed for more than one stool in a rowOften parasites, sometimes a feeder change or stressFresh sample in a sealed bag, fecal test
Blood, bright red or dark and tarryBleeding somewhere in the gutVet
Straining, or nothing passed with a swollen bellyImpaction, eggs, or a vent problemCheck the warm side, then vet (pages {{P:impaction}} and {{P:eggbinding}})
An unusually strong or off smellA gut problem, often parasitesFecal test
Urate yellow, orange, or gritty and crystallineDehydration at least; uncorrected, a urate or kidney problem, the chemistry behind goutSoak, refresh the humid hide and the water. If it persists, vet (page {{P:mbd}})

Any of these with lethargy or a skipped meal is worth a call that week, not a wait.

Hydration

  • Shallow water dish, always, emptied, rinsed and refilled every day.
  • The humid hide is hydration equipment, not only shed equipment. Keep the moss damp all year.
  • A light misting a few times a week in a dry house gives droplets as well as raising the air.
Signs of dehydration Loose or wrinkled skin with no shed due, sunken eyes, hard or yellow urates, stuck shed, and a drop in activity and appetite. A mild case: a 15 to 30 minute soak in shallow, warm water, never left alone, then a properly damp humid hide and fresh water. A gecko that stays wrinkled or sunken-eyed after that needs a vet, because dehydration that persists has something behind it, and dehydration alongside a stuck shed that runs past about a week can become fatal.
Bring a sample Take a fresh stool in a sealed bag to every wellness exam, even when nothing looks wrong. A baseline fecal turns a first vet visit into a real record, and parasite trouble shows there before it shows on the gecko.
