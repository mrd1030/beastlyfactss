Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling a calm gecko

Among the calmest, most handleable pet geckos there are. The rules are few and they matter.

Adults tolerate handling and usually stay calm through a session. Juveniles tend to be shyer and take longer to settle. Any gecko can bite if it is sick, scared or provoked, but for this species a bite is the exception. The calm has a context: in the wild this is a nocturnal savanna gecko that spends the day asleep under cover, so handling goes best in the evening, when it is already awake and moving.

Getting started

  • Wait about two weeks after a new gecko comes home, and do not start until it is eating regularly.
  • Approach from the side. A hand that comes in from the side is easier on it than one that scoops from above.
  • Support the whole body and let it walk across your hands rather than gripping it. Keep early sessions short.
  • Stay low, over a bed, couch or the floor. A gecko that walks off a hand at chest height is a fall risk.
  • Not right after a meal, and not during a shed.

Reading it in the hand

This species is more vocal than most pet geckos, and the sounds mean something. In the hand, a chirp or a squeak signals discomfort: the cue to ease off and put it down. A slow tail-swish combined with an arched back is a clearer "leave me alone". A slow tail-wave while stalking an insect is something else: hunting.

Never by the tail Like most geckos, a fat-tail drops its tail if grabbed roughly or badly frightened. This is called autotomy. The tail regrows within weeks, but comes back smoother and more bulbous, and the fat reserve it carried is gone. Never grab or restrain the tail, and tell every visitor the same.

Handling as a health check

Each session is the time to look the gecko over: a plump tail, no old skin on the toes or tail tip, clear eyes. Weigh it on a kitchen scale that reads in single grams and log the number once a month (page {{P:ownerlog}}).

Salmonella Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella bacteria in their droppings without looking ill. A United States public health investigation into infections linked to pet geckos, closed in January 2026, counted 113 cases and 31 hospitalizations across 36 states. Wash your hands with soap after touching the gecko, its food or anything in its enclosure. Keep it and its gear out of the kitchen, and never clean gear in a kitchen sink or a bathtub people use: keep a plastic tub for it. Do not kiss the gecko. Children under 5 should not handle reptiles at all, and adults over 65, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system are at higher risk.

Shedding, seasons, sounds & behavior

The shed cycle

Like a leopard gecko, a fat-tail goes through a complete shed cycle, in which the old outer skin loosens and comes off in pieces. In the days before a shed the colors go dull and the gecko hides more and may eat less. Fat-tails usually eat their shed skin, which recovers some of the nutrients in it, so an owner who never sees a shed is not missing anything. The day after, check the toes, the tail tip and both eyes.

Shed week routine Check the humid hide is damp and refresh it. No handling during the shed. If a meal is refused, skip it rather than pushing food. The day after, look at toes, tail tip and eyes in good light. Anything still attached gets the soak on page {{P:shed}}, never a fingernail.

Seasons

As daylight shortens in fall and winter, adults commonly eat less or stop for a period, and juveniles usually keep eating through it. That is normal in a healthy adult holding its weight. A cooler, drier winter cycle is also used. A humid hide that never dries matters more than an exact seasonal number, and the targets here hold all year. Weigh every two weeks through any slowdown: a resting gecko lives off its tail and should not lose real weight.

Enrichment, by priority

No enrichment study has been run on this species. The closest evidence is a 2016 study of leopard geckos, the same family, by Bashaw and colleagues, which tested five types of enrichment: thermal and feeding enrichment produced the strongest responses, and mirror-based visual enrichment the weakest. Where fat-tails differ is humidity, which they need more of, and heat choice, where their own females pick hotter spots.

1. A properly maintained humid hide

A standing feature, never allowed to dry. Everything else on this list comes after it.

2. Thermal choice

Hides at both ends, so the gecko is hidden and at its chosen temperature at once, and a warm end that reaches the top of the range rather than sitting near it.

3. Feeding as hunting

Tong feeding, short supervised insect hunts in the enclosure, and varying when and where prey appears, all in the evening.

4. Floor space and digging

Cork flats, stacked slate and a deep patch of diggable substrate do more than any branch.

5. A laying box for any female

Husbandry and enrichment at once: a covered box she can dig in, over the warm end.

6. Novelty, last

A new piece of cork now and then. Which object a gecko takes to is individual. Skip mirrors, which ranked last, and never add a second gecko as enrichment.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a gecko, quarantine & the law

The healthiest gecko is chosen well, kept apart until it has proved itself, and legal where you live.

Captive-bred, not wild-caught

For years most fat-tails offered for sale were wild-caught adults, exported from West Africa through Ghana, Togo and Benin, and wild-caught animals still turn up. Captive breeding has since caught up. Buy captive-bred. A parasite load matters most in a wild-caught gecko, and a new or wild-caught gecko gets a fecal exam. Ask the seller four things: the hatch date, whether it was captive-bred, what it eats and when it last ate, and whether it has been treated for anything. A good breeder answers all four without looking anything up.

Pass on any gecko showing a tail thinner than its neck, sunken eyes, skin stuck on the toes or tail tip, difficulty breathing, diarrhea, an injury, or real lethargy in the evening when it should be awake.

Quarantine: 3 to 6 months

If the new gecko will ever share a room, the air or your hands with another reptile, quarantine it. Quarantine for 3 to 6 months; the 60 to 90 days often repeated is the floor, not the target.

A separate room, not a second tank on the same shelf
Paper towel substrate, which shows mites, stool and a regurgitated meal
Its own tongs, dishes and scraper, never shared
Established animals cared for first, the new one last, hands washed between
A vet exam with a fecal test inside the window
Disinfect everything it touched before reuse

A clear fecal result is a good sign, not the end of quarantine: the window runs its full length. If this is and will remain your only reptile, the collection argument does not apply, but an early vet visit with a fecal test is still worth it.

Never release a gecko outdoors Not a healthy one, not an unwanted one. A gecko you cannot keep goes back to the breeder, to a rescue, or to a reptile society.

Where it is legal

The African fat-tailed gecko is legal to keep in most of the United States, and no state-by-state review of this species has been done for this package, so it carries no state table. Some rules catch every lizard or every gecko regardless of species: the District of Columbia's list of allowed animals includes no lizard; Hawaii allows an animal only if it appears on its approved lists, so anything never written down is barred; West Virginia treats a reptile brought in from out of state as imported wildlife that needs a permit; New Jersey's permit list covers geckos as a family; and Maine clears geckos for captive-bred animals only. Your state wildlife agency is the answer that counts, and a city or a landlord can be stricter than the state.

Sexing, weight & body condition

Telling male from female

Sexing is done by looking at the underside near the vent, the single rear opening that waste and eggs pass through, and it only works on maturing animals. Females mature at roughly 250 to 300 days after hatching, males at 300 to 350 days, and males grow larger than females from around then. A mature male shows:

  • A chevron-shaped row of enlarged preanal pores just in front of the vent: small openings that produce a waxy scent secretion.
  • A proportionately more bulbous tail base behind the vent, where the hemipenes, the paired male reproductive organs, sit.

Females lack the enlarged pore row and have a flatter tail base. Never hold the gecko by the tail to look.

Hatchlings: the incubation clue

Sex in this species is set by the temperature the egg was incubated at, which is called temperature-dependent sex determination. A breeder who records it can tell you which way a clutch was likely to go:

Incubated atMostly
24 to 28°C (75 to 82°F)Female
29 to 30.5°C (84 to 87°F)A roughly even split
31 to 32°C (88 to 90°F)Male

A temperature is a probability. A young gecko sold as "probable female" gets checked again once the pores appear, and it matters: two males must never share an enclosure.

Weight

Adults weigh 40 to 90 g, averaging about 50 g. The useful number is your own gecko's trend. A loss of 5 g is a tenth of an average adult and invisible by eye until the tail has already thinned. Weigh monthly, more often if anything looks off, and log it.

Body condition at a glance

Too thin

A tail thinner than the neck. A tail that thins to skin over bone, sometimes called stick tail, means underweight and a vet check for parasites. Rule out a cool warm side first.

Right

A plump tail, as thick as the neck or thicker; in a well-fed adult it can run about 1.25 in (3 cm) thick. Weight steady in an adult, rising in a juvenile.

Too heavy

Obesity in reptiles comes from too many calories and too little activity. Bring it down slowly over months, with the vet, by cutting fatty treats and spacing feedings. Not a crash diet.

Females, eggs & the laying box

A female kept entirely alone can still produce eggs. This page is for every female owner, not just breeders.

Eggs without a male

Female egg-laying reptiles can form eggs with no male present, so every adult female is at risk of egg binding, a female unable to pass the eggs she has formed. Those eggs are infertile and will not hatch, but the physical work of forming and laying them is the same. Females can also store sperm from an earlier mating, so a female who once lived with a male can lay fertile eggs later.

The laying season

WhatTypical
Breeding seasonRoughly 4 to 5 months a year
Eggs per clutch1 to 3
ClutchesUp to 5 in a season, many females fewer, often 15 to 22 days apart
AppetiteMay drop for a few days before laying
If fertile: incubation6 to 12 weeks, shorter when warmer

Signs she is about to lay

  • Restlessness and repeated digging, in corners, in the substrate or in the humid hide.
  • Going off food for a few days.

Give her somewhere to lay

A missing or unsuitable nesting site is one of the recognized causes of egg binding, and it is the easiest to remove. Every adult female gets a laying box: a covered box of moist substrate, such as damp sphagnum moss or a damp soil mix, deep enough for her to dig into, set over the warm end. Fat-tail females are particular about temperature when they lay: given a choice of temperatures, they picked nest sites averaging 32.4°C (about 90°F), warmer than leopard geckos chose, so the box belongs where the warm side reaches. Leave her alone with it.

After she lays Remove infertile eggs, which will not hatch. Offer food and water. Weigh her, so the next clutch is readable against this one. Poor nutrition is among the causes of egg binding, so a laying female's calcium dusting is not the place to cut corners.
Egg binding is a same-day vet call Straining, a swollen vent, restlessness that does not settle, repeated digging with nothing produced, or a gravid female, one carrying eggs, who stops eating and turns dull. Page {{P:eggbinding}} has the full entry.
