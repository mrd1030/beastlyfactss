Choosing a gecko, quarantine & the law

The healthiest gecko is chosen well, kept apart until it has proved itself, and legal where you live.

Captive-bred, not wild-caught

For years most fat-tails offered for sale were wild-caught adults, exported from West Africa through Ghana, Togo and Benin, and wild-caught animals still turn up. Captive breeding has since caught up. Buy captive-bred. A parasite load matters most in a wild-caught gecko, and veterinary care sheets single out new and wild-caught geckos for fecal exams. Ask the seller four things: the hatch date, whether it was captive-bred, what it eats and when it last ate, and whether it has been treated for anything. A good breeder answers all four without looking anything up.

Pass on any gecko showing a tail thinner than its neck, sunken eyes, skin stuck on the toes or tail tip, difficulty breathing, diarrhea, an injury, or real lethargy in the evening when it should be awake.

Quarantine: 3 to 6 months

If the new gecko will ever share a room, the air or your hands with another reptile, quarantine it. Veterinary husbandry guidance gives 3 to 6 months for a new reptile; the 60 to 90 days often repeated in the hobby is the floor, not the target.

A separate room, not a second tank on the same shelf
Paper towel substrate, which shows mites, stool and a regurgitated meal
Its own tongs, dishes and scraper, never shared
Established animals cared for first, the new one last, hands washed between
A vet exam with a fecal test inside the window
Disinfect everything it touched before reuse

A clear fecal result is a good sign, not the end of quarantine: the window runs its full length. If this is and will remain your only reptile, the collection argument does not apply, but an early vet visit with a fecal test is still worth it.

Never release a gecko outdoors Not a healthy one, not an unwanted one. A gecko you cannot keep goes back to the breeder, to a rescue, or to a reptile society.

Where it is legal

The African fat-tailed gecko is legal to keep in most of the United States, and no state-by-state review of this species has been done for this package, so it carries no state table. Some rules catch every lizard or every gecko regardless of species: the District of Columbia's list of allowed animals includes no lizard; Hawaii allows an animal only if it appears on its approved lists, so anything never written down is barred; West Virginia treats a reptile brought in from out of state as imported wildlife that needs a permit; New Jersey's permit list covers geckos as a family; and Maine clears geckos for captive-bred animals only. Your state wildlife agency is the answer that counts, and a city or a landlord can be stricter than the state.

Enrichment, by priority

No enrichment study has been run on this species. The closest evidence is a 2016 study of leopard geckos, the same family, by Bashaw and colleagues, which tested five types of enrichment: thermal and feeding enrichment produced the strongest responses, and mirror-based visual enrichment the weakest. Where fat-tails differ is humidity, which they need more of, and heat choice, where their own females pick hotter spots.

1. A properly maintained humid hide

A standing feature, never allowed to dry. Everything else on this list comes after it.

2. Thermal choice

Hides at both ends, so the gecko is hidden and at its chosen temperature at once, and a warm end that reaches the top of the range rather than sitting near it.

3. Feeding as hunting

Tong feeding, short supervised insect hunts in the enclosure, and varying when and where prey appears, all in the evening.

4. Floor space and digging

Cork flats, stacked slate and a deep patch of diggable substrate do more than any branch.

5. A laying box for any female

Husbandry and enrichment at once: a covered box she can dig in, over the warm end.

6. Novelty, last

A new piece of cork now and then. Which object a gecko takes to is individual. Skip mirrors, which ranked last, and never add a second gecko as enrichment.

Shedding, seasons, sounds & behavior

The shed cycle

Like a leopard gecko, a fat-tail goes through a complete shed cycle, in which the old outer skin loosens and comes off in pieces. In the days before a shed the colors go dull and the gecko hides more and may eat less. Fat-tails usually eat their shed skin, which recovers some of the nutrients in it, so an owner who never sees a shed is not missing anything. The day after, check the toes, the tail tip and both eyes.

Shed week routine Check the humid hide is damp and refresh it. No handling during the shed. If a meal is refused, skip it rather than pushing food. The day after, look at toes, tail tip and eyes in good light. Anything still attached gets the soak on page {{P:shed}}, never a fingernail.

Seasons

As daylight shortens in fall and winter, adults commonly eat less or stop for a period, and juveniles usually keep eating through it. That is normal in a healthy adult holding its weight. Some keepers, and one veterinary care sheet, run a cooler, drier winter cycle. A humid hide that never dries matters more than chasing an exact seasonal number, and this book uses one set of targets all year. Weigh every two weeks through any slowdown: a resting gecko lives off its tail and should not lose real weight.

Health red flags & finding a vet

Fat-tails are hardy when their two biggest needs, heat and humidity, are met. Nearly every common problem traces back to one of them.

See a reptile vet promptly for Wheezing, mucus at the nose or mouth, or open-mouth breathing. Lethargy, difficulty standing or walking, swollen joints, muscle twitches or a curving spine. Bloating, straining and appetite loss together. A female straining or digging with nothing produced. Retained shed tightening around a toe or the tail tip that home care has not cleared. Cloudiness, swelling or discharge in an eye outside a normal shed. Real weight loss, or a tail thinner than the neck. Tissue pushed out of the vent. Sunken eyes, diarrhea or an injury.

Finding a vet before you need one

Not every practice treats reptiles. The ARAV (Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians) keeps a find-a-vet directory, and when you call a clinic, ask how many reptiles it sees. Book an annual wellness exam with a fecal test even when nothing is wrong: it covers body condition, hydration and nutrition, and the weight log is the most useful thing to bring. A routine exam commonly runs $35 to $105 and a fecal test $65 to $110; a sick visit with tests is typically $150 to $300 or more.

Why weight comes up first A reptile hides illness until it is advanced, and a 50 g gecko hides weight loss until the tail has already thinned. A monthly number on a gram scale turns "seems thinner" into a fact a vet can use.

Retained shed & eye problems

The most common fat-tail-specific problem, and one of the most common reasons it sees a vet.

Retained shed (dysecdysis)

CauseHumidity too low, or no humid hide, or one that has dried out. Less often parasites, poor nutrition, infection, or nothing rough to rub against. Dysecdysis means an abnormal or incomplete shed
SignsOld skin left on the toes and the tail tip, and sometimes over the eyes. A ring of old skin left around a toe after the rest of the shed has come off
ResponseRaise humidity with a properly damp humid hide. Soak the gecko for 15 to 30 minutes in shallow, warm water, never left alone, then ease the softened skin off a toe with a damp cotton swab. Repeat over a couple of days rather than forcing it. Never peel dry skin. See a vet if a ring has already tightened, the toe is swollen or darkened, or tissue is dying. A vet may soften it over hours and remove it under magnification
RecoveryCaught early, home care and a corrected hide usually clear it. Skin left to constrict cuts off circulation and can cost the tip of a toe or tail, and that loss is permanent
Prevention is one box A proper moist hide and correct humidity prevent almost all of this. Check toes, tail tip and eyes the day after every shed, and keep the moss damp all year, not only when a shed is due.

Eye problems

CauseReported relatively often in this species, and named by one exotic practice as its second most common problem. Retained eye caps from a bad shed are the usual cause. Vitamin A deficiency can swell the eyelids, and infection can follow either
SignsCloudiness, swelling or discharge outside a normal shed cycle, or an eye held closed
ResponseA vet, promptly. Retained eye caps need veterinary attention rather than home removal. Never try to take anything off an eye yourself. Raise the humid hide's moisture in the meantime
RecoveryFollows the cause. The vet removes a retained cap safely, sometimes after softening it first. Fix the humidity, or it comes back with the next shed

Metabolic bone disease & vitamin problems

The most serious common problem in this species, and entirely a diet and setup disease.

MBD (metabolic bone disease)

CauseA shortage of calcium or vitamin D3, often with no UVB. A poor calcium to phosphorus balance in the diet, or a warm side that runs too cool, adds to it. Veterinarians call it nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism
SignsLethargy, difficulty standing or walking, swollen joints, muscle twitches, bones that feel unusually flexible, a curving spine. Later, a swollen or misshapen jaw, fractures from small falls, and loss of appetite
DiagnosisA vet exam with x-rays, which show thin, poorly mineralized bone and any fractures, and blood tests
TreatmentThe vet may give fluids, feeding support and calcium by injection if blood calcium is low. Correcting the diet and the setup is the core of treatment. The vet sets any dose; do not add extra D3 at home
RecoveryThe outlook is favorable, especially if the gecko is still eating. Caught early it can be managed, but deformities already present are permanent, and advanced cases can be fatal
In the meantime Any tremor, any softness in the jaw or new trouble walking is a vet appointment this week. Handle the gecko as little as possible, and take out anything it can climb and fall from: weak bone breaks on a drop that would normally mean nothing.

Vitamin A deficiency

CauseA diet short of preformed vitamin A, the ready-made form. Many reptiles cannot convert the plant form, beta carotene, so a multivitamin with preformed vitamin A is the prevention
SignsPoor growth, poor appetite, swollen eyelids, mouth inflammation, and respiratory infections that follow
DiagnosisFrom the diet history and the exam
Treatment and recoveryDiet correction and a supplement with preformed vitamin A, as the vet directs. Recovery follows the secondary problems, so the earlier the better

Gout

CauseA buildup of uric acid that deposits in joints or organs. Too much protein, or dehydration and kidney trouble. A calcium powder that contains phosphorus can damage the kidneys and lead to it
SignsPain severe enough that the gecko will not move, eat or drink. Gritty or discolored urates are the early warning of the same uric acid chemistry
Diagnosis and treatmentX-rays show the deposits. The vet corrects the diet or the underlying problem and may prescribe long-term medication
RecoveryTypically poor once advanced. Prevention is water, the humid hide, and phosphorus-free calcium

Impaction & respiratory infection

One comes from what goes into the mouth, the other from the air the gecko sits in.

Impaction

CauseA blockage of the gut. A ground gecko feeding on loose substrate can swallow some with its prey, and prey wider than the space between the eyes can lodge. It mostly happens in a gecko that is already unhealthy, dehydrated or kept too cool, because a cold gecko cannot move food through
SignsBloating, lethargy, appetite loss and straining, often with no normal stool
DiagnosisA vet exam and x-rays, which show the blockage or swallowed material
ResponseA vet visit, not a home remedy. Check the warm hide floor reads 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C) the same day, and move the gecko onto paper towel
RecoveryDepends on what was blocking and how it had to be removed. Afterward, paper towel until the vet clears it, correctly sized prey from tongs, and a warm side checked weekly
Prevention Paper towel for juveniles, quarantine and unwell geckos. A soil and sand mix rather than pure sand for adults. Prey no wider than the space between the eyes, offered from tongs or a dish. A warm side that is actually warm.

Respiratory infection

CauseAn enclosure kept too cool, too damp, or both. Poor hygiene, poor nutrition and vitamin A deficiency add to the risk
SignsWheezing, mucus, open-mouth breathing and lethargy. Discharge from the nose or eyes, and fast or labored breathing
DiagnosisA vet exam, often with x-rays of the chest
TreatmentA vet and antibiotics. It will not clear on its own. The vet sets the drug and the dose. At home: clean conditions, the warm side at target, and the gecko kept toward the middle to upper end of its temperature range, which helps the immune system and thins the mucus
RecoveryTreatment works best started early, with the setup fixed at the same time. A severe or drawn-out infection can spread into the bloodstream
The common thread Any gecko with either of these gets its warm hide floor, cool side and enclosure humidity measured the same day. The fix is usually in those three numbers, and the vet will ask for them.
