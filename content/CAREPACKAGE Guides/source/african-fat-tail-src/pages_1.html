How the numbers are written

Every temperature is given in °F (degrees Fahrenheit) and °C (degrees Celsius), and every length as in (inches) and cm (centimeters). Weights are in g (grams), because this gecko is small enough that a change of a few grams matters. Every abbreviation and technical word is spelled out the first time it appears.

Why this package exists The fat-tail is usually sold as a leopard gecko with a different pattern, and set up from a leopard gecko care sheet. Most of that sheet transfers. The part that does not is moisture: this gecko comes from the savannas of West Africa rather than the dry rock country of southern Asia, it needs a wetter enclosure and a humid hide that is never allowed to dry, and the most common fat-tail health problem traces straight back to that gap. Every figure here is cross-checked against the veterinary sources listed on page {{P:sources}}.
Not a substitute for A veterinarian who sees reptiles. This is a husbandry reference for day-to-day care, and anything urgent is a professional call, not a page in this book. Not every practice treats reptiles, so find one before you need one and write the number on page {{P:emergency}}.

Cost overview

Initial setup

The gecko is $75 to $600, with normals commonly around $100 and fancier color lines, called morphs, costing more. Equipment comes to $369 to $579. With a first exam and fecal test (a stool check for parasites), plan on $469 to $794 before the gecko. Page {{P:budget}}.

Ongoing

Feeders and supplements run $10 to $25 a month, or $120 to $300 a year. With a yearly exam and fecal test, a healthy adult runs about $220 to $515 a year. A sick visit with tests is typically $150 to $300 or more.

The number that should drive the decision Not the price. The lifespan. Over 15 years, the yearly costs alone come to about $3,300 to $7,700 on top of the gecko and the first setup. The purchase is the cheap part, and the routine on page {{P:routine}} is what you are actually signing up for.
Section 01 · Quick Profile

Fat-tail or leopard gecko? The differences

Same family, same shape, same shop shelf. Not the same care sheet.

The African fat-tailed gecko and the leopard gecko are both eublepharids: ground-dwelling geckos with eyelids, no climbing pads, a fat-storing tail, and a habit of dropping that tail when grabbed. Most of what a leopard gecko owner knows does transfer. Three things do not: humidity, temperament and origin. A fat-tail kept to a leopard gecko sheet usually goes wrong on humidity.

Why humidity is the one that matters

Stuck shed on the toes and the tip of the tail is the most common fat-tail-specific health complaint, and it shows up when the enclosure runs too dry or the humid hide is missing or dried out. A ring of old skin left on a toe tightens as it dries and can cut off the blood supply. A leopard gecko sheet, followed exactly, produces the drier enclosure that causes it. The fix is not a wet tank: the enclosure stays mostly dry, at 50 to 70%, and the moisture lives in one damp hide. Page {{P:humidity}} has the setup and page {{P:shed}} the problem.

Do not raise humidity everywhere Going the other way is its own mistake. An enclosure that is too damp and too cool at the same time is what causes respiratory infection in this species (page {{P:impaction}}). Humid in the hide, moderate in the air, warm at one end.
Same family, same habits What carries straight across from a leopard gecko: belly heat from a mat on a thermostat, three hides, live insects dusted with calcium, a tail that is never a handhold, and the tail as the body-condition gauge. If you have kept a leopard gecko, you already know most of this book. Read the humidity and substrate pages twice.
