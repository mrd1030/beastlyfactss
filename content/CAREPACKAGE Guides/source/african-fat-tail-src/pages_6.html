Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

Empty, rinse and refill the water dish
Squeeze the humid hide moss: damp, not dripping, not dry
Read the second thermometer, apart from the thermostat probe
Remove droppings and uneaten insects; glance at stool and urates
See the gecko at dusk. One that is not out in its own activity window is the earliest soft signal there is

Feeding days

Evening feeding on the age schedule, from tongs, dusted per the day
Next batch of insects on gut-load 24 to 48 hours ahead

Weekly

Infrared gun on the warm hide floor; hygrometer reading logged
Wipe down hides, dish and glass
Check the thermostat probe is still in place on the warm hide floor
Look at the tail and fill in a row of the Owner Log

Monthly

Weigh in grams and log it (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Deep clean and replace the substrate; replace the moss
Review the log for slow trends, not single readings

Seasonal

Fall: expect appetite to drop in an adult. Weigh every two weeks through it
Winter: check the night low in the room. Below 70°F (21°C), add heat with no light
Winter: heating dries the air. Watch the hygrometer and the humid hide
Summer: check the warm side on the hottest afternoon. The thermostat should be cutting out
Breeding season, for a female: laying box damp and in place

Yearly

Wellness exam and fecal test
Replace the UVB tube, even if it still lights
Replace opened supplement powders
Re-read pages {{P:checklist}} and {{P:emergency}} against the real setup. Things drift
If you only keep two habits The daily squeeze of the humid hide and the monthly weigh-in. One prevents this gecko's most common problem, and the other catches most of the rest before they show.
Section 07 · Owner Tools

Power outages, travel & transport

A fat-tail's normal night low is 70 to 75°F (21 to 24°C). Act once it is heading below 70°F (21°C). A small gecko loses heat faster than a heavy-bodied snake in the same room.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Put a battery or wind-up thermometer in the enclosure so you know the real number.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, or move the gecko in a ventilated tub to the warmest room, usually one without outside walls.
  • Set a wrapped hot water bottle or a commercial hand warmer beside the gecko, never touching it.
  • Stop feeding insects until heat is reliably back. Keep water available and the humid hide damp.
  • Past a day or two below the floor, board it with a vet, an experienced friend with power, or a reptile shop.

Don't

  • No candles, gas stoves or open flame near the enclosure: a fire and carbon monoxide risk.
  • No heat pack against the skin. A cold, sluggish gecko cannot move off it and will burn.
  • No food "to keep its strength up". Undigested food in a cold gut is a risk, not a benefit.
  • No hot water to warm a cold, unresponsive gecko.
Outage kit Two or three hand warmers or a hot water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated tub, and both vet numbers written on paper.

Leaving town

Trip lengthAdultJuvenile, or a gecko that is sick, shedding or gravid
1 to 2 nightsOften fine alone. Timers and thermostat running, fresh water and a damp humid hide before you goA sitter once a day for food, water, the hide and a temperature check
3 to 7 nightsA sitter every 1 to 2 days for water, the humid hide, a stool check and a temperature glance. Feed on the normal scheduleA sitter daily, with pre-portioned insects and written dusting days
Over a weekA daily sitter who has done a practice visit with you, or boarding with a reptile-experienced vet or shop

Transport and vet trips

  • A ventilated plastic tub with a towel to grip beats a wire carrier: it holds heat. Carry it in a dark bag or box. No loose substrate, no open water dish.
  • Skip the meal before travel. Warm the car first, and for any trip over about 30 minutes below 65°F (18°C), put a wrapped hand warmer beside the tub, never against the gecko.
  • Check the tub every 20 to 30 minutes at a stop. Never leave the gecko in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly.
  • Moving house: set up the new enclosure and check its temperatures before the gecko arrives.

Glossary, A to I

Every abbreviation and technical word used in this package, in plain words.

AmelanisticA morph lacking dark pigment, so it looks paler than a normal fat-tail.
ARAVAssociation of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians, which keeps a find-a-vet directory.
AutotomyDropping the tail on purpose when grabbed or frightened. The tail regrows within weeks, smoother and more bulbous.
Beta caroteneThe plant form of vitamin A, which many reptiles cannot convert. Hence a multivitamin with preformed vitamin A.
Black soldier fly larvaeA feeder grub that, unlike most feeders, carries close to as much calcium as phosphorus.
Calcium to phosphorus ratioHow much calcium a food holds against phosphorus. About 2 to 1 is the target; feeder insects run far below it.
Captive-bredHatched and raised in captivity. The opposite of wild-caught, and the one to buy.
CDCCenters for Disease Control and Prevention, the United States public health agency that tracks Salmonella outbreaks.
Ceramic heat emitterA screw-in heater that gives heat and no light, for cold nights. Always on its own thermostat.
CloacaThe chamber just inside the vent that waste, urates and eggs all pass through.
ClutchOne laying of eggs: 1 to 3 in this species.
CrepuscularMost active at dusk and dawn.
DustingCoating feeder insects in supplement powder just before they are offered.
DysecdysisAn abnormal or incomplete shed: retained shed. Page {{P:shed}}.
DystociaEgg binding: a female unable to pass eggs she has formed. A same-day vet problem. Page {{P:eggbinding}}.
EublepharidA member of the eyelid gecko family, Eublepharidae: the fat-tail, the leopard gecko and their relatives.
Fecal testA vet's check of a fresh stool sample for parasites.
Ferguson ZoneOne of four groups that reptiles are sorted into by how much ultraviolet light they seek in the wild. The fat-tail is Zone 1, the shade dwellers.
GoutA painful buildup of uric acid in the joints or organs. Page {{P:mbd}}.
GravidCarrying eggs.
Gut-loadingFeeding feeder insects a calcium-rich diet for 24 to 48 hours before they are offered, so the gecko eats it too. Page {{P:supplements}}.
HemipenesThe paired male reproductive organs, which sit in the base of the tail and make it look more bulbous.
Humid hideA closed hide of damp moss at 70 to 80% humidity inside, in an enclosure that runs 50 to 70%. Page {{P:humidity}}.
HygrometerA humidity meter. Digital ones are worth the small extra cost.
ImpactionA blockage of the gut, from swallowed substrate or prey too large to pass. Page {{P:impaction}}.
Infrared temperature gunA handheld thermometer that reads the temperature of a surface from a distance. The way to check the warm hide floor.
IUCNInternational Union for Conservation of Nature, which keeps the Red List of species at risk. The fat-tail is listed as Least Concern.
Reference

Glossary, M to Z

MBDMetabolic bone disease: weak, poorly mineralized bone from too little calcium or vitamin D3. Page {{P:mbd}}.
MitesTiny parasites that live on a reptile's skin. Paper towel during quarantine shows them.
MorphA captive-bred color or pattern line, such as amelanistic, Oreo, Whiteout or Zulu.
NocturnalActive at night, asleep by day.
Obligate insectivoreAn animal that must eat insects and cannot use plant food. That is the fat-tail.
Oxalic acidA compound in spinach, beet greens and chard, also called oxalates, that binds calcium so it cannot be absorbed.
OviductThe tube a female's eggs pass down before they are laid.
Preanal poresThe chevron-shaped row of small openings in front of the vent, enlarged in males, which produce a waxy scent. Page {{P:sexing}}.
Preformed vitamin AVitamin A in its ready-made animal form, which reptiles can use directly.
ProbeThe thermostat's sensor. It goes flat on the warm hide floor. Page {{P:heat}}.
ProlapseAn organ pushed out through the vent and stuck outside. Same-day vet. Page {{P:eggbinding}}.
Pulse proportional thermostatA controller that sends short bursts of power to hold a steady temperature. A dimming one turns power down like a light dimmer. Both suit fast heat sources; an on/off unit suits a mat.
QuarantineKeeping a new animal apart, in another room, for 3 to 6 months before it shares air, tools or hands with others. Page {{P:arrival}}.
SalmonellaBacteria that healthy reptiles carry in their droppings and that can make people ill. Hand-washing is the control. Page {{P:handling}}.
Stick tailA tail wasted down to skin over bone: an underweight gecko that needs a vet and a parasite check.
StomatitisMouth rot: infection and inflammation of the lining of the mouth. Page {{P:minor}}.
SubstrateWhatever covers the enclosure floor: paper towel, or a soil and sand mix.
Temperature-dependent sex determinationWhen the egg's incubation temperature, not genes, decides whether the hatchling is male or female. Page {{P:sexing}}.
Thermal gradientThe warm-to-cool range from one end of the enclosure to the other, which lets the gecko choose its temperature.
ThermostatThe controller that switches the heat source to hold a set temperature. The device that prevents burns.
UratesThe white to cream, chalky part of a dropping: uric acid, a reptile's version of urine. Yellow or gritty means dehydrated. Page {{P:poop}}.
Uric acidThe form most reptiles pass nitrogen waste in. When it builds up in the body, it is gout.
UVBUltraviolet B, the band of ultraviolet light a reptile uses to make vitamin D3 in its skin. Glass blocks most of it. Page {{P:lighting}}.
UVIUltraviolet index, the measured strength of UVB where the gecko sits. Zone 1 target: 0 to 0.7 across the enclosure, 0.6 to 1.4 at the closest point.
VentThe single rear opening that waste, urates and eggs pass through.
Vitamin D3The vitamin that lets a reptile absorb and use calcium. Made in the skin under UVB, or taken in from calcium with D3 powder. Builds up if overdosed.
Wild-caughtTaken from the wild rather than bred. Higher parasite risk and fecal tests matter most. Buy captive-bred.
