Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameCorrelophus ciliatus
Adult size7 to 9 in (18 to 23 cm)
Captive lifespan15 to 20+ years typical, some individuals living into their 30s
Native rangeNew Caledonia, South Pacific
Diet typeOmnivore; commercial crested gecko diet (CGD) as the staple, insects as an occasional supplement
Enclosure styleVertical and arboreal; more height than floor space, dense climbing structure
Heating needsMinimal; most homes need no supplemental heat at all
Handling difficultyBeginner, but delicate; tail loss (autotomy) is permanent

Cost overview

Initial setup

$300 to $600 typical, covering the gecko, enclosure, heating if your home needs it, thermostat, substrate, and furnishings.

Ongoing costs

CGD powder, occasional feeder insects, and misting/humidity supplies. Modest compared to setup, since heating needs are minimal for most homes.

Budget for the worst case too Emergency care isn't rare enough to ignore. Impaction and severe stuck shed can both require a vet visit and, occasionally, a minor procedure. Neither is likely if you follow this package, but both are real enough to plan for.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Crested geckos were considered extinct for decades until they were rediscovered on New Caledonia in 1994.
  • They have no eyelids and lick their own eyes clean instead of blinking.
  • Sticky toe pads plus claws let them climb almost any surface, including glass.

Housing & enclosure

Crested geckos are arboreal and live in the vertical space, not on the floor. A tall enclosure with dense climbing structure matters more than floor footprint. This species can be sensitive to layout changes once settled in.

Never Let temperatures anywhere in the enclosure exceed 85°F. Crested geckos overheat easily, and heat stress can turn dangerous fast. Most homes kept at normal room temperature need no supplemental heat at all.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Rearrange only occasionally, not with full overhauls, once your gecko is settled in.
  • A humidity spike from evening misting, then a genuine daytime dry-down, matters as much as any single temperature number (page 6).

Measure at gecko height with a digital thermometer and hygrometer, not by feel. Most homes need no added heat at all; add a low-wattage heat source only if your home runs cooler than the cool-area target.

Humidity needs a real cycle, not constant dampness

Mist in the evening to spike humidity toward 60 to 80%, then let it dry down to 40 to 50% by day. The dry period matters as much as the misting: an enclosure kept soggy around the clock invites skin and scale problems and doesn't match how this species actually lives (page 11).

UVB (optional)

Not strictly required, since a complete crested gecko diet (CGD) already supplies vitamin D3. Low-level, low-UVI UVB is commonly recommended as a health benefit if used; this species doesn't need the strong output a basking reptile requires.

Photoperiod

Roughly a 12 hour photoperiod: lights or UVB on for about half the day and off the other half, on a consistent schedule.

Nice to have A lighting timer and a misting reminder keep the photoperiod and humidity cycle consistent even on busy days.

Diet & feeding

A commercial crested gecko diet (CGD) powder mixed with water is the staple, complete diet for this species, not a supplement to something else. Occasional, appropriately sized insects can be offered as enrichment and a supplement, not as the base diet.

CGD basics

Mix CGD powder with water to the consistency the packaging recommends
Offer fresh, and replace it on the schedule the product recommends since it can spoil
Place the dish within the climbing structure, not only on the floor
Vary the dish location occasionally to encourage natural foraging

Insects, as enrichment, not the base diet

Offer appropriately sized insects occasionally alongside CGD. Size prey to your gecko, not the other way around: anything noticeably wider than the space between its eyes is a poor choice.

Never feed Insects sized wider than your gecko's head. This is a real impaction risk, covered in more detail on page 12.

Hydration

CGD supplies meaningful hydration on its own, and evening misting gives your gecko water droplets to drink directly off leaves and enclosure surfaces, which many crested geckos prefer over a still water dish. Offer a shallow water dish anyway as a backup source.

Handling

Handling a crested gecko is delicate, and one wrong grab can cost a tail permanently. Patience here isn't optional.

Wait until your gecko is ready

Hold off on regular handling until your gecko reaches at least 8 to 15 grams (around 10 g minimum), about 4 to 5 inches long, and roughly 6 months old. Give a newly arrived gecko about 2 weeks to acclimate before any handling beyond feeding and cleaning.

Session length

Start sessions at 1 to 2 minutes
Build up gradually toward around 15 minutes
20 minute per day cap, across all sessions combined
Support the full body, never grip or restrain
Never Grab or restrain by the tail. Tail loss (autotomy) is a real defense mechanism, and it is permanent, the tail will not regrow the way it did before. Let your gecko move across your hands rather than gripping it.

Reading your gecko

A gecko that jumps away, vocalizes (a soft chirp or bark), or repeatedly tries to bolt is telling you the session is over. End calmly rather than pushing through it; repeated stressful handling early on makes every future session harder, not easier.

Building trust Short, low-pressure sessions started only after full acclimation build tolerance far faster than one long session too soon. Let your gecko climb onto your hand voluntarily when possible.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Adding supplemental heat "just in case" and pushing any zone above 85°F, which is genuinely dangerous for this species.
  • Misting once and calling it done, with no real daytime dry-down, keeping the enclosure soggy around the clock.
  • Grabbing or restraining by the tail, causing permanent, avoidable tail loss.
  • Handling before a gecko has acclimated or reached a safe minimum size, weight, and age.
  • Rearranging the whole enclosure at once "to freshen it up," which this species can find genuinely stressful.
  • Skipping vertical climbing space in favor of floor space, this is an arboreal species, not a ground dweller.
  • Using loose or oversized-particle substrate, which adds an impaction risk on top of oversized insects.

Enrichment, by priority

Climbing structure

Branches, vines, and cork bark at varied heights let your gecko use the vertical space it's built for; this also matters directly for preventing floppy tail syndrome (page 12).

Live or realistic plants

Pothos, philodendron, sansevieria, and bromeliads all work well, providing cover, climbing surfaces, and places to rest during the day.

Occasional rearrangement

Reposition or refresh one item occasionally rather than overhauling the whole layout; total resets can be genuinely stressful for this species.

Feeding location variety

Placing the CGD dish at different heights within the climbing structure encourages natural foraging movement.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a vet promptly if you see Sunken eyes, repeated stuck sheds, a visibly limp or drooping tail, straining without producing waste, a visible lump along the belly, lethargy paired with any warm reading in the enclosure, or noticeable weight loss between log entries.

What to tell the vet

Current cool/ambient/basking readings and recent humidity pattern
Weight and shed history from the Owner Log
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening
Diet over the last two weeks: CGD plus any insects offered

Why temperature and humidity come up first

Most of the issues in this section, stuck shed, metabolic bone disease, and floppy tail syndrome, trace back to a temperature, humidity, or enclosure setup problem more often than anything else. A vet will ask about your setup before anything else for exactly that reason.

Stuck shed & metabolic bone disease

Stuck Shed (Dysecdysis)

Cause: humidity running too low, or no real evening spike to trigger a full shed, especially around the toes and eyes. Signs: retained skin on toes or around the eyes days after the rest of the body has shed. Response: raise evening humidity and misting frequency, and gently assist only where skin is already loosening, never force or peel unshed skin. Recovery: most stuck shed resolves within a cycle or two once the humidity cycle is corrected; retained shed left unaddressed on toes can restrict circulation over time.

Prevention beats treatment A consistent evening misting routine that actually spikes humidity into the 60 to 80% range (page 6) prevents most stuck shed before it starts.

Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Cause: inadequate calcium or vitamin D3 relative to need, over time; less common in crested geckos than in some other reptiles since a complete CGD already supplies D3, but still possible. Signs: soft or swollen jaw, difficulty climbing or gripping, tremors. Diagnosis: veterinary physical exam, often with an X-ray to check bone density. Response: vet diagnosis and treatment, this does not self-correct. Recovery: mild, early cases can improve over weeks to months with corrected diet and vet guidance; advanced bone changes often remain even once the disease itself is controlled.

Concrete signal A gecko that struggles to grip branches or climb normally needs a vet visit, not a wait-and-see approach. Climbing difficulty is often the first visible sign.

Floppy tail syndrome, impaction & tail loss

Floppy Tail Syndrome

What it is: a condition specific to crested geckos and related species where the tail droops, kinks, or hangs at an odd angle. Cause: often linked to enclosure setup, especially inadequate vertical and climbing space that changes how a gecko rests and grips. Response: review and improve the climbing structure and enclosure height (page 5); a vet visit rules out other causes if the tail doesn't improve.

Impaction

Cause: loose or oversized-particle substrate, or oversized insects. Signs: straining without producing waste, a visible lump along the belly, reduced appetite. Diagnosis: veterinary physical exam, sometimes an X-ray. Response: vet visit if straining or a lump persists, don't wait to see if it resolves on its own.

Concrete threshold Straining without producing waste, or a firm lump along the belly, is a vet visit, not a watch-and-wait situation.

Tail Loss (Autotomy)

What it is: a crested gecko's own defense mechanism, dropping its tail when it feels grabbed or seriously threatened. Reality check: it is permanent. Unlike some other gecko species, a crested gecko's tail does not grow back the way it did before. It's usually not medically dangerous on its own, but it's entirely preventable: never grab or restrain by the tail (page 8), and minimize situations that startle or corner your gecko.

Also worth knowing Sunken eyes paired with repeated stuck sheds often point to dehydration, check your humidity cycle first. Excess humidity with poor hygiene can lead to skin and scale issues. Persistent diarrhea or poor condition despite correct husbandry can indicate internal parasites, including cryptosporidiosis, which a vet diagnoses with a fecal exam.

First 30 days & days 31 to 90

Week 1

Confirm cool/ambient/basking readings and the humidity swing with real instruments, not by feel
Leave your gecko alone beyond feeding and cleaning; acclimation takes about 2 weeks
Schedule a wellness vet visit within the first few weeks

Week 2 to 3

Continue hands-off beyond feeding while acclimation finishes
Establish a consistent evening misting routine and confirm it actually spikes and dries down
Start the Owner Log on page 19

Week 4

If your gecko meets the size, weight, and age guidance on page 8, begin very short handling sessions
Review weight and shed pattern from the Owner Log

Days 31 to 90

Build handling sessions gradually toward the 15 minute target, staying under the 20 minute daily cap
Look back at the Owner Log for any drift in weight, appetite, or shed frequency

Daily, weekly, monthly & seasonal routine

Daily

Evening misting to spike humidity
Quick visual check: eyes, tail, posture, appetite
Confirm cool/ambient/basking readings
Offer or refresh CGD per label schedule

Weekly

Weigh and log in the Owner Log
Check thermometer/hygrometer batteries and calibration
Spot-clean substrate and wipe enclosure surfaces
Confirm the daytime dry-down is actually happening, not just the evening spike

Monthly

Full substrate change and deeper enclosure clean
Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings
Occasional, partial enrichment rotation, never a full overhaul

Seasonal

Reassess room temperature and humidity swings between seasons, especially with heating/AC changes
Recheck whether any zone risks creeping above 85°F during warm months

Glossary

AutotomyThe voluntary dropping of a tail as a stress or defense response; permanent in crested geckos, the tail does not regrow the same way.
CGDCrested Gecko Diet, a commercial powdered diet mixed with water that serves as this species' staple food.
CryptosporidiosisAn internal parasitic infection, diagnosed with a veterinary fecal exam.
DysecdysisIncomplete or "stuck" shedding, most often affecting the toes and eyes in this species.
Floppy Tail SyndromeA condition in crested geckos and related species where the tail droops or kinks, often linked to inadequate climbing or vertical space.
ImpactionA gastrointestinal blockage, often from loose substrate or oversized insects.
MBDMetabolic Bone Disease: weakened bones from inadequate calcium or vitamin D3.
PhotoperiodThe daily cycle of light and dark hours an enclosure is set to.
