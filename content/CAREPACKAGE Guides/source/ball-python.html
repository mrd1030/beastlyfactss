Cost overview

Initial setup

$255 to $845 for equipment, plus $50 to $150 for the first vet exam. The snake itself is $40 to $100 for a normal, $50 to $150 for a common morph.

Monthly ongoing

$17 to $47 for frozen-thawed rodents, substrate, electricity, and the annual exam averaged out. Roughly $205 to $565 a year.

Budget for the worst case too A sick or emergency visit runs $100 to $500 or more, and imaging or surgery pushes that higher. Neither is likely if you follow this package, but a 20 to 30 year animal will need a vet at some point. Set the money aside now rather than deciding in an emergency.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • The name comes from the defensive pose, not the diet. A stressed ball python curls into a tight ball with its head tucked in the center, which is why a new snake that won't uncurl isn't being difficult. It just hasn't decided yet that hiding is unnecessary.
  • They hunt with heat. Pits along the upper lip detect the body warmth of nearby prey, letting a ball python strike accurately in complete darkness.
  • A brooding female generates her own heat. Through shivering thermogenesis she contracts her muscles repeatedly to keep the clutch warmer than the surrounding air, living off fat reserves for months while she does it.

Housing & enclosure

4×2×2 ft (48×24×24 in, 120×60×60 cm, roughly 120 gallons and about 8 square feet of floor) is the adult standard, and the enclosure should be at least as long as the snake. Scale down while the snake is small: hatchlings up to about 300 g do well in a 10-gallon or 20×11×13 in (50×28×33 cm) enclosure, and juveniles under 3 ft in a 36×18×18 in (90×45×45 cm) setup.

PVC holds humidity far better than glass, which matters more for this species than for almost any other beginner snake. A glass tank works if you cover three sides to cut evaporation and reflections. Whatever you use, it must lock or latch: ball pythons are patient, strong escape artists and a gap at a corner is all it takes. House one snake per enclosure.

Never Run any heat source without a thermostat. An unregulated mat, tape, or panel is the direct cause of thermal burns and is a genuine fire hazard. Skip hot rocks entirely.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Clutter is not decoration for this species. Cork, plants, and leaf litter break up sightlines so the snake can be somewhere other than inside a hide without feeling exposed, which is what prevents the chronic stress that shows up as feeding refusal.
  • Size hides snugly. A ball python feels secure when its body contacts the sides at several points, not when it is floating in an oversized box. A hide the snake rattles around in provides no security at all.
  • Keep climbing structure low and braced at both ends. They are heavy-bodied and not agile, so stability matters far more than height.

Humidity55 to 70% baseline, raised to 70 to 80% from the moment the eyes go blue until the shed is complete.

Measure the warm side at the surface the snake contacts, with an infrared thermometer, not the air above it. Read humidity with a digital hygrometer at substrate level; analog dials are inaccurate and a reading near the lid runs drier than what the snake experiences.

Humidity is what actually determines success

This is where most ball python problems start, and it runs wrong in both directions. Too dry gives you bad sheds, retained eye caps, and a higher respiratory infection risk. Too wet without real ventilation swings the other way toward scale rot and respiratory trouble from damp instead of cold. The species needs a stable middle, not a maximum, which is why the fix is almost never a spray bottle.

What holds it steady

A moisture-retaining substrate 3 to 4 in (7.5 to 10 cm) deep, a water bowl large enough to soak in, and a dedicated humid hide packed with damp sphagnum moss the snake can choose to use. PVC does most of this work for you; a screen-topped glass tank fights you the whole way and usually needs partial covering. Mist only to bring a reading back up, never on a schedule.

Lighting

UVB is genuinely optional here. Ball pythons have been kept successfully without it for decades, though a low-level gradient is reasonable enrichment if you want to provide it. What is not optional is a consistent 12 hours light and 12 hours dark on a timer, with no bright or colored bulbs at night. If the room falls below the nighttime range, use a ceramic heat emitter on its own thermostat rather than a light, so sleep is not disrupted.

Thermostats, probes & heat sources

The thermostat is the safety device

A thermostat sits between the heat source and your snake and stops a mat that reads 90°F (32°C) this morning from climbing on a warm afternoon. An unregulated under-tank heater can reach temperatures well above anything a snake can survive lying on, and a ball python will stay on a hot surface rather than move off it. This is the single most common preventable injury in the species, and the fix costs $25 to $100. Budget for the thermostat as part of the heat source, never as an upgrade to add later.

Use an on/off or pulse thermostat for an under-tank heater, heat tape, a ceramic heat emitter, or a radiant heat panel, which is the usual ball python setup. Use a dimming (proportional) thermostat if you run an overhead halogen, which dislikes being switched on and off. Never put a UVB tube on a thermostat, and never run any heat source unregulated, not even briefly while you wait for a part.

Where the probe goes

Probe placement is the part people get wrong after buying the right equipment. Put the probe where the snake actually lies: on the substrate surface at the warm end, or inside the warm hide, taped down so the snake cannot shift it. A probe taped to the heat mat itself reads the equipment rather than the animal and will hold the mat at your set point while the surface above it runs hotter. Verify with an infrared thermometer gun for the first week, then spot-check monthly.

Probe placement at a glance

Wattage is a starting guess, not a target. Let the thermostat and the infrared gun settle it, and re-check after any change of room, season, or substrate depth.

Measure, don't assume An infrared thermometer gun costs $15 to $30 and is the only way to know what the surface under your snake actually reads. Check the warm side, the cool side, and the inside of both hides. Write the numbers on page 23 so a sitter or a vet can see them at a glance.

Substrate & furnishings

Substrate

Cypress mulch, or coconut coir and husk, 3 to 4 in (7.5 to 10 cm) deep. Both hold humidity at the level this species needs and let the snake burrow, which it will. Paper towel works fine temporarily, for quarantine or while treating a health issue, but it holds no humidity long term. Avoid aspen entirely: it molds at ball python humidity.

Never Pine or cedar. Both contain aromatic oils toxic to reptiles. This is a hard exclusion, not a preference, and it applies to shavings, chips, and bedding sold under any brand name.

Furnishings checklist

Two snug hides, one warm side and one cool side, sized so the snake touches the sides
Humid hide with damp sphagnum moss, kept year round
Heavy, tip-resistant water bowl the snake can fully enter
Cork bark tubes and flats for floor-level cover
A branch braced at both ends, low enough that a fall isn't one
Leaf litter or plants to break up open sightlines

Cleaning

Spot-clean waste and any soiled substrate daily, which takes under a minute and is most of the work. Scrub the water bowl weekly rather than topping it up, since a soaking snake will also defecate in it. Change the substrate fully every 4 to 8 weeks and wipe the enclosure down at the same time, using a reptile-safe disinfectant and rinsing thoroughly. Deep-clean and fully dry every hide and piece of decor at that point too: damp cork sitting on damp substrate is where scale rot starts (page 17).

A hide the snake rattles around in is not a hide The single most common furnishing mistake is buying hides that look comfortable to a human. A ball python feels secure when its body contacts the sides at several points, so a snug hide it has to squeeze into does more for its stress level than a large decorative cave ever will. Replace both hides as the snake grows out of them, and check the fit at every substrate change.

Handling & first days

How much, and how often

Don't handle a newly acquired ball python for the first one to two weeks, and don't start until it is eating on a normal schedule. Handling starts only once the snake has eaten successfully. After that, 2 to 3 sessions a week is a reasonable baseline, up to 3 to 5 for a snake that handles well, keeping each to 15 to 30 minutes. Overhandling causes real stress in this species and is a common cause of feeding refusal, so more is not automatically better.

Technique

Approach from the side rather than straight down from above, which reads as a predator strike. Support the body with both hands or along your forearms, lift from the middle rather than near the head or tail, and let the snake move at its own pace instead of restraining it. Keep sessions low over a bed or sofa rather than standing on a hard floor. Never grab or restrain the head.

  • Wait 48 to 72 hours after a meal before handling. Handling too soon is one of the most common causes of regurgitation, and regurgitation damages the esophagus if it repeats.
  • Don't handle during a shed. Cloudy or blue eyes mean the snake is functionally blind for a few days, which makes it more defensive and more likely to bite out of startlement rather than temperament.

Signs the session is over

Balling up tightly with the head tucked away, rapid or heavy breathing, hiding the head against your hand or arm, actively trying to move away, or musking, a foul-smelling release from the cloaca. A refused meal after a handling session counts too. None of these are aggression; put the snake back and shorten the next session. More on defensive behavior on page 20.

Salmonella Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella with no sign of it. Wash your hands with soap before and after handling, keep the animal and its gear away from food-prep areas, and never clean dishes in the kitchen sink. Children under five, pregnant people, and anyone immunocompromised should not handle the snake.

First days in a new home

Set the enclosure up and running for 24 to 48 hours before the snake arrives, so the readings are already stable rather than settling around it. Then leave it alone: fresh water and spot-cleaning only for the first week, no handling and no rearranging, and resist the urge to check on it hourly. Offer the first meal at the end of that week, using the same prey type and size the seller was using. A refusal in week one is ordinary and is not a reason to change anything.

Diet & feeding by age

Ball pythons are obligate carnivores fed whole prey. Unlike most pets, the schedule gets slower as the animal gets bigger, not faster, and that slow metabolism is a feature of the species rather than a problem to solve.

AgeWeightTypical preyFeedingsHow much
Hatchling, 0 to 6 monthsunder about 300 gHopper or small mouseEvery 5 to 7 daysOne item, matched to body width
Juvenile, 6 to 18 months300 to 800 gAdult mouse to small ratEvery 7 to 10 daysOne item, about 10% of body weight
Adult, 18 months and up800 g and upMedium ratEvery 10 to 14 daysOne item, about 10% of body weight

Prey size is the whole rule

Feed prey roughly the same width as the widest part of the snake's body, or about 10% of its weight. Too large risks regurgitation, too small provides inadequate nutrition over time. Resist the urge to overfeed on the theory that a bigger snake is a healthier one: obesity is real and common in captive ball pythons, and a snake that eats every ten days is doing exactly what it should.

Supplements

A whole-prey diet is nutritionally complete, bones and organs included, so ball pythons need no calcium dusting, no D3, and no multivitamin. That is a practice for insect-eating reptiles, and over-supplementing a snake causes mineral imbalance rather than helping. The one thing worth buying instead is a gram scale, because weight is how you actually judge whether the diet is working.

No dusting, no exceptions If a care sheet tells you to dust a ball python's prey with calcium or a multivitamin, it has been copied from a lizard guide. Whole rodents already carry the calcium in their skeletons, and there is no UVB-dependent D3 requirement to correct for in a species that does not need UVB at all.

Water

A heavy bowl large enough for the snake to soak in, on the cool side, refreshed daily and scrubbed weekly. Soaking supports shedding and hydration and is a behavior the snake will choose for itself when the option exists. A snake that suddenly soaks constantly is telling you something about heat, humidity, or mites, covered on pages 6 and 17.

Never feed Live prey. A live rodent can bite and seriously injure a snake, sometimes fatally, especially one that isn't hungry enough to strike immediately. The full never-feed list is on page 11.

Thawing, presentation & refusals

Thawing, step by step

  • Move the prey from the freezer to the fridge the night before, or thaw from frozen in a sealed bag submerged in cool water.
  • Finish in warm, not hot, water in a sealed bag for 20 to 30 minutes, until warm all the way through. The core should reach roughly 100 to 105°F (38 to 41°C), about the temperature of live prey and what the heat pits are looking for.
  • Pat it dry. A dripping wet rodent is less appealing and makes a mess of the substrate.
  • Never microwave prey. It heats unevenly, leaving hot spots that can burn a snake's mouth while the center is still frozen, and it can rupture.

Presentation

Feed in the enclosure, with long tongs. Moving a ball python to a separate feeding tub was standard advice for years, but the added stress is now understood to raise both regurgitation and refusal rates in an already food-shy species. Offer the prey head first with a little movement, and give the snake space rather than standing over the glass. Using tongs every time also builds the association between tongs and food rather than your hand and food.

The seven reasons a ball python stops eating

  1. Shedding. Appetite drops in the days before a shed, signaled by dull skin and cloudy or blue eyes.
  2. Breeding season, roughly October through March. Males especially go off food. Completely normal.
  3. Temperature or humidity out of range. The most common husbandry cause, and the first thing to check.
  4. Not enough cover. A snake that feels exposed does not eat. Add clutter before you change anything else.
  5. Recent change. A new home, a rearranged enclosure, or too much handling. Wait about 48 hours after any disturbance before offering again.
  6. Prey presentation. Prey too cold, too large, or a switch of prey type or color. Try warmer, smaller, or the type the seller used.
  7. Illness. Last on the list for a reason, and it almost always comes with other signs from page 16.
How long is too long A healthy adult in good body condition can go three to six months without eating and lose surprisingly little condition; a brooding female can fast four to six months. What turns a fast into a problem is company: real weight loss, lethargy, mucus at the mouth, or wheezing alongside the refusal. A hatchling has far less reserve, so a young snake refusing for more than about two weeks warrants a vet call rather than patience.
Don't chase a refusal Offering food more often, leaving prey in overnight, or handling the snake to check on it all make refusal more likely. Take the prey out, wait a week, fix anything on the list above, and try again.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Running a heat source without a thermostat, or taping the probe to the mat instead of the surface the snake lies on.
  • Treating the small tub as the goal rather than the compromise, then never moving the snake into a real enclosure.
  • Guessing at humidity, or trusting an analog dial, instead of reading a digital hygrometer at substrate level.
  • Panicking at a refused meal and responding by handling, moving, or offering food more often, all of which make refusal more likely.
  • Handling within 48 hours of a meal, then treating the resulting regurgitation as bad luck.
  • Leaving the enclosure bare because clutter looks untidy, in an animal whose stress response is driven by feeling exposed.
  • Skipping quarantine for a new snake, which is how mites and IBD enter a collection (pages 17 and 18).

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after correct heat, humidity, and secure hides, never instead of them. Kaufmann and colleagues, in PLOS ONE, compared 35 ball pythons in a conventional rack against furnished terrariums and concluded that from a welfare standpoint, racks are not an alternative to a furnished terrarium. In this order:

1. A furnished enclosure

The part with a study behind it. The whole furnished package outperformed the bare one, so treat the enclosure itself as the enrichment rather than something you add items to.

2. Cover across the whole floor

Not just two hides. Cork tubes and flats, leaf litter, and plant cover let the snake be out without being exposed. A cluttered enclosure gets used more, not less.

3. Stable climbing structure

Heavy-bodied and not agile, so the requirement is stability, not height. A branch braced firmly at both ends, or stacked cork, adds usable space to an enclosure that otherwise has only a floor.

4. A basin large enough to soak in

The study's terrariums included a bathing basin, not a drinking cup. It is one of the few genuinely passive enrichment items: you provide it, the animal decides.

5. Scent novelty

Snakes navigate chemically, so a novel scent is closer to a puzzle than a novel object is. A shed skin from another enclosure, or prey dragged across the substrate before offering, costs nothing.

6. Rearrangement, genuinely last

Shifting a branch or adding cork every few weeks is reasonable. Tearing the enclosure down weekly is not, and never at the cost of access to the warm spot.

Sexing, growth & body condition

Telling male from female

Ball pythons show almost no reliable external sex difference before adulthood, which is why even experienced keepers confirm sex by probing or popping rather than by looking. Both are done by a reptile vet or an experienced breeder, not by a first-time owner: done wrong, either can cause real injury. Two soft clues exist. Adult males tend to have larger cloacal spurs, the small claw-like remnants either side of the vent, and adult females grow noticeably larger. Neither is conclusive on its own, and a pet-store guess about a hatchling is exactly that.

Growth reference

Ranges are deliberately wide and healthy snakes fall outside them. What matters is the trend: steady gain for a juvenile, a stable weight for an adult. Weigh monthly in grams and log it on page 30.

Body condition at a glance

Underweight

A pronounced triangular cross-section with the spine ridged and visible along the back, loose skin folds outside a shed, and a narrow tail base. Rule out parasites, mites, and a cool warm side first (pages 17 and 18), then feed up on the normal schedule rather than by adding meals.

Ideal

A rounded, slightly loaf-shaped cross-section with the spine felt but not seen, smooth muscle tone along the body, and no fold or crease when the snake coils. Weight stable month to month in an adult, climbing steadily in a juvenile.

Overweight

A cross-section closer to round or flattened, skin visible between scales when coiled, and fat rolls or creases along the body. Common in adults fed on a juvenile schedule. Stretch the feeding interval and add floor space and climbing rather than cutting prey size.

When a weight change is a vet visit A juvenile that stops gaining for 4 to 6 weeks. An adult that loses about 10% of its body weight outside a known seasonal fast, or any weight loss that comes with lethargy, a refusal to use the warm side, or abnormal stool. Bring the Owner Log; a trend line tells a vet more than a single reading.

Females, eggs & egg binding

Relevant to every female owner, whether or not a male has ever been in the house.

Females can cycle without a male

From roughly two to three years old, and driven by weight rather than age, a female can develop egg follicles on her own. Most pet females kept alone never lay, and a female that does may produce infertile eggs, sometimes called slugs. Neither outcome is a problem in itself. What matters is recognizing the process, because everything that goes wrong with it goes wrong quietly.

Signs she may be building follicles

  • A long refusal of food that starts outside the usual breeding-season window and does not resolve when the setup checks out.
  • Noticeable mid-body swelling, and lying belly-up in loose coils, which is a well-known posture in gravid females.
  • Seeking the warm side far more than usual, and a pre-lay shed a few weeks before laying.
  • Steady weight loss across monthly weigh-ins while she stays otherwise alert. Never squeeze or palpate to check; that is a vet's job.

If she does lay

Give her a large, humid, dark hide she can coil in completely, on the warm side, and leave her alone. A brooding female will wrap the clutch and stay with it, generating heat by shivering thermogenesis, and she may not eat for four to six months while she does. Remove infertile eggs once she leaves them. She will come out of it thin and hungry, so return to the normal feeding schedule promptly and weigh her weekly rather than monthly until the weight comes back.

This is a vet conversation, not a home project A female cycling repeatedly loses condition every time, and a keeper who did not intend to breed has no reason to let that continue unmanaged. Talk to a reptile vet about her weight, her body condition, and the options, and get her examined rather than waiting to see how the season goes.

Egg binding (dystocia) is an emergency

Eggs she cannot pass. Call a reptile vet the same day if you see any of these:

  • Straining or restless digging for more than 24 to 48 hours with nothing produced, or a partial clutch and then she stops and looks exhausted.
  • Lethargy, sunken appearance, or a firm lump in the body that does not shift over days.
  • Any female past the pre-lay stage who is losing condition rather than laying, or anything protruding from the vent.

Treatment ranges from fluids and supportive care to induced laying or surgery, and the longer it waits the fewer options remain.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see Wheezing, clicking, or open-mouth breathing at rest · mucus or bubbles at the mouth or nostrils · belly scales discolored, soft, or blistered · pus, pinpoint bleeding, or a sour smell in the mouth · visible mites · a burn over the heat source · anything protruding from the vent · head wobbling, stargazing, or an inability to right itself · repeated regurgitation · refusal to eat alongside real weight loss

What to tell the vet

Warm side and cool side surface temperatures, and whether heat runs on a thermostat
Current humidity, and where in the enclosure you measured it
Date of the last meal, its size, and whether it stayed down
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening
Last normal bowel movement, and what it looked like
Weight trend from the Owner Log, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag if you can

Why temperature and humidity come up first

Almost everything in this section traces back to one of the two. Respiratory infection, scale rot, stuck shed, and a large share of feeding refusals are husbandry outcomes before they are illnesses, which is why a reptile vet will ask about your setup before anything else. Having the numbers ready shortens the visit and often changes the diagnosis. The Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians keeps a searchable directory of reptile-experienced vets; find one before you need one and write the number on page 23.

Respiratory infection & scale rot

Respiratory infection (RI)

Cause: temperatures running too low, incorrect humidity in either direction, a dirty enclosure, or ongoing stress. Vets see a seasonal spike each winter as enclosures cool along with the weather outside. Signs: wheezing, an audible clicking sound, open-mouth breathing, visible mucus or bubbles around the mouth or nostrils, nasal discharge, lethargy, and in severe cases head tilting. Diagnosis: physical exam and listening to the lungs, with the vet asking about your temperature setup directly. Response: vet-prescribed antibiotics, not a wait-and-see approach, and correcting the temperature and humidity in parallel or it recurs. Recovery: good when caught early; left untreated it can progress to pneumonia and become fatal.

Learn this one by sound RI is the signature ball python illness and the clearest audible signal in this package. If you hear clicking or wheezing, raise the warm side to target, check humidity at substrate level, isolate the snake if you keep others, and call a reptile vet.

Scale rot (infectious dermatitis)

Cause: damp, dirty substrate, excess moisture, or poor enclosure hygiene, which is the exact failure mode of chasing humidity numbers without ventilation. It also follows a water bowl that overflows or sits unchanged. Signs: belly scales discolored brown, reddish, or yellow, blisters or sores, scales that feel soft or damaged, and a foul odor. Diagnosis: visual exam, with a culture if it is advanced. Recovery: move the snake to a clean, dry setup on paper towel immediately and correct humidity; mild cases respond to antiseptic or antibiotic treatment, and affected scales usually resolve over the next one to two shed cycles. Anything spreading or blistering needs a vet.

Concrete threshold Any belly scale that is discolored and either spreading day to day or blistered is a vet visit this week, not a wait-and-watch. Photograph it against a ruler so the vet can judge progression.

Mouth rot, mites & thermal burns

One arrives on another problem's back, one arrives on a new animal, and one is entirely preventable by a single device.

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

Usually secondary to stress, injury, or ongoing poor husbandry rather than arriving on its own. Watch for pinpoint hemorrhages on the gums, thick excess mucus or pus, cheesy-looking buildup in the mouth, a sour smell, visible swelling, open-mouth breathing, and refusing food. It needs veterinary treatment and does not resolve on its own, and because it usually rides on top of another husbandry problem, the visit should come with an honest account of your setup.

Mites

Ophionyssus natricis, tiny blood-feeding external parasites, almost always introduced on a new snake, on used decor, or from a shop or expo. Look for tiny black or red specks on the scales, clustered around the eyes and heat pits, or floating in the water bowl, along with excessive soaking, rubbing against surfaces, restlessness, and a drop in appetite. A heavy infestation causes anemia and real stress. Treating the snake alone is not enough: the whole enclosure has to be decontaminated at the same time or the infestation simply returns, which usually means stripping substrate completely, switching to paper towel, and repeating on the schedule your vet sets.

Thermal burns

Caused by a heat source running without a thermostat. That is the entire cause. Look for discolored, blistered, or crusted patches on the belly or side, usually over the heat element, sometimes with the snake avoiding the warm side entirely. Burns are wounds and can become infected, so they need a vet. Prevention is page 7: every heat element thermostat-controlled, no hot rocks, and a probe placed where the snake actually contacts the heat.

Quarantine is the actual fix for mites Quarantine any new snake for 60 to 90 days before it goes anywhere near an established animal: a separate room where possible, paper towel substrate, a simple setup you can strip and disinfect, separate tools, and hand-washing between enclosures. Always work with the established animals first and the new one last. The same routine is what keeps IBD out of a collection (page 19), and there is a log for it on page 31.

Parasites, regurgitation, obesity & IBD

Less dramatic than the last two pages, and far more common.

Internal parasites

Show up as weight loss and diarrhea despite the snake eating normally, sometimes with visible material in the stool. Wild-caught and imported animals carry them far more often than captive-bred stock. Diagnosis is a fecal test, which is why the first vet visit is worth booking with a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag, and why an annual wellness exam with a fecal is the standard recommendation. Treatment is a prescribed antiparasitic plus a full enclosure clean.

Regurgitation

Bringing a meal back up hours to days after eating, most often from handling too soon after a feed, prey that was too large, or a warm side too cool to digest properly. It is hard on the esophagus and repeated episodes cause real damage. After one, leave the snake completely alone for 10 to 14 days, then offer a smaller than usual prey item. Repeated regurgitation is a vet visit, not a feeding adjustment.

Obesity

Real and common in captive ball pythons, and almost always the result of feeding an adult on a juvenile's schedule. Look for the body-condition signs on page 14: a rounded rather than loaf-shaped cross-section, skin visible between the scales when coiled, and creases along the body. The fix is stretching the feeding interval toward every 14 days and adding floor space, cover, and climbing, not cutting prey size while keeping the frequency.

Inclusion Body Disease (IBD)

A serious viral disease of boas and pythons, associated with arenaviruses. Signs include neurological symptoms such as head wobbling, stargazing, and an inability to right itself, alongside regurgitation and progressive decline. There is no cure, and it can spread between boids through mites, which is the practical link between this page and the last one. Prevention is the only tool available: source from a reputable captive breeder and quarantine every new animal for 60 to 90 days.

Burns and prolapse

Burns are covered on page 18 and are always a thermostat failure. A prolapse, anything protruding from the vent, is a same-day emergency: keep the tissue moist with clean lukewarm water or saline-dampened gauze, do not attempt to push it back, and get to a reptile vet immediately.

Shedding, refusal & defensive behavior

The shed cycle

A healthy shed comes off in one length. The cycle runs roughly a week to ten days: the skin dulls, the belly may pink up, the eyes go cloudy or blue for a few days, then clear again for two or three days before the snake sheds. Appetite drops through most of it and handling stops entirely, because a snake with clouded eyes is functionally blind and startles easily.

The shed routine From the moment the eyes go blue: raise humidity to 70 to 80%, check the humid hide is genuinely damp and top up the moss, make sure there is a rough surface such as cork for the snake to start the shed against, and leave it alone. Don't handle, don't feed, don't rearrange. After the shed, find the skin and check it for the eye caps.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Caused by humidity running too low, especially through the cycle itself. A bad shed comes off in flakes and patches. Increase humidity, offer a shallow lukewarm soak of 20 to 30 minutes, and gently roll the loosened skin off; never peel it by force. Check the eye caps every time. A retained eye cap is one of the easiest problems to miss because the snake's behavior looks completely normal, and it can cause permanent eye damage if left, so it needs a vet rather than a home attempt with tweezers.

Defensive behavior

Balling up tightly with the head tucked away is a fear response, not aggression, and it is where the species gets its name. Rapid or heavy breathing, hiding the head against your hand, actively trying to move away, or musking, a foul-smelling release from the cloaca, all mean the session is over. Bites are rare and minor, and are typically a feeding response from a snake that smells rodent on your hands rather than a temperament problem. Use tongs at every feeding and wash your hands after handling prey.

Quick differentiator A refused meal is a snake that never struck. A regurgitation is a meal that was eaten and came back up. The first is usually normal and needs patience; the second means something in the setup or the handling schedule is wrong, and it needs to be found before the next feed (page 19).

Hydration

Most of a ball python's water comes from drinking and from ambient humidity rather than from food, so the water bowl is not decoration. Beyond keeping it full:

  • Use a bowl heavy enough not to tip and large enough for the snake to fully enter, on the cool side. Refresh daily and scrub weekly, since a soaking snake will also defecate in it.
  • Keep humidity in the 55 to 70% band as the baseline hydration tool. A snake that is chronically dry sheds badly long before it looks dehydrated.
  • A shallow lukewarm soak of 20 to 30 minutes helps a stuck shed or a dehydrated snake. Chest-deep at most, never unsupervised, and never hot water.
Signs of dehydration Wrinkled or loose skin outside a shed, sunken eyes, thick or stringy saliva, yellow or hard urates, sheds coming off in pieces, and lethargy. Mild cases respond to corrected humidity and a soak; a snake that is sunken and lethargic needs a vet for fluids.

Power outages, travel & transport

A healthy adult tolerates one cool night without harm. The risk is days without heat, and feeding a snake that can't digest.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Keep the snake above 70°F (21°C). Below that for more than a day or two is when problems start.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold what heat is left.
  • Move the enclosure, or the snake in a ventilated tub, to the warmest room in the house.
  • Use hand warmers or a hot-water bottle wrapped in a towel, placed beside the snake, never touching it.
  • Stop feeding. Water only until the warm side is back to target.
  • Past two days below 70°F (21°C), board it with a vet, a friend with power, or a reptile shop.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, gas stoves, or open flame anywhere near the enclosure.
  • Don't put a heat pack in direct contact with the snake; it can't move away fast enough and will burn.
  • Don't feed "to keep its strength up." A meal in a cold gut rots and is regurgitated at best.
  • Don't warm a cold snake in hot water; lukewarm only, and only once it's alert.
Outage kit Hand warmers, a hot-water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated locking tub, and the vet numbers from page 23.

Leaving town

Trip lengthAdultJuvenile (under 18 months)

Transport and vet trips

  • A ventilated, locking plastic tub with a towel or crumpled paper to grip and hide in, inside a dark insulated bag or box. No loose substrate, no water dish sloshing.
  • Warm the car before loading in cold weather, and add a wrapped hand warmer beside the tub for trips over 30 minutes below 65°F (18°C).
  • Don't travel within 48 hours of a meal. Never leave the ball python in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly.
  • Moving house: set the enclosure up and running at the new place before the snake goes in, exactly as on day one, and expect a refused meal or two afterward.
