Fun facts worth knowing

  • The name comes from the defensive pose, not the diet. A stressed ball python curls into a tight ball with its head tucked in the center, so a new snake that won't uncurl isn't being difficult. It just hasn't decided yet that hiding is unnecessary.

  • A brooding female coils around her eggs without warming them. Brashears and DeNardo (2015) measured six python species at brooding temperatures and found no heat production in five, the ball python among them.

The species, morphs, size & lifespan

What you are buying, where it came from, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

One species

The ball python is Python regius, native to the grasslands, open forests and farmland of West and Central Africa. In the wild it eats small mammals, birds and amphibians, and it is an ambush predator that spends most of its life concealed, which is why cover matters so much in its enclosure. It is the most popular pet snake in the world.

Where pet ball pythons come from

Almost the entire wild supply traces to three countries at the center of the range: Benin, Ghana and Togo, which have exported close to four million ball pythons since 1978, most of them to the United States. Snakes were taken straight from the wild through the 1980s and 1990s; since the early 2000s most exports have been ranched, meaning eggs and gravid (egg-carrying) females are collected from the wild and the hatchlings raised briefly and shipped. The species makes up roughly 80 percent of all snake exports under Appendix II of CITES, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, the treaty that tracks trade in species that could become threatened by it. The captive-bred hobby grew out of the 1990s, when the first albino ball pythons were bred.

Captive-bred snakes make the better pets: less likely to bite, more likely to accept the food offered, and less likely to carry parasites. Wild-caught and imported animals routinely carry much heavier parasite loads, and because the two get mixed at shows and in the trade, no seller's word replaces the fecal test on page 20.

Morphs, and what a price does not tell you

A morph is a selectively bred line with a different color or pattern. There are hundreds of them, combined into more than 7,000 recorded combinations. A standard snake runs $40 to $100, a common single-gene morph $50 to $150, and designer morphs $250 to $5,000 or more. One widely bred gene carries a known health cost: the spider morph is linked to wobble syndrome, a heritable neurological disorder that shows as side-to-side or twisting head movements, poor coordination, and trouble striking or constricting prey. Rose and Williams (2014) first described it from a survey of breeders and keepers, and Starck and colleagues (2022) found malformed balance organs in the inner ear of every spider morph they imaged. A spider, or any combination carrying the gene, may struggle to feed, and its offspring can inherit the condition. A wild-type, the natural pattern with no morph gene, or a single-gene morph with no known defect is the lower-risk first snake.

Size and lifespan

GrowthHatchlings start at about a foot (30 cm) and reach roughly 3 ft (90 cm) by age three.
Adult length3 to 5 ft (90 to 150 cm). Females are significantly larger than males.
Lifespan20 to 30 years in captivity is common, against about 10 in the wild. Some live far longer: a zoo female laid eggs at 62 years old.

A ball python bought for a child can still be in the house when that child is grown. Plan for the whole span: the enclosure, the frozen rodents and a reptile vet for every one of those years.

Enclosure size, type & where it goes

4×2×2 ft (48×24×24 in, 120×60×60 cm, roughly 120 gallons and about 8 square feet of floor) is the adult standard, and the enclosure should be at least as long as the snake. Scale down while the snake is small: a hatchling up to about 300 g does well in a 10-gallon or 20×11×13 in (50×28×33 cm) enclosure, and a juvenile under 3 ft (90 cm) in a 36×18×18 in (90×45×45 cm) setup. Bigger, furnished enclosures have replaced the old advice that a small tub reduces stress.

PVC holds humidity far better than glass, which matters more for this species than for almost any other beginner snake. A glass tank works if you cover three sides to help it hold moisture. Whatever you use, it must be secure, locking and front-opening: ball pythons are excellent escape artists. House one snake per enclosure.

Never Run any heat source without a thermostat. An unregulated mat, tape or panel is the direct cause of thermal burns and a real fire hazard. Skip hot rocks entirely.

Where it goes

  • Out of the kitchen and away from anywhere food is made or served.
  • In a room you can keep from dropping below the night range, 72 to 75°F (22 to 24°C), with the heat the enclosure has. Winter cooling is when respiratory infection spikes.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Cover across the whole floor, not just two hides. An enclosure that feels open causes chronic stress, seen as refused meals and skittishness.
  • Hides sized snug, so the body touches the sides at several points. A hide the snake floats in gives no security.

Whichever you pick, wattage is a starting guess sized for an average room, not a target: wire it through the thermostat, then let the two measurements above settle it.

Heat that can't burn

  • Keep any overhead heat source at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest spot the snake can reach, behind a screened top or guard. A heater the snake can touch will burn it at any setting.
  • This species' warm side tops out at 92°F (33°C), so a surface reading well above that under the heat source is a fault, not a feature.
  • Skip hot rocks entirely. No thermostat, direct skin contact and no cutoff is the textbook recipe for a thermal burn.
  • A snake pressed against a hot surface can't always move away, especially while unwell, so sustained contact with an overheated spot is how burns happen.

Night heat has no light

A light bulb cannot be used for night heat. If the room falls below the 72 to 75°F (22 to 24°C) night range, use a heat mat, ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector, on its own thermostat. None of them puts out visible light.

Thermostats & probes

The thermostat is the safety device

A thermostat measures temperature at one point and cuts power to the heat source once that point reaches the setting, then restores it as the reading drops. Without one, a mat or bulb draws full power with no ceiling at all. Thermal burns are a common and entirely preventable injury in this species, so budget the thermostat as part of the heat source, never as an upgrade, and never run a heat source unregulated, not even briefly while you wait for a part.

ControllerHow it worksBest matched to
On/offCuts power fully at the set point and restores it fully below itUnder-tank heat mats, which change temperature slowly enough for a full cycle
Pulse proportionalSends short pulses of power to hold a steadier averageCeramic heat emitters and other sources that react faster than a mat and can overshoot
DimmingTurns the power down the way a light dimmer doesHalogen and incandescent bulbs, without the flicker of switching

The split is about how the device delivers power. What matters most is that every heat source has a controller at all.

Where the probe goes

A thermostat only regulates what its probe measures. For an under-tank mat, the probe goes on the floor of the warm hide, directly over the mat: the surface the snake actually lies on. Hold it there with a probe clip or a suction cup, never tape. Tape over a probe throws off its reading, and a snake can stick to exposed adhesive and tear scales. A probe against the mat itself reads the equipment, not the snake, and lets the surface above run hotter than the display shows. For an overhead heater, follow your controller's own instructions; the principle is the same, the probe reads at the snake's level, never up near the fixture.

How a thermostat fails

  • The probe moves. The most common failure, and a silent one: the display keeps showing an accurate reading for the wrong spot. Check the probe's position at every water change or spot clean.
  • The thermostat sticks on. Rarer and more dangerous, because nothing limits the heat. Read the warm-end thermometer daily. The cool end always reads lower, and that gap is the gradient. A warm end that keeps climbing is the warning of a stuck thermostat or a shifted probe.
  • Test it before the snake arrives. Run the whole setup and watch the thermostat cut the heat as the probe warms.

Humidity, light & optional ultraviolet

Humidity is where most ball python problems start, and it goes wrong in both directions.

HumidityTarget
Everyday baseline55 to 65%
Through a shed70 to 80% through the shed cycle itself

Too dry, too wet

Too dry gives bad sheds and raises the risk of respiratory infection. Too wet without real ventilation swings the other way, toward scale rot and respiratory trouble from damp instead of cold. The species needs a stable middle, not a maximum. An enclosure can look perfect at a glance, with the right temperatures, clean substrate and a snake that seems fine, while quietly running too dry: humidity problems tend to show up weeks later as a bad shed rather than right away.

What holds it steady

  • A moisture-holding substrate, 3 to 4 in (7.5 to 10 cm) deep.
  • An appropriately sized water bowl, big enough for the snake to soak in.
  • A humid hide packed with damp sphagnum moss, kept year round. A humid pocket the snake can enter and leave does a different job from a permanently damp enclosure.
  • PVC, or glass covered on three sides. PVC holds humidity far better than glass.
  • Misting only when needed, to bring a low reading back up.

Read it with a digital hygrometer (humidity gauge), not a cheap analog dial: dials are notoriously inaccurate.

Light: a timer, and dark nights

Run a 12-hour light, 12-hour dark day on a timer, using an LED (light-emitting diode, a low-heat, long-life lamp). Avoid bright white or colored bulbs at night. If the room needs night heat, it comes from a heater with no light (page 7).

UVB: optional, with a modest benefit

UVB (ultraviolet B) is the part of sunlight many reptiles need to make vitamin D3. Ball pythons have been kept successfully for decades with no UVB at all, so it is genuinely optional here, but a low-level gradient is a reasonable piece of enrichment if you want to add it. Ball pythons are partial-sun, occasional baskers (Ferguson Zone 2 in the grouping of species by natural sun exposure). That puts the UVI (ultraviolet index, the strength of the UVB at one spot) at 0.7 to 1.0 across the gradient and 1.1 to 3.0 at the closest point to the lamp, with a gradient the snake can move away from.

  • Glass or plastic between lamp and snake blocks most UVB; a mesh lid costs some of it.
  • A UVB tube keeps glowing long after its useful output has faded. Replace it on the maker's schedule, every 6 to 12 months depending on the model, and write the date down.

Substrate, hides & cover

Substrate

Cypress mulch, or coconut coir and husk, 3 to 4 in (7.5 to 10 cm) deep. Both hold humidity at the level this species needs. Paper towel works fine temporarily, for quarantine for example, where a bare floor is the point, but it won't hold humidity long term. Avoid aspen entirely: it tends to mold at ball python humidity.

Never Pine or cedar. Both contain aromatic oils that are toxic to reptiles. This is a hard exclusion, not a preference, and it covers shavings, chips and bedding sold under any name.

Furnishings checklist

At least two snug hides, one warm side and one cool side, sized so the body touches the sides at several points
A humid hide packed with damp sphagnum moss, year round
A heavy, tip-resistant water bowl the snake can fully get into
Cork bark tubes and flats for cover across the floor
Leaf litter and plants to break up open sightlines
A sturdy branch braced firmly at both ends, low enough that a fall is not a fall

Why clutter matters for this species

Plants, branches and leaf litter aren't decoration. Ball pythons are naturally shy, and an enclosure that feels open and exposed causes chronic stress, which shows up as refused meals and general skittishness. The more useful reading of "give it a tight hide" is that the whole floor should offer cover, so the snake can be somewhere other than inside a box without being exposed. A cluttered enclosure gets used more, not less.

Climbing, which most setups skip

Ball pythons are heavy-bodied and not agile, so the requirement is stability, not height: a branch braced firmly enough that the snake can push against it without it shifting. Cork rounds stacked into a raised platform work as well and double as cover. Vertical structure adds usable space to an enclosure that otherwise only has a floor, and it gets used.

A hide the snake floats in is not a hide The most common furnishing mistake is buying hides that look comfortable to a person. A ball python feels secure when its body touches the sides at several points, so a snug hide does more for its stress level than a large decorative cave ever will. Replace hides as the snake outgrows them, and check the fit at every full substrate change.

Cleaning & hygiene

Cleaning removes the waste. Disinfecting kills what is left. Hygiene keeps it off your hands.

Salmonella Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella bacteria in the gut and shed it in their droppings without being ill, on and off, so one negative test clears nothing. It travels from the snake, its enclosure and anything in it to hands, then to mouths or food, so wash with soap and running water before and after every contact. Do not kiss the snake or hold it to your face. Children under 5 should not handle the snake or touch anything in its enclosure, and adults over 65, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system are also at higher risk.

Three clocks

WhenWhat to do
As you see itSnake urates (the white part of the waste) and feces soil a lot of substrate at once, so spot clean as soon as waste appears, scooping out the wet substrate around it.
DailyChange the water, since the snake soaks in it.
WeeklyScrub the water bowl and any soiled decor with a reptile habitat cleaner, or a 3% bleach solution (about half a cup of household bleach per gallon of water) left on for at least 10 minutes. Rinse until no smell remains, and let everything dry.
Full changeReplace the whole substrate monthly if the snake is messy, and at least once every 3 months even if it is not. Move the snake to a secure tub, strip the enclosure, disinfect every surface the same way, and let it dry completely before new substrate goes in.

Disinfectant only works on a surface already scrubbed clean, because a film of waste shields bacteria from it. Plan on a full day of airing after bleach before the snake goes back in.

Never the kitchen sink Never wash the bowl, decor or enclosure in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink or a bath people use, or anywhere food is prepared. Keep a plastic tub for the job, or work in a utility sink or outdoors. If a bathtub does get used, clean it thoroughly afterward. Wash bedding and decor separately from anything used for food, and keep the snake out of the kitchen entirely.

If someone in the house gets sick

The signs are ordinary ones: diarrhea, fever, stomach cramps. That is why the link gets missed. Tell the doctor there is a reptile in the house, even if nobody remembers touching it, since enclosure surfaces are enough on their own. It matters most for a child under 5, an adult over 65, someone pregnant, or someone with a weakened immune system, in whom the infection can spread beyond the gut.

Diet & feeding by age

Ball pythons are carnivores fed whole prey: frozen-thawed mice and rats, the nutritionally complete staple from hatchling to adult. Unlike most pets, the schedule gets slower as the animal gets bigger, not faster. A healthy adult needs a meal only every one to two weeks, and that slow metabolism is a feature of the species, not a problem to solve.

AgeHow oftenWhat
Hatchling, 0 to 6 monthsEvery 5 to 7 daysFuzzy mice (a mouse with its first fur), moving up to hoppers (a just-weaned mouse)
Juvenile, 6 to 18 monthsEvery 7 to 10 daysHoppers to adult mice, then small rats as the snake grows
Adult, 18 months and upEvery 10 to 14 daysFor most adults, a medium rat

Two checks set the size

The prey is no wider than the snake at mid-body, its thickest point, and weighs about 10 to 15% of the snake's body weight: a 400 g snake takes prey of 40 to 60 g. Too large risks regurgitation; too small gives too little nutrition over time. Obesity is real and common in captive ball pythons, and a snake that eats every ten days is doing exactly what it should.

Where and how to feed

Feed inside the enclosure, with long tongs. Moving a ball python to a separate feeding tub was standard advice for years, but the added stress is now understood to raise both regurgitation and refusal in an already food-shy species. As long as loose substrate isn't a choking hazard, feeding in place is the better practice. Tongs every time also prevent the bites that come from a snake smelling rodent on your hand.

  • Wait 48 to 72 hours after a meal before handling. Handling on a full stomach is one of the most common causes of regurgitation, which is hard on the digestive system and slow to recover from.
  • Water: a heavy bowl big enough to soak in, changed daily, since the snake soaks in it as well as drinking from it.
  • No supplements. Whole prey is the complete diet. As a rule a snake fed healthy, well-fed rodents needs no vitamin or calcium powder, so the quality of the rodent matters more than anything dusted on it.
  • Weigh it. The scale catches overfeeding earlier than your eye does (page 16).

A new snake, and a young one

Leave a new ball python alone for one to two weeks before the first meal, and offer the prey type and size the seller was using. Refusals in the first weeks in a new home are common, and some snakes go weeks or longer before they settle. A young snake that has been eating for you is different: a hatchling or juvenile that refuses two meals in a row, about two weeks at its schedule, gets the refusal checks (page 15) and a weekly weigh-in, and any weight loss goes to a vet, since a youngster has no reserves to spare.

Never feed live prey A live rodent can bite and seriously injure a snake, sometimes fatally, especially one that isn't hungry enough to strike immediately. Frozen-thawed removes that risk entirely. The full never-feed list comes next.

Thawing, warming & freezer storage

The step most often done carelessly, and it matters more than the schedule.

Thawing, step by step

  1. Thaw it fully. In the refrigerator, the standard method, or sealed in a bag and submerged in cool to lukewarm water, changing the water as needed until there is no frozen core left.
  2. Never on the counter for hours, and never in a microwave, which heats unevenly: the inside can stay frozen while pockets on the outside get hot enough to burn the mouth, and the prey can rupture.
  3. Warm it gently. Don't feed it straight from the refrigerator. Prey goes in warm to the touch, never cold. A few minutes under a lamp, or warm water run over the outside of the sealed bag, does it.
  4. Judge by touch. It should feel warm to the touch. Warm gradually and check often rather than leaving it under heat and walking away.
Why warmth matters Ball pythons hunt partly by heat. The pits along the upper lip pick up infrared radiation from anything warmer than its surroundings, and a cold, thawed rodent doesn't register as prey the same way. Overheating is the opposite mistake: hot water or a lamp left too long can cook the prey inside, which risks the body cavity rupturing when the snake constricts or swallows, and can scald the inside of the snake's mouth.

Presenting it

Offer the warmed prey with long tongs, inside the enclosure (page 12). A snake that ignores a well-warmed meal is telling you something about its setup, its shed or the season, and the order to check those in comes next.

Freezer storage

  • Storage life. Food held at a steady 0°F (−18°C) stays safe indefinitely, but quality declines the longer it sits. Don't let stock sit a year or more just because it is technically fine.
  • Freezer burn, the gray, dried-out patches on long-frozen prey, is a quality problem rather than a safety one, and it happens when packaging isn't airtight. Keep prey in its original sealed packaging or rebag it well.
  • Thaw once. Never refreeze a rodent that has been thawed. If you thaw more than the snake will eat, the extra is a loss.
Handling it safely yourself Frozen feeder rodents have been linked to several Salmonella outbreaks in people. Wash your hands with soap and water right after handling frozen rodents or anything in the area where they were stored, thawed or prepared. Keep them away from where human food and drink are stored, prepared, served or eaten, and use containers and utensils that are only for feeder rodents. Don't thaw prey in a sink you wash dishes in, and never use a microwave or kitchen utensil meant for human food.

Growth, weight & body condition

The scale says whether the feeding schedule is working. The cross-section says what that weight looks like.

Weight, not length

Length is easy to picture and the wrong number to build a growth record on, because length and body mass don't grow in step. Falk, Snow and Reed (2017), measuring 248 wild Burmese pythons, found mass climbs disproportionately faster than length as a snake gets bigger, so two snakes of the same length can carry very different muscle and fat. A gram or kitchen scale captures muscle, fat and gut content in one number.

  • Monthly is a reasonable default for a growing juvenile, less often once an adult has leveled off.
  • Every 1 to 2 weeks through a long refusal or the winter slowdown, and weekly during quarantine.
  • Same scale every time, and write every reading on page 40. One weigh-in tells you almost nothing; three or four that climb, hold flat or drop tell you a great deal.

What growth looks like

Hatchlings start at about a foot (30 cm) and reach roughly 3 ft (90 cm) by age three; adults settle at 3 to 5 ft (90 to 150 cm), females significantly larger. Weight has a few fixed points; beyond them, a steady climb in a juvenile and a stable line in an adult are what matter.

  • A young female around 800 to 1,000 g often stops eating for a stretch as follicles start to develop (page 22). Smaller meals, offered less often, until she eats normally again.
  • Breeding females mature at around 1,500 g, from about 27 to 31 months. Males stay smaller.
  • A loss of more than 10% of body weight in any fast is the line where waiting stops being reasonable.

Body condition: the cross-section test

Pick the snake up, support it well, and look at it end-on, imagining a slice straight across the body. Run a gentle hand along the back.

Underweight

Triangular in cross-section, with the muscle sloping away from a visible ridge down the center of the back, where the spine sits closer to the surface.

Ideal

A rounded loaf or slightly rounded rectangle, full and even with no sharp angles. Along the back it feels like muscle over bone, not a sharp ridge or a padded gap.

Overweight

Round in a different way: skin folds along the sides, or a soft, doughy feel over the ribs where fat has built up under the skin.

The 9-point body condition scale used for dogs and cats does not apply to snakes. Use this check at every weigh-in.

When a body change is a vet visit A snake that reads triangular over several checks, especially with a falling weight line, refused meals or lethargy, needs a vet rather than more food: thinness in a reptile is as often parasites, illness or a husbandry problem as it is underfeeding. A snake that is consistently round needs its prey size or feeding frequency adjusted, never meals skipped outright, since sudden fasting brings its own problems in an animal already carrying extra weight.

Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Defensive as a baby, mellow as an adult. Most of what makes handling go well is timing, not grip.

Give it time to settle first

Don't handle a newly arrived ball python for the first one to two weeks, and don't start until it is eating regularly on a normal schedule. A snake still adjusting to a new enclosure, with no feeding rhythm yet, is not ready to be picked up. Until then: fresh water, spot cleaning, and nothing else. Juveniles are more defensive and high-strung than adults, almost without exception, and it settles as the snake matures. Patience early pays off later.

How often and how long

Once the snake is settled, 2 to 3 sessions a week is a reasonable baseline, up to 3 to 5 for a snake that handles well, keeping each one to 15 to 30 minutes at most. Overhandling causes real stress in this species and is a common cause of refused meals, so more is not better here.

The two timing rules

48 to 72 hours after a mealHandling too soon after feeding is one of the most common causes of regurgitation in pet snakes, and a regurgitation is hard on the digestive system and slow to recover from.
Never during a shedCloudy or blue eyes mean a shed has started and the snake is functionally blind for a few days. A snake that can't see is more defensive and more likely to bite out of startlement, not aggression. Wait until the shed is complete and the eyes are clear.

How to pick one up

Approach from the side, never straight down from above, which reads as a predator. Support the snake's full weight with both hands or along your forearms, lifting from the middle of the body rather than near the head or tail. Let it move across your hands at its own pace with gentle, steady control instead of restraining it. Check the enclosure lock after every session.

Put it back when you see Balling up tightly with the head tucked away · rapid or heavy breathing · the head hidden against your hand or arm · actively trying to move away · musking, a foul-smelling release from the cloaca (the single opening under the tail). None of these is aggression, and a refused next meal is the same message, late.

Bites

Rare, and usually not aggression. A bite is typically a feeding response to the smell of rodent on your hands, or a startled reaction. The wound is generally a small skin break, cleaned with soap and water. Feed with tongs, and don't handle the snake right after you have handled rodents.

Who should hold it

New handlers do best seated, with the snake held low over a lap, sofa or bed, and the same goes for a nervous snake. Supervise children around the snake at all times; the hygiene rules, and why children under 5 should not handle it at all, are on page 11.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Running a heat source without a thermostat, or putting the probe against the mat instead of on the surface the snake lies on.
  • Treating the small tub as the goal rather than the compromise, and never moving the snake into a real enclosure.
  • Guessing at humidity, or trusting an analog dial, instead of reading a digital hygrometer.
  • Leaving the enclosure bare because clutter looks untidy, in an animal whose stress is driven by feeling exposed.
  • Feeding in a separate tub, or handling within 48 to 72 hours of a meal, then treating the regurgitation that follows as bad luck.
  • Feeding an adult on a juvenile's schedule. Obesity is real and common in captive ball pythons.
  • Skipping quarantine for a new snake, which is how mites and inclusion body disease reach a collection (page 20).
  • Pine, cedar or aspen substrate, or live prey.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after a thermal gradient, correct humidity and secure hides, never instead of them. For this species the key question has been tested: Kaufmann and colleagues compared 35 ball pythons in a conventional rack with furnished terrariums (substrate, hides, climbing, a basin big enough to bathe in, raised basking spots, living plants) and concluded that for welfare, racks are no alternative to a furnished terrarium. They compared whole systems, not single items. In this order:

1. A furnished enclosure

The part with a study behind it. Treat the enclosure itself as the enrichment, not something you add items to.

2. Cover across the whole floor

Not just two hides. Cork tubes and flats, leaf litter and plants let the snake be out without being exposed. Two secure hides, warm and cool, stay the baseline.

3. Stable climbing structure

A branch braced at both ends, or stacked cork, low enough that a fall is not a fall. Racks cannot offer this at all.

4. Water big enough to get into

A basin the snake can fully enter supports shedding and hydration, and it is passive: you provide it, the snake decides. Keep it stable and clean, since a soaking snake also defecates in it.

5. Scent

Snakes navigate chemically. A shed skin from another enclosure, a scent-marked piece of cork, or prey dragged across the substrate before offering costs nothing. Untested, but it costs nothing.

6. Rearranging, genuinely last

A new piece of cork or a shifted branch every few weeks is fine. Tearing the enclosure down weekly is not.

What not to do Don't treat a rack as enriched by adding one item to it: the rack is the thing that lost. Don't stack decor so densely the snake can't reach the warm spot; thermal access outranks everything here. Don't use handling as enrichment, especially around a shed. And don't let feeding response drive the design: a snake that strikes at movement is not asking for a more exciting room.

Females, follicles & egg binding

Relevant to every female owner, whether or not a male has ever been in the house.

Maturity and follicles

Males can breed from about 16 to 18 months and females from about 27 to 31 months, and a female's readiness follows weight as much as age: breeding females mature at around 1,500 g, from about 27 to 31 months. Her ovaries carry small follicles, the yolk-filled eggs in waiting, all year. In the breeding season they grow, and without fertilization they are normally reabsorbed. A female does not need a male to cycle.

A young female around 800 to 1,000 g often stops eating for weeks while follicles develop. Offer smaller meals less often, weigh her weekly, and see a vet if she starts losing weight fast (page 15).

If she ovulates and lays

OvulationA firm swelling in the rear third of the body that stays for several days.
The pre-lay shedShe sheds once more before laying, and the eggs come about 30 days after that shed, or roughly 35 to 62 days after ovulation.
The clutchTypically about six eggs. A female kept alone can lay infertile ones.
BroodingShe coils around the clutch, and Brashears and DeNardo (2015) found she does not warm the eggs by shivering, as pythons are often credited with doing: they measured no heat production in the ball python. A brooding female can go 4 to 6 months without eating, living off fat built up beforehand.

A female kept alone can produce eggs in later life too. A zoo female laid seven eggs at 62 years old, more than 15 years after her last contact with a male, either from long-stored sperm or from facultative parthenogenesis, reproduction without a male.

Egg binding is a vet visit, promptly Egg binding, or dystocia, is eggs she cannot pass. A healthy female carrying eggs often refuses food but stays bright, alert and active. A female with dystocia usually stops eating, turns weak and may look swollen, and untreated cases slide into severe lethargy until she stops responding. Reptiles can hold eggs for weeks or months past the normal laying time, so the change is easy to miss. See a reptile vet promptly if she turns weak or lethargic around laying time, or is past it and losing condition. Anything protruding from the vent is a same-day emergency (page 29).

Causes and treatment

The usual causes are an enclosure too hot or too cold, humidity off, nowhere suitable to lay, dehydration and poor condition. Most dystocia cases are in single females that never met a male. Treatment ranges from fluids, calcium and hormone injections to surgery, and for a pet female a vet may recommend removing the ovaries and oviducts so it cannot happen again. Follicles that neither ovulate nor reabsorb, called follicular stasis, are a separate problem and a common reason for surgery too.

The winter appetite dip & brumation

A seasonal slowdown, not a medical crisis, once you have checked it is not illness.

Brumation is the reptile version of a seasonal slowdown, tied to temperature and daylight: less activity, less appetite, a slower metabolism. It is not mammal hibernation. A brumating snake stays capable of waking, moving and surfacing to drink. Many ball pythons show a natural dip in appetite from roughly October through March with no deliberate cooling at all, and they normally start eating again on their own in late winter or early spring. Wild ball pythons live with modest seasonal temperature swings, which is one reason the captive version usually looks like a mild appetite dip rather than a dramatic shutdown.

Does a pet ball python need brumating?

No. Deliberate cooling is a breeder's tool for triggering a breeding cycle, and a snake kept as a pet at steady, correct temperatures year round doesn't need it. Weight loss beyond the expected range, any breathing sign, or lethargy well beyond the season's quiet ends it: warm the enclosure back up. Warming happens as gradually as cooling, and the first meal waits until the snake has been back at normal temperatures for several days and is active and alert.

Respiratory infection & scale rot

One from too cold or the wrong humidity, the other from too wet and too dirty.

Respiratory infection

The signature ball python illness, and the one every keeper should be able to recognize. Cause: temperatures running too low, humidity wrong in either direction, a dirty enclosure, or ongoing stress. It spikes each winter as enclosures cool along with the weather outside, and a power outage that holds the snake below 72°F (22°C) for more than a day or two is the classic trigger. Signs: wheezing, an audible clicking, open-mouth breathing, mucus or bubbles around the mouth or nostrils, nasal discharge, lethargy, and sometimes gaping.

Response: it needs antibiotics prescribed by a vet, not a wait-and-see approach. Left untreated it can progress to pneumonia and become fatal. While you arrange the visit, raise the enclosure toward the top of its normal range, check the humidity, and isolate the snake if you keep others. A sick reptile is kept at the upper end of its preferred range because warmth there helps the immune system and the drugs work, which is also why an enclosure shouldn't sit at the bottom of its range by default.

Learn this one by sound Clicking or wheezing is the clearest audible signal in this package. If you hear it: warm side to target, humidity checked, the snake isolated from others, and a call to a reptile vet today.

Scale rot (infectious dermatitis)

Cause: damp, dirty substrate, excess moisture, or poor hygiene in the enclosure. It is the exact failure of chasing a humidity number without ventilation. Signs: discolored belly scales, brown, reddish or yellow; blisters or sores; scales that feel soft or damaged; and a foul smell.

Response: move the snake to a clean, dry enclosure immediately and correct the humidity. Mild cases respond to antiseptic or antibiotic treatment, but anything spreading or blistering needs a vet.

Concrete threshold Any belly scale that is discolored and spreading, or blistered, is a vet visit, not a wait-and-watch. The fix that prevents both conditions is the same: humidity held in its band with real ventilation (page 9), and the cleaning schedule on page 11.

Mouth rot, mites & thermal burns

One rides on another problem, one arrives on a new animal, and one is prevented by a single device.

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

Often secondary to stress, injury or ongoing poor husbandry rather than arriving on its own. Signs: pinpoint bleeding on the gums, thick excess mucus or pus, cheesy-looking buildup in the mouth, a sour smell, visible swelling, open-mouth breathing, and refusing food. Response: it needs veterinary treatment and does not resolve on its own. Because it usually rides on another problem, bring an honest account of your setup to the visit.

Mites

Snake mites (Ophionyssus natricis) are tiny blood-feeding parasites that spread fast through direct contact and shared equipment, which is how a new animal brings them in. Signs: tiny black, brown, reddish or orange specks on the scales, clustered around the eyes, the chin or under the scales; long soaking sessions, as if trying to drown them; rubbing against surfaces; a drop in appetite. A heavy burden kills through anemia or infection, and mites carry bacteria and viruses between snakes, inclusion body disease among them.

  • Check: gently rub the snake while it rests over a sheet of white paper, and look for anything that falls off.
  • Treat: see a vet once mites are confirmed. Treating the snake alone is not enough: the whole enclosure has to be deep-cleaned at the same time, because mites persist in the environment after the snake looks clear.

Thermal burns

A common and entirely preventable injury, caused by a heat source running without a thermostat, a probe that has slipped off the surface it should read, or a heater the snake can touch. Hot rocks are the textbook case. Every heat element runs through a thermostat, with its probe where the snake lies (page 8) and any overhead heater at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) out of reach. A burn is a wound: fix the heat source first, then see a reptile vet.

Quarantine is the real fix for mites A new snake kept apart for 3 to 6 months, on paper towel, with its own tools and cared for last, is how mites are kept out of a collection in the first place (page 20). On bare paper, checked daily, mites usually show within the first few weeks if they are there at all.

Internal parasites, regurgitation & obesity

Less dramatic than the last two pages, and far more common.

Internal parasites

Every newly arrived ball python should get a fecal exam before you assume it is healthy, even if it looks fine and eats well. Parasites often cause no visible signs for months, and by the time they do, the burden can already be significant. Wild-caught and imported animals carry far heavier loads than captive-bred snakes, and the two get mixed at shows and in the trade.

A fecal test findsWhat it means
Protozoa (single-celled parasites)Most found in reptile stool are harmless. Some, such as Entamoeba invadens, cause serious illness in snakes.
Nematodes (roundworms)Strongyloides and Rhabdias turn up often, ranging from minor to a cause of gut blockage or pneumonia, depending on the species and the load.
Cryptosporidium serpentisThe most serious one. It thickens the stomach lining and causes repeated regurgitation after meals, has no reliably effective treatment, and can be fatal.

Watch for: weight loss in a snake still eating normally, regurgitated meals, and abnormal stool: watery, heavy with mucus, or with visible worms. Treatment depends entirely on what the test finds. The vet picks the drug and the dose for the parasite identified; this is never a guess or an over-the-counter product.

Regurgitation

A meal eaten and then brought back up. It is hard on the digestive system and slow to recover from. The usual causes are handling within 48 to 72 hours of a meal, prey wider than the snake, the stress of a separate feeding tub, or an enclosure at the wrong temperature. It can also be a parasite sign, and in pythons an early sign of inclusion body disease.

After a regurgitation Stop feeding for a cycle and call a reptile vet, since in a python a regurgitation can be the first sign of inclusion body disease. Fix any of the causes above before the next meal.

Obesity

Real and common in captive ball pythons, and the result of overfeeding. Signs: a body round in cross-section, with skin folds along the sides or a soft, doughy feel over the ribs (page 16). Fix: adjust the prey size or the feeding interval to the schedule for its age, never by skipping meals outright, since sudden fasting brings its own problems. The scale catches it earlier than your eye does.

Inclusion body disease & prolapse

One has no cure and is kept out by quarantine. The other is always an emergency.

Inclusion body disease (IBD)

A fatal viral disease of boas and pythons, caused by reptarenaviruses. It shows as nerve signs, most recognizably stargazing, the head held up and back at an odd angle as if staring at the sky. Other signs: tremors, facial tics, abnormal tongue flicking, disorientation, loss of the righting reflex (the snake can't turn itself back over when flipped), regurgitation, and in advanced cases seizures.

How it spreadsThrough body fluids: bites during breeding, fight wounds, contaminated droppings, and snake mites carrying infected blood between animals.
Boas and pythons differA boa can carry the virus for months to years with few or no signs. A python goes the other way: once signs start, most die within days or weeks.
DiagnosisPCR (polymerase chain reaction, a lab test that finds a germ's genetic material) and a biopsy.
TreatmentNone. Once nerve signs appear, most vets discuss euthanasia rather than treatment.
Other reptilesNot established in corn snakes and other colubrids (the family most other pet snakes belong to), or in lizards, so treat it as a boa and python risk.
Quarantine is the only defense A carrier can look completely healthy for the whole of a short quarantine, which is why a real one runs 3 to 6 months, in a separate room, with the new snake cared for last (page 20). Any nerve sign in a new or established snake is an emergency: keep it well away from every other reptile and call the vet.

Prolapse

Internal tissue pushed out through the vent and visible from outside. Most often it is the cloaca itself, but it can be the hemipenes in a male or a section of the colon. It is always an emergency.

  • Keep the tissue clean and moist with a damp paper towel or a clean cloth, and get to a vet immediately. Exposed tissue dries and swells quickly, and the longer it stays out, the harder it is to save.
  • Never try to push it back in yourself.
  • It is a symptom, not a disease. Straining from constipation or a blockage, dehydration, internal parasites, egg binding or bladder stones can all push tissue out. The visit is about finding and fixing that cause too, or it tends to recur.

Shedding, stuck shed & eye caps

A healthy shed comes off in one piece. Look at every one.

The shed cycle

StageWhat you see
Pre-shedColors dull, eyes turn cloudy blue, the snake hides more and may refuse food. The eyes usually stay cloudy about 4 to 7 days.
ClearingThe eyes go clear again. The snake is not finished: the shed usually follows about 4 to 7 days later.
Active shedThe snake rubs against rough surfaces to start the peel, then crawls out of its skin.

Humidity doesn't separate the old skin from the new: a fluid the snake releases under the old skin, carrying enzymes, does that, and the cloudy blue eye is that fluid seen through the clear scale over the eye. Humidity matters at the end, when the loosened skin has to come away in one piece. So through a shed, raise humidity to 70 to 80%, make sure the humid hide is genuinely damp, give the snake cork, slate or a rough branch to rub on, and leave it alone: no handling.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Skin that comes off in flakes and patches instead of one piece. Low humidity is the most common single cause, but one of six: skin parasites, poor nutrition, infection, nothing rough to rub against, and low thyroid function all do it too. Rings of old skin left on the tail tip can tighten across shed cycles and cut off its blood supply.

  • To loosen it, a humidity chamber is the gentler first choice for this species: a lidded tub with a warm, damp towel inside, kept warm and covered so it is dark, with the snake in it for no more than an hour. Most of the remaining skin comes away within a day, and it can be repeated. The other option is a soak in chin-deep water at enclosure temperature for 30 minutes, supervised throughout, never longer. Then gently work the loose skin free; never peel unshed skin by force.
  • Repeated bad sheds in a correct setup point to parasites, nutrition or an underlying condition. That is a vet conversation, not a humidity problem.

Retained eye caps

The clear scale over each eye, the spectacle or eye cap, sheds with the rest of the skin. When it stays on, the snake's vision and behavior often look completely normal, which makes it one of the easiest problems to miss. After every shed, find the skin and check that both eye caps came off with it.

Never pick at an eye cap Removing a retained eye cap is a veterinary job: days of eye ointment until the cap falls off or can be lifted with fine forceps. The eye underneath is not replaceable. See a vet rather than reaching for tweezers.

Reading droppings, urates & hydration

Stool is the earliest free health check you get. Look at it before you clean it up.

Urates are the white part of a reptile's droppings. Instead of liquid urine, the body turns nitrogen waste into uric acid, made in the liver from protein digestion, and passes it as a white, chalky paste. A snake has a single opening for both, so the urate comes out with the stool. Its color and texture are the fastest daily read on hydration you have.

It looks likeUsually meansDo
A semi-formed stool, sometimes with fur or bone, and a white chalky urateNormal for a snake eating whole rodentsNothing.
Urate yellow, orange, or gritty and crystallineDehydration at minimum, and in a serious case a urate or kidney problemCheck the water bowl and humidity. Vet if it persists: the same uric acid chemistry is what causes gout.
Watery or unformed for more than one bowel movement in a rowA classic sign of parasites, such as Entamoeba or coccidiaVet, with a fresh sample in a sealed bag (page 28).
Bright red blood, or dark and tarryFresh bleeding near the vent, or digested blood from higher upVet the same day.
Straining, with nothing passedA cloacal problem or a blockageVet promptly; straining can end in a prolapse.
An unusually strong or off smellSomething has changedCompare against your snake's normal and watch for other signs.

How often is normal

A snake generally doesn't pass stool until a meal is mostly digested, so its droppings run on the feeding cycle, not a daily one, and a big meal means a longer gap. Expect at least one or two a month on a normal schedule. A clump of fur in the stool is normal: it is the indigestible part of a whole rodent passing through. A snake that is eating but has passed nothing in a month is outside that range, and if the gap continues or the snake stops eating, it needs a vet. Log the date of each one on the Owner Log.

Hydration

  • Urates first. Smooth, white urate generally means a well-hydrated snake. Dry, hard or gritty urate is the signal to act on.
  • The water bowl is not decoration. Heavy, big enough to soak in, changed daily and scrubbed weekly. A terrestrial snake that shows dry urates gets easier access to more water.
  • Humidity is part of hydration. Hold the 55 to 65% baseline. Air that runs too dry tends to show up weeks later as a bad shed.
  • Signs beyond the urates. Wrinkled or loose skin outside a shed, skin that stays tented when gently pinched instead of springing back, sunken eyes or dented eye caps, and sheds that keep coming off in pieces. A snake heading into a shed can look wrinkled too, so judge once the eyes have cleared. A shallow, supervised soak at enclosure temperature can get a mildly dehydrated snake drinking; one that is sunken-eyed and lethargic needs a vet for fluids.

Reptiles hide illness well. A change in the droppings often shows weeks before the snake looks sick.

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

A healthy adult tolerates one cool night without harm. The risk is days without heat, and feeding a snake that can't digest.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Keep the snake at or above its night range, 72 to 75°F (22 to 24°C). Below it for more than a day or two is the classic winter trigger for respiratory infection. A battery thermometer in the enclosure tells you the real number.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap.
  • Move the enclosure, or the snake in a ventilated tub, to the warmest room, usually one without outside walls.
  • Use a wrapped hot-water bottle or hand warmers beside the snake, never touching it.
  • Stop feeding. Water only until heat is reliably back.
  • Past a day or two below the floor, board it with a vet, a friend with power, or a reptile shop.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, gas stoves or open flame anywhere near the enclosure: a fire and carbon monoxide risk.
  • Don't put a heat pack in direct contact; a cold, sluggish snake can't move away fast enough and will burn.
  • Don't feed "to keep its strength up." Undigested food in a cold gut is a bacterial risk, not a benefit.
  • Don't warm a cold snake in hot water: lukewarm only, and only once it is alert.
Outage kit Hand warmers or a hot-water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated locking tub, and the vet numbers on paper.

In a heat wave

The air anywhere in the enclosure must stay at or below 95°F (35°C). A thermostat can cut the heat source but cannot cool the room, so read both ends through the day while the heat lasts.
