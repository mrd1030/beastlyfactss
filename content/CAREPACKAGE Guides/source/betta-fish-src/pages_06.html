Red flags & testing the water first

Almost every common betta illness traces back to water quality or water temperature, so the first move is nearly always the same.
Test the water before you treat anything Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and temperature come first, before a single dose of medication goes in. Ammonia and nitrite are invisible, and a very large share of sudden "sick fish" presentations trace back to ammonia, nitrite or low oxygen rather than disease. Medicating before you have ruled that out can add stress to a fish already struggling with its environment. Correcting the water is not doing nothing while you wait: it is the treatment.

Finding a vet & when it is a vet's job

Most betta problems are solved at the test kit. The ones that are not need somebody who has seen a fish before.

When it is a vet's job

Call in an aquatic veterinarian or fish-health specialist when illness is spreading across more than one fish; when you see an open ulcer, significant bloating or a badly protruding eye; when fin erosion or a white-spot outbreak is advancing day over day despite a clean water test; when a fish is listing on its side or resting on the bottom unresponsive; or whenever the honest answer to "what caused this" is "I don't know, and the water tested clean." For many parasites, only a microscope on a sample confirms the diagnosis, which no owner can do from outside the tank.

Finding a vet who treats fish

Most small-animal clinics do not see fish, and a general search will not say which do. The American Association of Fish Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet map that covers North America only. If it shows no one within reach, there are three next moves: ask a local vet, ideally one who already sees your other pets, whether they would take the case with guidance from a fish vet (the association will connect the two); contact the association directly, and it will email its members to look for someone near you; or ask an aquarium shop or a local aquarium society, which usually know who in the area treats fish. Do this now, while nothing is wrong, and write the number on the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}}).

What to have ready when you call Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH, and the kit used. The temperature. Tank volume and how long it has run. The last water change. What the fish is doing, what it eats, anything added recently, and any medication already used, at what dose.

Fin rot, regrowth & clamped fins

A ragged fin is a symptom with several causes, and the fin tells you more over a week than in a single look.

Fin rot

What it isA bacterial infection that shows as frayed, ragged or discolored fins
TreatmentMild cases: clean water and aquarium salt, dosed for scaled fish only. Add antibiotics if it is more advanced or not improving, which is a vet's call (page {{P:meds}})

The complication is that a ragged fin has several possible causes. Infection is only one of them: poor water quality, abrasion against unsuitable surfaces, disease and rough handling all produce the same frayed margin. Fin erosion is also a secondary consequence of bacterial and fungal disease, so damage that began as a torn fin can end up infected later. In a single betta tank the list is short: sharp decor, a filter intake, and water left too long between changes.

Direction of travel

Because the appearance overlaps, the reliable test is direction of travel. Photograph the fish from the side in good light, then compare the same view a week later. Damage that is holding still, or slowly filling back in, is wear. Damage that has moved further toward the body is active and needs treating. One picture cannot tell those apart, which is why fin rot gets diagnosed so often in fish that do not have it.

New growth looks like damage

Fins regrow, and the new tissue arrives without its color. In zebrafish, where regeneration has been measured most precisely, new tissue appears beyond the injury within a day or two and stays whitish for several days. Color returns from the base of the fin outward, and the fin's size and shape are restored after roughly three weeks. The clear or pale band sits at the outer edge the whole time, exactly where an owner has been told to watch for rot. A thin translucent margin on an otherwise clean fin is usually the fish repairing itself, and treating it as an infection works against the thing that closes the gap.

Age changes the timeline

Regrowth slows with age. In turquoise killifish, a species kept as a laboratory model for aging, young fish regrew 98 percent of an amputated tail fin within four weeks, middle-aged fish 78 percent, old fish 57 percent and the oldest 46 percent. A betta past its second birthday is genuinely old. Split and thinning fins on a fish that eats well, swims normally and holds its color are consistent with age, and clean water still helps, but it stops being a cure.

Clamped fins are a posture, not damage Clamped fins are held shut and pressed against the body instead of spread and moving. They travel with signs that affect the whole fish, such as lethargy and darkened color. A fish with torn fins held fully open is showing wear on the tissue. A fish with undamaged fins held clamped is showing distress, and the water is the first place to look.

Ich & velvet

Two parasites, one of which kills in days. Know the difference by the look of the dust.

Ich (white spot disease)

CauseThe parasite Ichthyophthirius multifiliis, usually brought in on a new fish and set off by a chill or a stress event
SignsSmall white grains, evenly scattered like salt, across the body and fins, with scratching against decor. Clamped fins and lethargy often go with it
Why timing mattersThe white spot is the one stage you cannot kill: the fish's own skin armors it, and the cyst on the gravel is armored by its wall. Only the free-swimming stage can be killed, so every treatment keeps the water lethal long enough for that stage to die before it finds a fish
Warmer is fasterAt 75 to 79°F (24 to 26°C) the whole cycle completes in about 3 to 6 days. Colder stretches it to weeks

Treating ich in a betta

  1. Heat. A betta can take the heat method. Raise the tank slowly, a couple of degrees a day, to 86°F (30°C) with an air stone running, since warm water holds less oxygen, and hold it for two weeks. That is a treatment ceiling for the course only, above the 76 to 82°F a betta lives at.
  2. Or salt. About a tablespoon of aquarium salt per 5 gal, added gradually so the fish can acclimate. This is for scaled fish, and it is not for shrimp or snails.
  3. Or medication, for a heavy outbreak: a malachite green or formalin product, used to its label or a vet's direction, in the hospital tank, with shrimp and snails out first.
  4. Keep going. Continue until every spot has been gone for days and no fish has died from it. Stopping when the spots vanish leaves the cysts in the gravel to hatch a fresh wave.
  5. During treatment: vacuum the gravel with each water change, pull the carbon, aerate, and count the fish daily.
  6. After: quarantine for 30 days (page {{P:quarantine}}).

Velvet (gold dust disease)

CausePiscinoodinium, the freshwater velvet parasite. Oodinium is its marine counterpart, and the two names get swapped constantly
SignsA fine gold or rust-colored dusty sheen over the body
Why it is urgentIt moves fast and can kill quickly: freshwater Piscinoodinium infections can cause high mortality once established
ResponseDarken the tank and use an antiparasitic or copper treatment, started early, in the hospital tank (page {{P:meds}} has the rules)
Do not guess between ich and columnaris The temperature moves point in opposite directions: ich is treated by warming the tank, while columnaris calls for cooling toward the low end of the betta's range. Getting it backward accelerates the infection.

Columnaris

A bacterial infection that moves in hours to days, and the one illness where the heater is the wrong reflex.
CauseFlavobacterium columnare, a bacterium that is highly contagious between fish, and between tanks on shared equipment
SignsPale or grayish saddle-shaped patches over the back, ragged white edges on the fins and mouth, and sometimes a cottony fuzz that gets it mistaken for a fungus. It is not a fungus, and it moves far faster than fin rot
VirulenceStrains range from low to high. Mortality can be acute, and by the time it is visible the outbreak may already be hard to control, so act the same day rather than watch for a week

The temperature rule runs the other way

If you have already treated ich, the temperature move is the opposite one. Ich is managed by warming the tank, because the parasite runs its life cycle faster in warm water, so warming brings it sooner to the free-swimming stage that medication can reach. Columnaris runs the other way. In a review of the disease in Veterinary Research, Declercq and colleagues report mortality rising steeply with water temperature: none at 49°F (9.4°C), 4 to 20 percent at 54°F (12.2°C), and up to 100 percent in some species at 69°F (20.5°C), with the bacterium's grip on gill tissue enhanced as the water warms.

Never Reach for the heater because it worked on the white spots last time. It is the worst thing you can do to a betta with columnaris. Cooling toward the low end of a betta's safe range, and not below about 76°F (24.5°C), is the move instead, and it only buys time for the antibacterial treatment: it is not a cure.

What to do

  1. Test the water first, as for any illness.
  2. Move toward the low end of the range slowly, and never below about 76°F.
  3. Isolate the fish in the hospital tank.
  4. Treat with an antibacterial medication, or veterinary antibiotics, dosed to the label or a vet's direction.
  5. Disinfect nets, siphons and buckets before they touch another tank.
Narrow band, narrow room A betta is a tropical fish with a narrow comfortable band, so there is less room to move the temperature in either direction than there is for a goldfish. That is why getting the direction right matters more here than it does in a coldwater tank.

Swim bladder, dropsy & fish tuberculosis

The internal problems, and the body signs that mean something is wrong inside.

Swim bladder disorder

CauseUsually overfeeding, constipation or infection
SignsBuoyancy problems: floating, sinking, or swimming at odd angles
ResponseFast the fish for 2 to 3 days, which often resolves mild cases. Afterward, offer a single cooked, de-shelled pea to help things move along. Buoyancy problems that do not clear after the fast need a vet
AfterCut the portion for good (page {{P:growth}}). Overfeeding is the usual cause, so it comes back otherwise

Dropsy

Raised, pinecone-like scales are the telltale sign. Dropsy is not really a single disease: it is a visible symptom of organ failure, from fluid building up inside the body. By the time the sign shows, the underlying problem is usually advanced and the prognosis is poor, so it is a same-day call to an aquatic vet rather than something to treat at home.

Mycobacteriosis (fish tuberculosis)

Rare, but worth knowing because it is zoonotic: it can pass to people through contact with infected water, especially through broken skin. Use gloves if you have any open cut and are doing maintenance on a tank you suspect is affected.

Other body signs

Reading waste, breathing & oxygen

Three signals a keeper can see without a microscope, and what each can and cannot tell you.

Waste: the earliest signal

Normal fish waste is a solid, somewhat compact strand that roughly matches the color of what the fish has been eating, and it does not trail behind for more than a few seconds before it drops or breaks off. The signal to watch is a shift to pale, clear or white waste that trails behind the fish in a long, stringy thread.

It is an early warning, not a diagnosis. Weight loss and a pot-bellied look are signs of internal worms, while weight loss and a refusal to eat are signs of an intestinal parasite. A fish that has simply stopped eating for a stretch, for any reason including a water problem, will often pass mostly mucus, which looks identical from outside. A single odd dropping in an eating, active fish is worth noting and rechecking, not panicking over. A pattern over several days, especially with weight loss or lost appetite, is the point to isolate the fish in a hospital tank and watch it closely.

Gasping and the oxygen question

Gasping at the surface is one of the most commonly misread signs. It is easy to assume a respiratory infection or a gill parasite. Far more often it is plain physics: dissolved oxygen above 5 milligrams per liter (mg/L, which for water is the same as ppm) supports optimum fish health, most species become distressed once it drops to roughly 2 to 4 mg/L, and deaths follow below about 2 mg/L, with surface gasping ("piping") as the visible warning. A betta has its labyrinth organ and takes air at the surface routinely. Constant, urgent gasping, with the gills working hard, is not that.

  • A fish piping after a heat wave, a power outage that stopped the filter, or an overfed, overstocked tank is telling you about the water, not about a pathogen.
  • A fish that hangs in the current from the filter outflow or an air stone is often chasing the most oxygen-rich water in the tank, so test the water.
  • Nitrite converts hemoglobin into methemoglobin, the condition known as brown blood disease, and affected fish congregate in oxygen-rich water or gasp at the surface.
  • Ammonia is toxic: the toxic form above roughly 0.05 mg/L is already enough to damage gill tissue, and acute ammonia poisoning can cause spinning and disorientation that looks like an exotic disease.
What to do Test ammonia, nitrite and nitrate and read the thermometer. Change water by the rules for the reading (page {{P:changes}}) and add surface agitation. Clear water does not mean safe water.

Behavior signs, in one list

Flashing, where the fish rubs against objects to relieve itching. Piping at the surface. Loss of appetite, loss of condition, dulled color and rapid breathing. Clamped fins. Abnormal buoyancy. Isolation or hiding in a fish that is normally active. Fish hide illness as a survival instinct, so a keeper who looks only once in the morning and once at night is working from incomplete information. Thirty seconds of watching, every day, is the habit that catches these early.

Medication rules & when a fish will not recover

The rules that apply whichever product you use, and the kindest option when treatment is past its use.

The universal rules

RuleWhy
Treat in the hospital tankSeveral medications do not tell the pathogen from the bacteria running the filter, and copper is extremely toxic to invertebrates and plants, which must come out before treatment
Pull the carbon firstActivated carbon adsorbs the medication out of the water before it can work. Take it out before dosing and keep it out until the course is finished
Turn up the aerationSeveral treatments cut the oxygen. Vigorous aeration is essential with formalin and with the raised temperature of an ich treatment
Dose to the label or a vetEvery product and condition has its own concentration and course length. Treat any number you read elsewhere as a starting point to verify
Watch the tank, not just the fishA hospital tank's filter is smaller and newer than the main tank's. Check ammonia and nitrite partway through a course

Aquarium salt: the limits

Aquarium salt is plain sodium chloride sold for aquarium use, not table salt. The starting dose for ich is about 1 tablespoon per 5 gal, added gradually so the fish can acclimate, and it is for scaled fish only. Catfish such as corydoras, loaches and shrimp do not tolerate it, which is a reason not to dose a tank with those tankmates in it. Other uses of salt are written in different units and are different jobs, so never convert one dose into another's.

  • There is no fixed number of days. Once the infection has cleared, several water changes remove the salt, rather than a course that runs a set time regardless of how the fish is doing.
  • Salt does not evaporate, so top off with plain, conditioned water, and remove salt only by a real water change.

When a fish will not recover

Some fish will not recover, and a fish vet is the one to judge whether a case is past treatment. Once it is, ending the fish's life humanely is part of keeping it. The first choice is a vet who treats fish: an overdose of anesthetic is the humane method, and a vet has a range of anesthetics and the training to use them.

Where no vet can be reached, the method for aquarium fish is a clove oil overdose, since clove oil contains the sedative eugenol. Use about 0.4 milliliters (ml) of clove oil per liter of water, in a container of water taken from the fish's own tank. Mix the oil with a little warm water first and add it slowly over about five minutes. The fish loses consciousness, stops breathing and dies. Leave it in the solution for at least 30 minutes after the last gill movement. Only then, as a final check, can it go in the freezer.

Methods to rule out Flushing, which puts a live fish into chlorinated water. Freezing a live fish, boiling, cutting off the head without first stunning the fish, and leaving it out of water to suffocate. All cause the suffering the exercise is meant to end.
