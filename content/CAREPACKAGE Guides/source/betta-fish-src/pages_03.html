Section 03 · Feeding

Diet & feeding schedule

Overfeeding is the single most common mistake in betta keeping, and it fouls the water too.

What a betta eats

Bettas are obligate carnivores and surface ambush feeders: in the wild they snatch insects, larvae and small crustaceans off the water's surface. In a tank the staple is a betta-specific, high-protein pellet. Tropical flakes and goldfish pellets are the wrong food, with the wrong nutrient profile and more filler.

How much: portion matters more than frequency

A betta's stomach is roughly the size of its own eye, which is the real basis for the standard guidance of 2 to 4 pellets per meal. Depending on pellet size and brand that can run from 2 or 3 up to 6 or 7, so the more reliable rule is whatever the fish clears within 1 to 2 minutes, with nothing left sitting on the substrate afterward. Uneaten food is the fastest way to foul a small tank.

How often

Once or twice a day is the usual range, and up to three smaller meals spaced 6 to 8 hours apart also works. There is no single correct number: pick a routine you can hold consistently, and watch the fish's body condition rather than chasing an exact figure (page {{P:growth}}). Build in one fasting day a week, with no food at all, to support digestion and reduce the risk of bloating and constipation.

Pre-soak expanding pellets If a pellet expands in water, soak it first to reduce the risk of bloat. Freeze-dried foods get the same treatment: rehydrate them before feeding, because fed dry they raise the constipation risk.

Pellets, protein foods & the food chart

Staple every day, occasional a few times a week, limited, and never at all.

Reading a pellet label

Look for a betta-specific pellet that lists dried meats such as brine shrimp, krill or fish meal near the top of the ingredient list, and roughly 30 percent protein or higher. A good range for a betta is about 35 percent protein and about 5 percent fat. Not tropical flakes, and not goldfish pellets.

Variety is the cheapest enrichment

Round out the pellet with frozen, freeze-dried or live protein foods 1 to 3 times a week. Move the feeding point around the tank rather than dropping food in the same corner every day, and vary the food itself. It is the cheapest item on the enrichment list, and the one most consistent with the wider fish enrichment research, where structural and feeding variation both show effects (page {{P:enrichment}}).

Treats & the never-feed list

What is safe in moderation, and what does harm.

Safe, in moderation

Frozen or freeze-dried bloodworms, brine shrimp, daphnia, tubifex worms, mysis shrimp and mosquito larvae, and a blanched, shelled pea now and then as a remedy for constipation. All of them sit on top of the pellet staple and never replace it.

Overfeeding, and what it looks like

Overfeeding leads to bloating, constipation, swim bladder trouble and fouled water from what is left uneaten (page {{P:internal}}). An overfed fish shows a swollen belly and may pass stringy feces or none. The remedy is smaller portions, the fasting day, and a look at the fish from above every week (page {{P:growth}}), not a different food.

If a pellet swells in water, it swells in the fish Soak expanding pellets before feeding, and rehydrate anything freeze-dried. This one habit prevents a large share of bloat.
A fish that looks hungry is usually a fish that always looks hungry A betta's stomach is about the size of its own eye, so a few pellets fill it. A betta that begs at the glass is not a betta that needs more.

Why a betta stops eating

Bettas refuse food for ordinary reasons and for one worrying one. Work them in order.

The six reasons

  1. Environmental stress. A new tank, a recent move, handling or an aggressive tankmate can suppress appetite temporarily.
  2. Poor water quality. An ammonia, nitrite or nitrate spike is one of the most common causes of both appetite loss and general illness, and it is the first thing to check, every time.
  3. Water that is too cold. A betta needs roughly 76 to 82°F (24.5 to 28°C). Cold water slows metabolism and appetite, and most homes without a heater run too cold.
  4. Picky eating. Bettas are known for rejecting a new brand or a new type of food outright. Try a different food before assuming illness.
  5. Overfeeding, constipation or swim bladder disease. Eating too fast or too much can swell the stomach against the swim bladder. Watch for a bloated belly, stringy feces, or floating and sinking unnaturally.
  6. Illness. Bacterial infection or parasites can suppress appetite, often alongside white spots, clamped fins or labored swimming.

What to try, in order

Step
1Test ammonia, nitrite and nitrate, and read the thermometer. Do this before anything else
2Fix whatever the test found. Correcting the water is the treatment, not a delay
3Look the fish over: swollen or bloated belly, stringy or absent feces, lethargy or hiding, clamped fins, white spots, a curved or S-shaped spine, floating or sinking, labored swimming
4Offer something different. A new brand or a different protein food is the usual cure for a picky eater
5Check what changed around the tank, and give a new tank or a move a few days to settle

When a fast becomes a symptom

The numbers A healthy adult can physically survive up to about two weeks without food, but that is an emergency ceiling, not a target. Start investigating at 5 to 7 days of refused food, after you have already checked water quality and temperature and tried a different food. Start sooner, at once, if any physical symptom from step 3 shows up alongside it. A week or more of refusal plus a physical symptom is the real concern threshold.

A short absence of a day or two, a weekend away, is low risk. Most refusals come down to stress from a move or a tank change, pickiness about a new food, or water that is too cold or of poor quality, which is why those are the first things to check. Bring the Owner Log to any vet call (page {{P:ownerlog}}).

Body condition & portion control

You cannot weigh a betta at home. You can read its shape from above.

The top-down check

Look down into the tank from the top, not through the side. A betta in good condition looks like a streamlined torpedo, with the "shoulders" just behind the gill covers the same width as the middle third of the body. If the sides bulge outside the shoulders, the betta is too fat. If the shoulders stand out and the middle is small, it is too thin.

The dashed guides mark the shoulder width. The middle third should reach them and go no further.

ShapeWhat you seeWhat to do
Too thinProminent shoulders and a small middleWork through the refusal checklist
CorrectShoulders and the middle third the same widthHold the portion. Look again each week
OverweightSides bulge outside the shouldersCut each meal by one pellet, keep that for 2 weeks and look again. If nothing has changed, take away another pellet

Setting the portion

Begin with the number of pellets that could, in theory, fit inside the fish's eye, which is the basis of the 2 to 4 pellets a meal in the diet section. Keep the weekly fasting day. A good staple pellet runs about 35 percent protein and about 5 percent fat (page {{P:foods}}).

Why it matters Overfeeding leads to bloating, constipation, swim bladder trouble and fouled water. A weekly top-down look catches the trend long before the belly is swollen, and it is the closest thing a betta owner has to a weigh-in. Write the result in the Owner Log (page {{P:ownerlog}}).
