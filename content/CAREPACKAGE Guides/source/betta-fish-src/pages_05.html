Choosing a healthy betta & bringing it home

The price tag says nothing about the fish's condition, and the cup it waits in says less than it seems to.

Judging the fish, not the water

Bettas are sold in small cups or bottles without filtration or aeration, and they tolerate that better than most fish would. A 2023 study by Kajimura and colleagues put the 96-hour lethal level of the toxic form of ammonia for bettas at 7.7 milligrams per liter, and the authors suggest generations of shipping in small bags that hold about 90 percent air and a little water may have selected for that tolerance. A betta can sit in fouled water for days and still be alive, so the cup tells you little. Whether the fish is healthy is a question for the fish itself.

What a healthy betta shows

Bright, vibrant color. Intact fins, held open. Normal activity, swimming around the cup and not hiding or lying on the bottom all the time. A good appetite, if the store will let you watch it eat.

What to walk away from

A fish that is not eating, or is lethargic or swimming abnormally. Frayed fins, or fins that look worse on a second visit. New spots or growths, including the gold dust of velvet or the salt-grain spots of ich. Fins pressed flat against the body.

A sick $5 fish is the most expensive betta in the store once the medication and a hospital tank are added to the bill. Paying a few dollars more for a lively fish in cleaner water is the cheapest insurance in betta keeping.

Before it comes home

  • The tank is cycled: a full ammonia dose reads zero within 24 hours, nitrite reads zero and nitrate is present (page {{P:cycle}}).
  • The heater has been running long enough to hold 78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C) steadily.
  • You have found a veterinarian who treats fish. The American Association of Fish Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory that covers North America, and if it shows no one within reach, ask a local vet to take the case with a fish vet's guidance (page {{P:redflags}}).

The trip and the introduction

  1. Float the sealed cup or bag on the tank's surface for 20 to 30 minutes so the water inside comes to the tank's temperature.
  2. Net the fish out and release only the fish. The cup water carries the shop's ammonia and whatever its tanks were carrying, so it never goes into your tank.
  3. When the cup and the tank differ by more than 10°F, or the pH and hardness are very different, acclimate slowly by adding tank water to the cup a little at a time instead. A gap past about 18°F stretches that to two hours or more.
  4. Add one fish at a time, and watch the water parameters for a day or two after any addition.
The first days Stress from a new tank or a move can suppress appetite temporarily. A refused meal in the first days is an expected stress response, not yet a symptom, and the five-to-seven-day investigation line on the feeding pages applies after the water and temperature have been checked (page {{P:refuse}}).

Quarantine & the hospital tank

A fish that looks fine on arrival is the normal state of a fish whose symptoms have not shown yet.

Why every new fish is quarantined

Quarantine means keeping a new or sick fish in a separate aquarium for a set time before it joins others. A pathogen picked up somewhere between breeder, wholesaler and shop does not always show on day one, and by the time it does in a shared tank, your other fish have already been exposed. The isolation also makes the fish easy to watch and to treat.

How long At least 30 days. For a fish you especially do not want to lose, or do not want introducing something to an established tank, 30 to 60 days. Thirty days is the floor, not a target to hit and stop, because some parasites and infections take that long to show.

The bare hospital tank

"Bare" matters: no substrate, no driftwood, nothing with crevices a parasite or its eggs can hide in, and nothing that makes the tank harder to see into or scrub clean between uses. The home version is cheap.

Tank10 to 20 gal (38 to 76 L), kept bare, sized to what you are quarantining
FilterA sponge filter on an air pump: gentle biological filtration, and no cartridge to replace mid-quarantine
HeaterMatched to the species and the treatment. For a betta, its normal range
Air pumpRuns the sponge filter and keeps oxygen up during any treatment
Test kitA hospital tank's filter is smaller and more fragile than the main tank's, so ammonia and nitrite checks matter more

A brand-new sponge has no bacteria in it. Seed the hospital tank's sponge from an established, running filter: keep a spare sponge cycling permanently in the main tank's filter, or borrow one for a few days before you need it, so the filter works on day one and you are not running a fish-in cycle in a tank you are also trying to treat (page {{P:fishin}}).

What to watch for in quarantine

Feed normally, do routine partial water changes, and check daily for anything that was not there on arrival: clamped fins, unusual spots or patches, rapid gill movement, flashing against decor, appetite loss, cloudy eyes, or a fish that hangs listlessly. A fish that arrives thin or stressed can take a few days to settle before its true baseline shows, so do not read the first day or two too literally in either direction.

Keep it separate

  • The quarantine tank keeps its own nets and siphon hoses, which never touch another tank.
  • Between uses, the tank and equipment are disinfected and stored dry. A tank that still has residue from the last sick fish is not starting the next 30 days from zero.
  • Treat in the hospital tank, not the display. Several medications kill the bacteria that run the filter, and copper is extremely toxic to invertebrates and plants (page {{P:meds}}).

Tankmates

A betta does not need company. Anything you add is a risk you choose on the fish's behalf.

The fighting-fish name is earned between males. A male lives alone or with tankmates that are peaceful, short-finned and not interested in his fins. The tank also has to be bigger than a lone betta's: plan on at least 10 to 20 gal (38 to 76 L) for peaceful nano fish.

The four checks

A pairing has to pass all four: the temperature ranges overlap, the water chemistry overlaps, neither adult fits in the other's mouth, and neither is a known nipper or bully toward the other's body shape. The pairings that go wrong pass three. The temperature matched, the water matched, the sizes matched, and the barbs took the betta's tail apart in a week.

Adding a tankmate

Quarantine the newcomer first. Rearrange the decor while the bag floats, which breaks up the established territorial markers, and feed the betta at the moment the new fish is released. Then watch the betta's reaction: it is the thing to read.

When it is failing, separate them Flared gill covers and spread fins at a newcomer are a display. A display that turns into chasing and torn fins, on either fish, means the pairing has failed and one of them needs another home. Do not wait to see.

Males, females, bubble nests & fry

How breeders do it, and how little of it a pet owner needs.

Telling the sexes apart

Males and females only become distinguishable at about 3 to 4 months old. Breeders rear young bettas together as a group until then, and the males must be separated into their own containers at that point, because a male spreads his fins and flares his gill covers almost at once when he meets another male. Females are reared in communal tanks. Males are the ones with the flowing, showy fins and vivid color, which is why shops price them higher.

Bubble nests

A bubble nest is a raft of saliva-coated bubbles a male builds at the surface. It lets eggs develop in water that is warm, low in pH and low in dissolved oxygen, relatively free of predators. A male builds one with no female in the tank, and he keeps adding bubbles when the nest needs them, so a nest on its own is not a sign of breeding and is not a reason to worry. A healthy, content male will often maintain one indefinitely.

How breeding works

Breeding is a managed routine, not something that happens by accident in a pet tank. It is also a separate project from keeping a betta: it needs its own containers and a plan for every fry.

Setting upA single male and female go into a container with floating vegetation or a small piece of foam, where the male builds the nest
SpawningTypically within 1 to 3 days. The male wraps his body around the female and fertilizes the eggs as she releases them, a few at a time, about 300 eggs in a spawning
The nestHe catches the eggs in his mouth as they fall and places them in the nest, then tends them and adds bubbles when needed
The femaleShe is removed when spawning is finished
The fryRemoved to their own tank once they have absorbed their yolk sacs, about 3 days after hatching. First foods are infusoria (very small aquatic organisms) and newly hatched brine shrimp

The fry then follow the feeding stages on the diet page (page {{P:diet}}): 4 to 5 small meals a day once free-swimming, 3 to 4 a day by weeks 3 to 4, 2 to 3 a day as juveniles. Juveniles move onto the adult routine once they are separated into their own space.

What the aggression research means for you

Rearing sets temperament The timing of isolation from an enriched environment shapes later aggression and the age of sexual maturity. A betta's temperament was set before you bought it, and none of that research makes two adult males safe together.
