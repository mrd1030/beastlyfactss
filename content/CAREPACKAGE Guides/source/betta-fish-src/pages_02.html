Tank size, type & where it goes

The volume decision comes first, before a single plant or heater is bought.

How big

Five gallons (19 L) is the current standard minimum, and it is a floor rather than a target. Bettas can technically survive in smaller volumes, but a bigger tank is easier to keep stable: temperature and water chemistry swing less in more water, which means fewer sudden stresses on the fish. Ten gallons (38 L) gives you more margin again, and it is the size to choose if the budget allows.

The 5 gal floor holds for two reasons: more water holds steadier, and restricted space raised hiding and inactivity in the enrichment research (page {{P:enrichment}}). Water quality is a matter of management, not of container size alone, but a small tank leaves far less room for error.

TankWhat it is for
5 gal (19 L)The minimum for one betta, heated, filtered, cycled and lidded
10 gal (38 L)More stable water for one betta, and the minimum for a group of females (a sorority)
10 to 20 gal (38 to 76 L) or moreThe floor for peaceful tankmates, and a group of six standard corydoras needs a 20 gal long tank on its own count

The lid and the surface

Use a tight-fitting lid: bettas are surprisingly good jumpers. Keep clear access to the surface under it. A betta breathes air directly through the labyrinth organ, so reaching the surface is a genuine physiological need. Keep decor and plants from blocking the way up.

What does not count as a tank

Never A bowl, a vase, an unheated desk container or the cup the fish came in. The cheap bowl-and-fish bundle is not a complete or humane setup: it is missing a heater and proper filtration, both of which matter for a genuinely tropical fish. It also restricts space, which the research links directly to hiding and inactivity.

Where it goes

  • Out of direct sunlight. Sunlight through a window adds heat and light that neither the heater's thermostat nor the light timer controls.
  • Away from drafts.
  • Away from constant foot traffic and vibration. Tapping the glass startles the fish and adds stress with no benefit.
Order of buying Tank first, then the heater, the filter and the test kit. The fish comes last, after the tank has cycled (page {{P:cycle}}). Setting the tank up weeks before the fish is part of the plan, not a delay.

Temperature, heater & thermometer

A betta is a tropical fish, and the heater is the item that makes every other reading more forgiving.
Sweet spot78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C), held steady
Tolerable range76 to 82°F (24.5 to 28°C)
Heater powerAn adjustable submersible heater of 15 to 25 W for a 5 gal tank, which is about 3 to 5 watts per gallon
RuleConsistency matters more than hitting an exact number. Avoid rapid swings

Why a betta cannot live at room temperature

Cold water suppresses a betta's immune function and digestion, and it slows recovery from everything else in this book. Below roughly 76°F (24.5°C) the metabolism and the appetite both slow noticeably, and most home temperatures without a heater run too cold. That is why a heated tank is the single biggest difference between a fish that struggles and one that thrives, and why the heater is not decor: it is the parameter that makes every other parameter more forgiving.

Cold also slows the bacteria that clear ammonia. A swing as small as 1°C (about 2°F) can cut their ammonia processing by around 30 percent, so a cold snap or a failed heater can bring ammonia back into a tank that was reading zero.

Choosing and setting the heater

  • Choose an adjustable submersible heater sized for the tank: 15 to 25 W for 5 gal (19 L). For a bigger tank, work from the same 3 to 5 watts per gallon, for example 30 to 50 W for 10 gal (38 L).
  • Set it to 78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C) and let it settle before the fish arrives, so the tank is warm and steady on the day.
  • Read the tank with a thermometer, and check it regularly. The thermometer, not the heater's dial, tells you what the water is really doing.
  • Keep the tank out of direct sunlight and away from drafts, since sun and drafts push the temperature around in ways the heater cannot see.

When the temperature moves on purpose

Two common illnesses call for opposite moves. Ich is treated by warming the tank slowly, because the parasite completes its life cycle faster in warm water (page {{P:ichvelvet}}). Columnaris runs the other way: the bacterium is far more aggressive in warm water, so the move is toward the low end of the betta's range, never below about 76°F (page {{P:columnaris}}). Getting the direction wrong makes either one worse, which is why the two are never guessed between.

Warm water holds less oxygen In a hot room or a hot spell, and any time the tank is warmed for a treatment, dissolved oxygen drops. Add surface agitation or an air stone.

Filtration & gentle flow

A betta needs a filter that cleans the water without pushing the fish around.

Why a sponge filter

A betta's long, flowing fins catch water like a sail. A power filter's outflow that a tetra barely notices can exhaust a betta and shred its fins over time, and bettas prefer calm water. A sponge filter driven by a small air pump is the standard for this species: it provides filtration with almost no current. It needs a separate air pump, and it offers limited chemical filtration, which plenty of sponge-filter setups run without.

If you use another type, choose one with an adjustable flow and set it gentle. The gentle flow is the point, not the filter's brand or size.

What a filter actually does

The part you can see is the least important. The sponge or media is a surface for bacteria to live on, and those bacteria are the real filter: they turn ammonia from waste and uneaten food into nitrite, then into far less harmful nitrate. A tank can look spotless and still be quietly poisoning the fish if that colony is missing or damaged.

Media typeWhat it doesHow to handle it
Mechanical (sponge, floss, foam)Traps uneaten food, waste and visible debrisRinse freely, even weekly, in old tank water
Biological (sponge, ceramic rings)Hosts the bacteria that remove ammonia and nitriteBarely ever. Rinse gently in old tank water only, and never replace it all at once
Chemical (activated carbon)Removes dissolved toxins, color, odor and leftover chlorineA consumable: replace on a schedule, and take it out while medicating (page {{P:meds}})

In a sponge filter the sponge does both the mechanical and the biological job, so treat the whole sponge as the colony: gentle rinse in old tank water, only when flow drops, and never under the tap (page {{P:cleaning}}).

The number on the box

The flow rating printed on a filter box is a best case, measured in clean water with no media loaded and no height to lift the water against. Real flow is lower, especially as the media collects debris. For a betta that is a reason to check the surface, not to buy a stronger filter: what matters is that the water is moving enough to bring oxygen in and gentle enough that the fish can rest.

What happens when a filter stops The colony needs continuous, oxygenated flow. Nitrifying bacteria become inefficient once dissolved oxygen in the filter drops below roughly 2 ppm, and biological filters do not take rapid change well. A pause while you clean is harmless. A longer stop is an emergency with its own steps (page {{P:outage}}).
Where it goes Put the sponge filter in the tank before the cycle starts, and leave it running. A filter that has never run has no bacteria in it yet.

The nitrogen cycle & fishless cycling

Weeks of work with no fish in the tank. This is the part that gets skipped, and it decides everything.

Fish waste and uneaten food break down into ammonia, which is toxic even in small amounts. One group of bacteria converts ammonia into nitrite, which is also toxic. A second group converts nitrite into nitrate, which is far less harmful and is what routine water changes remove. "Cycling" a tank is growing both groups until they keep pace with the ammonia the fish produces every day.

StageWhat is happeningWhat a test shows
1. AmmoniaWaste and leftover food break down into ammoniaAmmonia climbs, nitrite and nitrate at 0
2. NitriteBacteria convert ammonia to nitrite, which blocks oxygen uptake in the bloodAmmonia falls, nitrite spikes
3. NitrateA second group converts nitrite to nitrateNitrite falls to 0, nitrate appears
4. CycledBoth colonies keep pace with the daily wasteAmmonia 0, nitrite 0, nitrate present

Fishless cycling, step by step

  1. Set the tank up completely, filter running and heater at its normal setting. Warm, stable water cycles faster, and cold slows the bacteria.
  2. Fill with tap water dosed with a conditioner that handles both chlorine and chloramine. Either one kills the bacteria you are trying to grow.
  3. Dose pure ammonia, with no surfactants or dyes, to 3 ppm, inside the usual 2 to 4 ppm range. Food also works as an ammonia source, but it rots and fouls the water while you wait.
  4. Seed the tank if you can: filter media, gravel or sponge from an established, healthy tank carries a living colony and is the most reliable way to shorten the process. Bottled bacteria have a mixed record, so treat any single product's claims with some skepticism.
  5. Test daily or every other day, and dose ammonia back up to 3 ppm whenever it drops, so the bacteria stay fed. Skip water changes during the cycle, because they remove the ammonia you are dosing.
  6. The cycle is done when a full ammonia dose reads zero within 24 hours, nitrite also reads zero, and nitrate has started to build. All three must be true before a fish goes in.
  7. Then change enough water to bring nitrate under 20 ppm (page {{P:changes}}), keep the heater running, and bring the fish home.
How long Most fishless cycles take four to six weeks at a warm, stable temperature. In real home aquarium filters, two tanks reached undetectable ammonia and nitrite by about week three and a third took until week eight. Cold water, no seeding and an inconsistent ammonia dose all push a cycle toward the slow end. There is no way to rush the biology, only ways to seed it. More ammonia is not faster: high ammonia holds back the group that converts nitrite, so stay at 3 ppm.

Fish-in cycling & when a cycle stalls

The rescue plan for a fish that came home before the tank was ready, and the checklist for a tank that was fine and no longer is.

If the fish is already home and the tank is not cycled

This is a fish-in cycle. It is the riskier path, and it is a rescue, not a plan, but it is manageable with attention every day.

  • Test daily. Ammonia and nitrite are what you are watching; nitrate matters far less at this stage. Use a liquid reagent test kit.
  • Act on a rising reading, not on a crisis. The action line is 0.1 ppm of ammonia or nitrite, which calls for a water change of at least a third of the tank. A reading that is clearly climbing toward toxic calls for 50 percent or more, done without hesitation, because a large change that interrupts the cycle is a far smaller risk than leaving the fish in rising ammonia. Day to day, most fish-in cycles are managed in the 0.1 to 0.25 ppm range, with the size of the change scaling up as the reading gets worse.
  • Use a conditioner that detoxifies ammonia between changes. It buys a margin of safety without killing the bacteria you are growing, but it is a bridge, not a substitute for testing and water changes.
  • Never rinse filter media under the tap. Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine because they kill microbes, and the media is covered in the microbes you are trying to keep alive.

The new tank problem

In the first two to three weeks of a new tank, ammonia rises because the filter's bacteria have not finished establishing, and overfeeding and overstocking make it worse. If a fish in a tank running less than a month suddenly looks sick, the nitrogen cycle is the first suspect, not disease.

Why a cycle stalls or restarts

A tank that was reading zero can suddenly show ammonia or nitrite again. The usual causes are all avoidable:

  • Overdosing ammonia, which inhibits the nitrite-converting group.
  • A crash in pH or in KH. pH is how acidic the water is, and KH (carbonate hardness) is the buffer that holds it steady. The bacteria need both to stay stable.
  • Untreated tap water. Chlorine or chloramine kills bacteria the way it kills pathogens.
  • Medicating the tank. Several common medications, including antibiotics, kill filter bacteria, and copper is harder still: ammonia and nitrite can stay elevated for weeks to months afterward (page {{P:meds}}).
  • Rinsing media under the tap, or replacing all of it at once instead of a portion at a time.
  • A temperature drop, since the bacteria are measurably slower in cold water.
Work backward If a cycled tank reads ammonia or nitrite again, run down that list before assuming something is wrong with the fish. It is fixable the same way an initial cycle is managed: test, change water, and give the bacteria time to recover.

Water quality, testing & hardness

Most betta illness is a water problem wearing a costume. These are the numbers behind that.
ReadingTargetWhy it matters
Temperature78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C)Cold water suppresses immune function and digestion
pH6.5 to 7.5Wide tolerance. Stability matters more than the exact number
Ammonia0 ppmEven 0.25 ppm causes gill damage. Any detectable reading is an emergency
Nitrite0 ppmBlocks oxygen uptake in the blood. Toxic at any measurable level
NitrateUnder 20 ppmThe byproduct of a healthy cycle, controlled by regular water changes

Ammonia and nitrite are the only two readings with no safe nonzero baseline: a cycled tank reads exactly 0 ppm for both. A kit reads total ammonia, and a higher pH pushes more of it toward the toxic form, so the same reading can be more dangerous in one tank than another.

Test with a liquid kit, not strips

A liquid reagent kit that reads ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH is the only reliable way to know whether a tank is cycled. Paper strips are notoriously imprecise at the low end of the ammonia and nitrite scales, exactly where the answer matters.

WhenWhat to test
New or recently set up tankAmmonia and nitrite every 1 to 2 days until both read zero for at least a week
Established, cycled tankWeekly at minimum. Established tanks can still spike if the filter is disrupted, the media is rinsed in tap water, or the tank is overfed
The betta seems offImmediately. Clamped fins, lethargy and lost appetite often show in the water before they show on the fish
After a water change over 30%A quick recheck confirms the new water did not bring chlorine or chloramine in

Hardness: GH and KH

General hardness (GH) is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium, a mineral supply that does not buffer anything. Carbonate hardness (KH) is the water's buffering capacity: its ability to neutralize incoming acid and hold pH still. Every tank makes acid, and the bacteria use up carbonate as they work, so a tank can hold a normal pH for weeks on a borrowed buffer and then drop hard when it runs out.

Bettas tolerate a fairly wide range of both. The risk is very soft, poorly buffered water, common with reverse-osmosis or heavily filtered tap water, because pH can crash after a water change, which is more stressful than a steady pH slightly off target. Test KH before reaching for any pH adjuster. Bottled pH-up and pH-down force the pH away from its buffered baseline without fixing the cause, and can overshoot badly. Straight reverse-osmosis water has almost no buffer, so it is blended with tap water. If KH stays very low and pH swings, a small, consistent dose of a buffering product resolves it, after ammonia, nitrite and temperature are right.

Water changes & conditioner

The single most useful habit in fishkeeping, and the easiest one to do in a way that undoes itself.

What a change removes that a filter cannot

The filter's bacteria turn ammonia into nitrite and nitrite into nitrate, and then stop. Nothing in an ordinary freshwater filter removes nitrate, so it accumulates until water carrying it leaves the tank. A water change is not cleaning in the sense of removing visible dirt: it is dilution of something invisible. A clear tank is not a clean tank.

The number, and the test that moves it

For a heated, filtered betta tank the baseline is 25 to 30 percent of the water once a week. In a 5 gal tank that is about 1.25 to 1.5 gal (4.7 to 5.7 L). Small tanks concentrate waste faster than the volume suggests, and a smaller tank can need more frequent partial changes. Test nitrate before the weekly change and let the reading set the size:

Nitrate before the changeWhat it meansWhat to do
Under 20 ppmThe schedule matches the loadKeep the 25 to 30 percent weekly change
20 to 40 ppmNitrate is winning slowlyMove to 40 to 50 percent weekly, or add a midweek change, and feed less
Over 40 ppmPast the safe lineA 50 percent change now with matched water, retest in two days, find the cause

Small changes lose the arithmetic: a tank that adds 20 ppm of nitrate a week and gets a 25 percent change settles at about 80 ppm, while a 50 percent change settles near 40.

Putting the new water in

  1. Chlorine first. Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine, and chlorine damages gills within minutes with no treatment afterward. Chloramine is chlorine bonded to ammonia, so use a conditioner labeled for both. Dose it as the water goes in, a third before the water starts, a third at the halfway mark and a third at the end.
  2. Temperature next. Mix hot and cold to land within a couple of degrees of the tank, checked with the same thermometer you read the tank with. A change that drops the tank several degrees is the classic trigger for a white-spot outbreak a week later.
  3. Siphon from the substrate, not the surface. A gravel vacuum pulls out the waste that settles into the gravel, which is where most of the nitrate is made. Work over a different section each week.
  4. Rinse the filter sponge in the bucket of old tank water while it is out, never under the tap.
Do not do the big catch-up change A tank that has gone months without a change has drifted far from the tap. Swapping most of the water at once shocks fish that were coping. That tank gets small daily changes instead: 10 to 15 percent a day for the first week, every other day in the second, then 20 to 25 percent twice a week for two weeks, testing as you go. A new tank that is still cycling follows the fish-in rules (page {{P:fishin}}).

Substrate, plants, light & decor

The inside of the tank, and the one fact about it that surprises people: cover makes a betta swim more, not less.

Substrate

Choose a soft substrate: sand or smooth gravel. Nothing sharp, since a betta's fins tear on anything rough. The gravel vacuum pulls waste out of it at each water change.

Plants and hides

Provide hides and plants. Live plants are the better choice: fish in aquaria planted with Sagittaria subulata showed the longest average active swimming time and fewer negative behaviors, and live planting also affects water quality and the tank's microbial community. If you use artificial plants, choose soft silk ones. Stiff plastic tears betta fins, a real and common injury and an open door for fin rot (page {{P:finrot}}).

Add something near the surface for resting, such as a leaf hammock or a broad-leaved plant. Bettas are labyrinth fish and surface breathers, so resting places near the top matter as much as cover below. Do not add so much decor that the fish cannot reach the surface, because air access outranks everything else on this page. Driftwood and decor placed to break the tank into zones give a betta somewhere to be that is not simply the middle of a glass box.

Driftwood also releases tannins, which tint the water faintly amber and soften the light. That is closer to the blackwater conditions the species comes from, and it is cosmetic.

Light

A betta needs no special light, but it needs a day and a night. Put the light on an outlet timer so it switches on and off at the same time every day.

SituationHours of light a day
A newly planted tank6 to 8, while the plants settle
Once the plants have grown8 to 12
Algae taking overShorten the day again

Keep nights dark. Light left on around the clock does not help the plants, which still need their night, and it hands the extra light to algae. In a 2012 breeding study, Giannecchini, Massago and Fernandes kept pairs under five schedules: pairs on 12 or 16 hours of light spawned most often and laid the most eggs, while pairs under 20 hours or constant light laid far fewer. For a pet betta, the working rule is a normal-length day, a dark night and no light left on overnight. A betta lit hard from above in a bare tank has nowhere to be out of the glare.

The research behind it Restricted, bare conditions raised hiding and inactivity, while planted tanks produced the most active swimming. Cover makes a betta more willing to use open water, because being exposed is only a risk when there is nowhere else to be (page {{P:enrichment}}).

Cleaning & hygiene

Every other cleaning step in fishkeeping is forgiving. Filter care is not.

The one rule that matters

Never under the tap Rinse biological media in a bucket of water siphoned out of the tank during a water change, swish it, and put it straight back. Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine, added to kill bacteria, and the media is covered in the bacteria colony the tank depends on. A few seconds of tap water on a sponge is a small-scale version of a disinfection pass that kills the colony and sends the tank back to day one.

What to clean, and how

WhatHow
Sponge filterGentle rinse in old tank water, only when flow drops. Never replace the whole sponge at once: swap a portion at a time, weeks apart, so the untouched media keeps the colony going while the new media is colonized
SubstrateVacuum a different section at each water change rather than the whole floor at once
Uneaten foodRemove anything left after 1 to 2 minutes. Every pellet that reaches the gravel becomes ammonia
Activated carbon, if usedReplace on a schedule, and out of the filter while medicating

Keep equipment from tank to tank

  • A quarantine or hospital tank keeps its own nets and siphon hoses, and they never touch another tank. A net that moves between the quarantine tank and the main tank undoes the separation the second tank exists for.
  • Between uses, disinfect the hospital tank and its equipment and store it dry.
  • Columnaris is highly contagious between tanks on shared equipment, so nets, siphons and buckets are disinfected before they touch another tank (page {{P:columnaris}}).

Hands, gloves and the tank

Fish tuberculosis is rare but it can pass to people through aquarium water, especially through broken skin. Use gloves if you have any open cut and are doing maintenance on a tank you suspect is affected (page {{P:internal}}).

After medicating A treatment can stall the bacteria and cause an ammonia spike on top of the illness, and copper keeps nitrite and ammonia elevated for weeks to months. Keep testing ammonia and nitrite long after the course itself has finished.
