Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

[] Read the thermometer before anything else [] Feed the measured portion, moving the spot around, and remove anything uneaten after 1 to 2 minutes [] Thirty seconds of watching: fins, color, breathing and how much of the tank it uses [] Check the filter is running and the surface is moving [] Check the light timer switched on and off as it should

Weekly

[] Test ammonia, nitrite and nitrate on the same day each week, nitrate before the change, and log it (page {{P:ownerlog}}) [] Change 25 to 30%, conditioned and matched, vacuuming a different section of substrate (page {{P:changes}}) [] One fasting day [] A top-down look at body shape (page {{P:growth}})

Monthly

[] Rinse the filter sponge in old tank water only if flow has dropped, never under the tap (page {{P:cleaning}}) [] Check the heater holds its setting against the thermometer [] Read the log for slow trends rather than single readings [] Rotate one piece of enrichment (page {{P:enrichlog}})

Seasonal

[] Winter: the room cools, so check the heater is keeping up and the tank is still 76 to 82°F (24.5 to 28°C) [] Summer: in a hot room warm water holds less oxygen, so add surface agitation if the tank runs hot, and keep it out of direct sun (page {{P:heat}})

[] Keep testing ammonia and nitrite after the course ends (page {{P:meds}})

Power outages

An aquarium looks like it depends on light. It depends on motion: the filter and air pump keep the surface moving, and that is what brings oxygen in. Aerate before you do anything else.

What kills fish in an outage

Oxygen depletion is the fast risk. Once the filter stops, the surface stops moving, gas exchange slows, and dissolved oxygen starts falling within a couple of hours, sooner in a warm tank. Temperature drift is the slower risk: most home tanks are insulated by their own volume and the room, so a few hours without power rarely swings the water enough to be dangerous. Waste is the risk you create by getting either of the first two wrong.

Do

[] Aerate right away. A battery-powered air pump with an air stone is the cleanest fix [] No pump: scoop water out with a cup and pour it back from a few inches up for a minute or two, roughly every 10 to 15 minutes in a small tank [] Insulate with towels or blankets and keep the lid on, rather than adding any new heat source near water and electronics [] Unplug the filter at the wall, so you control when it comes back [] Check on the tank regularly. A fish gasping at the surface means aerate more often

Don't

Feed. Uneaten food consumes oxygen and rots in still water
Restart a filter that has been off for hours without checking it
Add a space heater, heat lamp or open flame near the tank
Panic-swap large amounts of water. A big change adds stress, and a second problem if the new water is not matched

When the power comes back

A filter that has sat dead for hours is a small sealed pocket where oxygen ran out and waste kept decomposing. Switch it on without checking and all of that is pushed into the tank at once: an ammonia spike and a slug of oxygen-poor water. Open the filter and rinse the media in a bucket of the tank's own water, never tap water. If the outage ran a full day or more, test ammonia and nitrite once things are running again and keep testing for a few days, since a weakened colony can take time to catch up.

Blackout kit A battery air pump with a spare air stone, a cup or jug for the pour-back method, two thick towels, a thermometer and the numbers from the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}}).
Coast, but aerate A well-established tank can generally coast through several hours without heat or filtration as long as oxygen is being managed, and the heater's 76 to 82°F (24.5 to 28°C) range is the number to watch. Aeration is the whole job.

Travel, transport & moving

A betta travels differently from most fish, because it breathes the air in the bag.

Leaving town

TripWhat to do
A weekendLow risk for a healthy adult with an automatic feeder. A healthy adult can survive up to about two weeks without food, but that is an emergency ceiling, not a target
LongerA sitter, with the meals pre-portioned and the sitter sheet filled in. Refusal of food for 5 to 7 days is the point to investigate

Bagging a betta

A bag beats a rigid bucket for most short trips, such as a local drive or a vet visit: it holds the fish in the dark, which is calming, and it flexes instead of sloshing on turns. A bucket or tub with a tight lid earns its place on longer trips, since it is harder to puncture and easier to aerate with a battery pump.

  • Leave headspace. For a betta the air gap matters even more than for most fish, since it takes air directly. Air should be the larger share. Commercial betta bags run 90 percent air with only enough water to keep the fish submerged.
  • For a short drive at home, fill the bag no more than a third with water and leave the rest as air.
  • For a longer trip, pure oxygen is standard practice: it saturates the water with far more dissolved oxygen than air can.
  • Keep the bag out of direct sun and never in a hot car. After several hours, start thinking about an insulated box around the bag.

Moving the tank

A full move is the outage and the transport problem at once, plus one more piece: the filter's colony has to survive being out of the tank. That colony lives mostly on the media, so the media can travel separately, as long as it stays wet.

  • Pack the sponge in a sealed bag or tub of the tank's own water, never tap water, and never dry. For a longer drive, drop a battery air stone into the same container.
  • Set the filter up as soon as the new tank is filled, using that reserved water, so there is no gap where the colony sits idle. Handled this way, most moves do not need a fresh cycle.
  • Move the fish last, after the new tank's water is conditioned and close to the old tank's temperature, and get it out of the bag as soon as you reasonably can.
The first week after a move Test ammonia and nitrite for a few days: a weakened colony can need time to catch up, and a new room is a new ambient temperature, so read the thermometer too. Keep the tank out of direct sun and away from drafts.

Owner log

One row a week, filled in on test day. Photocopy this page, or track digitally. Consistency matters more than the format.

DateTemp °FAmmonia (NH3)Nitrite (NO2)Nitrate (NO3)pHChange %Fins, color, appetite, behavior
Why bother logging You cannot weigh a betta at home, so your test results and a written description of the fish, with the top-down body shape, are the only trend data you will ever have. A nitrate reading creeping up week on week tells you the water change is too small before the fish does. Sixteen rows is four months, long enough to see a real trend rather than a bad day.

What not to do Do not use a mirror as a daily routine: it provokes a threat display and a measurable stress response. Do not use stiff plastic plants. Do not add so much decor that the fish cannot reach the surface, because air access outranks everything else on this page.
