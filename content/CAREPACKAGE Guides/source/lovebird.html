Cost overview

Initial setup

$190 to $360 for equipment, plus $25 to $50 for the first vet exam. The bird itself is $50 to $150 for a common peach-faced or Fischer's, $200 to $400 or more for a rare color mutation, and $20 to $100 to adopt. Full breakdown on page 28.

Monthly ongoing

$25 to $50 for one bird, or $45 to $90 for a pair: pellets, fresh produce, replacement toys and vet care averaged over the year.

Budget for the worst case too A routine avian checkup runs $25 to $50. An emergency does not: egg binding, a broken blood feather, or a night fright injury can run into the hundreds or thousands. Egg binding is the single likeliest emergency in this species and it is a same-day problem, so an emergency fund matters more here than the price of any item on the shopping list.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Agapornis is Greek for "love bird," after the bonded pairs that preen each other and sit pressed together for hours. The name is about pair bonding, not about being gentle with people.
  • A hen lays eggs with no male anywhere in the house. It is not a sign of anything going right, and page 17 is about keeping it from becoming a habit.
  • Lovebirds shell their own seed, so unlike some cage birds they need no grit at all. Offering it risks a crop or gizzard obstruction for no benefit.

Cage size, bar spacing & placement

18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) is the working minimum for a single lovebird, and 24×18×24 in (61×46×61 cm) for a bonded pair. Length matters more than height here: lovebirds fly side to side rather than climbing, so a wide flight-style cage beats a tall narrow one of the same volume. Note that VCA's lovebird profile sets a considerably larger floor, 3 ft long by 2 ft wide by 2 ft tall (90×60×60 cm). Treat the smaller figure as the least you can responsibly buy and the larger one as the target, and read every number on this page as a floor rather than a goal.

Bar spacing runs 3/8 to 5/8 in (10 to 16 mm), with 1/2 in (13 mm) the ideal middle. This one is a safety figure: wider gaps let a lovebird push its head through and trap it, which can be fatal. Buy powder-coated or stainless steel only. Galvanized wire, homemade cages and cages with wooden components all carry a real zinc or lead exposure risk, and this is a species that chews everything it can reach. Skip round cages, which give a bird no corner to settle into.

Never Buy a cage in the $30 to $50 bracket. Cheap cages rust, and a rusting or galvanized cage is a genuine zinc poisoning risk in a bird that chews its bars daily. Signs of heavy metal poisoning include appetite loss, regurgitation, weakness and excessive thirst and urination, and it is diagnosed on blood levels, not by looking at the bird.

Placement

  • Against a wall at roughly eye level, so the bird can see the room without feeling exposed from behind.
  • Out of direct sun, out of drafts, and out of the kitchen. The kitchen rule is not about mess, it is about cooking fumes; see page 8.
  • In a room the family actually uses. A lovebird kept in a spare bedroom loses most of the social contact this species needs.

Perches, dishes & what to leave out

Perches

Natural wood branches of varied diameter, roughly 1/2 to 3/4 in (13 to 19 mm), beat uniform dowels. A bird gripping the same diameter all day loads the same points on its feet every hour, which is how pressure sores start. Vary the material too: one rope or cement conditioning perch alongside the wood is plenty. Site perches so nothing sits directly above a food or water dish, and keep the highest perch far enough below the ceiling of the cage that the bird's tail is not permanently bent against it.

Dishes and liner

Three dishes, for pellets, fresh food and water, changed daily. Exterior-mounted dishes are worth the extra few dollars: with a cage-territorial lovebird you can feed without putting your hand inside the cage, which is the commonest way owners get bitten (page 14). A plain paper liner, changed regularly, is the simplest hygienic floor. Avoid loose bedding that holds moisture and grows mold. This species needs no grit at all.

Furnishings checklist

Three or four perches, varied diameter and material
Exterior-mount food, fresh-food and water dishes
Shreddable items: untreated paper, cardboard tubes, palm leaf
A foraging toy that hides part of the daily ration
Soft wood chew items, replaced as they disappear
A plain, breathable cage cover for nighttime
Leave these three out, deliberately A nest box, a happy hut or any enclosed tent-shaped hide, and a mirror. All three are documented triggers for hormonal, territorial behavior and chronic egg laying in this species, in a hen with no mate present. This is not a style preference, it is the single most avoidable cause of the reproductive problems on page 17. If you want the bird covered at night, use a plain breathable cover over the outside of the cage.

Toy safety

Lovebirds are unusually determined chewers for their size, which rules out several things that are fine for a calmer bird. Avoid small detachable parts, loose threads or rope fibers that can wrap a toe, open metal clips a beak can pry apart, and anything galvanized. Lead turns up in curtain weights, blind pulls, costume jewelry and cheap mirror backings; zinc in galvanized chain, bells, keys and mesh. Both are common enough in bird toys that buying from a bird-specific maker rather than a general pet aisle is worth doing.

Light, sleep & household climate

Target°F°C
Room temperature65 to 80°F18 to 27°C
Tolerable short-term range60 to 85°F16 to 29°C
HumidityNormal household humidity. No misting system or humidifier needed

No basking lamp, no heat mat, no thermostat. A lovebird is comfortable wherever a lightly dressed person is comfortable. What it cannot tolerate is a draft or a sudden swing, so keep the cage off the floor, away from exterior doors, and out of the path of an air conditioning vent.

Sleep is a husbandry requirement, not a preference

A lovebird needs 10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness a night, every night. Sleep deprivation in parrots is not a minor comfort issue: it is one of the recognized behavioral triggers for feather plucking (page 22), and a household that keeps the living room lit until midnight is effectively running the bird on a long summer photoperiod year-round, which is exactly the signal that starts a hen laying. Moving the cage to a quiet, dark room at a fixed bedtime is the reliable fix. A cover on the everyday cage helps some birds, but UC Davis's avian behavior guidance is that covering may not give a bird optimal rest on its own, because a cover stops the light and not the television or the people walking through. Consistency matters more than the exact hour.

The photoperiod lever Daylight length is the strongest single control you have over hormonal behavior. Routine care is 10 to 12 hours of darkness, and Merck names an extended photoperiod of more than 12 hours of light as a documented risk factor for excessive laying, which is the line a lit living room crosses most evenings. For a hen who has already started laying repeatedly, VCA's guidance is to cut her exposure to daylight or room light to 8 hours a day until the cycle breaks, and LafeberVet gives the same intervention as a slightly wider 8 to 10 hours. See page 17 for the full protocol.

Light quality and vitamin D3

Window glass filters out the UVB wavelengths a bird uses to make vitamin D3, which is what lets it absorb calcium. A bird sitting in a sunny window is getting warmth and daylight but not that. An avian-specific full-spectrum lamp above the cage, run on the same timer as the room lights, covers the gap for an indoor bird, and safe supervised outdoor time in a secure cage does the same thing better. This matters most for laying hens, where calcium demand spikes.

Household hazards & bird-proofing

A bird's respiratory system is efficient enough that fumes harmless to you are lethal to it. This page is the one most likely to save the bird's life.

PTFE fumes kill birds with no warning Overheated nonstick cookware releases polytetrafluoroethylene gas at around 536°F (280°C), a temperature an empty preheating pan reaches in minutes. The gas is colorless and odorless, and the Merck Veterinary Manual is blunt that acute death is often the only sign. Merck also names irons and ironing board covers, some self-cleaning ovens, heating elements in some reverse-cycle heat pumps, and some heat lamps. Keep birds out of the kitchen entirely, and if a pan scorches, get the bird out of the house and open every window before anything else.

The rest of the airborne list

The same physiology applies to everything else you spray or burn. Aerosols, air fresheners and plug-ins, scented candles, incense, oven cleaner, bleach and ammonia fumes, hairspray, nail polish and remover, and fresh paint or varnish all warrant getting the bird into another room with the door shut and the windows open. Tobacco smoke is a documented cause of sneezing, discharge, eye inflammation and feather-destructive behavior in birds, and it lingers on hands: anyone who smokes should wash before handling. Keep a working carbon monoxide detector in any room with gas heat or a gas range: CO is odorless to you, and the bird has no margin.

Bird-proofing a room for out-of-cage time

Windows and mirrors covered or blinds drawn, so a flying bird does not hit glass
Ceiling fans off at the wall, not just the pull chain
Toilet lids down, pots covered, sinks and any standing water emptied
Doors shut, and a rule that nobody opens an exterior door while the bird is out
Cables tucked away, and any lead curtain weights or blind pulls removed. Lead tastes sweet, so a bird that finds it keeps chewing
Cats and dogs out of the room. Any cat contact is a same-day vet visit: Pasteurella can turn septic within hours

Toxic plants and foods in reach

Avocado is the one that matters most: every part of the plant carries persin, smaller birds are the more sensitive, and respiratory distress typically appears about 12 hours after ingestion with death possible inside one to two days. Never offer it, and never leave it within reach. Onion and garlic damage red blood cells. Chocolate, caffeine and alcohol are all off the list, as is anything containing xylitol. Common houseplants worth moving out of the flight room include philodendron, dieffenbachia, pothos, oleander, lily and sago palm. The full never-feed list is on page 12.

Night frights

Lovebirds thrash in the dark when startled by a headlight, a noise, or a shadow, and a panicking bird in a dark cage can break a blood feather or damage its cere or wings against the bars. A dim night light near the cage lets a startled bird orient instead of thrashing blindly, and it is worth adding for any bird that has done this once.

One bird or two

The decision that shapes everything else, and the one hardest to reverse.

Parrots are flock animals and need a social bond with something. A lovebird will form that bond with a person or with another lovebird, and whichever it forms first is largely the one you keep. This is not a preference to settle later. Make it before you buy.

ChoiceWhat you actually getWhat it costs you
One hand-raised birdBonds to its person. Genuinely tame and interactive: steps up, rides on a shoulder, seeks contact and vocalizes back at youRoughly 2 hours of direct daily interaction, every day, for 10 to 15 years. Not a bonding phase you graduate out of. Hand-rearing carries a documented behavioral cost too: Schmid, Doherr and Steiger (2006) found hand-reared parrots imprint on people and become socially dependent on them, with more aggression, feather picking and stereotyped behavior than parent-reared birds
A bonded pairBond to each other. Fascinating to watch, constantly busy, and largely uninterested in you. Closer to keeping a pair of finches than a tame parrotA bigger cage, double the food and toys, and accepting that the birds are rarely reliably handleable
Two birds, not bondedThe risky middle. Two lovebirds of the wrong pairing can fight seriously, and lovebirds are territorial enough to injure each otherA slow supervised introduction, and a second cage kept ready in case it does not take
Decide before you buy, because pairing is one-way Once two lovebirds bond to each other, you cannot undo it to get an interactive pet back. And separating an established pair is not a clean break: bonded birds can visibly grieve a separation for 4 to 8 weeks. If your schedule genuinely cannot deliver daily hours, a pair is the kinder choice, as long as you go in wanting birds to watch rather than a bird to handle.

If you choose a single bird

Two hours a day is the working figure, and it does not have to be two hours sitting still. Out-of-cage time in a bird-proofed room, a short training session, food prep with the bird on a stand nearby, and talking to it across the room all count. What does not count is the bird being in the same house as you while you ignore it. A single lovebird that stops getting that contact is the classic candidate for screaming and feather plucking.

If you choose a pair

Buy a bonded pair from one source rather than assembling one yourself, and size the cage to the pair minimum from the start rather than adding a second bird to a single-bird cage. Both birds still need their own toys and their own foraging: a cage mate solves loneliness, not boredom. Have a second cage available before you introduce any two birds, and never mix lovebirds with other parrot species in the same cage.

A pair is not a way to avoid hormones A male and female housed together will breed if given anywhere nest-like to do it, and chronic laying is harder on a hen than any other routine part of her life. A same-sex pair or a pair with no nest site at all is the low-drama version. Everything on page 17 applies to a paired hen more, not less.

Diet: pellets, seeds & converting a seed eater

A formulated pellet should make up roughly 75 to 80% of what a lovebird eats, with fresh vegetables and a little fruit making up the remaining 20 to 40%. Seed is the part everyone gets backwards. It is a treat and an enrichment vehicle, never the base of the diet. An all-seed bowl is low in vitamin A and calcium and high in fat, and it is the single commonest cause of illness in pet birds: fatty liver, poor feather quality, chronic sinus and eye problems, and, in a hen, the calcium shortfall that turns routine laying into egg binding.

GritNoneLovebirds shell their own seed. Grit is unnecessary and carries an obstruction risk

Converting a seed-addicted bird

Lovebirds are stubborn converters, and a bird that has never seen a pellet may not recognize it as food at all. Do not starve a bird onto pellets: a small bird has almost no fat reserve and can go downhill in a day. VCA gives two workable schedules. Weigh the bird before you start and every day through the change, before the first feed. Lafeber's limits are the ones to hold to: never let a small bird go more than 12 hours without eating, and stop and call an avian vet at a 10% drop from the starting weight, or if the droppings go minimal or black.

ScheduleHow it runs
Gradual, 3-day steps75% seed and 25% pellets for 3 days, then 50/50 for 3 days, then 25% seed and 75% pellets, until the bird is converted
Faster, 10-day taperStart at 90% seed and 10% pellets on day one, then reduce the seed share by 10% a day until the bird is on pellets alone by day 10
If she stallsVCA's own escape valve: hold at 20% seed and 80% pellets until the bird converts rather than forcing the last step. Expect days, weeks or months

Tricks that help: eat the pellets yourself in front of the bird, offer them warm and slightly damp, sprinkle a few over a favorite vegetable, and put them in the foraging toy while the seed sits in the plain dish. Confirm the bird is actually eating them, not just cracking and dropping them, by checking the tray.

Supplements are for seed-fed birds, not pellet-fed ones A bird eating 75 to 80% pellets does not need added vitamins or minerals, and over-supplementing is its own problem. Give supplements only on an avian vet's instruction. A cuttlebone or mineral block is the usual exception and is worth leaving in the cage for a hen.

Never feed avocado, onion, garlic, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, or anything containing xylitol. The full never-feed list is on page 12.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • A happy hut, nest box or mirror in the cage. The most avoidable cause of hormonal aggression and chronic egg laying in this species, and it comes packaged as a kindness.
  • Seeds as the base of the diet. Cheap, well liked, and the underlying cause of a long list of conditions in section 03.
  • Buying two birds while expecting a tame one. A bonded pair bonds to each other. There is no version of this where you get both.
  • Buying one bird without the daily time. Two hours a day for 10 to 15 years is the deal. A single bird that does not get it screams, plucks, or both.
  • The cage in the kitchen. Convenient, sociable, and the most likely place in the house for the bird to die suddenly of PTFE fumes (page 8).
  • Bar spacing chosen by eye. Anything wider than 5/8 in (16 mm) can trap a lovebird's head.
  • Lights on until midnight. Under 10 hours of darkness is both a plucking trigger and a laying trigger.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after correct cage size, diet, sleep and social contact, never instead of them. Wild parrots spend an estimated 60 to 80% of their waking hours foraging, and a bowl of food deletes almost all of that at a stroke. In this order:

1. Foraging

Hide part of the daily ration rather than serving all of it. A foraging box, pellets wrapped in a paper cup, or food buried under shredded paper. Parrots will often work for food while a free bowl sits nearby, which tells you the work is the point.

2. Shredding

Untreated paper, cardboard tubes, palm leaf mats and balsa. Lovebirds are enthusiastic shredders and soft wood disappears fast, so keep a cheap rotating supply rather than one expensive toy.

3. Chewing

Harder bird-safe wood for the beak to actually work at. This is also what keeps a beak in condition without a trim.

4. Out-of-cage time

One to two hours daily in a bird-proofed room, supervised. Flight is exercise a cage cannot provide at this size.

5. Training

Short positive-reinforcement sessions, a few minutes at a time. Step up, target training, recall. Cognitively demanding in a way toys are not, and it builds the handling relationship at the same time.

6. Rotation

Three to five toys in the cage at once, swapped weekly. More than that crowds out flight space. A toy that has been away for a few weeks reads as new again, which stretches a small collection a long way.

Handling, taming & the bite problem

The name oversells the temperament. Veterinary sources are direct that lovebirds can be very territorial and aggressive when frightened or threatened, especially hens and especially during hormonal periods, and a lovebird will readily chase a bird several times its size, or the family dog, out of what it considers its space. None of that makes it a bad pet. It makes it a bird that has to be handled on its own terms.

Reading the bird before you reach

Almost every bite is preceded by a warning that the person missed. Learn these four and you will get bitten a fraction as often: eye pinning, where the pupils rapidly shrink and widen; feathers held tight to the body with a forward lean; a raised foot or an open beak held toward your hand; and lunging or a sharp click. A relaxed bird is loosely fluffed, grinding its beak, or standing on one leg. That bird will step up. The other one will not, and pushing it teaches it that warnings do not work, which is how you get a bird that bites with no warning at all.

Taming, in order

  • Sit beside the cage and talk, doing nothing else, until the bird stops moving away from you.
  • Offer a piece of millet through the bars, then from an open palm inside the cage, then from your fingers.
  • Teach step up onto a stick first, outside the cage, in a neutral room. Stick training is safer and faster than reaching in with a hand, and it gives you a way to move an angry bird without a fight.
  • Move to a finger for step up once the stick is reliable, always in the neutral room before ever trying it in the cage.
  • Keep sessions short, several times a day, and end while it is going well rather than pushing for one more repetition.
Cage aggression has a mechanical fix Most cage aggression is territorial, so the fastest wins are structural rather than behavioral: remove any nest-like object, fit exterior food dishes so you never reach in to feed, and take the bird to a neutral room before asking anything of it. Aggression very often eases the moment the bird is away from the cage it is defending.

If you are bitten

Do not shout, jerk your hand away, or flick the bird. A dramatic reaction is interesting, and an interesting result reinforces the behavior. Put the bird down calmly, end the session without ceremony, and work out what warning you walked past. Wash any broken skin properly: a bird bite is a puncture, and it is worth a doctor's opinion if it swells.

Where lovebirds sit against other small parrots Beakier and more territorial for their size than a budgie or a cockatiel, more determined chewers, and never to be housed with other species. A lovebird that is well socialized and handled from young is no harder than any other small parrot. One that is not can be a genuinely difficult bird, and hormonal seasons make any of them worse for a few weeks at a time.

Wing clipping & flight

A decision nearly every new owner is asked to make in the first week, usually by someone selling the trim.

Clipping means trimming some of the primary flight feathers so the bird cannot gain height, until it molts those feathers out and grows them back. It is not permanent and it is not surgery. It is also not the routine default it once was: professional and owner opinion has shifted considerably toward keeping companion birds flighted, on the grounds that flight is exercise, coordination and confidence, not just transport.

The case for clipping

  • A bird that cannot gain height cannot fly out of a door or window that someone opens without thinking.
  • It cannot reach a ceiling fan, a hot stove, or a window it does not know is there.
  • Handling and step-up training are easier early on, because flying away is not an option.

The case against

  • Flight is the bird's main exercise. A clipped bird builds less muscle and less coordination.
  • An over-clipped bird falls rather than glides, and falls cause keel injuries and split ceres.
  • Clipping does not fix a behavior problem. A bird that bites clipped will bite flighted.
If it is done, it is done right or not at all Only mature feathers are ever cut. Merck's method is four to seven of the outermost primary feathers on both wings, cut below the coverts that overlay them, leaving the secondaries closer to the body alone, because those are what let a bird slow down and land. A heavier bird needs fewer taken and a lighter, more agile one more, and clipping only one wing is not recommended. A blood feather is a growing feather with a live blood supply in the shaft, and cutting, breaking or pulling one causes real bleeding that a small bird cannot afford. A correct trim leaves the bird able to glide down to the floor under control, not drop. A trim that leaves it dropping is too short, and it has to grow out. Have an avian vet do the first one and watch how it is done. A pet shop trim done in two minutes is where most bad clips come from.

If you keep the bird flighted

The safety burden moves to the room instead of the bird, which is the trade. Train a recall cue with a food reward so you can call the bird back rather than chase it, and run this checklist before every session.

The full bird-proofing checklist is on page 8. Run it before every session.

Either way, do these two things

  • Microchip or leg band details written down, and a clear photograph of your bird, kept where you can find it in the first ten minutes of a bird getting out. A clipped bird still gets carried by a gust of wind.
  • Check the wings at every weekly weigh-in. Clipped feathers grow back unevenly, and a bird with three regrown primaries on one side is more likely to crash than one that is fully clipped or fully flighted.
A broken blood feather is a first-aid situation It bleeds more than the size suggests. Apply firm, steady pressure with clean gauze, and put styptic powder or cornstarch on the exposed broken tip of the shaft, never down into an open follicle, which can damage the follicle permanently. If it has not stopped within two to three minutes, call an avian vet. Do not pull the feather: VCA's current guidance is that pulling one at home is not recommended, because it leaves the shaft open and can cost more blood than it saves, and even in a clinic it is a last resort.

Sexing, weight & body condition

Telling male from female

You mostly cannot, by eye. In the commonly kept species there are no reliable external differences between the sexes; hens typically weigh a little more than cocks, which is a tendency rather than a test. Blood or feather DNA testing is how it is actually determined, it is inexpensive, and an avian vet can run it at the first wellness exam. Behavioral hints exist and are worth noticing, but none of them are conclusive, and a bird laying an egg is the only free answer that is.

Why it is worth knowing Sex changes what you watch for. A hen needs the hormone and egg-laying management on page 17, the calcium note on page 10, and a lower threshold for calling a vet about straining or lethargy. If you keep two birds and one is a hen, knowing whether the other is a cock decides whether you are managing a bonded pair or a breeding pair.

Weight is this species' vital sign

A lovebird hides illness until it is genuinely sick, and weight is the measurement that moves first. Hens, chronic egg laying & egg binding

Lovebirds are one of the species most over-represented for both. This page is prevention first, because treatment is worse than prevention every time.

Why a lone hen lays at all

A hen does not need a male, or even another bird, to lay. Laying is triggered by a combination of long day length, abundant food, a nest-like place to sit, and a bond to something she treats as a mate, which can be her owner. Budgerigars, cockatiels and lovebirds are the three species named most often for chronic laying, and in small birds there is likely a genetic component too. Chronic laying drains calcium faster than a hen can replace it, which is what turns a normal clutch into a soft-shelled egg, a fracture, or a bird that cannot pass the next one.

Turning the triggers off

Repeated clutches on a calcium-poor diet drain a hen faster than she can replace them. Signs run from weakness and tremors to seizures and to fractures that happen with no obvious injury. The likeliest emergency this species will ever present you with, and the one where hours genuinely decide the outcome.

An egg that cannot pass presses on the kidneys, on the nerves running to the legs, and on the airway, because a bird has no diaphragm to protect its lungs from abdominal pressure. It kills quickly and it does not resolve on its own. Lovebirds are named alongside cockatiels and budgerigars as the species this happens to most.

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact an avian vet promptly if you see Sitting fluffed with eyes closing during the day · tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, wheezing or a tail-bob at rest · a hen straining, sitting on the cage floor, or standing wide-legged · discharge from the nostrils or eyes · feather or beak deformity · plucking down to bare skin · a drop in appetite or droppings · a fall, a bleeding feather, or any contact with a cat or dog.

Why "wait and see" does not apply to birds

A prey species that shows weakness in the wild is the one that gets taken, so a lovebird conceals illness until concealment stops working. PBFD & polyomavirus

Two viruses with no treatment, both of which you manage by keeping them out of the house.

Psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD)

Cause: psittacine circovirus, documented in lovebirds among many parrot species. Signs: the classic form starts with loss of the powder down that normally dusts the beak, then abnormal feather development, clubbed or hemorrhaging feathers that shed easily and regrow poorly, and beak deformity. A peracute form in young birds shows as weight loss, enteritis and pneumonia rather than feather change. Transmission: inhalation or ingestion of the virus, possibly also from parent to chick, and the virus is very stable in the environment, so cages, toys and clothing carry it. Diagnosis: PCR on blood, feces or feather dander; PCR also detects birds carrying it without signs. Reality check: there is no cure and no vaccine, and clinically affected birds are generally isolated with a poor outlook.

Avian polyomavirus

Cause: avian polyomavirus, most dangerous to young birds; infection is often fatal in psittacines under four months old. Signs: in nestlings, sudden lethargy, a crop that stops emptying, and death within 24 to 48 hours; survivors and older birds may show feather dystrophy. Diagnosis: DNA testing on a cloacal swab and blood. Prevention: a vaccine exists, with the first dose given after 35 days of age and a booster 2 to 3 weeks later. Discuss it with an avian vet if you are buying young birds or keeping several. Adults typically resist infection but can shed the virus for up to 90 days before clearing it, which is exactly why a healthy-looking new bird is still a risk to a resident one.

Quarantine every new bird. This is the whole prevention strategy. A minimum of 30 days apart, which is the public-health floor. Merck's figure for adding a bird to an aviary or an established multi-bird house is 90 days of quarantine with testing, because an infected adult can shed polyomavirus intermittently for up to 90 days before clearing it. A separate room, ideally not sharing an air vent, with separate dishes and separate cleaning gear. Care for your established birds first and the new one last, and wash your hands and change your shirt in between. Book an avian vet exam in the first week and ask specifically about PCR screening for PBFD, polyomavirus and psittacosis. This is the one procedure that keeps an incurable virus out of a household, and it is skipped constantly.

Where exposure happens

  • Bringing home a second bird without quarantine, which is the commonest route by a wide margin.
  • Bird fairs, swaps, and shops where birds from many sources share air and surfaces.
  • Secondhand cages and toys. A used cage is worth having, but strip it, scrub it, and disinfect it before a bird goes in.
  • Your own hands and clothes after handling someone else's bird.
A deformed feather is not automatically PBFD Damaged feathers also come from plucking, a bad molt, malnutrition, mites, and physical trauma to a follicle. The difference is not something to judge by eye, and guessing wrong in either direction is expensive. PCR testing is how it is settled, and it is worth doing rather than watching.

Respiratory disease & psittacosis

The category that moves fastest, in the animal with the least reserve.

Recognizing it early

Watch for repeated sneezing, wet or crusted nostrils, a stained or discolored cere, discharge around the eyes, a change or loss of voice, fluffed posture, and a tail that bobs with each breath. Open-mouth breathing and audible wheezing are late signs in a small bird and mean an emergency, not an appointment next week. Causes range from bacterial and fungal infection to inhaled irritants and vitamin A deficiency, which thins the lining of the respiratory tract and makes recurrent sinus infections likely in a seed-fed bird.

Rule out the air before the bird Before assuming infection, account for what the bird has been breathing: an overheated nonstick pan, an aerosol or air freshener, a scented candle, new paint, cigarette smoke, or a dusty room. Fume exposure and infection look similar from across the room and are treated completely differently. Page 8 has the full list.

Psittacosis, and why it matters to you as well as the bird

Cause: the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. How it spreads: shed in feces, urine and respiratory secretions, and aerosolized into fine dust when birds beat their wings, so it is inhaled rather than needing contact. Signs in the bird: respiratory distress, nasal or eye discharge, appetite loss, bright green droppings, and regurgitation. Some birds carry it with no signs at all, and lovebirds specifically are among the species that may show nothing before sudden death. Diagnosis: PCR is the preferred test, sometimes alongside bloodwork and radiographs. Treatment: doxycycline for 45 days, because the organism hides inside white blood cells and a short course does not clear it, followed by retesting to confirm.

This one is zoonotic People catch psittacosis from birds. Symptoms appear roughly 5 to 14 days after exposure, occasionally up to a month, and start as headache, muscle aches, high fever especially at night, and flu-like respiratory illness. If anyone in the house develops that after a bird has been unwell, tell your own doctor about the bird. It is treatable in people and it is missed when nobody mentions the exposure. Ventilate when cleaning the cage, dampen droppings rather than sweeping dust into the air, and wash your hands afterward.

Supportive care while you get to the vet

  • Warmth, around 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C), in a quiet draft-free room. Feather plucking & behavioral health

Plucking is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and it has both medical and behavioral causes that look identical from across the room. The order matters: rule out the medical causes first, then fix the environment. Guessing behavioral and being wrong means an infection or a deficiency runs untreated for months while you rotate toys.

Medical causes to rule out

  • Vitamin A deficiency, which lists feather picking among its signs
  • Bacterial or fungal skin and follicle infection
  • Mites and other external parasites
  • Internal disease, including liver disease from a high-fat diet
  • Pain from an old injury or an irritated follicle
  • Airborne irritants, including tobacco smoke

Behavioral causes to fix

  • Boredom, and a cage with nothing to destroy
  • Too little sleep, under 10 hours of darkness
  • A cage too small to move properly in
  • Sexual frustration and hormonal triggers (page 17)
  • Loneliness in a single bird not getting daily contact
  • A household change: a move, a new pet, a schedule change
Start with the four basics before anything complex Cage size (page 5), sleep at 10 to 12 hours of darkness (page 7), a pellet-forward diet (page 10), and daily interaction (page 9). A surprising share of plucking cases trace back to one of those four, and all four are free to fix. Add foraging before you add toys: making the bird work for its food is the enrichment with the most evidence behind it.

The other boredom behaviors

Plucking is one of three signals that the same problem produces. The other two are repetitive pacing, walking the same stretch of perch or cage front over and over, and screaming that is new, louder, or goes well past the normal dawn and dusk contact calling. Treat all three the same way: a vet check to exclude a medical cause, then a hard look at sleep, foraging, cage size and daily contact. Screaming in particular is often accidentally trained, because running into the room is exactly the response the bird wanted. Reward the quiet, answer a normal contact call, and ignore the escalation.

What not to do

  • Do not fit a collar or a "no-pluck" spray as a first move. They treat the visible behavior and leave the cause running.
  • Do not punish it. Plucking is a stress behavior and adding stress makes it worse.
  • Do not assume feathers will grow back. Repeatedly damaged follicles can stop producing, so early intervention matters more than a perfect diagnosis.

Nutritional disease, mites & injuries

Less dramatic than the last three pages, and far more likely to be what you actually deal with.

Vitamin A deficiency

The quiet one behind a long list of other problems. All-seed diets, and even half-seed half-pellet diets that are not portion controlled, are deficient in vitamin A. It shows as nasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eyes, conjunctivitis, labored breathing, increased thirst and urination, poor feather quality, feather picking, and appetite loss, and white plaques can appear around the mouth and eyes. Chronic sinus infections in a seed-fed bird usually mean this is underneath them. The fix is the diet on page 10, with the orange and dark green vegetables on page 11 doing most of the work. Injectable vitamin A is a veterinary treatment, not a home one.

Obesity and fatty liver disease

Seed, nuts and table food, plus a bird that never flies, deposit fat in the liver. Signs are subtle at first: poor feather condition, a rounded chest, exercise intolerance, and lameness or sore feet from carrying the weight. It is diagnosed on examination and bloodwork, and it is one of the reasons an annual wellness panel is worth the money in a seed-fed bird. Correct it gradually through diet and flight, never by sharply cutting food, which can tip a bird into a worse metabolic problem.

Scaly face and leg mites

Knemidocoptes mites burrow into the skin of the cere, beak corners, eyelids, legs and vent, producing a white, crusty, honeycombed encrustation. It is common in budgerigars and much less common in other parrots, so in a lovebird it is worth testing rather than assuming, but it does occur and lovebirds are named among the species affected. It is treated with a prescription antiparasitic and, because mites hide in porous surfaces, by replacing wooden perches and toys and disinfecting the cage at the same time. Untreated, it deforms the beak permanently.

Injuries and the overgrown beak

The common ones are a broken blood feather (page 15), a night fright collision, a fall from an over-short wing clip, a toe caught in a loose fiber or an open clip, and a cat or dog bite, which is always a same-day visit. Bleeding from a nail or the beak tip is handled with cornstarch and pressure; bleeding from anywhere else is a vet call. An overgrown or flaking beak is not normal wear in a bird with things to chew, and it can point to liver disease, mites, or malnutrition, so it warrants an exam rather than a trim.

A cat or dog bite is an emergency even when the bird looks fine Bacteria carried in cat saliva can kill a small bird within a day or two of a puncture that barely breaks skin. Any contact with a cat's mouth or claws is a same-day avian vet visit for antibiotics, whether or not you can see a wound.

Molt, hormones & seasonal behavior

Molting

Feathers wear out and get replaced, and in a healthy bird this happens gradually rather than all at once. Merck's picture is a full molt at least once a year, with many birds also running a smaller partial molt about six months after the main one. The tells that a molt is normal are that it is symmetrical, roughly matching on both sides of the body, and that new feathers are already pushing through before the old ones drop, so bare skin never shows. New feathers arrive as pin feathers, each in a keratin sheath the bird flakes off by preening. Head pins are the ones the bird cannot reach itself, which is why a bonded bird gets them preened by its partner and a single bird may appreciate very gentle help from you.

Molting is metabolically expensive Growing a full set of feathers is hard work, and a molting bird is often quieter, sleeps more, and can be touchier about being handled. Keep the diet up, offer baths two or three times a week to help the sheaths come away, and do not read the extra grumpiness as a training problem.

Bald patches, feathers missing only where the bird can reach, broken shafts, or bleeding pin feathers are not molting. Go to page 22 and book a vet exam.

Bathing and helping a molting bird

Offer a shallow dish of clean water two or three times a week, or a light misting for a bird that dislikes dishes. Bathing keeps feathers supple and helps a molting bird work the keratin sheaths off its new pin feathers. Never use soap, and let the bird dry in a draft-free room rather than under a hair dryer, which can carry PTFE-coated heating elements (page 8).

Hormonal seasons

Lengthening days, plentiful food and a secure territory push a lovebird into breeding condition, and the behavior that comes with it is genuine and temporary. Expect more territorial defense of the cage, more biting, regurgitating onto a favorite person or toy, shredding and carrying paper, sitting low on the cage floor, and in a hen, tail lifting when her back is stroked. This is the season to be least ambitious about handling and most disciplined about the triggers on page 17. Cut the day length, take out the nest-like objects, stop stroking anywhere but the head and neck, and wait it out.

Normal noise, and when it is not

Lovebirds are loud for their size and are especially vocal at dawn and dusk. That contact calling is normal flock behavior and cannot be trained away, only accommodated. What is worth investigating is a change: a bird that suddenly screams through the whole day, or one that has gone quiet and still. Both are worth attention, and the quiet one is the more urgent of the two.

Quick differentiator Molt: feather loss even on both sides, pins visible, skin covered, bird otherwise normal. Plucking: loss only where the beak reaches, head untouched, broken shafts. PBFD: deformed or clubbed new feathers, feathers that shed easily and regrow badly, beak changes, and no powder down. The first needs nothing, the second needs a vet and an environment audit, the third needs a PCR test.

  • Fresh water in a clean dish daily, changed more often if the bird bathes or drops food in it. Wash the dish, do not just top it up: a biofilm builds fast in a warm room.
  • Fresh vegetables contribute real water, which is part of why a bird eating well produces slightly wetter droppings than one on dry food alone.
  • Signs a bird is dehydrated: dull, sunken-looking eyes, skin over the keel or toes that stays tented when lifted, thick sticky saliva, and dark, scant urates with almost no clear rim.
  • A dehydrated bird is a vet visit, not a home rehydration project. Small birds need fluids given by a vet, and a bird too weak to drink will inhale water from a syringe.

Power outages, travel & transport

A lovebird needs no heat lamp, so an outage is mostly a question of room temperature and of what people start burning for light and warmth.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Move the cage to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Cover three sides and the top with blankets, leaving one side open for air. Trapped body heat does most of the work at this size.
  • Keep feeding normally. A 50 g bird has almost no reserve and skipping meals is a bigger risk than being cool.
  • Keep the day and night cycle roughly normal with a battery lantern rather than leaving the bird in the dark all day.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, kerosene, gas stoves, or any open flame near the bird. Smoke and combustion fumes are the real danger of an outage for a bird, not the temperature.
  • A generator runs outside and at least 20 feet from any door, window or vent, which is the CDC's figure. The CPSC's wording rules out running one in or near an enclosed space, so an open garage door does not qualify.
  • Don't use a camping heater, a self-cleaning oven cycle, or a hair dryer to warm the room. Several carry PTFE-coated elements.
Outage kit Battery lantern, blankets, a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel and set near the carrier rather than against it, a week of pellets in a sealed tub, bottled water, a working carbon monoxide detector, and the carrier itself kept where you can reach it in the dark. Skip chemical hand warmers: some vent fumes, and nothing that off-gasses belongs beside a bird.

Leaving town

Trip lengthSingle birdBonded pair

A single hand-raised lovebird is bonded to you, so a long absence is a social problem as much as a husbandry one. Expect a period of loud contact calling when you get back, and some birds pluck after a long separation.

Transport and vet trips

  • A small hard-sided carrier with a low perch, or a folded towel on the floor for a sick bird, is safer than the cage. Cover it: a covered bird travels calmly.
  • Belt the carrier in, keep the car quiet, and never run the air conditioning or heat straight onto it.
  • Take the morning's cage liner to the appointment. It is the sample the vet actually wants.
  • Never leave the bird in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly. A car interior kills a small bird faster than it would a dog.
  • Clip a spare wing check and a photograph of the bird into the carrier lid, in case it gets out at the clinic door.

Glossary

AgapornisThe genus of the nine lovebird species, from the Greek for "love bird," after the pair bond.
Blood featherA growing feather with a live blood supply in the shaft. Cutting, breaking or pulling one causes real bleeding.
Bonded pairTwo lovebirds paired to each other, often for life. A bonded pair bonds to each other rather than to a keeper.
CereThe fleshy area above the beak containing the nostrils. Staining or crusting there is a respiratory sign.
Chronic egg layingRepeated clutches beyond an occasional egg, driven by hormonal triggers and hard on a hen's calcium reserves.
Contra-freeloadingThe documented tendency of parrots to work for food even when identical food is freely available, which is why foraging toys matter.
Egg bindingAn egg that cannot pass. A life-threatening emergency requiring same-day veterinary care.
Happy hutAn enclosed, tent or tunnel-shaped fabric hide sold for cage birds. A documented hormonal trigger, and not for a pet lovebird.
Hepatic lipidosisFatty liver disease, usually from a high-fat diet and too little exercise.
HypocalcemiaLow blood calcium. In hens it follows repeated laying on a calcium-poor diet, and causes weakness, tremors and fractures.
KeelThe ridge of breastbone down the center of the chest. Feeling it either side is how body condition is judged.
KnemidocoptesThe mite that causes scaly face and leg disease, seen as white crusty encrustation on the cere, beak corners and legs.
MoltThe normal replacement of feathers, symmetrical on both sides, with new feathers emerging before old ones drop.
PBFDPsittacine beak and feather disease, an incurable circovirus affecting feathers, beak and the immune system.
PhotoperiodThe daily light and dark cycle. 10 to 12 hours of darkness is routine; 8 hours of light is the level used to break a laying cycle.
Pin featherA new feather still in its keratin sheath, which the bird flakes off by preening.
PolyomavirusA virus most dangerous to young birds, often fatal under four months. A vaccine exists.
PolyuriaExcess urine: a watery dropping whose solid part is still formed. Different from diarrhea, and usually less serious.

PsittacosisInfection with Chlamydia psittaci, treatable with a 45-day course of doxycycline, and transmissible to people.
PTFEThe nonstick coating that releases a gas lethal to birds when a pan is overheated to about 536°F (280°C).
QuarantineKeeping a new bird separate, ideally on separate air, for 30 days minimum before it meets resident birds.

Where the sources disagree

Six figures where credible references do not match, and what this package prints instead.

  • Cage minimum. Common husbandry guidance gives 18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) for a single bird; VCA's lovebird profile gives a considerably larger 3×2×2 ft (90×60×60 cm). Both are printed on page 5 rather than picking one, because the smaller figure is a floor and the larger is a better target.
  • Diet split. Pellet shares between 60 and 80% appear in circulation. This package uses the 75 to 80% pellets and 20 to 40% fresh figures from VCA's lovebird feeding guidance, which is the species-specific source.
  • Adult weight. This package prints 40 to 60 g, which is the range the site's own lovebird guides carry. LafeberVet's basic information sheet gives 45 to 70 g, mean 50. Both are printed here rather than silently picking one, and the practical answer on page 16 is unchanged: your own bird's stable baseline matters more than either range.
  • Captive life span. VCA and the site's cost guide give 10 to 15 years typical and about 20 at most, which is what pages 4 and 16 print. LafeberVet gives a mean of 15 years with up to 30 reported. The planning consequence does not change: this is a bird you keep for well over a decade.
  • Room temperature. The site's lovebird cage-setup guide gives 65 to 80°F (18 to 27°C), which page 7 prints. LafeberVet gives 70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C) as generally acceptable, adding that healthy birds tolerate more in both directions. The draft and the sudden swing matter more than the number.
