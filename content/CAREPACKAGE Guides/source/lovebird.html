Cost overview

Getting one

$50 to $150 for a common color type; $200 to $400 or more for rare color mutations, with exceptional individuals reaching $1,000. Adoption typically runs $20 to $100.

Monthly ongoing

$70 to $125 for pellets, fresh vegetables, toys, and vet care averaged over the year.

Budget for the unexpected too Egg binding and other emergencies need immediate vet care and cost more than the routine $25 to $50 checkup. You won't need it if you follow this package, but it's real enough to plan a cushion for.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Agapornis, the genus name, comes from Greek for "love bird," a nod to how tightly bonded pairs perch pressed together for hours.
  • Lovebirds are native to Africa and Madagascar, not South America or Australia like many commonly kept parrots.
  • A well-cared-for lovebird can live up to 20 years, a longer commitment than most owners expect from a bird this small.

Section 02 · Full Care Guide

Cage size & bar spacing

18×18×24 inches is the minimum for a single bird. A bonded pair needs at least 24×18×24 inches, real extra footprint, not the same cage with a second bird squeezed in. Bar spacing should run 3/8 to 5/8 inch, with 1/2 inch the ideal middle ground: wide enough that the cage doesn't feel like a barrier, narrow enough that a head or foot can't get caught.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Both dimensions are minimums, not targets. More floor space and more length are always fine as long as bar spacing stays in the 3/8 to 5/8 inch range.
  • Perches at different heights give a bird somewhere to go besides straight across, and keeping them clear of the dishes keeps droppings out of food and water.
  • A pair's extra space is length, not height. A tall, narrow cage doesn't substitute for the added floor and flight room two birds actually need.

Diet & feeding

A lovebird's diet should be pellet-forward with fresh vegetables added daily. Seeds are a smaller supplementary part of the diet, not the base, even though a seed-only bowl is still the default a lot of new owners reach for first.

FoodRole in the dietNotes
PelletsBase of the dietShould make up the majority of daily intake
Fresh vegetablesDaily additionOffered fresh alongside pellets each day
SeedsSmaller supplementNot the base, despite being the traditional default
Never Build the diet around seeds. An all-seed or seed-heavy bowl is the single most common feeding mistake with this species, and the most common way pellet-forward guidance ends up ignored in practice.

Making the switch to pellets

A bird raised on seed doesn't always take to pellets right away. Offer both side by side rather than swapping cold, and give it time. This is a gradual transition, not an overnight one, and it's worth the patience given how much of the diet pellets are supposed to make up long term.

Water & dish hygiene

Fresh water daily, in a dish positioned away from perches so droppings don't end up in it. Vegetables spoil faster than pellets in a warm room, so remove anything uneaten rather than letting it sit for hours.

Handling & social needs

This is the single most important decision in this whole package, and it needs to be made before you bring a bird home, not after. Lovebirds are intensely social, but who they bond to depends entirely on whether you keep one bird or two.

ChoiceWhat to actually expectTime commitment
Single, hand-raised birdCan become genuinely tame and interactive with its keeperRoughly 2 hours of daily interaction to stay well-adjusted
Bonded pairBonds to each other, not to you. Largely ignores the owner, closer to keeping a pair of finches than an interactive petMinimal direct interaction needed; the birds meet their own social needs
Decide before you buy You can't undo pair bonding to get an interactive pet back. Once two lovebirds bond to each other, they stay focused on each other, not on you. If you want a bird that interacts with you, get one hand-raised bird. If you want low-maintenance companionship between two birds that entertain each other, get a bonded pair. Trying to have both from the same setup doesn't work.

Why this happens

Parrots are flock animals and need a social bond with something. A single bird without a same-species companion will bond to its human keeper as a substitute flock, which is what makes it affectionate and interactive, but only if the keeper actually shows up for that role. A bonded pair fulfills that same social need with each other, which is easier on a busy household but means the birds stay essentially standoffish toward people.

If you choose a single bird

Roughly 2 hours of daily interaction is what keeps a single, hand-raised lovebird well-adjusted. That's a real daily commitment, not a one-time bonding period you graduate out of. A single bird left alone for long stretches without that interaction is missing the social contact it would otherwise be getting from a mate.

Section 02 · Full Care Guide

Common mistakes & enrichment

Never Give a pet, non-breeding lovebird a nest box or "happy hut" style covered sleeping tent. These are among the most common and avoidable triggers for hormonal aggression and chronic egg-laying in this species (see page 10). A plain, breathable cage cover for nighttime is fine. Nest-like enclosed furnishings are not.

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Buying two birds expecting an interactive pet, then feeling ignored once they bond to each other instead of the owner.
  • Buying a single bird and underestimating the roughly 2 hours of daily interaction it needs to stay well-adjusted.
  • Sizing the cage for one bird, then adding a second without upsizing to the 24×18×24 in pair minimum.
  • Letting seeds become the base of the diet instead of the smaller supplement they're meant to be.
  • Adding nest boxes, huts, or other enclosed "cozy" furnishings without realizing they encourage hormonal behavior and egg-laying.
  • Choosing bar spacing outside the 3/8 to 5/8 inch range, either a safety risk if too wide or an unnecessary expense if too narrow.

Enrichment, by priority

Chew toys

Wood and other bird-safe chew toys give a natural outlet for a beak that's built to gnaw.

Foraging

Hiding pellets or vegetable pieces instead of only using an open dish engages natural foraging behavior.

Toy rotation

Swapping toys every so often keeps the cage mentally engaging without a full teardown.

Supervised out-of-cage time

Time in a bird-safe room, always supervised, adds activity and variety beyond the cage itself.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a vet promptly if you see Straining, fluffed posture, or tail-bobbing while breathing with no egg produced; repeated clutches beyond an occasional egg; open-mouth breathing, wheezing, or discharge; feather or beak abnormalities; feather plucking down to skin; lethargy or appetite loss.

What to tell the vet

Diet breakdown: pellets vs. seeds vs. vegetables
Single bird or bonded pair, and breeding status
Egg-laying history, if female
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening

Why housing and hormones come up first

Chronic egg-laying, egg binding, and a fair share of feather plucking cases in this species trace back to hormonal triggers, nest-like furnishings especially, more often than anything else. A vet will ask about cage setup and any nest box or hut before anything else for exactly that reason.

Normal vs. concerning

NormalConcerning
An occasional single egg, even without a mate present, that passes normallyRepeated clutches, or straining without producing an egg
Brief fluffed feathers while resting or settling in for the nightPuffed-up posture, lethargy, or tail-bobbing that persists through the day
Normal preening and occasional feather loss during a moltFeather plucking down to bare skin
Quiet, settled behavior once the dark cycle beginsOpen-mouth breathing, discharge, or wheezing at any time

Chronic egg-laying & egg binding

Chronic egg-laying

Cause: hormonal stimulation, often worsened by nest-like furnishings such as a nest box or "happy hut," extended light exposure, or in some cases even without a male present at all. Signs: repeated clutches beyond a normal, occasional egg. Response: a vet can advise on hormone-reducing changes to housing, lighting, and diet. Removing nest-like furnishings (see page 8) is usually the first and most effective step. Recovery: often improves over weeks once the trigger is removed, but it can become a recurring pattern if those triggers come back.

Egg binding

Cause: an egg that fails to pass normally, more likely in birds laying chronically, running low on calcium, or laying without a mate. Signs: straining, a fluffed-up appearance, tail-bobbing while breathing, and lethargy. Diagnosis: physical exam, sometimes an X-ray to locate the egg. Response: this is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate vet care. Don't wait to see if it resolves on its own. Recovery: outcome depends heavily on how quickly treatment starts.

This is an emergency A hen straining without producing an egg, or showing tail-bobbing breathing and fluffed lethargy, needs a vet the same day. Egg binding does not resolve on its own and can be fatal if left untreated.
Prevention Skip nest boxes and happy huts entirely for a non-breeding bird, keep the photoperiod near the natural 12 hours of darkness a day, and watch for early signs of chronic laying before it becomes a pattern. See page 8 for the full housing rule.

PBFD, polyomavirus & respiratory disease

Three conditions worth knowing by name before you ever see them.

Psittacine Beak and Feather Disease (PBFD)

What it is: a viral disease affecting feathers and beak. Diagnosis: vet testing is required to confirm it, appearance alone isn't enough. Reality check: there is no cure. Prevention: limit exposure to birds of unknown health status, especially at pet stores, swaps, or when introducing a new bird to an existing one.

Polyomavirus

Who it affects: primarily young birds. Signs: feather abnormalities. Prevention: a vaccine is available, ask an avian vet whether it makes sense for your situation, particularly if you keep multiple birds or plan to add more.

Respiratory disease

Signs: open-mouth breathing and discharge. What it can include: a range of causes, one of which is psittacosis. Response: any of these signs warrants a vet visit rather than a wait-and-see approach.

Psittacosis is zoonotic Psittacosis can pass from birds to people. If you or a family member develop flu-like symptoms after a lovebird's respiratory illness, mention the exposure to your own doctor.

Feather plucking & behavioral health

Feather plucking has both behavioral and medical causes, and they can look identical from the outside. Don't assume it's "just behavioral" without ruling out a medical cause first.

Behavioral causes

  • Boredom
  • Insufficient sleep
  • Sexual frustration, often tied to the same hormonal triggers described on page 8
  • A cramped cage

Medical causes

  • Infections
  • Mites
  • Vitamin A deficiency

Diagnosis

Response: a vet visit helps distinguish which category you're dealing with. Medical causes need treatment; behavioral causes need a change to environment, sleep schedule, or interaction level. Guessing wrong wastes time on the wrong fix while the underlying cause keeps going.

Start with the basics Confirm cage size (page 5), sleep schedule near 12 hours of darkness, diet (page 6), and daily interaction level (page 7) before assuming anything more complex. A surprising number of plucking cases trace back to one of these four.

If keeping a single bird, begin building toward roughly 2 hours of daily interaction
