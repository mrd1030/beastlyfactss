Enclosure size, shape & flooring

Measured in square feet of unobstructed floor, not in the label on the box.

Guinea pigs are ground animals. They do not climb, they do not use height, and they run in short bursts along the floor, so the only measurement that means anything is unobstructed floor area. The Merck Veterinary Manual gives a minimum of 7.5 sq ft (0.7 m²) for one guinea pig, plus roughly 2 to 4 sq ft for each additional animal. The welfare figure in wide use for a bonded pair is 10.5 sq ft (1 m²), and since a pair is the normal way to keep this species, that is the number to buy against.

GroupFloor areaRoughly
One guinea pig7.5 sq ft (0.7 m²)30×36 in (76×91 cm)
A bonded pair10.5 sq ft (1 m²)30×50 in (76×127 cm)
Three13 sq ft (1.2 m²)30×62 in (76×158 cm)
Four16 sq ft (1.5 m²)30×76 in (76×193 cm)
What does not work, and why Wire or mesh flooring. A solid floor is essential, because wire causes pododermatitis and foot and limb injuries. This is not a comfort preference. Page 25.
Glass aquariums and plastic bins. Not recommended: ventilation is poor and ammonia from urine builds up at floor level, where the animal breathes.
Multi-level cages with ramps. An upper shelf is not floor space for a species that does not climb, and a ramp is a fall risk for an animal with a fragile spine.
Anything sold as a "guinea pig starter cage." Almost all of them are under 4 sq ft. They are hamster cages with a different picture on the box.

What to buy instead

A C&C enclosure, built from wire storage cubes and a corrugated plastic base, is what most rescues and welfare organizations recommend, because it gives real square footage for the price of a small commercial cage and it can be extended as the group grows. A large commercial habitat around 8 sq ft is the ready-made alternative. Either way the sides need to be at least 12 in (30 cm) high, which is enough: a guinea pig does not jump, and a lid is only needed where a cat or a dog lives in the house.

Where it goes

  • A room the household uses. Guinea pigs are social with people as well as with each other, and a garage or spare room is isolation.
  • Out of direct sun and off a draft. Both are on page 7, and the sun one is genuinely dangerous.
  • Off the floor if there is a dog or a cat, and out of the path of a vacuum cleaner or a slamming door. A prey animal reads all of it.
  • Somewhere you can carry hay to and bedding from twice a week without resentment. The setup you can actually maintain is better than the ideal one you cannot.
  • Not outdoors, in most climates. An outdoor hutch exposes the animals to temperature swings, predators and flies, all of which this species handles badly.

Bedding, hides & cage layout

What goes on the floor, what goes on top of it, and where.

Bedding

Bedding should be recycled paper product, fleece, or another material free of loose fibers and strings. Wood shavings and chips should be avoided, because the aromatic oils are associated with respiratory irritation and disease; cedar is the worst and raw pine is not much better. Kiln-dried pine is the one wood product that is generally considered acceptable, since the drying drives off most of the oils, but paper and fleece avoid the question entirely.

OptionTrade-off
Paper beddingBest odor control and the most forgiving if you cannot clean often. Ongoing cost, and it is the largest single consumable after hay
Fleece linersCheapest over a year and comfortable underfoot. Needs washing every 2 to 3 days, needs an absorbent layer underneath, and needs sweeping daily or it holds moisture against the feet
Kiln-dried pineAcceptable, cheap, and dusty. Avoid entirely if any pig has a respiratory history
Cedar, raw pine, sawdustNot acceptable at any price

Hides, and why there is always one spare

A hide is not decoration. A prey animal with nowhere to retreat stays on alert even in a completely calm room, and research on captive guinea pig welfare links secure hiding options directly to lower stress indicators. Provide one hide per guinea pig plus one extra, and give every hide two openings. A single hide in a shared cage becomes something one animal guards rather than something both can use, and a one-door tunnel becomes a place a lower-ranking pig gets cornered.

Water: offer both a bottle and a bowl Bottles stay clean and are easy to read for volume; bowls are how a guinea pig naturally drinks and let a sick animal drink without effort. Offering both removes the failure mode where a blocked or airlocked bottle goes unnoticed for a day. Check the bottle actually runs every morning by touching the ball, and wash both weekly.

Temperature, heat stress & household climate

This is a health page, not a comfort page. Heat kills guinea pigs.

Guinea pigs have no sweat glands, so they cannot lose heat by sweating, and a coated animal in a cage cannot move somewhere cooler. The comfortable range is 65 to 79°F (18 to 26°C) at 30 to 60% humidity. Above about 82°F (28°C) the risk of heat stress becomes real, and it climbs steeply from there. Welfare bodies put the genuinely comfortable band narrower still, around 64 to 73°F (18 to 23°C), and warn that fatal heatstroke can occur from the high seventies upward in a poorly ventilated spot. A cheap digital thermometer and hygrometer next to the cage removes all the guesswork, and it is the single most useful $15 item in this book.

Signs of heat stress, in order Lying stretched flat with the back feet trailing behind. Panting or open-mouth breathing. Drooling or slobbering. Weakness and reluctance to move. Then convulsions, then death. A guinea pig lying stretched out on a warm day is not relaxed, it is trying to dump heat, and it is the earliest sign you get.
If you find a pig overheating Move it somewhere cool immediately and dampen the fur with cool, not cold and not iced, water. Cold water and ice cause the surface vessels to clamp shut and slow the cooling down. Then go to the vet, without waiting to see whether it improves: treatment is oxygen and intravenous fluids alongside controlled cooling, and a pig that looks recovered can still be in trouble internally.

Keeping a room cool without air conditioning

  • Move the cage to the coolest room in the house, usually a north-facing ground-floor one.
  • Close curtains and blinds on the sunny side in the morning, before the room heats.
  • Ceramic or stone tiles in the cage. They pull heat out of an animal lying on them and cost nothing.
  • Frozen water bottles wrapped in a towel, placed so the pigs can choose to lie against them or not.
  • A fan moving air across the room, never blowing directly at the cage.
  • Extra water, in more than one container, and wet vegetables such as cucumber and romaine.

Cold and drafts

Cold is much less dangerous than heat for a healthy adult in a normal house, but drafts are a genuine risk factor for respiratory infection, which is a leading cause of death in this species. Keep the cage off cold exterior walls and away from doors, windows that get opened, and air-conditioning vents. Extra hay and a covered hide are what a pig actually uses to stay warm. Never use a heat pad inside the cage: a guinea pig cannot reliably move away from one and it will chew the cord.

Light and routine Guinea pigs need no special lighting, but they do need a predictable day. They are crepuscular by preference, most active in the morning and evening, and they sleep in short bursts rather than one long block, which means a cage in a room that is dark and silent for sixteen hours is as unnatural as one lit all night. A normal household rhythm suits them.

Foraging first

Wild cavies spend up to 80% of the day foraging and feeding, and a bowl removes the entire behavior. Restoring it is free. Scatter the hay across the enclosure rather than piling it in one place, which does double duty as diet and enrichment because hay should be unlimited anyway. Hide chopped vegetables in a foraging mat, a wooden puzzle feeder, or under a layer of hay rather than in a dish, and change where they go every few days so it stays a search.

Chew items

Guinea pig teeth grow continuously, all of them, so gnawing is a physical requirement rather than a pastime. Untreated apple or willow wood, hay-based chew sticks, plain cardboard tubes and boxes, and seagrass mats all hold up. Rotate two or three at a time; individual pigs are surprisingly particular about what they will actually chew. Hay does most of the dental work, and no chew toy substitutes for it.

Floor time, and proofing the room

Even a correctly sized enclosure cannot replace open floor. Supervised time in a proofed room or a playpen lets a guinea pig run, popcorn, and investigate new smells and textures. Twenty minutes to an hour daily is a reasonable target.

  • Every cable moved, covered or unplugged. Chewing a live cable is the commonest way floor time goes wrong.
  • Gaps blocked: under sofas, behind appliances, into a cupboard. A pig that gets behind something will not come out.
  • Houseplants out of the room. If you cannot identify one, it goes.
  • Other pets shut out, doors closed, and everyone in the house told the pigs are out.
  • A hide in the pen, so there is somewhere to bolt to.
Popcorning A sudden vertical jump with a twist, often repeated across the floor. It is play, it is a sign of a comfortable animal, and it is most common in young pigs and after a cage clean or the start of floor time. Older pigs do it less. Nothing is wrong with a pig that does not popcorn, but a pig that used to and has stopped is worth a second look.

Pairs and groups: choosing your guinea pigs

The decision that shapes everything else, and the one where the honest answer is not a preference.

Guinea pigs are herd animals descended from cavies that lived in groups, and a solitary guinea pig is an anxious, understimulated animal that tends toward withdrawal and depression. Nearly every credible welfare source recommends pairs or small groups, and several countries require it by law. A bonded pair chases, grooms, follows and talks to each other all day. A solo pig sits and waits. The difference is not subtle and it is visible within a week of a pair being introduced.

Which combination

PairingWhat to expect
Two sowsThe easiest pairing and the usual recommendation. Rumbling and mounting for a few days as a hierarchy settles is normal, not fighting
Two boarsWorks well when they are littermates or both young, or when an adult is paired with a baby. Needs more floor space and two of everything. Adding a third boar is where trouble usually starts
Neutered boar with one or more sowsOften the most stable long-term group. The boar must be neutered and then given at least four to six weeks before he goes near a sow, because he stays fertile after surgery. Page 17
Intact boar with a sowDo not. Guinea pig pregnancy carries real risk to the sow, sows can conceive from 4 to 5 weeks old, and a sow bred for the first time after about 6 to 9 months faces a serious complication. Page 17
With a rabbitNo. Different dietary needs, different language, a size mismatch that injures the guinea pig, and rabbits can carry Bordetella without symptoms while it causes fatal pneumonia in guinea pigs. Page 22

Where to get them

A rescue is the best starting point and usually the cheapest: rescue pigs are already sexed correctly by someone who knows how, often already bonded as a pair, and typically health-checked. Pet shops mis-sex guinea pigs often enough that a surprise litter is a well-known outcome, and shop pigs are frequently sold too young. A breeder is worth it if you want a specific coat type. Wherever they come from, check the sex yourself using page 17 before the two of them share a cage.

What to look at before you take one home

  • Bright eyes with no discharge, and a dry nose.
  • Clean, dry fur with no bald patches, scabs or crusting.
  • Breathing quiet and even. No clicking, no snuffling.
  • A clean, dry rear end. Staining means diarrhea, recent or current.
  • Alert and interested, moving normally on all four feet.
  • Eating hay while you watch. It is the single best sign there is.
Quarantine, and it is not optional even when they look fine A new guinea pig entering a household that already has pigs is kept in a separate room for two to three weeks before any introduction, on separate bedding, with separate hands or a wash between. Ringworm and mange mites are the two things this catches, and both are miserable to clear once they are in a group. Ringworm also transfers to people. Page 25.

Introducing & bonding two guinea pigs

Most introductions work. The ones that fail usually failed at the setup, not at the pigs.

Two guinea pigs meeting for the first time have to establish which of them is dominant, and they do that with noise and posture. Most of what alarms a first-time owner during an introduction is normal negotiation. The job is to give them a space where negotiating is easy and cornering is impossible, then let them get on with it.

The sequence

  1. Quarantine finishes first. Two to three weeks, separate room. Page 9.
  2. Cages side by side for several days. They can smell and hear each other without contact. Swap a hide between the cages so each smells of the other.
  3. Neutral ground. A pen or a floor area neither pig has used, set up fresh: no hides with one entrance, several piles of hay spread out, two water sources, and vegetables scattered widely so eating happens side by side. Neutral is the single most important word on this page.
  4. Let them run. Sit and watch for one to two hours. Do not intervene for noise.
  5. A cleaned cage, then together. Strip the resident cage completely, wash everything, rearrange the layout, and put them in together. It has to stop smelling like one pig's territory.
  6. Watch for the first 48 hours and keep a spare cage assembled for another week.

Normal negotiation, leave it alone

  • Rumbling and rumble-strutting, a low purr with a swaying walk.
  • Mounting, in either sex and either direction.
  • Teeth chattering, raised heads, wide yawns showing teeth.
  • Chasing in short bursts, nose-to-nose standoffs.

Separate now

  • A lunge with teeth that connects, or any blood.
  • Two pigs locked together, rolling, not breaking apart.
  • Fur pulled out in mouthfuls.
  • One pig unable to reach food, water or a hide.
Never break up a fight with bare hands A guinea pig bite through a finger web is a deep, dirty puncture. Use a dustpan, a piece of card or a towel dropped over both animals. Keep oven gloves near the pen during an introduction and you will never have to think about it.

Two of everything, permanently

Most conflict in an established pair is about there being one of something. Two hides with two doorways each, two water sources, two hay piles at opposite ends, and a bowl big enough that two heads fit or two separate bowls. This is also why the floor area on page 5 matters: a pair in a cage that is technically the minimum has nowhere to go when one of them wants space.

If it genuinely does not work A small number of pairings fail, most often two adult boars introduced late. That is not a reason to keep either pig alone. Rescues run bonding services precisely because this happens, and a pig who cannot live with one particular individual will usually live happily with a different one. Two cages side by side, so they can see and talk to each other, is a distant second-best but far better than isolation.

Handling, sounds & body language

Being lifted off the ground is, to a guinea pig's instincts, exactly what a predator does.

Picking one up correctly

  1. Approach from the side, never from above. A hand descending from overhead is a bird of prey. Come in level, and let the pig see the hand first.
  2. One hand under the chest, just behind the front legs.
  3. The other hand scoops and supports the hindquarters at the same time. Both ends, always. A guinea pig's spine is not built to hang.
  4. Bring it in against your chest so it is supported rather than dangling.

Stay low. Sit on the floor or a bed. A fall from a modest height can be fatal for an animal this size, and there is no version of handling a guinea pig standing up that is worth the risk. Never lift by the limbs, the scruff or loose fur, and never flip one onto its back, which can damage the spine and is highly stressful even when it does not.

Children A guinea pig is often the first pet a child is given, and the combination of a squirming animal and unsteady hands is how falls happen. The workable rule is that a child sits on the floor and the guinea pig is placed on their lap by an adult, and is lifted only by an adult. That is not a restriction on the child's relationship with the animal; lap time on the floor is most of what the relationship consists of.

Hay: the 80% nobody budgets for

It is not bedding, it is not filler, and it is the largest line in the budget.

Unlimited grass hay is the most important thing in a guinea pig's diet and should make up 70 to 80% of daily intake. A guinea pig eats roughly its own body size in hay every day. It does two jobs nothing else does: the long-strand fiber keeps a hindgut fermenter's digestion moving, and the sideways grinding chew wears down teeth that never stop growing. Almost every problem on the health pages traces back to one of those two failing.

HayWho forNotes
TimothyAdults, standardThe default. Coarse, low calcium, good stem-to-leaf balance
Orchard grassAdultsSofter and sweeter. A good choice for a fussy pig, or for an owner allergic to timothy
Meadow or oat hayAdults, mixed inVaried texture and seed heads make it interesting. Fine alongside timothy rather than as the only hay
AlfalfaUnder about 6 months, pregnant, nursing, recoveringA legume, not a grass. Higher protein and calcium, which growing and pregnant animals need. Not for healthy adults: it contributes to obesity and the calcium load feeds bladder stones. Page 23

Judging hay, and storing it

  • Green, not yellow or gray. Yellow hay is sun-bleached and has lost nutritional value.
  • Smells sweet and dry. Anything musty, sour or dusty goes straight in the bin. Mold is a serious risk to a small herbivore.
  • Mixed stem and leaf. All leaf is soft and does less dental work; all stem gets refused.
  • Store it loose and dry, in a cardboard box or a cloth bag, never sealed in plastic, which traps moisture and grows mold.
  • Buy in bulk. It is the single biggest saving available in guinea pig keeping, and a pair will get through it.
Never let it run out, and never pile it in one corner Hay is offered continuously, refilled at least twice a day, and scattered in two or three places rather than heaped in one. Scattering it is free enrichment (page 8), it stops one pig guarding the supply, and it means a shy pig always has somewhere to eat. Pigs also eat hay lying down, so a rack alone is not enough: there should always be a pile on the floor as well.
Dietary neophobia is real in this species Guinea pigs fix their food preferences early in life and do not adapt well to sudden changes, so a new hay brand or cut can be refused simply for being unfamiliar. Introduce a new hay by mixing it into the old one over a week or two rather than switching outright, and treat a pig that has genuinely stopped eating hay as a health problem rather than fussiness. Vitamin C: the rule that makes this species different

The one requirement generic small-pet advice always misses.

Guinea pigs are among a small handful of mammals, alongside humans and some primates and fruit bats, that cannot manufacture their own vitamin C. Rabbits can. Hamsters can. Chinchillas can. A guinea pig has no internal reserve to draw on between meals, so the supply has to arrive every single day, from food. Without it, collagen production fails within days to weeks, and collagen is what holds together joints, gums, blood vessels and healing wounds. That is scurvy, and it is on page 21.

RequirementAmount
Healthy adult10 to 25 mg per kg of body weight per day
Growing, pregnant, nursing or ill30 mg per kg per day or more
Commonly quoted flat figure10 to 50 mg a day. For a typical 900 g adult the two overlap at roughly 10 to 25 mg

The per-kilogram figure is the one to work from, because it scales with the animal. A 700 g sow and a 1,200 g boar do not need the same dose.

Where it should come from, in order

  1. Fresh vegetables, daily. Bell pepper is the standout: red, yellow and orange peppers carry substantially more vitamin C than green, and about a quarter of a medium pepper covers most of an adult's daily requirement on its own. Page 14.
  2. Fortified pellets, as a floor rather than the plan. See the warning below.
  3. A direct supplement, as tablets or oral drops given by mouth, when a pig is ill, recovering, pregnant, or genuinely refusing vegetables. It closes a gap; it is not the primary route.
Never put vitamin C in the water Two separate reasons, and both matter. It oxidizes fast in light and air, so within hours the bottle contains far less than the label promised. And it changes the taste, which makes some guinea pigs drink less, working directly against a species already prone to bladder problems. Supplementation in water is specifically not recommended in the veterinary guidance.
Why fortified pellets alone are not enough Pellets are fortified at milling, and vitamin C is notoriously unstable: it degrades with heat, light, air and time, and a meaningful share is gone within weeks even in a sealed bag. Use pellets within about 90 days of the manufacture date, not the sell-by date, buy smaller bags more often than feels necessary, and store them sealed, cool and dark. Relying on a nutrition label as the only vitamin C source is one of the commonest routes to a scurvy case in a household that is feeding a "complete" diet.
The five-second version A quarter of a red bell pepper per pig, every day, and the question is closed. Everything else on this page is what to do when that is not possible.

Daily vegetables & greens

One to two cups per pig per day, rotated, and led by bell pepper.

Fresh produce is where the vitamin C actually comes from, and the veterinary guidance is 1 to 2 cups of fresh produce per guinea pig per day, offered as one or two servings and rotated across several vegetables rather than the same thing every day. Rotation matters for two reasons: it spreads the calcium and oxalate load, which is what the bladder pages care about, and it keeps a species prone to fixed food preferences willing to try things.

Pellets, fruit, treats & the never-feed list

The smallest part of the diet, and the part with the real dangers in it.

Pellets

Pellets are a supplement to hay, not a replacement, and the portion is small: the veterinary figure is 1 to 2 teaspoons per guinea pig per day, and popular care guides commonly give about 1/8 cup, roughly 2 tablespoons. Those disagree by a factor of several, and the honest reading is that a guinea pig eating plenty of hay and 1 to 2 cups of fresh produce needs the smaller amount, while the larger figure suits a growing, pregnant or underweight animal. Start at 1 to 2 teaspoons and let hay intake and body condition decide. A pig eating all its pellets and little hay is being given too many.

  • Guinea-pig pellets specifically. Rabbit and other rodent pellets contain no added vitamin C, because those animals make their own.
  • Timothy-based, with stabilized vitamin C on the label, and used within 90 days of manufacture. Page 13.
  • Plain uniform pellets only. Anything with seeds, nuts, corn, dried fruit or colored pieces is a muesli mix, and pigs selectively eat the sweet parts and leave the rest.
  • Alfalfa-based pellets belong with young, pregnant or nursing pigs, not healthy adults. Page 12.

Fruit and treats

Treats of all kinds, fruit included, stay under about 10% of the diet, which for an animal this size means a piece the size of a fingernail rather than a slice. Small amounts of orange, kiwi, apple, pear, strawberry, blueberry or melon are fine a couple of times a week, with pits and seeds removed. There is no commercial treat that is better than a piece of bell pepper, and the yogurt drops, honey sticks and seed bars on the pet shop shelf are actively worse than nothing.

Sexing, neutering & the breeding deadline

Mis-sexing is common, and the consequences arrive fast.

How to sex a guinea pig

Sit down, rest the pig on your lap with its back against you so it is upright and supported, and look at the genital area with the animal's rear tipped gently toward you. Press very lightly just above the opening with a fingertip.

  • Boar: gentle pressure above the opening extrudes the penis, and there is a distinct rounded shape you can feel under the skin. The whole area reads as a shallow "i" shape, a small circle above a line.
  • Sow: the same pressure opens a "Y" shaped slit, and nothing extrudes. The genital opening sits closer to the anus than in a boar.
  • Both sexes have nipples, so nipples tell you nothing. Testicles in a young boar are not obvious, so their apparent absence tells you nothing either.

If you are not certain after two goes, the vet will do it in thirty seconds at the first health check, and pet shops get this wrong often enough that a rescue's word is worth more than a shop's label.

The timings that make this urgent A sow can conceive from 4 to 5 weeks old, and boars start mounting at about a month. Mixed-sex litters are separated by three weeks of age, no later, and that includes separating a young boar from his own mother. Gestation is 59 to 72 days, averaging 68, which is very long for a rodent, and a litter is usually 2 to 4 but can be as many as 13. A sow can also be mated again within hours of giving birth.
Why a first litter after about 6 to 9 months is dangerous A guinea pig's pubic symphysis, the cartilage joint at the front of the pelvis, stays flexible in a sow who has given birth, but in one who has not it ossifies and fuses over her first year. After that the pelvic canal cannot widen enough to pass a guinea pig's very large, fully-furred, open-eyed newborns, and a natural birth becomes impossible: the outcome is obstructed labor and a caesarean, or death. This is the single strongest reason not to keep an intact boar and a sow together and hope. It is also why "we will just let her have one litter" is a bad plan for an adult rescue sow of unknown history.

Neutering

Neutering in guinea pigs is done on boars, not sows: a sow spay is major abdominal surgery reserved for a medical reason such as ovarian cysts (page 24), while a boar castration is comparatively straightforward. It does not change temperament and it is not a solution to a boar-boar conflict; the reason to do it is to allow a boar to live with sows.

The four to six week rule A neutered boar stays fertile for weeks after surgery while sperm already present clears. Keep him away from any sow for at least four to six weeks after the operation, and ask your vet for their own figure. Litters conceived a fortnight after a castration are a well-known and entirely avoidable event. Anesthesia in this species also carries real risk and needs a vet who does it regularly. Page 26.

GI stasis, bloat & a pig that stops eating

The emergency this species is most likely to have, and the one with the shortest fuse.

Gastrointestinal stasis is the gut slowing or stopping. It follows from inadequate fiber, dehydration, pain from anywhere in the body, stress, or a sudden diet change, and it then feeds itself: a pig that hurts stops eating, and a pig that stops eating hurts more. Bloat is the related emergency in which gas accumulates faster than it can be passed, distending the abdomen and pressing on the diaphragm. Both are urgent. Neither is treatable at home.

The seven reasons a guinea pig stops eating

  1. Dental disease. The most commonly cited cause by a distance, and usually invisible without a vet's examination because the problem is at the back of the mouth. Page 20.
  2. GI stasis itself, once it is under way.
  3. Pain or illness elsewhere. Respiratory infection, bladder stones, arthritis or an injury all suppress appetite as a secondary sign.
  4. Environmental stress. A new home, a moved cage, a new cage mate, building noise. Real, and usually short-lived, but never assume it.
  5. Dietary neophobia. A new hay or pellet brand refused because it is unfamiliar. Page 12.
  6. Pregnancy toxemia, in an overweight or late-term sow. Page 24.
  7. Vitamin C deficiency. Reduced appetite is one of the earliest signs and appears within 2 to 3 weeks of a deficient diet. Page 21.
Signs, and the line Reduced or absent droppings, or droppings gone small and dry. Lethargy or reluctance to move. A hunched or tense posture. Audible teeth grinding. A bloated or firm abdomen, sometimes with a hollow drum sound when tapped very gently. Reduced drinking. Labored breathing as a distended gut presses upward. Call at 8 to 12 hours without food or droppings. Not tomorrow morning.

What the vet will do

Assisted feeding with a syringe of recovery formula is the core of it, alongside pain relief, fluids, and a prokinetic drug to restart gut movement where appropriate. An x-ray distinguishes gas from an obstruction, which matters because a true obstruction is a surgical problem and gas is not. Underlying causes are chased at the same time, and the teeth are always checked. Recovery generally begins within 24 to 72 hours of treatment starting.

Syringe feeding at home, only under direction Your vet will likely send you home syringe-feeding a critical care formula every few hours, including overnight. Two things make it safer: give small amounts and let the pig chew and swallow at its own pace rather than pushing the plunger, and aim the syringe into the side of the mouth behind the incisors, never straight down the throat. A guinea pig that is fighting hard is a guinea pig at risk of inhaling the food. Stop, wait, and try again in ten minutes. Keep a syringe and a bag of recovery formula in the house before you need them; it is the one supply that is always needed at the worst possible hour.

Dental disease & malocclusion

Every tooth grows for life, and the ones that go wrong are the ones you cannot see.

A guinea pig's incisors, premolars and molars all grow continuously. The front teeth are easy to look at and are rarely the whole story; the cheek teeth at the back are where malocclusion usually starts, and in this species the lower cheek teeth characteristically overgrow inward and bridge over the tongue, trapping it so the animal cannot swallow properly. An owner sees a pig that has gone off its food and looks otherwise normal.

Signs, which are nonspecific and that is the problem Anything from mild pickiness to a complete refusal to eat. Difficulty chewing or swallowing. Drooling, or a permanently wet chin and chest, which is the classic one. Dropping food after picking it up. Preferring soft foods and abandoning hay. Weight loss. Reduced activity. Unusually small droppings, because less is going in.

Why it happens

Teeth stay the right length by grinding against each other, and the grinding comes almost entirely from chewing hay sideways. A pig on a pellet-and-vegetable diet with hay as an afterthought does not do enough of that motion, and the teeth drift out of alignment. Once they are out of alignment they wear unevenly, which makes the misalignment worse. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why page 12 is the prevention for this page. Vitamin C deficiency also affects the ligaments that hold the teeth in their sockets, which is a second route to the same place, and injury or a genetic jaw fault are the less common ones.

Treatment, and what to expect

Diagnosis needs a proper examination, which means an otoscope or endoscope and usually sedation: a conscious guinea pig's mouth is a narrow tube and the important teeth are at the far end of it. Treatment is crown reduction and correction of the occlusal angle under anesthesia, and severely diseased teeth are extracted. Chronic cases often need the procedure repeated every 4 to 6 weeks, which is why prevention is worth so much: this is a lifelong, recurring cost once it starts. Ask directly whether the practice has the equipment and does this routinely, because a general small-animal clinic often does not.

What you can actually check at home Look at the incisors weekly: the top pair should be shorter than the bottom pair and meet squarely, not splay sideways or cross over. Feel along the jawline for lumps, which can be root overgrowth pushing down through the bone. Then weigh the pig, because the real early-warning system for a problem you cannot see is a number that has started falling. Page 27.
Never trim a guinea pig's teeth yourself Clipping incisors with nail clippers shatters the tooth lengthwise and can split it into the root, causing an abscess. It is done with a dental burr, under anesthesia, by a vet. Anything sold for home use should be ignored.

The most common preventable illness in pet guinea pigs, and the easiest to miss early.

Page 13 covers the requirement. This page is what happens when it is not met. Without dietary vitamin C, collagen production breaks down over days to weeks, and collagen is structural: it holds together joints, gum tissue, blood vessel walls and healing wounds. The clinical picture follows exactly from that. The early signs are vague enough to be blamed on age, weather or mood, which is why this remains common in households that believe they are feeding well.

Treatment

This is a vet visit, not a wait-and-see, and particularly so once joint pain or gum bleeding is visible. Respiratory infection & pneumonia

A leading cause of death in this species, and it moves fast.

Pneumonia is a leading cause of death in guinea pigs, especially in young ones, and it is usually bacterial. Bordetella bronchiseptica and Streptococcus pneumoniae are the two agents named most often. Respiratory infections here progress quickly, and there is one anatomical fact that makes them more dangerous than they would be in another animal: guinea pigs are obligate nasal breathers. They cannot switch to breathing through the mouth when the nose blocks. An open-mouthed guinea pig is in serious trouble.

Same-day signs Sneezing, especially repeatedly. Discharge from the nose or eyes, or crusting around either. Any noise on breathing: clicking, wheezing, rattling. Labored breathing, or the flanks visibly working. Open-mouth breathing. Loss of appetite and lethargy alongside any of the above. A pig sitting hunched with its neck extended.

Where it comes from

  • Drafts and damp. The commonest environmental contributor and the easiest to fix. Page 7.
  • Ammonia from bedding that is not changed often enough, concentrated at floor level where the animal breathes. This is also why glass tanks are a poor choice. Page 6.
  • Dust, from wood shavings, very dusty hay, or a cage in a busy room being swept around.
  • Rabbits. A rabbit can carry Bordetella without any signs at all and pass it to a guinea pig, in whom it causes fatal pneumonia. This is the concrete reason not to house the two species together, over and above the dietary and size mismatch. Page 9.
  • Stress and crowding, particularly in a newly bought young pig that has been through a shop.
  • Vitamin C deficiency, which suppresses the immune response. Page 21.

Treatment

Antibiotics, chosen carefully, plus supportive care: fluids, warmth, assisted feeding, and sometimes oxygen and nebulization. The choice of antibiotic matters enormously in this species and page 26 is the page to take with you. Treatment courses are typically longer than an owner expects, and stopping early because the pig looks better is a common route to a relapse with a harder infection.

A sneeze is not always an infection, but it is always worth watching Hay dust, a dusty bedding change and a stray strand up the nose all produce an isolated sneeze in a completely healthy pig. What separates that from the start of something is repetition, discharge, and any change in appetite or activity. One sneeze on a Tuesday is nothing. Sneezing several times a day for two days is a call.

Bladder stones & urinary disease

Common, painful, and the one area where the daily vegetable list has consequences.

Guinea pigs form urinary stones readily, and the stones are typically calcium carbonate and related crystals. This is a species that excretes surplus calcium through the kidneys rather than storing or discarding it another way, so a persistently high calcium load in the diet ends up in the bladder as sediment and then as stones. Blood in the urine, hematuria, is a characteristic finding.

Ovarian cysts & reproductive disease

Very common in sows, easy to see once you know the sign, and often silent until then.

Ovarian cysts

Ovarian cysts are the most common reproductive tract disorder in guinea pig sows, and they are common enough that any middle-aged or older intact sow with vague signs deserves to be assessed for them. Fluid-filled cysts develop on one or both ovaries, and because some of them produce hormones, the effects reach well beyond the abdomen.

The sign that gives it away Symmetrical hair loss along both flanks, running down each side of the body, with the skin underneath looking normal rather than inflamed or scabby. That pattern in a sow is ovarian cysts until proven otherwise, and it is what separates this from mites, which itch and damage the skin (page 25). Other signs are a swollen or pear-shaped abdomen, crusty or enlarged nipples, reduced appetite, lethargy, and a sow becoming unusually grumpy or aggressive with a cage mate.

Diagnosis is by ultrasound. Treatment options are hormonal, which shrinks some cysts and can be a reasonable choice for an older sow, draining, which usually refills, or ovariohysterectomy, which is definitive and also removes the risk of uterine disease later. The surgery is a real abdominal procedure and needs a vet who does guinea pig anesthesia routinely. Page 26.

Pregnancy, and pregnancy toxemia

Guinea pig pregnancy is risky, which is the reason page 17 is written the way it is. Gestation runs 59 to 72 days, averaging 68, and the young are born fully furred with their eyes open and start eating solid food within hours. The two serious complications are the fused pubic symphysis in a sow bred for the first time after about 6 to 9 months, covered on page 17, and pregnancy toxemia.

Pregnancy toxemia A metabolic collapse in late pregnancy or just after birth, and it is potentially fatal. The risk is highest in overweight sows and in late-term pregnancy, and it can be triggered by any period of not eating, by stress, or by a sudden change in food. It presents as sudden appetite loss and lethargy in a pregnant or newly delivered sow, and it moves quickly. Any pregnant sow that goes off her food is an emergency, at once, not at 8 to 12 hours.

Mammary and other reproductive disease

Mammary tumors occur in both sexes, boars included, and are found as a firm lump near a nipple. They are frequently treatable when caught early and removed, so the weekly hands-on check on page 27 is the thing that catches them. Uterine and cervical disease presents most often as bleeding from the vulva outside of any normal cycle, which is always a vet visit.

Mites, lice, ringworm, lumps & bumblefoot

The outside of the animal, and two of these transfer to you.

Mange mites

Trixacarus caviae is a burrowing mange mite and it causes severe itching. The veterinary description is worth quoting because owners routinely misread it: the scratching can be so intense that the animal appears to be having a seizure. A guinea pig that suddenly runs in circles, throws itself over, or shrieks and convulses is very often a mite case rather than a neurological one, and it is one of the most treatable serious-looking things in this book. Hair loss, scabbing and thickened crusted skin along the back and flanks come with it. Mites are often latent in a pig for a long time and flare when the animal is stressed, pregnant, or ill.

Treatment Ivermectin at 0.4 mg/kg by injection under the skin, repeated every 10 to 14 days for four treatments, or selamectin at 15 mg/kg applied to the skin once, are both effective. Treat every pig in the household, not only the one showing signs, and clean and replace bedding and porous cage furniture during treatment. Pain relief matters too: a badly affected pig hurts.

Lice

Guinea pig lice are host-specific, do not live on people, and cause milder disease than mites. You may see them as tiny moving specks along the neck and behind the ears. Heavy infestations produce hair loss and crusted lesions. Treated similarly, and again treat everyone in the cage.

Ringworm

Not a worm but a fungal skin infection, usually Trichophyton mentagrophytes or T. benhamiae. It appears as circular patches of hair loss with flaky, crusty skin, classically starting around the face, nose and ears and spreading. It transmits from guinea pigs to humans, so it is worth diagnosing properly rather than guessing, and worth gloves while treating. Treatment is antifungal, topical or oral depending on extent, together with a thorough environmental clean, and it takes weeks.

Lumps in the neck

Cervical lymphadenitis produces large, one-sided swellings in the neck, sometimes called lumps, from infected lymph nodes. Treatment is surgical removal of the affected nodes with systemic antibiotics. Any firm lump anywhere on a guinea pig warrants an appointment: abscesses, cysts, lipomas and tumors all present as a lump and telling them apart is a needle and a microscope, not a look.

Bumblefoot, or pododermatitis Inflammation and ulceration of the soles, showing as redness, callus-like swellings, open sores, and eventually cellulitis and deep infection. It is caused by pressure and contamination: wire flooring, damp or dirty bedding, obesity, and hard surfaces with nothing soft to stand on. Early cases are treated with topical care, bandaging, pain relief, vitamin C, and correcting the husbandry that caused it. Advanced cases can reach the bone and are very hard to resolve, which makes the solid clean floor on page 5 a genuinely medical requirement rather than a comfort one. Check the soles during the weekly handling check.

Antibiotics, anesthesia & the vet conversation

Print this page. It is the one to hand over at a clinic you have not used before.

A guinea pig's gut flora is predominantly Gram-positive, and the fermentation it performs is what digests the animal's food. Antibiotics that act preferentially against Gram-positive organisms wipe out that flora and allow toxin-producing bacteria, particularly Clostridium difficile, to overgrow. The result is antibiotic-associated enterotoxemia, and it kills. This is not a rare idiosyncratic reaction: it is a predictable consequence of the wrong drug in this species, and it is the single most important thing a guinea pig owner can know about veterinary care.

Antibiotics implicated in enterotoxemia in guinea pigs Penicillin, ampicillin, streptomycin, clindamycin, lincomycin, spiramycin, chlortetracycline (aureomycin), erythromycin and bacitracin have all been implicated, given by mouth or by injection. Narrow-spectrum drugs aimed at Gram-positive organisms are the highest risk. If a prescription for your guinea pig is on this list, ask about it before the first dose. Ask, do not refuse: there are situations where a vet chooses one of these deliberately and manages the risk. What should never happen is it being prescribed without anyone noticing the species.

Any antibiotic can disturb the flora, so all of them are used with caution here, and enterotoxemia is also triggered by stress, an abrupt diet change, and gastrointestinal stasis itself. Signs are diarrhea, a drop in appetite, lethargy and collapse, appearing during or shortly after a course, and it is an emergency.

Questions worth asking, at any clinic

  • How many guinea pigs does the practice see in a month?
  • Do you anesthetize guinea pigs routinely, and for dentals specifically?
  • Do you have the equipment to examine the cheek teeth properly?
  • Who is on out-of-hours, and do they see exotics?
  • What is your policy on fasting before anesthesia?
  • Can I bring a cage mate to the appointment?
Two things that are different from a dog or a cat Guinea pigs are not fasted before anesthesia. They cannot vomit, so the reason for fasting a dog does not apply, and a herbivore that stops eating before surgery arrives at the operating table with a slowing gut. A short period to clear food from the mouth is all that is normally involved. If a practice tells you to withhold food overnight, ask them to confirm that against exotic-species guidance.
Bring the cage mate. Guinea pigs are separated badly, both by the animal that leaves and the one left behind, and a pig recovering alone in a strange room eats less. Most exotics practices will house a companion alongside, and the food intake after surgery is usually what determines the outcome.
And the everyday version A guinea pig recovering from anything eats less than it should, and eating is the whole game in this species. Ask for pain relief as a matter of course rather than only when the animal looks sore, ask about assisted feeding before you leave, and take a syringe and recovery formula home with you.

Droppings, weight & body condition

Two minutes a day and five minutes a week, and they catch nearly everything on the last ten pages.

Droppings

Normal guinea pig droppings are firm, oval, dark brown, uniform in size, and plentiful: a healthy pig produces a great many every day. They are the fastest read you get on how the gut is doing, and reading them is free.

What you seeWhat it usually means
Fewer than usual, or noneThe gut is slowing. Page 19. At 8 to 12 hours with none, call
Small, dry, hard, or pointed at one endNot enough is going in, or not enough water. Often the first sign of dental disease
Soft, clumped or stuck togetherToo much fresh food or a sudden diet change, most often. Pull back the vegetables for a day and watch
Truly liquid, or a soiled wet rearDiarrhea. Not the same as soft droppings, and a same-day call
Slimy, mucus-covered, or with bloodVet, today
Chalky white residue in the urine patchesNormal calcium carbonate. Gritty sediment or pink staining is not. Page 23

A guinea pig also eats some of its droppings directly from the vent. This is normal and necessary: the caecum produces a softer, nutrient-rich dropping that the animal reingests to absorb what a single pass through the gut misses. An obese, arthritic or long-coated pig that physically cannot reach is missing part of its nutrition, and that is worth a vet conversation rather than a shrug.

Weight, weekly

A digital kitchen scale reading in grams is the most useful diagnostic tool an owner of this species can own. Weigh every pig on the same day each week, at the same time, and write it down.

ReadingWhat to do
Stable within about 30 g week to weekNormal fluctuation. Carry on
Down 30 to 50 g, or about 5%Watch closely, check hay intake and droppings, weigh again in 2 to 3 days
Down more than 50 g, or falling three weeks runningVet appointment. Dental disease and megabacteria-style quiet losses show here first
Climbing steadilyToo many pellets or too much fruit. Check the portion against page 15
The weekly hands-on check, done while the pig is on the scale Feel along the spine and hips: in good condition they are palpable but not sharp, and the body reads as a rounded loaf rather than a pear or a ridge. Then check, in order: eyes and nose clear, teeth meeting squarely, ears clean, coat free of scabs and bald patches, feet with no redness or swelling on the soles, rear end clean and dry, and a run of both hands over the body and belly feeling for lumps. Boars over two get the rectal pouch check from page 16. Five minutes, once a week, catches most of section three early.

Heat waves, power outages & travel

For this species the emergency that matters is heat, and the rule in an outage is keep feeding.

A heat wave, with or without power

Losing air conditioning in hot weather is the genuine emergency for a guinea pig, far more than losing heating in cold weather. Work down this list in order.

  1. Move them to the coolest room in the house, usually a ground-floor room on the shaded side. A basement is often the best place in the building.
  2. Close curtains and blinds early, before the sun reaches that side, not after the room is already hot.
  3. Ceramic or stone tiles in the cage. Keep spares in the fridge and rotate them.
  4. Wrapped frozen bottles, positioned so the pigs can lie against them or move away.
  5. Damp towel over part of the cage with air moving past it, never sealing the cage in.
  6. Extra water in more than one container, and water-rich vegetables such as cucumber and romaine.
  7. If a pig shows heat stress, damp the fur with cool, not iced, water and go to the vet. Page 7.
In a power cut, keep feeding A guinea pig's danger in an outage is not cold, it is a gut that stops moving. Hay, pellets and vegetables all continue exactly as normal, and water is offered more often rather than less. This is the opposite of the advice for a reptile, where temperature comes first, and it is the single most important sentence on this page. Extra hay to burrow into is how a guinea pig keeps warm; a heat pad in the cage is a chew hazard and a burn risk.

Travel

Never leave a guinea pig in a parked car Not for five minutes, not with the windows cracked. A car interior reaches lethal temperatures for this species faster than for a dog, and heat is the thing this animal is least able to survive.
