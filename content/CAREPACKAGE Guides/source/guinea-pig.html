Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameCavia porcellus
Adult size8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm); 1.5 to 2.6 lb
Captive lifespan4 to 8 years typical with consistent care
OriginDomesticated species; wild ancestors native to the Andes region of South America
Diet typeHerbivore, hay-forward grazer
Floor space minimum7.5 sq ft (one guinea pig); about 10.5 sq ft for a pair; 12 in minimum height
Handling difficultyBeginner; social animals that generally do well with gentle, regular handling
Social needsHerd animal; generally does better paired or grouped than solitary

Cost overview

Monthly ongoing

$30 to $50 for pellets for a pair, plus hay, bedding, fresh vegetables, and vitamin C supplementation.

Annual total

Commonly $400 to $1,620, with around $1,200 a year as a typical middle-ground figure, including a $50 to $100 annual wellness exam.

Budget toward the higher end The wide annual range mostly comes down to hay and vet costs. Planning toward the $1,200 middle-ground figure rather than the $400 floor avoids surprises.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Guinea pigs aren't pigs and aren't from Guinea. They're rodents, domesticated thousands of years ago from wild cavies native to South America.
  • A "wheek," a loud, distinct squeal, is a guinea pig's classic food-anticipation call. Owners often learn to recognize their own guinea pig's version of it.
  • As herd animals, guinea pigs communicate constantly through sound and body language, part of why they generally do better with company than alone.

Housing & enclosure

Floor space, not height, is what defines a guinea pig enclosure. A single guinea pig needs a minimum of 7.5 sq ft of usable floor space; a pair needs roughly 10.5 sq ft. Height matters far less for this species: 12 in is the minimum, and going taller doesn't make up for a small footprint. Many owners build a C&C-style grid enclosure or use a commercial cage sized to these minimums; either works as long as the floor space clears the bar.

Never Use cedar or raw (non-kiln-dried) pine shavings as bedding. Both release oils that can irritate a guinea pig's respiratory system. Paper-based bedding, fleece, and kiln-dried pine are all fine.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Give each guinea pig its own hide, plus one larger shared hide if housing a pair, so no one has to choose between hiding and easy access to food and hay.
  • Keep the hay area separate from the water and food zone. Hay should be available at all times, and keeping it away from spills keeps it from getting soiled.
  • Bigger than the minimum is genuinely better here. 7.5 and 10.5 sq ft are floors, not targets to aim for.

Temperature & climate

ZoneTarget
Room / enclosure temperatureKeep below roughly 80°F
Heat stroke riskRises quickly once temperatures climb above about 80°F
Cold toleranceConsiderably better than heat tolerance; cold is the smaller concern for this species

This is a ceiling, not a range. Guinea pigs handle cold far better than heat, so the number that matters most is the upper limit, not a lower one.

No heat lamp or UVB required

Unlike some reptiles, guinea pigs don't need supplemental heating, UVB, or specialized lighting to stay healthy. Ordinary indoor room temperature, kept below roughly 80°F, is the target. Put your setup budget toward floor space and vitamin C instead.

Placement matters

Keep the enclosure out of direct sun and away from heating vents or drafty windows. Good airflow around the enclosure helps prevent heat from building up in one spot.

Watch for heat stress

Labored breathing, drooling, and lethargy are all warning signs, whatever the cause. Any of them warrants prompt attention; see page 10.

Simple rule of thumb If a room is comfortable for a person in a sweater, it's very likely too warm for a guinea pig once it climbs past about 80°F. When in doubt, move the enclosure to the coolest reasonable spot in the home.

Diet & feeding

Guinea pigs are herbivores, and hay is the foundation of the diet, not a side item.

FoodShare / amountFrequency
Hay (grass hay)Roughly 70 to 80% of intakeAlways available, unlimited
PelletsAbout 1/8 cup per guinea pigDaily
Fresh vegetablesPart of daily dietDaily
Vitamin C (diet and/or supplement)10 to 50 mgDaily target
Never / avoid Alfalfa hay for adults (too high in calcium; fine for very young guinea pigs), iceberg lettuce (no real nutritional value), and high-sugar fruit as anything more than an occasional treat.

Vitamin C is the single most important nutritional fact

Guinea pigs can't produce their own vitamin C. Without enough of it in the diet, deficiency (scurvy, page 11) follows. Target 10 to 50 mg per day through a combination of vitamin C rich vegetables and, if needed, a dedicated supplement such as a chewable tablet or drops. Check any supplement's freshness or expiration date regularly rather than assuming it still works at full strength; log this in the Equipment Log on page 20.

Hay first, always Unlimited hay isn't optional or a nice extra. At 70 to 80% of intake, it's the single largest part of the diet, and it's what keeps teeth worn down properly. See Dental Disease on page 11.

Handling & social needs

Handling

Guinea pigs are prey animals and startle easily, especially from sudden movement or being approached from directly above. Let a new guinea pig settle for several days before handling beyond feeding and cleaning. Always support the full body, including the hindquarters, rather than lifting under the front legs alone, and keep early sessions short and low to the ground in case of a jump or wriggle.

Building trust Short, frequent, low-pressure sessions build confidence faster than occasional long ones. Talking quietly and offering a small piece of vegetable before picking a guinea pig up helps it associate handling with something good rather than something to escape.

Social needs

Guinea pigs are herd animals. As a rule, they generally do better paired or grouped than kept solitary. If keeping a single guinea pig, plan on extra daily interaction to help make up for the lack of a companion. It doesn't fully replace one, but it helps.

Consider a companion A same-sex pair or a spayed/neutered pair, introduced carefully and gradually, is generally the easiest path to a stable social group. Never assume two unfamiliar guinea pigs can simply be placed together without a supervised introduction.

First days in a new home

A rough timeline: days 1 to 3, hands-off except feeding and spot-cleaning, so your guinea pig can learn the sounds and smells of its new space; days 4 to 7, brief calm presence near the enclosure; week 2 onward, first short handling attempts if eating and behavior look normal.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Treating 7.5 sq ft (or 10.5 for a pair) as a target instead of a bare minimum. Bigger is genuinely better for this species.
  • Assuming a taller enclosure makes up for a small footprint. Floor space, not height, is what guinea pigs actually use.
  • Using cedar or raw pine shavings, when paper-based bedding, fleece, or kiln-dried pine are all safe and available.
  • Assuming pellets alone cover the 10 to 50 mg daily vitamin C target without checking labels or adding fresh sources.
  • Feeding alfalfa hay to an adult long-term instead of grass hay.
  • Letting the hay supply run low between refills, when it should make up 70 to 80% of intake and be available at all times.
  • Keeping a guinea pig fully solo with no plan for company, when the species is a herd animal that generally does better paired or grouped.
  • Assuming a warm room is fine because it's "just a bit warm." Heat stroke risk rises quickly once temperatures climb above roughly 80°F.

Enrichment, by priority

Hides & tunnels

Multiple hides and tunnels let a guinea pig feel secure while still moving freely around its floor space.

Foraging

Scattering hay piles or fresh vegetables in a few spots encourages the natural grazing behavior guinea pigs would show in the wild.

Companionship

Paired or grouped housing, where possible, is the single biggest enrichment upgrade for a herd animal.

Chew items & routine

Safe chew toys support natural tooth wear (page 11), and a consistent daily feeding routine doubles as a stress-lowering constant.

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a vet promptly if you see Refusal to eat, drooling, labored breathing, diarrhea, lethargy, or a hunched posture. All of these warrant immediate vet care, not a wait-and-see approach.

What to tell the vet

Recent hay, pellet, and vegetable intake
Recent weight trend from the Owner Log (page 20)
When symptoms started, and whether they're worsening
Whether housed alone or with others, and any recent changes

Why hay and vitamin C come up first

Two of the four conditions in this section trace directly back to two things covered earlier in this package: hay and chewing (dental disease, page 11) and vitamin C (scurvy, page 11). A vet will usually ask about both before anything else, for exactly that reason.

Vitamin C deficiency & dental disease

Vitamin C Deficiency (Scurvy)

What it is: guinea pigs can't produce their own vitamin C, so a diet or supplement routine that falls short of the 10 to 50 mg daily target (page 7) can lead to deficiency over time. Signs: lethargy, joint pain, a rough coat, and poor wound healing. Response: vet-guided vitamin C supplementation alongside a diet correction, not just adding more of whatever isn't working. Recovery: generally improves once adequate vitamin C intake is restored; a vet should also check for and treat any secondary issues, like slow-healing wounds, that developed along the way.

Prevention beats treatment Hitting the 10 to 50 mg daily vitamin C target consistently (page 7) prevents nearly all scurvy cases. It's the single highest-leverage nutritional habit in this whole package.

Dental Disease

Cause: overgrown teeth from insufficient hay intake or chewing. Guinea pig teeth grow continuously, and hay is what wears them down. Signs: drooling, weight loss, and dropping food while trying to eat. Response: a vet dental check and correction. This isn't something to manage at home once it's underway.

Concrete signal Drooling combined with dropping food or reduced eating is a dental-disease pattern until a vet rules it out. Don't wait to see if appetite "comes back on its own."

GI stasis & respiratory infections

GI Stasis

What it is: the gut slowing down or stopping. This is a real emergency, not something to monitor overnight. Signs: reduced or absent droppings, a hunched posture, and refusal to eat. Response: immediate vet visit. Don't wait for it to resolve on its own; a stalled gut can decline quickly.

Treat this as urgent Reduced or absent droppings combined with a hunched posture or loss of appetite means a vet visit now, not later today or tomorrow. GI stasis is one of the fastest-moving problems in this package.

Respiratory Infections

Signs: labored breathing, discharge, and lethargy. Response: a prompt vet visit; antibiotics are the typical treatment once a vet confirms it.

Don't wait it out Labored breathing or discharge is never a "give it a few days" situation for a guinea pig. Both conditions on this page move quickly, and both respond far better to early treatment than late treatment.

Bladder stones & ovarian cysts

Bladder Stones (Urolithiasis)

Signs: straining to urinate, blood in the urine, and vocalizing while urinating. Any one of these is worth a same-week vet visit; all three together warrant going sooner. Response: vet diagnosis, which sometimes leads to surgical removal depending on stone size and location.

Ovarian Cysts

Who this affects: intact (unspayed) females only. Signs: hair loss along the flanks and behavior changes. Response: a vet exam, which sometimes leads to a spay depending on findings.

Both are a "see the vet" situation Neither condition on this page is something to diagnose or manage at home. Straining to urinate, blood in urine, unusual vocalizing, or flank hair loss with behavior changes in an intact female are all reasons to book a vet visit.
If you're not planning to breed Ask a vet about the tradeoffs of spaying an intact female. It's a bigger decision than a routine checkup, but it's worth an informed conversation rather than defaulting to "wait and see."

First 30 days, and days 31 to 90

Week 1

Confirm floor space and temperature against the targets on page 14, not by estimate
Let your guinea pig settle: minimal handling, focus on feeding and observing appetite
Schedule a wellness vet visit within the first two weeks

Week 2 to 3

Begin short, gentle handling sessions if appetite and behavior look normal
Establish a consistent hay, pellet, vegetable, and vitamin C routine
Start the Owner Log on page 20

Week 4

Review weight, appetite, and droppings trends from the Owner Log
Check your vitamin C supplement's freshness or expiration date
Introduce first enrichment items from page 21

Days 31 to 90

Book the follow-up wellness visit most vets recommend after initial setup
Look back at the Owner Log for any slow drift in weight, appetite, or droppings
If housed solo, consider whether a companion makes sense; guinea pigs generally do better paired or grouped
Start rotating enrichment on the monthly schedule from page 21

Daily, weekly, monthly & seasonal routine

Daily

Refill hay so it's never empty
Feed pellets and fresh vegetables per the schedule on page 7
Refresh water and confirm the bottle or bowl is working
Quick visual health check: eyes, breathing, posture, appetite, droppings

Weekly

Spot-clean bedding
Weigh and log in the Owner Log
Check water bottle for clogs or leaks (Equipment Log, page 20)
Rotate or refresh one enrichment item

Monthly

Full bedding change and enclosure deep clean
Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings
Check vitamin C supplement freshness or expiration date

Seasonal

Reassess room temperature swings between summer and winter
In warm months, watch more closely for the 80°F ceiling; guinea pigs handle cold far better than heat
