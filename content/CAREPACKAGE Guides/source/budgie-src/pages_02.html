Section 02 · Housing & Environment

Cage size, bar spacing & placement

The purchase that is hardest to undo, and the one number that is a safety limit.

Size and shape

An absolute minimum for a single budgie is 18×18×18 in (46×46×46 cm). A wide flight-style cage serves a budgie far better: closer to 30×18×18 in (76×46×46 cm) for a pair, with 32 in (81 cm) or more of length a genuine improvement over the minimum. The cage sold as a "budgie starter cage" is usually a bare minimum at best.

Prioritize width over height. A budgie flies side to side in short bursts rather than climbing, so a tall, narrow cage wastes the exact space it would use, while a wide one gives it real flying room. A cage sized for a pair from the start stays the same cage if a second bird joins later.

Bar spacing: half an inch or less

A safety limit, not a preference Bars must be half an inch (1.3 cm) or closer. Anything wider risks a budgie squeezing through and escaping, or catching its head while it tries. Avoid round cages entirely.

Check the finish before buying. A cage that uses zinc or lead in its finish is toxic if the bird ingests it through normal chewing and preening, and galvanized wire is a zinc source (page {{P:hazards}}). Stainless steel or powder-coated hardware avoids the problem.

Perches, dishes & what to leave out

What goes in the cage, where, and what does not.

Perches: variety matters more than material

Provide a mix of diameters, textures and materials: natural branches such as manzanita, apple and willow, plus some rope perches for texture. A foot that has to adjust its grip through the day stays healthy. Smooth wooden dowels as the only perch give a foot one surface and one diameter, which contributes to arthritis and pressure sores over time. Skip sandpaper perch covers: they do not file nails effectively and can irritate the feet.

A bird that stands on rough, varied perches wears its nails down through normal use. One confined to smooth dowels or the cage floor needs trims more often (page {{P:grooming}}).

Layout for flight

A budgie cage has one flight path, end to end, and the layout either keeps it open or fills it. Toys go around the edges near the perches, never in the middle, because a swing or hanging toy in the center blocks the only flight the bird gets inside the cage. Put perches at opposite ends so the bird flies between them, and set the food and water dishes at opposite ends too, so eating and then drinking means a flight each time.

Dishes

Position food and water dishes away from perches so droppings do not end up in them. That small detail meaningfully cuts cleaning and health risk. A budgie shells its seed and leaves the husks behind, so a dish can look full while holding almost nothing: empty it rather than topping it up.

Cuttlebone or mineral block

Keep a cuttlebone or mineral block in the cage at all times. It supports calcium intake and gives the beak something appropriate to condition itself against. A budgie's beak never stops growing, and without something to chew it can overgrow and interfere with eating.

Leave these out Nest-like spaces. Any dark, enclosed hideaway reads as a nest site to a hen and is one of the triggers for chronic egg laying (page {{P:laying}}).
Mirrors. A bird can pair-bond to a mirror or a favorite toy strongly enough to trigger the same reproductive behavior a real mate would.
Zinc or lead. Galvanized hardware, and any metal toy or bell you cannot identify.
Sandpaper perch covers, and round cages.
Line the tray with plain white paper Plain white paper under the grate, changed every day, shows color changes in the droppings that a patterned liner hides, and it keeps droppings out of the bird's reach so they do not dry into dust it breathes.

Temperature, light & sleep

A budgie needs no heater or thermostat. It needs a steady room, the right light, and real darkness.
SettingTarget
Room temperatureAbout 65 to 85°F (18 to 29°C), with 70 to 75°F (21 to 24°C) ideal. Avoid drafts, direct sun and sudden swings. A budgie has no sweat glands and is more heat-sensitive than that range suggests.
Darkness10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted sleep a night, at about the same clock time every night
UV lightA budgie kept indoors away from unfiltered sunlight needs a full-spectrum UV (ultraviolet) light designed for birds, run 10 to 12 hours a day, inside the day length that leaves the hours of darkness
Sick or recovering bird75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C); the added warmth stimulates appetite and digestion

Why the dark hours matter

A bird reads day length as a season and uses it to decide when to go into breeding condition. A household that keeps the lights on from before dawn until 10 or 11 at night hands the bird an endless summer. A day longer than 12 hours is a documented risk factor for excessive egg laying, and a budgie hen is one of the small parrots most often seen as an emergency for reproductive disease. Birds under ongoing hormones are also less friendly, more aggressive and more vocal.

Sleep matters for the bird itself, too. Sleep deprivation sits alongside boredom and sexual frustration among the recognized contributors to feather picking, though medical causes still need to be ruled out first.

What counts as uninterrupted

"Uninterrupted" is doing real work. A bird that gets 11 dark hours but has a hallway light flick on twice, or a television glowing across the room, is not getting the same rest as one with 11 genuinely dark, quiet hours. A timer keeps lights-out consistent when the household's evening is not.

A cover on the main cage blocks light and is a simple support, but it does nothing about a television's sound or people walking through the room. A dedicated sleep cage in a quiet, dark room solves both: a smaller, simple cage with a perch and water, moved there at the same time every night.

Heat is the bigger risk than cold A budgie is overheating if it pants with its beak open, holds its feathers sleek and flat and holds its wings away from its body. Move it to shade or air conditioning, mist it with room-temperature water, never cold water, and call an avian vet. A bird that lives indoors should spend under 20 minutes in direct sunlight, with shade it can reach.

Air quality: fumes & airborne hazards

A bird's lungs make ordinary household air dangerous. Nothing else in this book has a shorter path from a normal act to a dead bird.

Air moves through a bird's lungs in one direction, pushed along by a system of air sacs that keeps oxygen flowing even on the exhale. That efficiency is the problem indoors: a bird pulls far more air, and far more of whatever is in it, through its lungs than a person in the same room does. The rule of thumb is simple: if you can smell it, it may harm your bird's respiratory tract. A small bird like a budgie goes down faster than a larger parrot on the same exposure, because it has less body mass to buffer it.

Nonstick cookware

PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) is the nonstick coating on many pans. Past 280°C (536°F) it breaks down into gases that cause direct, caustic damage to a bird's lungs. An empty pan on a hot burner, or one preheated longer than a recipe calls for, can cross that line in minutes without looking scorched or smelling wrong to a person nearby. Sudden death is often the only sign. When signs do appear they are rapid or labored breathing, wheezing, weakness and seizures.

PTFE is not limited to frying pans. Irons, ironing board covers, some self-cleaning ovens and certain heat lamps can carry it. Cook with stainless steel or cast iron, keep the cage out of the kitchen, run the exhaust fan during high-heat cooking, and never run a self-cleaning oven cycle with a bird anywhere in the house.

Metals, pets, plants & everyday traps

The hazards that are not fumes: slow poisons, other animals, and ordinary rooms.

Zinc and lead

Zinc poisoning, sometimes called "new wire disease," comes mainly from galvanized cage hardware. Chewing galvanized wire or metal toys has caused it, because zinc can leach off hot-dipped galvanized coatings without the bird swallowing a visible flake. Electroplated galvanized metal is considered safe; hot-dipped, the more common and cheaper coating, is not. Early signs are stomach upset and appetite loss, then weakness, pale gums and discolored droppings. Powder-coated or stainless steel hardware avoids the problem, and checking a cage's construction before buying beats treating poisoning afterward.

Lead works differently and is just as dangerous. Curtain weights, stained glass windows and lamps, costume jewelry and paint in homes built before 1978 are common sources. Lead tastes sweet, which is part of why a bird that finds it keeps chewing. Signs run from weakness and an upset stomach to circling, tremors and leg paralysis, and by then the bird is often seriously ill. Both metals respond to treatment if caught early, so walk the room for galvanized hardware, old paint and vintage glass before a bird has access.

A cat or dog bite is a same-day emergency Any mouth contact between a cat or dog and a bird needs a vet the same day, even with no visible wound. Cat saliva carries Pasteurella bacteria, which at a bird's body temperature can multiply fast enough to cause fatal blood poisoning within hours of a bite that does not even break the skin visibly through the feathers. A dog's grab adds crushing injury. A vet will typically flush any wound and start antibiotics at once rather than wait for lab results.

Open water, fans, windows and other animals

  • Water. Most pet birds do not swim well, so an open toilet bowl, a full sink or a pot of hot water is a drowning or scalding risk.
  • Ceiling fans. A flying bird cannot see spinning blades as an obstacle. A bird flies free only with the fans off.
  • Glass. Birds generally do not perceive windows and mirrors as barriers and can fly into them hard enough to cause real injury. Open doors and windows are a separate escape risk.
  • Other pets. Cats, dogs, ferrets and pet reptiles all carry a hunting instinct toward a bird however calm they seem. Out-of-cage time is supervised time, with the other animals out of the room.
  • The room. Before the cage door opens: ceiling fans off, windows shut, and nothing within reach that a chewing bird can hurt itself on.

Foods and plants within reach

A countertop within flight range of an out-of-cage bird is enough exposure to avocado, chocolate, onion or garlic (the full list is on page {{P:never}}). A bird that chews leaves out of curiosity cannot tell a safe plant from a toxic one, so check any houseplant against a current toxic-plant list before it comes into a room the bird can reach.

Cleaning & hygiene

The daily health check hides inside the daily chore.

Every day

The paper on the tray is there for daily cleaning, and changing it is also the daily health check: a budgie's droppings are the first place illness shows, so look at them before they go in the bin. It takes thirty seconds.

  • Fresh plain paper under the grate, and spot-clean dropped food.
  • Every food and water bowl emptied, scrubbed with soap and water and rinsed. Soaking in a disinfectant and rinsing again comes next.
  • Replace yesterday's food with one day's fresh portion. Empty the seed dish rather than topping it up.
  • Fresh produce comes out within a few hours.

A pair or a small flock soils the paper faster, so check it more often than a single bird needs. A bird that does not bathe on its own should be offered a bath or a misting a few times each week.

The deep clean

A full scrub of the cage follows an order. Every dropping and scrap of food comes off first with detergent, because disinfectant does little against organic debris. Then the disinfectant goes on, and most need 5 to 10 minutes of wet contact to work. Then the cage is rinsed in clean running water, every surface, and left to dry completely before it is put back together.

Fumes reach the bird fast The bird waits in a clean cage or travel cage in another room while the cage is scrubbed. The room being cleaned stays ventilated, and no disinfectant is ever mixed with another product. Many disinfectants irritate a bird's airway as much as a person's.

Dust and the people in the house

Dried droppings and feather dust lift into the air when swept. Spray the floor under the cage with water before sweeping, because a vacuum can throw the same particles around. Wooden perches and rope toys cannot be disinfected reliably, so after a sick bird they are thrown out instead of cleaned.

Psittacosis (page {{P:respiratory}}) can spread from birds to people. Wash your hands with soap and water after handling a bird or its equipment, and between handling one bird and another.
