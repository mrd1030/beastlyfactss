Health red flags & what to tell the vet

A budgie hides illness until it cannot. By the time it looks sick, it has been sick a while.

Prey animals conceal weakness because looking ill in a flock makes you the one the hawk picks. That instinct does not switch off in a cage, so a budgie that finally looks unwell is usually well into the problem rather than at the start of it. This page exists to move the moment you notice it earlier.

Same day, no waiting Sitting on the floor of the cage instead of perching.
Fluffed up and sleeping during the day, particularly on both feet rather than one.
Tail bobbing with every breath, or open-mouth breathing, or any audible click, wheeze or change of voice.
Not eating, or droppings that have stopped or shrunk sharply.
Bleeding from anywhere, including a broken blood feather.
A hen straining with a wide stance. Page {{P:eggbinding}}.
Any contact with a cat or dog's mouth, even with no visible wound. Page {{P:hazards}}.
Sudden collapse or seizure, especially if anything was cooking or spraying in the house.

Within a few days

  • Weight down more than about 10% off the bird's own baseline, or three weighings trending down.
  • A change in droppings lasting more than 24 hours: color, consistency, number or volume. Page {{P:droppings}}.
  • Sneezing, a wet or stained cere, or swelling around the eyes.
  • New lumps anywhere, particularly on the chest or belly.
  • Lameness, a foot that will not grip, or a bird favoring one side. This is not always an injury. Page {{P:liver}}.
  • Feather loss that is not symmetrical, or feathers that come away with sheaths still on. Pages {{P:viral}} and {{P:molt}}.
  • A crusty cere or beak, especially in a cock. Page {{P:minor}}.
  • Any change in an established behavior: a bird that always chatters going quiet, or a tame bird suddenly refusing to come out.

Normal values worth knowing

MeasureNormal
Body temperatureAbout 107°F (41.8°C). Far higher than a mammal, and not something you measure at home

Respiratory rate60 to 75 breaths per minute at rest, with no visible tail movement

What to have ready when you call The bird's current weight and its usual weight. How long the sign has been present. What it eats, in percentages rather than brand names. Whether anything was cooking, cleaning, spraying or burning in the house. Whether it has been out of the cage, and where. Whether any other bird in the house is affected. Take a photograph of the cage tray from this morning; droppings are diagnostic and they do not travel well. Write the vet's number and an after-hours number on page {{P:emergency}} today, not on the day you need them.

Obesity, fatty liver & tumors

The condition group that shortens more budgie lives than everything else on the site combined.

Obesity and hepatic lipidosis

Obesity in a bird is defined as roughly 20% over ideal weight, or a body condition score of 4 out of 5 on the keel. An obese budgie is at increased risk of fatty liver disease, in which fat accumulates in the liver and displaces working tissue, and also of atherosclerosis and heart disease. The cause is almost always the same: a calorie-dense seed diet in a cage that offers no flight. It is the single biggest preventable problem in pet budgies, and it is preventable by two things that are free, converting to pellets (page {{P:diet}}) and letting the bird fly (page {{P:mistakes}}).

SignWhat you notice
EarlyNothing visible. Weight creeping up on the scale is the only sign, which is the argument for the scale
EstablishedA rounded lower belly, the keel hard to feel, breathlessness after short flight, reluctance to fly at all
Liver involvementAn overgrown, soft or flaking beak, overgrown nails, poor feather color, green staining in the urates, and lethargy
AdvancedLabored breathing, abdominal swelling, and in some birds sudden death with no preceding illness
Weight loss is done slowly, and with a vet Crash-dieting a 30 g bird mobilizes fat into a liver that is already struggling, which makes it worse rather than better. Convert the diet, measure portions, add flight, and take the weight off over months while weighing weekly. A bird already showing an overgrown beak or green urates needs a veterinary work-up before any diet change, not after.

Lipomas and xanthomas

Lipomas are benign fatty growths, most often on the chest or belly, and xanthomas are yellow thickened areas of skin loaded with cholesterol, both associated with high-fat seed-heavy diets. A budgie with one is telling you about its diet. They are not usually urgent, but a growing lipoma interferes with flight and can ulcerate, and any new lump is worth a vet appointment rather than a decision made at home about what kind of lump it is.

Tumors, and the sign nobody expects

Budgerigars have an unusually high rate of tumors for a pet bird, and kidney tumors in particular are noted as common in this species. The presenting sign is often not a lump at all: a tumor sitting against the nerves that run past the kidney causes lameness or paralysis in one leg or both, because the nerve supply to the leg is compressed. A budgie that starts limping, drags a foot, or cannot grip a perch on one side is a vet visit, and it is a mistake to assume the bird knocked itself during a flight.

The cere is a tumor sign in cocks Reproductive tumors change the hormone balance, and in a mature male budgie one of the visible results is a cere shifting from blue toward brown. A hen's cere turning brown is ordinary breeding condition; a cock's is not, and it earns an appointment. Page {{P:sexing}} covers the normal version so you can tell them apart.

Respiratory disease & psittacosis

Fast-moving, and one of them is catching for people in the house.

Any breathing sign in a budgie is urgent. The respiratory system that makes a bird so efficient also leaves very little reserve, and birds that are visibly struggling to breathe have often been compensating for days. Tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, a click or wheeze, a change in the voice, and a tail that pumps at rest are all the same message.

The common causes, in rough order

CauseWhat points to it
Bacterial or fungal infectionOften secondary to poor air quality, chronic stress, or a diet-driven vitamin A deficiency. Sneezing, a wet or crusted cere, and nasal staining above the beak
Vitamin A deficiencySeed-fed bird. Sneezing, nasal discharge, swelling around the eyes, conjunctivitis, poor feather quality. Reverses with diet, but the infection on top of it still needs treating. Page {{P:diet}}
GoiterA stridor, wheeze or click with no discharge, caused by an enlarged thyroid pressing on the airway. Page {{P:minor}}
Inhaled toxinSudden onset, often in more than one bird at once, often with nothing else wrong beforehand. Page {{P:hazards}}
PsittacosisBelow
Non-respiratory causesAn abdominal mass, egg binding, or obesity can all present as breathlessness. Page {{P:liver}}

Psittacosis, or parrot fever

Caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci, and especially common in cockatiels, budgerigars and small parrots. In the bird the signs are non-specific: lethargy, nasal and eye discharge, weight loss, diarrhea, and characteristically lime-green or yellow-green droppings, because the organism attacks the liver. It is treatable. The treatment is doxycycline for 45 days, given orally or by injection, and the length is the point: a short course does not clear it and the bird relapses. Every bird in the house is treated, not just the sick one.

This one infects people Psittacosis spreads to humans by inhaling dried droppings and respiratory secretions, so cleaning a cage is the exposure. In people the incubation is 5 to 14 days and the illness starts abruptly with fever, chills, headache, aching muscles and a dry cough, and it can progress to pneumonia. Fewer than 1% of properly treated patients die, but it is frequently misdiagnosed because nobody mentions the bird. Tell your own doctor you keep a bird. When cleaning a cage during a suspected case, dampen the surfaces first so nothing goes airborne, wear gloves and an N95 respirator, and never dry-sweep or vacuum dried droppings.

What you can do before you get there

Keep the bird warm and quiet, around 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C), leave food and water within reach at perch height so it does not have to move to reach them, and stop any spraying, cooking or cleaning in the house. If a toxin is suspected, get the bird into fresh air first and call from outside. Do not give any human medicine, and do not use over-the-counter bird antibiotics sold without a prescription; they are the wrong drug at the wrong dose and they delay the visit that would have worked.

Polyomavirus, French moult & PBFD

Two viruses that both cause feather damage, and telling them apart matters.

Avian polyomavirus

This virus is so closely tied to this species that its other name is budgerigar fledgling disease. It hits young birds hardest. In unweaned chicks the presentation is an acute one: sudden lethargy, the crop stops emptying, and death follows within 24 to 48 hours, with signs appearing 7 to 10 days after exposure. Budgerigars that survive past about three weeks of age typically do not die of it but develop the feather form instead, which is where the old aviary name comes from.

French moult The feather form of polyomavirus. Flight and tail feathers are dystrophic: they fail to grow properly, break, or drop out, leaving a fledgling that cannot fly. Aviculturists have called these birds runners or, where the down persists without proper flight feathers, feather dusters. Many affected birds go on to live normal lives as non-flying pets, but the bird is a virus source for other birds in the house. A vaccine exists: manufacturers recommend the first dose once a chick is over 35 days old, with a booster 2 to 3 weeks later. That is a breeder's decision more than a pet owner's, and it is a fair question to ask the breeder you buy from.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

PBFD is caused by a circovirus and is the other feather-destroying virus people worry about. The species it is reported in most are African grey parrots, eclectus parrots, lovebirds, lorikeets and historically cockatoos, so it is a much less likely explanation for a scruffy budgie than polyomavirus is. When it occurs, the picture is abnormal feathers that are pinched or clubbed at the base and shed easily with slow or absent regrowth, beak lesions, and immune suppression that lets other infections in. Diagnosis is a PCR test on feces, feather dander or blood, and the test detects infected birds that look completely healthy, which is exactly why it belongs in a quarantine panel. Page {{P:quarantine}}.

The four-way differential on a bird losing feathers

CausePatternRegrowth
Normal moltSymmetrical, left matching right, new pin feathers pushing up as old ones drop. No bald skinNormal. Page {{P:molt}}
PluckingOnly where the beak reaches. Head and face untouched, chest and legs bare. Broken shafts left behindNormal if the behavior stops. Page {{P:plucking}}
PolyomavirusYoung bird, flight and tail feathers, often unable to fly at fledgingPoor or absent
PBFDProgressive over molts, feathers pinched or clubbed at the base, beak involved, head feathers affected tooSlow, abnormal, worsening

The line that separates the first two from the last two is the head. A budgie cannot reach its own head with its beak, so a bird with bare or abnormal head feathers is not plucking itself, and that finding moves the case from a behavioral problem to a veterinary one immediately. A second bird plucking the first is the exception, and it is why the pairing question on page {{P:flock}} matters here too.

Scaly face mites, megabacteriosis & goiter

Three conditions this species gets more than most, and all three are treatable.

Scaly face and leg mites

Caused by the mite Knemidocoptes pilae, and one of the few budgie problems visible from across the room. It produces white, porous, proliferative crusts at the corners of the mouth, on the cere and beak, and sometimes around the eyes, on the legs or at the vent. The honeycombed texture is characteristic. Left alone it deforms the beak permanently, so it is worth catching early, and it is contagious to other birds in the house.

Treatment Ivermectin at 0.2 mg/kg by mouth or by injection, or moxidectin at 0.2 mg/kg by mouth or applied to the skin, repeated after two weeks. This is a prescription from an avian vet at a dose calculated for a 30 g bird, not a pet-shop mite spray and not a dog product measured by eye. Clean and replace wooden perches and porous cage furniture while treating, and have any other birds in the house checked.

Megabacteriosis, or avian gastric yeast

Despite the old name, the organism, Macrorhabdus ornithogaster, is a yeast rather than a bacterium, and it is more common in small pet birds such as budgerigars than in anything else. It lives in the proventriculus and interferes with digestion. The classic picture is the "going light" bird: a budgie eating normally or even ravenously while steadily losing weight, sometimes regurgitating, sometimes passing whole undigested seed in the droppings. Mortality across affected birds runs anywhere from 10 to 80% depending on the bird and the strain, which is why the weekly weighing on page {{P:sexing}} earns its place: this disease is a falling number long before it is anything you can see.

  • Diagnosis is by finding the organism in a fresh dropping under the microscope, or by a PCR test on droppings. It sheds intermittently, so one clear sample does not rule it out.
  • Treatment with the best published success rate is amphotericin B at 100 mg/kg by mouth twice daily for 30 days. Shorter courses fail. Voriconazole at 10 mg/kg twice daily has been reported anecdotally.
  • During treatment the vet will recheck liver enzymes, bile acids and uric acid roughly every four weeks, because antifungal drugs can affect the liver and kidneys.

Goiter, from iodine deficiency

A seed diet is iodine-poor, and without iodine the thyroid enlarges. The gland sits at the base of the neck, right next to the airway, so the sign is respiratory rather than a visible swelling: a stridor, a wheeze or a repeated click when the bird breathes, sometimes with regurgitation as the enlarged gland presses on the crop and esophagus. It is much less common now that pellets are normal, and it remains one of the clearest arguments for them. Treatment is Lugol's iodine, one drop per 250 mL of drinking water, until the bird is converted onto a pelleted or properly fortified diet, which then fixes the underlying cause.

Overgrown beak and nails are a symptom, not a grooming task A healthy budgie on good perches and a cuttlebone maintains its own beak. A beak that keeps overgrowing points at liver disease (page {{P:liver}}), at scaly face mites, or at a past injury to the growth plate. Trimming it addresses the shape and not the cause, so a bird needing repeated trims needs a work-up. Never trim a beak yourself: it carries a blood supply and nerves.

Feather plucking & behavioral health

Rule out the medical causes first. Most owners work this list backwards.

Feather destructive behavior is a symptom with a long list of causes, and the mistake that costs the most time is treating it as a boredom problem from the start. Budgies pluck less often than the larger parrots do, so a plucking budgie deserves a proper look rather than another toy. Work the list in this order.

CheckWhat it looks like, and what to do
1. Is it plucking at all?Head feathers intact and only reachable areas bare means plucking. Symmetrical loss with pin feathers coming up means molt (page {{P:molt}}). Abnormal head feathers means a virus (page {{P:viral}})
2. MedicalSkin disease, mites, an internal problem causing pain over one spot, liver disease, and vitamin A deficiency all cause plucking. A vet visit with a skin and dropping work-up is step two, not step ten
3. SleepFewer than 10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness is a common and easily missed driver. Page {{P:light}}
4. HormonesChronic hormonal stimulation drives plucking around the vent and legs particularly. Page {{P:eggs}}
5. Air qualityCigarette smoke, aerosols, scented products and very dry heated air all irritate skin. Page {{P:hazards}}
6. Foraging and companyOnly now. Restore foraging, increase out-of-cage flight, and address the loneliness question honestly. Pages {{P:mistakes}} and {{P:flock}}
What the enrichment research actually supports Work on captive parrots links consistent environmental enrichment to less feather-destructive behavior compared with birds kept without it, and foraging opportunities specifically stand out as one of the higher-impact changes available. That is a real finding and it is why page {{P:mistakes}} leads with foraging rather than with toys. It is not, on its own, a treatment for a bird that is plucking because it is ill.

Other behavior worth reading

  • Pacing the same stretch of perch repeatedly. Usually a cage that is too small or too empty, sometimes frustration at something visible outside it.
  • Screaming beyond contact calls. Budgies chatter constantly and that is normal. Persistent screaming is usually a learned behavior that gets rewarded by someone returning to the room, and it is unlearned by returning before it starts rather than after.
  • Regurgitating onto a mirror, a toy or a person. Affection, in budgie terms, and a hormonal signal. Remove the object. Page {{P:eggs}}.
  • Head bobbing and beak tapping in a cock. Courtship display, entirely normal, and a signal that the bird is in breeding condition.
  • Sudden fear of a person the bird liked. Something changed: glasses, a hat, a hair color, a nail color. Budgies are strongly visual and it usually resolves in days.
Never use a bitter spray or a collar as a first move Both treat the behavior while the cause continues, and a collar on a bird this small interferes with feeding and balance. They have a place, prescribed by a vet, for a bird injuring its own skin. They are not a starting point.

Molt & seasonal behavior

Normal, uncomfortable, and misread as illness roughly every time it happens.

A budgie replaces its feathers gradually rather than all at once, and a molting bird looks worse and behaves worse than a healthy bird should. Knowing what a normal molt looks like saves an unnecessary vet visit, and more usefully it stops an abnormal one from being dismissed.

Normal moltNot a molt
Symmetrical. Feathers lost from the left match the rightBare patches only on one side, or only where the beak reaches
Pin feathers pushing up before the old ones drop, so no bald skin showsBald skin visible, or feathers that never regrow
Pins are gray-blue shafts with a waxy sheath the bird flakes off by preeningFeathers pinched or clubbed at the base. Page {{P:viral}}
The bird is quieter, sleeps more, and is touchier about being handledThe bird is fluffed, on the cage floor, or off its food. Page {{P:redflags}}
Head pins are itchy and a paired bird will preen them for its partnerHead feathers missing in a single bird. That is not self-inflicted

Helping through a molt

  • Bathe more often. Water softens the sheaths and makes preening them off easier. Page {{P:light}}.
  • Feed properly. Feathers are protein, and a molt is the most expensive thing a budgie's body does. It is the worst moment to be on a seed diet.
  • Handle less, and expect grumpiness. Pin feathers on the head are genuinely tender, and a bird that bites during a molt is not regressing.
  • Never pull a pin feather. They carry a blood supply while growing.
  • Keep the room warm and draft-free. A part-feathered bird loses heat faster.
A broken blood feather bleeds seriously A growing feather has a blood supply, and a snapped one can bleed enough to matter in a bird this size. Apply firm gentle pressure with a clean cloth or a little cornstarch to the base of the feather, keep the bird calm and warm, and get to a vet the same day. Do not pull it out yourself unless a vet has told you to and shown you how.

Seasonal behavior

Budgies breed opportunistically rather than to a fixed calendar, which is why an indoor bird under household lighting can come into condition at any time of year and often does. Expect chattier, more territorial, more restless behavior when a bird is in condition, and in hens a browner, thicker cere. It is normal biology. It only becomes a problem when the environment keeps a hen in that state, which is the entire subject of page {{P:eggs}}. If seasonal behavior in your house is really year-round behavior, look at the light before anything else.

Reading droppings & hydration

The cheapest daily health check you have, and the reason the cage tray gets plain paper.

A bird dropping is three things at once, and separating them is what makes the check useful. The fecal part is the solid green or brown coil. The urates are the chalky white portion, uric acid, which is how a bird excretes nitrogen while conserving water. The urine is the clear liquid. Each of the three changes independently and each means something different, which is why "the droppings look wrong" is not a useful thing to tell a vet and "the urates have turned green" is.

ComponentNormalWorth acting on
FecesFirm, coiled, green to brown. Color follows the food, so pellets with dye give reddish or orange feces and blueberries give purple for a few hoursMushy or semi-liquid fecal material is true diarrhea and needs a vet. Black or tarry suggests bleeding higher up. Bulky and pale, or undigested seed passing through whole, suggests the gut is not absorbing. Page {{P:minor}}
UratesChalky whiteGreen or yellow urates point at the liver, and lime-green is the classic psittacosis picture. Page {{P:respiratory}}. Red or brown discoloration can mean blood, which raises lead among other things. Page {{P:hazards}}
UrineA small clear haloA large watery halo with the fecal part still formed is polyuria, not diarrhea. It can be a normal response to stress or to a lot of watery fruit, and it can be kidney disease or infection
Number and sizeSteady, day to day, on the same dietFewer or smaller droppings means less food is going in, whatever the dish looks like. In a hen, none at all with straining is an emergency. Page {{P:eggbinding}}
Polyuria and diarrhea are not the same thing Owners report both as "wet droppings" and the difference decides how worried to be. Look at the solid part. If the fecal coil is still formed and there is simply a lot of clear liquid around it, that is polyuria. If the fecal material itself is mushy or liquid, that is diarrhea, and diarrhea in a budgie is a same-week vet visit at the latest. Any abnormality that persists beyond 24 hours is worth a call.

How to actually do the check

Plain paper on the tray, changed every morning. Glance at it while you change it: number, size, and the three colors. That is the whole routine and it takes five seconds. Loose litter and grit paper both make it impossible, which is the real reason page {{P:furnishings}} rules them out. When something looks wrong, photograph the paper before you throw it away. A photograph travels to the vet; a description does not.

Hydration

Budgies come from an arid environment and are efficient with water, so dehydration is not obvious. The signs are a bird that is quiet and fluffed, a tacky rather than moist mouth if a vet checks it, and skin over the eyelid or the toes that is slow to spring back when gently tented. Sunken eyes are late. Wet vegetables contribute real water, which is one more argument for the fresh share of the diet, and a bird that has stopped eating has usually stopped drinking too. Never try to syringe water into a budgie's mouth at home; a bird this small aspirates easily and it kills.

Quarantine & adding a second bird

Thirty days of inconvenience against a disease that can take the whole household.

Every bird that enters your home is quarantined, with no exceptions for a bird that looks perfectly healthy, came from a good breeder, or is only visiting. Looking healthy is the specific thing this species is good at, and the two diseases that spread fastest between pet birds, psittacosis and polyomavirus, both have carriers with no signs at all. The public health guidance is to isolate newly acquired birds, including birds that have been to shows, exhibitions and fairs, for 30 to 45 days, and to test or prophylactically treat them before adding them to a group.

RuleWhy
30 to 45 daysTake 30 as the floor and 45 if you already keep birds. Longer is better and shorter is not quarantine
A separate room, door closedIdeally on separate air. The main routes here are airborne dust and dried droppings, so the same room with a sheet between the cages achieves nothing
Separate everythingDishes, perches, toys, cleaning cloths, and the tray liner. Nothing crosses between the two rooms and goes back
Established birds first, the new bird lastFeed, water and clean for the resident birds before going anywhere near the new one, every single day. Then wash your hands and change your shirt
A vet visit during quarantineThe point of the 30 days is to use them. A well-bird exam plus a PCR panel covering psittacosis, polyomavirus and PBFD is the version that actually protects the household
Weigh every few daysA new bird losing weight in quarantine is the earliest signal you will get. Page {{P:sexing}}
The single-bird household is not exempt If this is your first bird, quarantine still applies, because psittacosis infects people (page {{P:respiratory}}). A new bird's first month is when it is likeliest to be shedding, and it is the month when a new owner handles it most and cleans the cage least confidently. Wash hands after handling, dampen the tray before you lift the paper, and do not kiss a bird on the beak, however much it invites it.

After quarantine: the introduction

  1. Cages side by side, a few feet apart, for a week or more. The birds will call to each other, which is the whole point. Watch for interest rather than alarm.
  2. Cages closer, then adjacent. Beak contact through bars is fine. Toes through bars is where a fight does damage, so keep an inch of gap.
  3. Neutral ground first. A play stand or a table, never inside the resident bird's cage. A budgie defends its cage and will meet the same bird peaceably on a perch it does not own.
  4. Shared cage last, and supervised. Two food dishes, two water dishes and two of every favorite perch. Most resource fights are about there being one of something.

Chasing, beak fencing and a bit of shouting are negotiation. Grabbing a foot, pinning a bird down, or one bird unable to reach food are fights, and they mean separate the birds and go back a step. If the pairing is a cock and a hen, read page {{P:eggs}} before they share a cage, not after.
