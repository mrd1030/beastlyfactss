Section 03 · Feeding

Diet: pellets, seed & grit

Where a lot of budgie care goes wrong, and the biggest lever on how well a budgie lives.

Put a seed mix in front of a budgie and it works the bowl over for the millet, leaves most of the rest, and by the end of the week the dish looks full while the bird has eaten one thing. The trouble with a seed mix is not one missing nutrient. It runs high in fat and carbohydrate, low in protein, and short on vitamins and minerals generally, and selective eating makes the real intake narrower than the label suggests. A seed-only diet is directly linked to malnutrition, obesity and fatty liver disease.

How much a day

The measured part of a budgie's day is the seed. Alongside pellets that are available all day, the seed allowance is about 1 level teaspoon per bird per day. A budgie still eating a seed diet gets 1.5 to 2 level teaspoons a day, in a shallow dish. With more than one bird, each gets its own dish so the bird at the bottom of the pecking order still eats. Seed left in the dish at the end of the day means too much went in, and it is the leftover that lets a budgie eat its millet and skip everything else.

Pellets are not rationed by the spoon. A gram scale shows what the bird really eats: weigh the food going in, weigh what is left 24 hours later, and average the difference over seven days. That average is the bird's real daily intake and the amount to keep offering.

Skip the grit

Grit is sold beside budgie seed and it is one of the few products actively worth avoiding. The logic behind grit applies to birds that swallow seed whole and need help grinding it. A budgie shells the seed and eats the kernel, leaving the husk behind, so grit has no job to do, and a bird that eats it in quantity can end up with a dangerous gastrointestinal obstruction.

The iodine problem Many seeds are low in iodine, the raw material for thyroid hormone, and budgies are unusually prone to thyroid trouble compared with other pet birds. The result is goiter, which sounds like a breathing noise rather than looking like a swelling (page {{P:respiratory}}). Formulated diets carry iodine in adequate amounts, which is why the diet itself is the prevention.

Converting a seed eater to pellets

A few weeks of mild inconvenience, if it is done slowly. A real emergency risk, if it is rushed.
Never starve a bird onto pellets A bird raised on seed may not recognize a pellet as food, and if the seed disappears before it does, some birds simply go without eating. A budgie should never go more than 12 hours without eating: its fast metabolism leaves a narrow margin.

Two schedules

Both substitute gradually while the bird keeps guaranteed access to the old food. They were written for conures, but the mechanism is the same for any strongly seed-imprinted parrot.

MethodSteps
Stepped75% seed and 25% pellets for three days, then 50/50 for three days, then 25% seed and 75% pellets until the bird is fully converted.
Tapered90% seed and 10% pellets on day one, 80/20 on day two, and so on, down to 0% seed on day 10. A bird that balks partway holds at 20% seed and 80% pellets until it converts.

If the bird stalls, hold at that ratio and let the timeline stretch: days, weeks or months are all normal.

What helps with a budgie

  • Offer pellets first thing in the morning, before any other food, when the bird is hungriest and most willing to try something new.
  • Scatter them on a flat surface such as a shallow tray rather than a bowl. Foraging across a surface is closer to how a budgie eats anyway, and tapping a fingertip among them gets a hesitant bird started.
  • Use a moist food as a bridge. Grind pellets to powder and mix it into a small amount of a favorite moist food, such as cooked egg, mashed vegetable or soft fruit, then coarsen the pellet and reduce the moist food over time.
  • Pull the seed only once the bird is eating pellets alongside fresh vegetables and fruit.
  • Keep a second kind of pellet on hand. A bird that refuses one for weeks may take another within days.

Check these every day

Vegetables, fruit & fresh food

The 20 to 25% that pellets do not cover, weighted toward vegetables rather than fruit.

Vegetables carry what seed does not, and a budgie introduced to them early takes them far more readily than one meeting them at three years old. The portion is small: a thumbnail-sized amount of any fresh food is the equivalent of a dinner-plate serving for a budgie. It comes out of the cage after a couple of hours, sooner in a warm room, before it spoils.

GroupSafe choices
Vegetables and greensAsparagus, beet, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, carrot, corn, cucumber, dandelion leaves, endive, kale, peppers, pumpkin, romaine, spinach, squash, sweet potato, tomato, zucchini

Choose by color The bright yellow, red and orange foods carry the most vitamin A: bell peppers, broccoli, carrots, sweet potatoes, squash, mango, papaya and cantaloupe. They are the ones worth offering most often.

Little nutrition

Iceberg lettuce is mostly water with little nutritional value, and celery stalks are high in water with few nutrients. Birds like the crunch, but neither earns a regular place, because they displace better food rather than adding anything.

Washing

Birds are very sensitive to pesticides, chemicals and sprays. Wash all produce well and offer organic where you can. Produce from a garden that has been sprayed is off the list.

Getting a suspicious bird to try something new

A budgie that refuses a new vegetable has not decided anything yet. Offer the same fruit or vegetable every day for 3 to 5 days before giving up on it. Scattering food on a flat surface, as in the conversion, works for fresh food too, since it matches how a budgie forages.

The clock

Fresh food goes in when you get up and comes out a couple of hours later. Water needs to be there, clean, all the time. Dishes and bottles want washing daily with soap and water and a thorough rinse, because a dish that has held vegetables and droppings for a day is not somewhere a bird should drink.

Treats, supplements & the never-feed list

The smallest part of the diet, and the part where the real dangers are.

Treats

Millet spray is the treat that gets overfed, because every budgie prefers it to everything else on offer. Small quantities once or twice a month is the allowance. It is also the most useful taming reward there is (page {{P:handling}}), so break a spray into short pieces and count them. Honey sticks are not recommended at all by most avian vets.

Cuttlebone and supplements

A cuttlebone or mineral block belongs in the cage at all times, for calcium and for the beak. A bird eating 75 to 80% of its diet as pellets does not need vitamin supplements. A bird eating mostly seed is the one that does need a proper vitamin and mineral supplement, and any supplement is a vet's decision, not a guess.

Never feed Avocado. Every part of the plant contains persin, a compound with documented cardiac toxicity in birds, and smaller birds, budgies and canaries specifically, tend to be more sensitive. Respiratory distress typically shows up about 12 hours after eating it, and death is possible within one to two days.
Chocolate. Theobromine and caffeine cause hyperactivity, tremors and potentially seizures. Darker, more bitter chocolate carries more risk.
Caffeine and alcohol, in any amount.
Onion and garlic. They contain chemicals that may harm a bird's blood cells, especially in concentrated forms like garlic powder or dry soup mix.
Very salty food, processed food, and anything cooked with butter or oil.
Pits Cherries go in with the pit removed. A countertop within flight range of an out-of-cage bird is enough exposure to any of the foods above, so a bird does not need a dramatic accident to be poisoned.

The seed dish trap

A budgie shells its seed and leaves the husks behind, so a dish can look full while holding almost nothing. The tidy-looking, barely touched seed dish is the single most misleading sight in the cage. Empty the husks out before deciding the bird has food, and expect a bird still on seed to need the conversion on page {{P:convert}} rather than a bigger dish.

Weight, body condition & the daily scale

A budgie hides illness under its feathers. Weight is the vital sign that does not.

A gram scale catches what your eyes and hands cannot. Weight loss shows up on a scale days to weeks before a bird looks visibly thin, sick or fluffed up, because feathers hide body condition extremely well. A bird that finishes its seed dish every day can still be quietly losing body mass, which is exactly how avian gastric yeast works (page {{P:mites}}).

WhatTarget
Typical adult weight25 to 35 g. Your bird's own settled weight is what matters, because a healthy weight for one frame is not automatically healthy for another.
How oftenEvery day, at the same time, before breakfast, on the same scale. Write the number down.
ScaleA kitchen scale that reads in grams, not ounces. The swings worth catching in a small bird are often a handful of grams. A perch-style scale a bird can stand on naturally makes the habit easier to keep.
During a diet changeDaily without fail. A loss of more than 1 to 2% in a week means the change is too fast; more than 10% below the starting weight means call the vet.
ObesityRoughly 20% over the bird's ideal weight, with a body condition score of 4 out of 5

A trend, not a number

A single weight reading tells you almost nothing. A logged trend, taken at the same time of day on the same scale, tells you everything. A vet asking what the bird normally weighs is not making conversation: the difference between 34 g today and 38 g a month ago is a diagnosis, and a bird with no history is a guess. The Owner Log on page {{P:ownerlog}} is built for it.

Feeling the keel

Weight alone does not say whether that weight is muscle, fat or an empty crop. Body condition scoring in birds is built around one landmark, the keel: the blade-like extension of the breastbone down the center of the chest, where the flight muscles attach on either side. It is assessed by feel, not by eye, because feathers make a visual check nearly useless. The scale runs from 1 (emaciated) to 5 (obese).

What the finger findsWhat it points to
A subtle ridge, with muscle rounded out on either sideGood muscle condition
A sharp, prominent keel with little padding either sideUnderweight
A keel you can barely find, cushioned in fatOverweight
Do not chase a number The value of the keel check is tracking the same bird over time. A keel that has gotten sharper since last month is the signal. The familiar nine-point scale used for dogs and cats does not transfer to birds at all.
