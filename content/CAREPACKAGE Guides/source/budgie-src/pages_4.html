Setup checklist & targets

Print this one. Audit an existing setup against it, or buy against it.

Housing

Cage at least 18×18 in (46×46 cm) in footprint; 20×20×30 in (51×51×76 cm) or wider is the target
Bar spacing measured at 3/8 to 1/2 in (10 to 13 mm)
Rectangular, not round; stainless or powder-coated, not galvanized
Against a wall, at or near eye level, in a room the family uses
Not in or adjoining the kitchen. No exceptions
Out of all-day direct sun and off any draft
Cover or sleep cage available for 10 to 12 hours of darkness
Tray lined with plain paper, changed daily

Inside the cage

Perches of varied diameter and texture, natural wood among them
No perch directly above a food or water dish
A clear flight line between two perches
Cuttlebone or mineral block, always available
Three to five toys, at least two of them destructible
A foraging feeder or paper wrap in daily use
No nest box, happy hut, tent or enclosed cavity
No mirror
No frayed rope or loose fiber anywhere
No grit or gravel

Diet and equipment

Budgie-sized formulated pellets, 60 to 80% of the diet
Fresh vegetables offered daily, 20 to 25% with fruit
Seed limited; millet only in small quantities, once or twice a month
Two sets of dishes, washed daily, water changed daily
Digital gram scale, reading in single grams
Travel carrier, ready before you need it
Avian vet identified, number written on page {{P:emergency}}
Nonstick cookware audited, aerosols moved out of the bird's air

Targets at a glance

Room temperature70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C), no drafts, no sudden swings
Darkness10 to 12 hours, uninterrupted, every night
Weight25 to 35 g, stable against the bird's own baseline, weighed weekly
Out-of-cage flightDaily, supervised, in a bird-proofed room
VetA well-bird exam on arrival, then a wellness check every 6 to 12 months

That is above what most starter guides quote, and the difference is the gram scale, the carrier, the sleep cage and a real vet line, which are the four items that change outcomes. Section 05 · Owner Tools

First 30 days checklist

In order. Most of week one is doing less than you want to.

Before the bird arrives

Cage built, sited and furnished, running for a few days
Bar spacing measured, not assumed
Avian vet found and the number written down
Quarantine room ready if you already keep birds. Page {{P:quarantine}}
Nonstick cookware audited, aerosols relocated
Ask the seller what the bird is currently eating, and buy some of it

Days 1 to 7: leave it alone

Food, water and cleaning only. No handling, no reaching in beyond the dishes
Sit near the cage and talk. Let the bird watch the household happen
Keep the same food it arrived on. Change one thing at a time, and not this week
Check droppings every morning. A new bird's droppings are the first thing to go odd. Page {{P:droppings}}
Establish the sleep routine on day one: 10 to 12 hours of darkness, same time nightly
Weigh at the end of the week if you can do it without a chase. Write the number down

Days 8 to 14

Well-bird exam with the avian vet, whether or not anything looks wrong
Hand resting inside the cage, doing nothing, in short sessions. Page {{P:handling}}
Start offering vegetables daily, clipped to the bars. Expect refusal for a while
Introduce a foraging feeder alongside the normal dish, not instead of it

Days 15 to 30

Begin the pellet conversion if the bird came on seed. Weigh daily through it. Page {{P:diet}}
Millet from the fingers, then the step-up cue once food is taken calmly
Bird-proof one room properly and take the first flight session there. Page {{P:hazards}}
Weekly weighing established and logged on page {{P:ownerlog}}
Toy rotation started: swap two of the three to five in the cage
End of quarantine, if applicable, and the introduction sequence begins
Change one thing at a time A new cage, a new diet, a new room and a new person in the same fortnight gives you no way of telling which change caused the problem when one appears. Space them out. The bird is not in a hurry.
What normal looks like by day 30 Eating and drinking visibly, droppings consistent, chattering, preening, sleeping on one foot with the head tucked, and grinding the beak quietly when settling down at night. Beak grinding is contentment and it worries every new owner exactly once.

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Ten minutes a day, half an hour a week, and an hour a season.

Every day

Change the water and wash the dish
Fresh pellets; blow the husks off any seed dish before judging it full
Vegetables in, and out again a few hours later
Change the paper on the tray and read the droppings
Look at the bird properly: posture, breathing, feathers, both feet gripping
Supervised out-of-cage flight in a bird-proofed room
Interaction: talking, training, or a session on the hand

Weigh, same time of day, and log it

Section 05 · Owner Tools

Power outages, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in a blackout is usually what people light, not the cold.

A budgie does not depend on powered equipment the way a reptile or a fish does. There is no heater to fail and no filter to stall. What actually kills birds during an outage is the response: candles, kerosene and propane heaters, generators run too close to the house, a gas oven left open for warmth, and a fireplace lit in a room that has not been swept. Every one of those puts combustion products into the air of a small animal with an exceptionally efficient respiratory system.

During an outage No candles, no kerosene or propane heaters, no gas oven as a room heater, no generator anywhere near the house. Use flashlights and battery lanterns. If the only available warmth is a combustion source, the bird goes to a friend's house or to a boarding facility instead, and that is not an overreaction.

Keeping a budgie warm without power

  • Move the cage to the smallest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Cover the cage on three sides with blankets, leaving one side open for air. A covered cage holds the bird's own heat well.
  • Keep feeding. A 30 g bird burns through its reserves in hours, and a bird that is cold needs more food, not less. This is the opposite of the advice for a reptile.
  • Hot water bottles wrapped in towels, placed outside the cage, are safe. Chemical hand warmers are not: some release fumes, and none should be inside a cage a bird can chew.
  • A healthy budgie tolerates a cool house better than most owners expect. Draft and damp are worse than temperature.

Travel and transport

SituationWhat to do
Vet tripSmall carrier, paper towel lining, no tall perch to fall off. Food and water at floor level. Cover it. Keep the car warm and the vents off the carrier
A long driveThe carrier is belted in and never in the trunk. Offer water at every stop. Do not open the carrier inside the car, ever
Moving houseThe bird travels with you, not in the removal van. Set the cage up in a quiet room at the new house before anything else is unpacked, and expect a week of settling
HolidaysA budgie should not be left alone for more than 24 hours, even with full dishes. Water fouls, a dish tips, a bird gets stuck. Use the sitter sheet on page {{P:sitter}}
FlyingAirline rules for live birds vary widely and change often, and some destinations require permits. Check with the airline directly, months ahead, and expect a health certificate to be required
Never leave a bird in a parked car A car interior heats fast enough to kill a budgie in minutes, and it is the single commonest way a routine vet trip goes wrong. In cold weather the same car chills just as fast. The bird comes with you or it stays home.

Glossary

Terms used earlier in this book, in the sense they were used.
Blood featherA feather still growing, with a blood supply running into the shaft. Breaking one bleeds seriously in a bird this small
CereThe fleshy patch above the beak holding the nostrils. Blue in adult cocks, tan or brown in hens, pink or pale in juveniles
Chlamydia psittaciThe bacterium that causes psittacosis, or parrot fever. Infects people as well as birds
CropA storage pouch at the base of the neck where food waits before digestion. A crop that stops emptying is a serious sign
Dystrophic featherA feather that has grown abnormally: bent, clubbed, pinched at the base, or failing to emerge
French moultThe chronic feather form of avian polyomavirus in budgerigars, leaving a fledgling unable to fly
GoiterAn enlarged thyroid, from iodine deficiency. Presents as a breathing noise rather than a visible swelling
Hepatic lipidosisFatty liver disease. Fat accumulating in the liver, most often from a seed diet
KeelThe ridge of breastbone down the center of the chest, used to score body condition by feel
Knemidocoptes pilaeThe mite that causes scaly face and scaly leg in budgies
LipomaA benign fatty tumor, usually on the chest or belly, associated with high-fat diets
Macrorhabdus ornithogasterAvian gastric yeast, once called megabacteria. The classic cause of a budgie eating well and losing weight
MoltThe ordinary, gradual replacement of feathers. Symmetrical, with pin feathers rising before old ones drop
MonomorphicA species whose sexes look alike. Budgies are not: the cere sexes them, which is unusual among parrots
Pin featherA new feather still in its waxy sheath. Tender, and preened out by the bird or its partner
PolyuriaToo much liquid urine, with the solid fecal part still formed. Different from diarrhea, and often confused with it
PhotoperiodHours of light per day. The main lever on hormonal behavior and egg laying
PBFDPsittacine beak and feather disease, a circovirus causing progressive feather and beak damage
PTFEPolytetrafluoroethylene, the nonstick coating. Releases a gas lethal to birds at around 280°C (536°F)
PsittacineOf the parrot family. Budgies are small psittacines, not finches
QuarantineIsolating a new bird in a separate room, on separate equipment, for 30 to 45 days
SalpingohysterectomySurgical removal of a hen's reproductive tract. The definitive answer to repeated egg binding
StridorA harsh breathing noise caused by narrowed airway. In budgies, classically goiter
UratesThe chalky white part of a dropping: uric acid, how a bird excretes nitrogen while conserving water
XanthomaA yellow, thickened, cholesterol-laden patch of skin. Another sign of a high-fat diet
