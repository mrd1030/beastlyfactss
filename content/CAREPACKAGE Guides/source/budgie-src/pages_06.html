Red flags & finding a vet

A budgie hides illness until it cannot. By the time it looks sick, it has been sick a while.

Birds are prey animals, and hiding illness is a survival instinct that does not switch off in a cage. A budgie that finally looks unwell is usually well into the problem. This page exists to move the moment you notice it earlier, and finding an avian vet before you need one makes a real difference.

Same day: a vet within 24 hours, not a few days of watching Fluffed-up feathers combined with sleeping during the day, particularly standing on both feet.
Tail-bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, or any click, wheeze or change of voice.
Sitting on the floor of the cage instead of perching.
A hen straining, sitting low, or with no egg passed in 48 hours.
Not eating, or droppings stopped or sharply reduced.
Bleeding that has not stopped after two to three minutes of pressure.
Any mouth contact from a cat or dog, even with no wound.
Sudden collapse or a seizure, especially if anything was cooking, spraying or burning in the house.

Within a few days

  • Weight trending down over several weighings, or a loss of 10% from the bird's own baseline.
  • A change in the droppings that lasts more than 24 hours: color, consistency, number or amount.
  • Limping, dragging a leg, a weak grip or falling off the perch, which is often not an injury.
  • A cere that has changed color in a mature male, or a crusty, honeycombed cere or beak.
  • A new lump anywhere, especially on the chest or belly.
  • Abnormal feathers on the head, which a bird cannot do to itself.
  • Any change in an established behavior: a chatterer gone quiet, a tame bird refusing to come out.

Normal values worth knowing

Body temperatureAbout 107°F (41.8°C). Far higher than a mammal's, and not measured at home.
Heart rateRoughly 274 beats a minute at rest
Breathing60 to 75 breaths a minute at rest
Weight25 to 35 g, stable against the bird's own baseline
DroppingsOne about every 20 to 30 minutes, with a larger one first thing in the morning

Finding and using a vet

The vet should be an avian vet, at minimum a member in good standing of the Association of Avian Veterinarians, and one who sees budgies routinely. Its find-a-vet search lists members by location. When something is wrong, have ready today's weight and the usual weight, what the bird eats, how long the sign has lasted, and a phone photo of the droppings: a single odd dropping described accurately is far more use than "it looked off yesterday too." Write the vet's number and an after-hours number on the emergency card today, not on the day you need them.

Obesity, fatty liver, lipomas & tumors

The condition group that diet drives, and one that diet does not.

Fatty liver disease and obesity

Driven almost entirely by all-seed diets. Seeds are calorie-dense and nutritionally incomplete, and over time an all-seed diet leads to obesity and hepatic lipidosis, a fatty liver condition serious enough that it can cause sudden death. It is the single biggest preventable health issue in pet budgies. A pellet-forward diet from the start prevents most of it. Obesity means roughly 20% over the bird's ideal weight, a body condition score of 4 out of 5 on the keel (page {{P:weight}}).

An overgrown beak and overgrown nails together can be a sign of liver disease, which is why a beak that keeps growing back needs a vet workup, not another trim.

Lipomas and xanthomas

Benign fatty growths, also linked to high-fat, seed-heavy diets. Budgies are notably prone to tumors compared with many other pet birds, which is another point in favor of a pellet-forward diet. Any new lump is worth a vet visit rather than a guess at home about what kind of lump it is.

Tumors, and the sign nobody expects

Budgies have an unusually high tumor rate for a small bird, and kidney and gonadal (testicular or ovarian) tumors show up often enough to deserve their own explanation.

A limp may not be an injury A budgie that suddenly starts limping, drags a leg, grips the perch weakly on one side or falls off its perch is often not injured. Kidney and gonadal tumors sit right next to the nerve that supplies the leg, and a growing mass presses on it. A leg problem can be the first visible sign of a tumor you cannot see from outside the bird.

The pressure on the sciatic nerve (the nerve that supplies the leg) causes lameness in the leg on the affected side, and the leg itself looks completely normal, so it is easy to mistake for a sprain or a fall. Other signs to watch for alongside it include abdominal swelling, general weakness, fluffed feathers, and weight loss even when the bird is still eating normally, since a tumor can grow for a while before appetite drops off. A change in a mature male's cere color is another clue.

Because the mass is not visible from outside, diagnosis needs imaging rather than a hands-on exam alone: usually X-rays to check kidney size and density, sometimes an ultrasound or a look inside the body with a small camera, and blood work. The outlook is not good once a tumor like this is found. There is no surgical option for a mass in this location on a bird this small, and no treatment that cures it, so avian vets shift toward pain control and supportive care aimed at comfort. Getting imaging early does not change that outlook much, but it does rule out the more treatable causes of a limp first.

The takeaway Feed for the liver, weigh for the trend, and treat a limp as a vet visit.

Breathing problems, psittacosis & goiter

Fast-moving, and one of them is catching for people in the house.

Any breathing sign in a budgie is urgent. A bird has very little respiratory reserve, and one that is visibly struggling has often been compensating for days. Tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, a click or wheeze, and a change in the voice are all the same message: a same-day vet visit.

Goiter

The thyroid needs iodine to make its hormone, and a bird eating mostly seed runs short. The gland enlarges and, because it sits where the windpipe branches toward each lung, presses on the airway and the voice box. The sound comes from the airway rather than the voice. Other signs can include vomiting and a visibly engorged jugular vein, and a bad case may hold its head up to keep the airway open. It builds slowly: the noise grows louder over months, and a keeper who hears it daily stops noticing.

A mild case corrects with supplemental iodine, and a severe one needs a vet, hospitalization and injections. A thyroid tumor can look exactly like goiter; the difference is that goiter resolves quickly once iodine arrives and a tumor does not. The fix is the diet itself.

Psittacosis

Psittacosis, or parrot fever, is caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci, and it is zoonotic, meaning it can spread to people. Signs in the bird are respiratory symptoms, diarrhea, weakness and an enlarged liver, and the droppings can turn lime green in both the stool and the urates. Some infected birds look healthy and shed the bacterium only intermittently, which is why a bird that looks clean on day 10 of quarantine can still test positive later.

  • Treatment is the antibiotic doxycycline for a confirmed case, typically 45 days, or 30 days for a budgerigar.
  • If you suspect it, mention the possibility to your vet directly, and keep any newly introduced bird separate from the rest of your flock.
  • For people, cleaning a cage is the exposure. Spray surfaces with water before sweeping so dried droppings do not lift into the air, and wash your hands after handling birds or equipment.
While you get to the vet Keep the bird warm, around 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), keep food and water within reach, and stop any spraying, cooking or cleaning in the house. If a toxin is suspected, get the bird into fresh air first and call from outside.

Polyomavirus, French molt & beak and feather disease

Two viruses that both leave a bird with feathers that look wrong, and the budgie is the species the first one is named for.

Avian polyomavirus

This virus causes disease in young parrots, and in budgies it is called budgerigar fledgling disease. It is two diseases wearing one name, and the gap between them is mostly the bird's age at infection.

  • Unweaned chicks: signs appear 7 to 10 days after exposure: lethargy, and a crop that stops emptying. Death follows within 24 to 48 hours. Mortality can reach 100% in birds under 15 days old, and many juveniles die quickly with no signs at all.
  • Survivors older than three weeks often develop feather dystrophy, known as French molt or feather dusters. Down feathers around the head and neck grow poorly, and the contour and flight feathers are abnormal. A bird with stubby or missing flight feathers that cannot fly is sometimes called a runner. It keeps that abnormal feathering rather than replacing it with a normal coat.

Adult birds are typically resistant to the disease itself but can shed the virus for up to 90 days before clearing it, with no signs. A bird that has recovered can remain a carrier. The virus travels in feather dust, droppings, urine and secretions, and budgerigars have one route no other affected species has: passing from an infected parent straight into the egg. That is why a clean-looking breeding pair is not proof of anything, and why a new bird goes through full quarantine (page {{P:quarantine}}).

A vaccine exists for selected parrots. Its documented schedule for chicks starts after 35 days of age with a booster 2 to 3 weeks later, and breeding birds get two doses two weeks apart in the off-season. That 35-day start was documented in cockatiels and Quaker parakeets, not budgie chicks, so the schedule for a given bird is a vet's decision and a fair question to ask a breeder.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

PBFD (psittacine beak and feather disease) is caused by a circovirus that attacks the fast-growing skin cells behind the beak and the feather follicles, so both grow visibly wrong. It occurs mainly in parrots native to Africa, Australia, New Zealand and Indonesia. Feathers look stunted, club-shaped or pinched off, changing symmetrically across the body; the beak can grow long, flaky or oddly shiny; and feathers are lost even where the bird cannot reach. It can occur at any age but is most common in birds under three. There is no cure and no vaccine, and most affected birds eventually die of secondary infections. It spreads in feather dust, dander and droppings.

Scaly face mites, canker & avian gastric yeast

Three conditions this species gets more than most, and the first two are treatable.

Scaly face and leg mites

Caused by the mite Knemidocoptes pilae. Look for crusty, honeycomb-like buildup on the cere, the beak and the legs, and sometimes around the eyes and the vent. It is treatable with ivermectin from an avian vet, but it needs a proper diagnosis rather than a home remedy.

Trichomonas (canker)

Trichomonas is the parasite behind canker. It causes vomiting, sticky head feathers and crop swelling, and it is a common cause of death in young budgies. Some avian practices test every new budgie for it as routine, so ask for it at the first exam.

Avian gastric yeast, the "going light" disease

Avian gastric yeast, once called megabacteriosis, is a yeast, Macrorhabdus ornithogaster, not a bacterium. It sits where the true stomach meets the muscular gizzard and disrupts the acid environment a bird needs to digest. Budgerigars, parrotlets, lovebirds, cockatiels and finches are the small pet birds most often affected, and budgies carry the longest history with it. The term "going light" for a chronic wasting pattern comes from decades of watching this exact picture.

Eating normally and losing weight A bird with this yeast can keep eating on schedule, sometimes even eating more, and still lose ground by the gram, because it digests less of what it swallows. Other signs are regurgitation, lethargy, diarrhea, and undigested seed passed whole in the droppings. The only reliable early catch is the daily gram scale. An uncommon acute form has been documented in budgerigars in otherwise good condition that collapse and die within 12 to 24 hours, usually from bleeding inside the stomach, so any of these signs plus sudden weakness is a same-day visit.
  • Diagnosis is a fresh dropping examined under a microscope, sometimes with a stain, and PCR at some laboratories. The yeast sheds on and off, so one negative result does not clear a bird, and a vet may want droppings checked on more than one day.
  • Outlook varies widely: mortality runs from 10 to 80% of affected birds, depending on the bird's condition and the strain.
  • Treatment is oral amphotericin B twice a day for a minimum of 30 days, the protocol with the highest success rate. Failures are common when the course is shorter, and the most common reason is stopping early because the bird looks better. Expect a recheck fecal exam during the course and another after it ends, because a bird can look recovered while still shedding.
  • Reducing risk: the yeast shows up alongside stress and immune suppression. Quarantine and fecal-check every new bird, keep cages and dishes genuinely clean, and weigh daily.

Feather plucking & feather loss

Rule out the medical causes first. Most owners work this list backwards.

A bird missing feathers sends most owners straight to the scariest answer. Before anything else, compare what you see against a normal molt (page {{P:molt}}), then use the one built-in physical limit of plucking: a bird can only chew, pull or over-preen feathers its own beak can reach.

What the feathers look likeWhereFirst move
Normal moltWhole feathers shed gradually, new pins coming inScattered across the bodyBathe or mist; let it run
PluckingChewed at the skin, ragged tips, or pulled outOnly where the bird can reach; the head is sparedVet visit to rule out a medical cause, then environment and behavior
PBFDStunted, club-shaped or pinched, changing symmetrically; beak flaky or overgrownIncludes places the bird cannot reachAvian vet diagnosis; no cure
Polyomavirus (French molt)Dystrophic flight and tail feathers in a young birdWing and tail; down on the head and neckAvian vet diagnosis
The head tells you A bird with bald or damaged feathers on its chest, back or under its wings but a full set on top of its head is very likely doing it to itself. A bird losing feathers from the top of its head while everything else looks fine is not plucking, because it cannot reach that spot. A bird preening a partner's head, or scratching its own on a cage bar, is doing normal maintenance.

Work the list in this order

  1. Is it plucking at all? Use the table above.
  2. Medical. Skin inflammation or infection, wider disease, cancer, malnutrition and toxins can all produce the same picture, and environmental changes only help once those are treated or ruled out. A vet visit is step two, not step ten.
  3. Sleep. Sleep deprivation sits alongside boredom and sexual frustration among the recognized contributors. Protect 10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness (page {{P:climate}}).
  4. Hormones. Sustained hormonal stimulation brings territorial aggression, screaming and feather-destructive behavior.
  5. Foraging and company. Restore foraging, increase out-of-cage time, and address loneliness honestly (page {{P:flock}}).

How common is it in budgies?

In a Japanese survey of 2,331 pet parrots (Ebisawa and colleagues, 2021), 11.7% showed feather-damaging behavior, but only 4.9% of budgerigars did, against 30.6% of cockatoos. Adults were about three times as likely as juveniles to do it, and birds showing signs of separation anxiety had 1.81 times the odds. Hours spent alone and having a cage mate did not independently predict it once species and age were counted, so the quality of the bird's relationship with its people appears to matter more than a count of hours. Plucking in a budgie still deserves a proper look rather than another toy.

Reading droppings & water

The one health check that costs nothing and happens by itself every 20 to 30 minutes.

A normal dropping is three separate outputs mixed together, because a bird's digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts all empty into one compartment and leave through one shared opening, the vent. The feces are the solid, formed part, green or brown. The urates are the white or cream, chalky part: uric acid, the solid form of urine, which lets a bird excrete nitrogen while conserving water. The urine is the clear liquid that rings the rest of the dropping.

PartNormalGenuine red flag
FecesGreen to brown, shifting with diet, sometimes red or orange on colored pelletsBlack or tarry, bright red or blood-tinged, or watery with no formed stool
UratesWhite to creamBright yellow or lime green, especially if the stool turns lime green too
UrineClear, a small ringLarge volumes of clear liquid on every dropping, or a marked drop in how often the bird goes
TextureSolid, curled, holds its shapeUndigested whole seed, pea-soup consistency, or bubbles

What the red flags can mean

  • Lime-green urates and stool together point to psittacosis (page {{P:respiratory}}), a common cause of liver disease in birds.
  • Undigested seed or pellets in the droppings can mean avian gastric yeast, which damages the stomach enough to block digestion even while the bird keeps eating (page {{P:mites}}).
  • Bloody urine turning droppings red or black can mean lead poisoning (page {{P:hazards}}).

Polyuria is not diarrhea

Polyuria is extra clear liquid around an otherwise normal, formed stool. If the solid part is still there and still shaped, it is excess urine, commonly triggered by stress, a diet change, or a lot of watery food like fresh fruit, and on its own it is usually not an emergency. True diarrhea is the fecal part itself turning mushy or semi-liquid, with no formed stool left, and it shows up alongside several serious conditions. One wrinkle: polyuria without an obvious dietary cause can be the first sign of kidney disease from zinc poisoning.

The 24-hour rule Once you know your bird's normal, any deviation counts, and if it lasts longer than 24 hours it is a same-day call to an avian vet. Call sooner for blood in a dropping, black or tarry droppings, a bird that has stopped producing droppings anywhere near its normal frequency, or any change paired with a fluffed-up, quiet or off-food bird.

Building the habit, and water

Line the tray with plain white paper, swap it daily, and glance at it at the same time. Note anything unusual with a date, even as a phone photo. Water must be there, clean, all the time, in a dish washed daily.
