Setup checklist & targets

Print this one. Audit an existing setup against it, or buy against it.

The cage

At least 18×18×18 in (46×46×46 cm); about 30×18×18 in (76×46×46 cm) for a pair
Bars half an inch (1.3 cm) apart or closer; not round
Stainless or powder-coated; no zinc or lead in the finish (page {{P:hazards}})
Against a wall, at or near eye level, off drafts and out of direct sun
Not in or next to the kitchen
Cover or a quiet sleep cage for 10 to 12 hours of darkness
Plain white paper on the tray, changed daily
Full-spectrum UV light made for birds, if no unfiltered sunlight (page {{P:climate}})

Inside the cage

Perches of varied diameter, texture and material
Dishes away from perches, at opposite ends from each other
Cuttlebone or mineral block, always available
Toys around the edges, a clear flight path, chewable items rotating
A foraging feeder or paper wrap in daily use
No nest-like hideaway, no mirror, no sandpaper perch cover
No grit

Diet and equipment

Pellets 60 to 80% of the diet; vegetables with a little fruit 20 to 25%
Seed about 1 level teaspoon a day; millet in small quantities, once or twice a month
Fresh water, dishes washed daily
Gram scale reading in single grams; weigh daily
Travel carrier and first aid kit ready
Avian vet found; number on the emergency card
Nonstick cookware replaced, aerosols and scented products out of the bird's air

First aid kit

Styptic powder (cornstarch or flour as a backup)
Small nail clippers
Clean gauze squares
A small hand towel kept for this
A small flashlight or phone light
Avian vet and emergency clinic numbers

Emergency & quick targets

Call an avian vet now if The bird is on the cage floor, fluffed, or sleeping in the day · tail bobbing with each breath, or open-mouth breathing · bleeding that does not stop in two to three minutes · not eating, or droppings stopped · a hen straining, sitting low, or no egg passed in 48 hours · any mouth contact from a cat or dog · collapse, a seizure, or a suspected fume exposure (fresh air first) · a sudden limp or a foot that will not grip.

First 30 days

In order. Most of week one is doing less than you want to.

Before the bird arrives

Cage built, stocked and sited before pickup day (page {{P:enclosure}})
Avian vet found and the number written down (page {{P:quarantine}})
Quarantine room ready if you already keep birds
Nonstick cookware replaced, aerosols and candles moved out (page {{P:air}})
Gram scale, carrier and first aid kit bought
Pellets, vegetables and a cuttlebone in the house

Days 1 to 7: let it settle

Cage at eye level against a wall. Food, water and cleaning only; no taming yet
Look at the droppings every morning; they are the first thing to go odd (page {{P:droppings}})
Start the sleep routine on day one: 10 to 12 hours of dark, the same time nightly (page {{P:climate}})
Weigh before breakfast every day and write the number down (page {{P:weight}})

Days 8 to 14

First avian exam, within one to two weeks of arrival, whether or not anything looks wrong
Hand resting in the cage, doing nothing, in short sessions (page {{P:handling}})
Offer the same vegetable daily for 3 to 5 days before giving up on it (page {{P:veg}})
Add a foraging feeder alongside the normal dish, then move part of the ration into it

Days 15 to 30

If the bird came on seed, begin the pellet conversion, weighing daily (page {{P:convert}})
Millet in short pieces from your fingers, then the step-up cue once food is taken calmly
Check one room for hazards and take the first out-of-cage session there (page {{P:hazards}})
Rotate the toys weekly; replace worn ones monthly (page {{P:enrichment}})
End of quarantine at 30 to 45 days, if you keep other birds; then the introduction stages (page {{P:flock}})
What normal looks like by day 30 Eating and drinking, droppings consistent, chattering, preening, sleeping on one foot, and grinding its beak quietly at bedtime, which is contentment.

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Ten minutes of care, an hour of company, and a few jobs that keep the rest working.

Every day

Weigh before breakfast; log it (page {{P:weight}})
Change the paper; read the droppings (page {{P:droppings}})
Fresh water; wash the bowls
Empty the seed husks; one day's seed in a shallow dish
Fresh pellets; vegetables in, and out again after a couple of hours
Look at the bird properly: posture, breathing, feathers, both feet gripping
An hour out of the cage in a checked room, and a short training session
Cover the cage or move to the sleep cage at the same hour

Every week

Rotate the toys; hide food a new way
Offer a bath or misting a few times
Look at nails, cere and beak

Every month

Replace worn toys
Deep clean the cage, with the bird in another room (page {{P:cleaning}})
Review the weight log as a trend
Re-check the room: new candles, sprays and cookware arrive quietly (page {{P:air}})

Every year

Wellness exam, twice if you can; replace worn perches

Power outages, heat, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in a blackout is usually what people light, not the cold.
The opposite of the reptile plan A budgie is warm-blooded and carries almost no fat between meals. During an outage, keep food and water in front of it the whole time, checked and refilled by hand if anything depends on power. A bird that goes off food during a stressful outage is a reason to call the vet, not to pull the bowl.

During an outage

  • No candles, no kerosene or propane heater, no gas stove or oven as a heater, in any room the bird is in. Flashlights and battery candles beat open flame.
  • A generator runs outside, at least 20 feet (about 6 meters) from doors, windows and vents, and never in or near a garage, house or shed. An open window is not enough.
  • Keep a working carbon monoxide detector in any room with gas heat, a gas range or a nearby generator. People need levels above about 70 parts per million before they feel anything, and a bird has no such buffer.
  • For warmth, cover most of the cage with a thick towel or blanket, leaving a gap for airflow, and move it to the warmest interior room, away from exterior walls and windows. A wrapped hot water bottle goes near the cage, never touching it.

Hot weather

A bird indoors should spend under 20 minutes in direct sunlight, with shade it can reach. An overheating bird pants with its beak open, feathers sleek and flat, wings held away from its body. Fluffed feathers are the opposite signal: cold or sick. Move it to shade or air conditioning, mist it with room-temperature water, never cold, and call an avian vet on the way.

Travel and the carrier

What to do
The carrier, not the cageA compact carrier with a low, secured perch or a padded floor, belted in or strapped, in the back seat away from an active airbag. A full cage's shape and dish placement do not secure well.
Stress and waterCover part of the carrier with a light, breathable cloth, and keep one side open for air. Loose toys and swings come out before a trip. Skip the water dish for the drive and offer a slice of fruit or a leafy green.
Never a parked carA cabin can climb 20°F in 10 minutes and 50°F in an hour, so a 75°F day puts it near 95°F by the time a quick stop becomes a checkout line. In cold weather it chills just as fast. The bird comes in with you.
Direct sunA carrier on a sunny seat offers no shade at all. Keep it out of direct sun through the windows.
