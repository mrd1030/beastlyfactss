Choosing a budgie

Most of what makes or breaks a bird-owning household is decided before the bird comes home.

The commitment

A budgie lives 7 to 15 years, which is a shorter commitment than a larger parrot's, not a short one. Match that span to your own life stage and your household's long-term stability, not just to how appealing or affordable the bird is today. Be honest about daily time too: a bird needs real out-of-cage interaction, not just a full dish. The bird itself is the cheap part of owning one (page {{P:budget}}).

Hand-raised or parent-raised

A hand-raised bird is pulled from the nest partway through development and fed by a person. A parent-raised bird stays with its parents through weaning and meets people afterward. Hand-raised birds usually step up more readily, tolerate handling better and bond with people faster. That tameness is not free: Schmid, Doherr and Steiger (2006), studying African grey parrots, linked hand-rearing to more frustration-related behavior disorders, including aggression and feather picking, and to more dependence on people. Most hand-raised birds live fine lives, and the study was in greys, not budgies. A parent-raised budgie takes longer to tame but starts from a more typical developmental baseline.

Where to get one

SourceAsk
BreederTo see the parent birds; whether the chick was hand-raised or parent-raised, how old it was when it left the nest, and whether it is weaned; about diet and health issues in their line. Visit in person.
Pet shopA shop's birds come from several breeders, which mixes health histories. Ask how long the bird has been in the store, whether a vet has examined it, and whether new arrivals are quarantined.
RescueA first option, not a fallback. Ask about the bird's history and any known behavior problems before taking it home.

Assessing the bird in front of you

Watch the whole cage before you focus on one bird. A healthy budgie tracks movement, responds to sound, and interacts with its cage mates or surroundings. Its feathers lie smooth and it moves around rather than sitting motionless in a corner. Age shows in the face: full forehead bars and solid dark eyes mean a young bird, which is the easiest to tame.

Do not take this one home A bird that stays fluffed up and still for an extended stretch, sits with its eyes half or fully closed during normal waking hours, ignores activity around the cage, or does not react when you approach and tap gently on the bars is not the bird to take home that day, whatever the price or the selection. If one bird looks off compared with the others, ask the seller what they have seen.
The vet visit is part of the purchase A bird can look fine in a cage and still carry something nobody saw. The first exam also gives you a baseline to compare against later.

Quarantine & the first vet visit

A new bird can look completely normal for weeks while carrying something that would spread through a flock in days.

How long, and where

Thirty days is the floor, not a suggestion. It applies to every newly acquired bird, including one that has been to a show, and to a household with no other bird yet. Plan on 30 to 45 days in a separate, isolated room while the bird is screened. For a multi-bird household or aviary, run closer to 90 days, because an infected adult can shed polyomavirus intermittently for up to 90 days.

RuleWhy
A separate room with its own door, kept shutIsolation means separate air space, not a second cage in the same room
Its own dishes, perches, toys and cleaning toolsA shared dish, sponge or scoop can carry bacteria or a virus across the gap
Established birds first, the new bird lastHealthy before isolated, every day. Reversed, what is on your hands reaches the birds you are protecting.
Wash your hands between birdsWith soap and water, or change gloves. A smock kept for the room helps: dander and droppings travel on clothing.
Medicine is not a shortcut Routine preventive antibiotic treatment is discouraged: it can harm the bird and encourage resistant bacteria. Doxycycline is for a confirmed case of psittacosis (page {{P:respiratory}}), not for skipping quarantine.

The first exam

It belongs inside quarantine, within one to two weeks of bringing the bird home, so results are back before the new bird meets anyone. Expect a weight, a head-to-tail physical, and then tests chosen from there:

  • Blood work: a complete blood count and a chemistry panel.
  • A fecal check for intestinal parasites, and a Gram stain for abnormal bacteria or yeast; swabs of the mouth and vent. Whole-body X-rays are sometimes added.
  • Screening for psittacosis, polyomavirus, avian bornavirus and PBFD (psittacine beak and feather disease), as the bird's history calls for. A PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test can detect infection in a bird that still looks completely healthy, and because shedding comes and goes, one negative result is not a clean bill of health.
  • Two budgie extras worth asking for by name: trichomonas, the parasite behind canker, which is a common cause of death in young budgies, and the yeast behind going light (page {{P:mites}}). Scaly mites, if present, show on the cere and legs during the physical.

Finding the vet

At a minimum, the vet should be a member in good standing of the Association of Avian Veterinarians, whose find-a-vet search lists members by location. Choose one who sees budgies routinely, and ask how many the practice sees. Find the vet before the bird comes home. After the first visit, one checkup a year is the floor and two is the stronger schedule. The first exam costs $45 to $65, and tests add to it.

Sexing by cere & the color exceptions

One of the few parrots you can sex by looking, with limits.

The cere is the fleshy patch above the beak where the nostrils sit. In a budgie it is one of the most useful pieces of information a keeper has, for working out sex and for noticing when something is off. In standard-colored birds it works well enough that keepers trust it without DNA testing.

CereWhat it usually means
Blue to purple-blue, a bright deep true blueA mature male
Tan, brown or pale white; thicker and rougher in breeding conditionA mature female
Pink or pale purpleA juvenile of either sex. Adult color usually settles between 6 and 12 months, so a very young bird cannot be sexed by cere.
Pinkish lavender at any agePossibly a pastel or solid-color mutation. The cere stays this color for life in those varieties, so the bird could be either sex.

The blue-for-males rule holds for standard blue and standard gray budgies. For anything else, the answer is a DNA test, not another month of waiting. Anyone buying to pair should get that test before trusting a seller's call. Blood is the more accurate sample; a feather is easy to collect at home and mail in.

A hen in breeding condition

A female's cere responding to hormones is normal biology, not a symptom. More daylight, a mate or nesting material can trigger a browner, thicker, sometimes crusty cere that smooths out once she is out of that state.

When a cere change is a warning

Context matters more than the color alone. A brown, crusty cere in a female is usually breeding condition. The same change in a mature male is a different story.

What you seeWhat it can mean

The reverse happens too, when an ovarian tumor gives a lifelong-brown female a sudden blue cere. Lameness in one leg often travels with these tumors (page {{P:obesity}}).

Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

A single hen with no male in the house can lay, and repeated laying is what harms her.

A hen does not need a mate or even another bird in the house. Give a single female budgie the right combination of light, food and a spot that feels like a nest, and her body will build and lay eggs on its own schedule, clutch after clutch. Budgerigars, cockatiels and lovebirds are the hens most often seen as emergencies for chronic laying and egg binding.

Typical clutch4 to 6 eggs on average
IncubationAbout 18 days, shorter than the 21 to 28 days quoted for parrots in general

What chronic laying costs her

Each clutch drains the calcium used to build eggshells. The low blood calcium that follows shows as weakness, lethargy, brittle bones that can fracture during normal handling, egg binding, seizures and death. After months or years of laying, the oviduct itself can become diseased and produce abnormal or soft-shelled eggs, and a hen under sustained hormones is often less friendly, more vocal and more aggressive.

The triggers: take away as many as you can

Chronic laying is almost always several triggers stacked together.

  • A long day. A day over 12 hours is a documented risk factor. Cut it back first (page {{P:climate}}).
  • Abundant, unlimited rich food. A steady surplus mimics the conditions for raising a wild clutch.
  • A nest box or nest-like site: a dark enclosed hideaway, a cupboard, a drawer, an unwatched corner of the cage floor.
  • A mate, or something treated as one: a mirror, a favorite toy, or the owner.
  • Petting. A hand running down the back toward the tail reads to a hen as a mate's contact. While she is laying, keep touch to the head and neck and shorten sessions.
Do not pull the clutch Removing each egg as it appears prompts her to lay more to replace it. Leave her to sit on the eggs for a complete incubation, which for a budgie is about 18 days. At the end, remove the eggs gradually, roughly one every one to two days, rather than all at once, which can restart the cycle the same way. Take away the nest-like furnishings, mirrors and favorite toys at the same time.

Light, diet and the vet

Cutting daylight or room light to 8 hours a day, or 8 to 10 as a short-term measure, can reduce the stimulus to lay. It is a managed intervention to discuss with an avian vet, not a permanent setting, and it takes weeks; the light is lengthened gradually afterward. A pelleted diet with proper calcium is standard advice for a chronic layer, because seed-based diets are short on calcium, selenium and vitamins D and E, and a cuttlebone supports the calcium side.

For a hen that keeps cycling anyway, an avian vet has hormonal options. Leuprolide acetate by injection stops laying for only three to four weeks, so it is repeated on a schedule. A deslorelin implant under the skin lasts three to six months or longer. Neither replaces removing the triggers. A hen that lays heavily, is thin and slow to recover between clutches deserves a vet visit before anything gets stuck.

Egg binding

A hen who cannot pass an egg has hours, not days. This is the page to know by heart.

Egg binding is an egg that forms but fails to pass and lodges in the oviduct. It is the point where chronic laying stops being a management problem and becomes a same-day emergency. Small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg-bound, as the trapped egg compresses circulation and presses on the air sacs. A hen kept alone can bind, so the risk exists whether or not you breed her. Calcium and vitamin D deficiency and obesity are the links, and diet and light are the prevention.

StageWhat to watch for
EarlyDepression, closed eyes, sitting low or on the cage bottom, and no egg passed in 48 hours by a hen that may be laying
ProgressingWeak and not perching, straining as if to lay or defecate, noticeable abdominal swelling
Advanced: act nowA bobbing tail and labored breathing, an egg bulging at the vent or a prolapse, or a hen unable to stand or grip the perch, because the egg presses on the nerves to her legs
What to do, in order 1. Call an avian vet now, the same day and ideally the same hour. There is no home remedy that safely dislodges a bound egg, and waiting for tomorrow's appointment is never the right call.
2. Keep her warm on the way. A sick bird recovers best at 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), so a warm room and a covered carrier are the sensible target. A loose perch or toy comes out of the carrier, and a low, secured perch or a padded floor is safer.
3. Keep food and water within reach. A budgie has almost no energy reserve to spare.

What the vet will do

Mildly affected birds may respond to supplemental heat, injectable fluids, calcium, vitamin D3 and oxytocin, a hormone that makes the oviduct contract and helps push the egg out. In a more severe case the vet may pass a needle through the vent and into the eggshell to draw out the contents, or remove the egg surgically.

Preventing the next one

  • A nutritionally complete, pelleted diet, and a cuttlebone or mineral block always available.
  • Light limited to 8 hours daily when a hen is cycling, and a quiet, dark sleep every night.
  • No nest-like furnishings, no mirror, and no petting down the back.
  • Weight kept in check: obesity is one of the predisposing causes.
One clutch is normal A single unbred hen laying an egg with no male anywhere near surprises many new owners. It is a normal reproductive quirk of the species. It is the repeating that is the medical problem.

Molt & seasonal changes

Normal, uncomfortable, and mistaken for illness often enough to learn.

What a normal molt looks like

Birds normally lose and replace most of their feathers at least once a year, and many also run a smaller partial molt about six months after the main one. Timing is not fixed: nutrition, sunlight, day length and humidity all move it. The defining feature is that it is slow and gradual, and the feathers that come out are whole and undamaged, not shredded or snapped off partway.

New feathers start as pin feathers, growing feathers encased in a waxy sheath that stays soft while the feather is growing and supplied with blood, then dries and flakes away as it matures. Most birds work the dry sheaths loose through ordinary preening. Molting is also demanding: the pin feathers can be uncomfortable, and an otherwise easygoing bird can turn cranky or short-tempered. A bird that suddenly does not want to be handled is usually just having a grumpy molt.

A normal moltPast a normal molt
Slow, with whole, undamaged feathers shed graduallyBald skin appearing quickly, or feathers that look chewed, pinched or stunted
New pin feathers coming in as old ones drop; no bald skinMissing patches, or loss from a spot the bird cannot reach, such as the top of its head
Scattered across the bodyLoss only where the beak reaches
A quieter, touchier birdA bird fluffed on the cage floor, off its food, or unwell

Helping through a molt

  • Bathe or mist regularly. Raised humidity keeps skin from drying out and encourages the preening that works sheaths off. A shallow cage bath left in a few times a week during a heavy molt is a simple way to offer it.
  • Do not pull a pin feather. A growing one carries a blood supply, and the steps for a broken one are on the grooming page.
  • Expect grumpiness and handle accordingly. A bird that fusses when you try to help a pin feather that is not ready is behaving normally.

Seasonal changes

Day length is the seasonal signal a budgie reads, and an indoor bird under household lighting can be fooled into breeding condition at any time of year. Expect a browner, thicker cere in a hen and more territorial, cage-protective behavior in a bird in breeding condition. It becomes a problem only when the environment keeps a hen in that state, which is the whole subject of page {{P:laying}}: look at the light before anything else.

Heat is the other season to plan for. A parked car can climb 20°F in 10 minutes and 50°F in an hour, and a bird is never left in one (page {{P:outage}}).

Wing feathers grow back at the molt A clipped flight feather regrows the next time that feather is molted out, so the molt is also when a trim stops working. Which month that is cannot be predicted from the calendar.
