The vertical enclosure & where it goes

18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) is the minimum for one adult, with the 24 in as height, and taller is genuinely better: a 2×2×2 ft (61×61×61 cm) enclosure is the common upgrade. A crested gecko is arboreal, so height matters more than floor space. Front-opening glass or PVC (polyvinyl chloride, a rigid, waterproof plastic) holds humidity better than a top-opening tank.

Hatchlings under about 12 to 13 g do better in a smaller grow-out enclosure first, where they find food easily and you can find them. A 12×12×12 in (30×30×30 cm) terrarium is the standard hatchling size. Move up to the adult enclosure once the gecko passes that weight and is feeding reliably.

Never House two males together: they fight badly enough to injure or kill. Don't group hatchlings either, which bite each other's tails, and those tails never grow back. One gecko per enclosure. Even two females need at least double the space of an 18×18×36 in (46×46×91 cm) enclosure, plus their own dishes and hides.

Where it goes

  • Out of direct sun. A glass box in a sunny window traps heat, and for this species too much heat is a bigger danger than too little.
  • Out of the kitchen and away from anywhere food is made or served.
  • In a room that sits at 72 to 78°F (22 to 26°C) by day. Many homes already do, which is why many setups need no heat at all.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Height only counts once it is furnished. A tall enclosure with a bare, empty vertical space is missing the part the gecko uses most.
  • Anchor every branch and piece of cork. A perch that collapses mid-jump is a fall, or a dropped tail.

The ceiling is the whole story

A crested gecko's home forest in New Caledonia only reaches 79 to 85°F (26 to 29°C) even in the hot season, and prolonged exposure above that risks fatal heat stroke. Too much heat is the bigger practical danger with this species, and it catches out keepers coming from a bearded dragon or a leopard gecko. Cold is the milder failure: temperatures at or below about 72°F (22°C) for long stretches, day and night, bring on lethargy and a dropped appetite.

If the room runs cool

Add a low-wattage heat bulb, 25 to 40 W, in a dome over one part of the screen top, so the top branches reach the warm range and the floor stays cooler. Keep every lamp at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest point the gecko can reach, with the screen between them. A heat bulb alone drifts, so any heat source runs on a thermostat. A dimming or proportional thermostat, which eases the power down as the target nears instead of switching fully on and off, is the type that holds a ceiling this strict. If the room already holds the range, no heat source is needed, and then no thermostat either.

The probe and the gauges

  • The probe reads at the gecko's level, on or beside the top branches, never up near the bulb, and wherever your controller's manual puts it. Hold it with a probe clip or suction cup, never tape: tape over a probe throws off the reading, and a gecko can stick to exposed adhesive.
  • A digital probe thermometer and hygrometer (humidity gauge), never a dial gauge. Read the top of the enclosure daily: that is the number the ceiling applies to.
  • Plan for the failure. A probe that slips keeps showing an accurate reading for the wrong spot, so check its position at every spot clean. A thermostat stuck on has no ceiling at all: a thermometer apart from the probe that reads well above the thermostat's display is the warning.
  • Test first. Run the whole setup for a day or two before the gecko goes in, and confirm the top reading stays under 85°F (29°C) through the warmest part of the day.
Any reading over 85°F (29°C) Turn the heat source off now, then find out whether the bulb, the thermostat or the room did it. In summer the room itself can do the heating, and a thermostat can switch a bulb off but cannot cool anything. The heat-wave plan is on page {{P:outage}}.

The humidity cycle, misting & airflow

Wet nights, drier days. The shape of the day matters more than the average.

Aim for 60 to 80% relative humidity overnight, with 70 to 80% the ideal band inside it. The number that matters is not the average but the daily swing: humidity spikes right after the evening mist, holds high overnight, and falls through the day before you mist again. That wet-dry cycle copies the forest and keeps mold and respiratory trouble away.

TimeTarget
Right after the evening mist80% or higher
Overnight60 to 80%
Late afternoon, before the next mist45 to 50%

Holding the cycle in practice

  • One heavy mist at lights-out does most of the job. Soak the walls, leaves and substrate, not the gecko, until droplets bead on the glass. If you need to mist several times a day, something else in the setup is leaking humidity.
  • Read the hygrometer twice: after the evening mist and again in the late afternoon, with the probe mid-enclosure, away from where you spray. Those two readings tell you whether the cycle is working. Dial gauges drift and read wrong in moist air.
  • Dries out too fast? A screen top releases humidity quickly: cover 50 to 70% of it with glass or acrylic, but never block the vents entirely. Live plants give off moisture of their own, which smooths the swing, and a room humidifier helps in a dry winter.
  • An automatic mister removes the inconsistency of hand spraying once it is dialed in. Check it against the hygrometer for the first few weeks.
Too high is a problem too An enclosure that never dries grows mold on the substrate and decor, and cutting ventilation to hold humidity in frequently causes skin and respiratory disease. Mold, or droplets that never clear between mistings, means mist less and open the vents rather than seal the enclosure tighter; a small fan across the top helps if the air is stagnant.

UVB light & the day length

Not strictly required, because the diet carries vitamin D3. Still worth providing, at a low level.

UVB light on the skin is how a reptile makes vitamin D3, and without D3 it cannot use the calcium it eats. A crested gecko on a complete diet powder gets its D3 from the food, so UVB is not strictly required. Low-level UVB is still a solid idea for overall health, and it is one of the three things that prevent metabolic bone disease (page {{P:mbd}}). This is a gecko that spends its days in leaf shade, so the right light is a weak one.

SettingNumber
TubeA 12 in (30 cm) low-output T5 HO tube in the 5.0 range, which means about 5% UVB. T5 is the slim fluorescent tube size, 5/8 inch across, and HO means high output, the tube format; the low rating is what keeps it gentle.
Strength at the geckoA UVI (ultraviolet index, the standard scale for UVB strength) of about 0.6 to 1.4 at the warm area near the top, falling to 0 to 0.7 across the rest of the enclosure.
Day length12 hours on, 12 hours off, on a plug-in timer. No colored night bulbs.
Replace the tubeEvery 6 to 12 months, depending on the model, even if it still lights. Write the date on the equipment log.

Why so low

UVB needs are graded by Ferguson Zone, four groups that match a species' natural sun exposure to a UVB strength. Crested geckos are Zone 1, the shade dwellers active at dusk and after dark, which is the lowest of the four. Matching the zone matters more than buying the strongest tube on the shelf.

Distance and glass change the dose UVB falls off sharply with distance, so mount the tube at the distance its maker's chart gives for this strength. Screen mesh between the tube and the gecko cuts output substantially, and glass or clear plastic blocks most of it, so a tube on a glass lid or a gecko by a sunny window gets far less than it seems to. Mount the fixture over open mesh.

The tube that still glows

UVB output decays months before the visible light dies, so a year-old tube that "still works" is often decorative. That is why it is replaced by the calendar rather than when it looks tired. A UV index meter is the only way to know the tube is at the right height and when to replace it; UV cards show total UV, not the UVB that makes vitamin D3.

Light needs shade beside it The gecko should be able to get fully out of the light. Dense leaves and cork around the top of the enclosure let it choose between shade and a little light, the same way it controls its own heat by moving.

Furnishings checklist

Branches, vines and bamboo at many heights, mostly thin, a few thick, run diagonally so the gecko can travel floor to ceiling without touching glass
Cork tubes, birdhouse-style or magnetic wall hides in the top third, where it sleeps, with at least two at different heights
Dense foliage, live or good artificial, for daytime cover
An elevated feeding ledge: crested geckos eat off the ground
A humid hide lined with damp sphagnum moss, separate from the sleeping hides above and at any height
A shallow water dish, though most drinking is droplets licked off leaves and glass
Real gaps between perches: open space is where the jumping happens

Plants

Plants do three jobs here: cover, climbing, and drinking water, since live leaves collect droplets after misting. Bromeliads such as Guzmania and Vriesea, pothos, philodendron, snake plant, bird's nest fern and Ficus are gecko-safe choices. Live plants need a 6500 K (kelvin, the scale for the color of light) full-spectrum plant light to survive. High-quality artificial plants give the same cover and still hold mist.

Bioactive setups

A bioactive enclosure has a drainage layer, a soil mix and leaf litter, worked by a cleanup crew of springtails and isopods, tiny invertebrates that break down waste. It adds continuous low-level stimulation and cuts how often you intervene. It is a long-term option once the basics are dialed in, not a requirement to get started. It changes the floor and the cleaning, nothing else: the heat ceiling, the humidity cycle and the diet stay exactly the same.

Cleaning & hygiene

Cleaning removes the waste. Disinfecting kills what is left. Hygiene keeps it off your hands.

Salmonella Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella bacteria in the gut and shed them in their droppings without being ill, on and off, and one negative test clears nothing. It travels from the gecko, its enclosure and anything it touches to hands, then to mouths or food, so wash with soap and running water after every contact. Do not kiss the gecko or hold it to your face. Children under 5 should not handle the gecko or touch its enclosure at all. Adults over 65, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system are at higher risk too. A federal investigation into infections linked to pet geckos, closed in January 2026, counted 113 cases and 31 hospitalizations across 36 states.

Three clocks

WhenWhat to do
DailyEmpty, wash and disinfect the water dish and the food dish, then refill the water fresh. Crested geckos feed at night, so the morning is when leftover diet comes out. Pick up droppings, uneaten food and shed skin.
WeeklyA non-bioactive enclosure gets a full clean and disinfect at least once a week, more often if more than one gecko shares it: glass, walls, decor and ledges.
MonthlyMove the gecko to a secure temporary tub. Take everything out, scrub it with detergent and hot water, disinfect, rinse, dry, and put in all-new substrate.

A bioactive enclosure skips the weekly and monthly strip-downs and relies on the cleanup crew, with its substrate changed every 3 to 4 months. The dishes still get their daily wash.

Disinfecting, step by step

  • Scrub first. A film of waste shields bacteria from disinfectant, so it only works on a surface already cleaned.
  • Dilute household bleach 1:32, which is half a cup of bleach per gallon of water.
  • Keep it wet for at least 10 minutes. Bleach needs that long in contact to work, so don't wipe it straight off.
  • Rinse until no bleach smell remains, then let the enclosure and every item dry completely and air out before the gecko goes back in. Plan on a full day of airing.
Never the kitchen sink Never wash the gecko, its dishes, decor or enclosure in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink, or a bath people use, or anywhere food is prepared. Keep a plastic tub for the job, or work in a utility sink or outdoors. If a bathtub does get used, clean it thoroughly afterward. Wash decor and gear separately from anything used for food, and keep the gecko out of the kitchen entirely.

If someone in the house gets sick

The signs are ordinary ones: diarrhea, fever, stomach cramps. That is why the link gets missed. Tell the doctor there is a reptile in the house, even if nobody remembers touching it, since enclosure surfaces are enough on their own. It matters most for a child under 5, an adult over 65, someone pregnant, or someone with a weakened immune system, in whom the infection can spread beyond the gut.
