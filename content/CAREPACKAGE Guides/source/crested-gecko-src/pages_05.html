Red flags & finding a vet

What to watch for, what to tell the vet, and how to find one before you need one.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see A soft, rubbery or misshapen lower jaw · weak or floppy limbs, a kinked spine, tremors, or new trouble climbing · open-mouth breathing, wheezing, mucus or discharge from the nose · no bowel movement for a week or more, or a bloated belly with straining · ongoing weight loss, or a bony spine, hips or tail base · sunken eyes with repeated stuck sheds · any neurological symptom · watery stool more than once in a row, or any blood · tissue protruding from the vent, a prolapse · a gravid female who stops eating, turns weak, or strains past her laying date · a gecko that stays limp or unresponsive after the enclosure has been too hot or too cold.

What to tell the vet

Both humidity readings: after the evening mist, and late afternoon
The highest temperature the enclosure reached, not just today's reading
Substrate, and exactly what the gecko eats, including the diet powder
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening
Last normal bowel movement, and what it looked like
Weight trend from the Owner Log, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag if you can

Why the setup comes up first

Nearly everything in this section traces back to husbandry: calcium, humidity and substrate. Metabolic bone disease is a calcium and vitamin D3 problem, stuck shed is a humidity problem, impaction is usually a substrate or prey-size problem, and respiratory infection is a cold-and-wet problem. That is why a vet asks about the setup before the symptom.

Finding a reptile vet

Not every practice treats reptiles, and the time to learn that is not during an emergency. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory of its members. Ask a clinic how many reptiles it sees, whether it routinely treats crested geckos, whether it runs fecal tests in house, and who covers reptile emergencies after hours, then write the number on page {{P:emergency}}. Bring a new gecko in within the first 30 days, then plan on a yearly wellness exam with a fecal test as a baseline, not just when something looks wrong.

Metabolic bone disease

MBD, short for metabolic bone disease: progressive, partly permanent once advanced, and close to fully preventable.

What it is: without enough usable calcium, the body pulls calcium out of the skeleton to keep the blood level steady, and the bones soften and bend. The veterinary name is nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism. Cause: a deficiency of calcium or vitamin D3. In a crested gecko that usually means a diet that is not actually a complete diet powder, or not being eaten, insects fed without dusting, or a gecko kept too cool to digest the calcium it does eat. It is progressive, which is why catching it early matters.

StageSigns
EarlyA soft or rubbery lower jaw, often the first thing an owner notices
AdvancedFloppy or weak limbs; a visibly misaligned jaw; a kinked spine; tremors; difficulty climbing, which in a climbing species shows early, because the gecko simply stops going up

Diagnosis: a vet, through blood work and X-rays. Response: if you notice a soft jaw, don't wait to see whether it gets worse on its own; book the vet this week. Outlook: caught early and corrected, the outlook is good. Once the disease reaches the advanced stage, some of the damage can be permanent even with treatment, because bone that has been pulled apart does not come back neatly.

The prevention is three things Feed a complete crested gecko diet powder as the base, dust the insects with calcium, and provide low-level UVB. That combination makes MBD one of the easiest serious illnesses in this species to prevent.

Floppy tail syndrome & tail loss

Two tail problems, one of them permanent, and neither the crisis it looks like.

Floppy tail syndrome (FTS)

What you see: the tail hangs over the gecko's head, or sits at an odd angle to the body, when it rests upside down. Cause: typically chronic upside-down resting against glass, over a long time. It is sometimes associated with calcium or pelvic problems and occasionally overlaps with metabolic bone disease, though it is not a sign of it on its own. Response: a vet visit is worth it to rule out an underlying calcium problem.

Prevention: give the gecko better places to rest than bare glass. A climbing network that runs floor to ceiling without touching the glass, cork and dense leaves high up, and cover where it sleeps all make a flat glass wall the least inviting spot in the enclosure.

Tail loss

Like many geckos, a crested gecko can drop its tail on purpose as a defense, called autotomy. The tail detaches along built-in fracture points and keeps wiggling on its own to distract a predator while the gecko escapes. The drop itself is thought to be painless or close to it.

It never grows back A crested gecko is incapable of regrowing its tail, ever. Its close relative the gargoyle gecko does regrow a tail, and so does a leopard gecko. Once a crested gecko's tail is gone, it is gone for good.

Why it happens

CausePrevention
Fright, rough or rushed handlingCalm, unhurried sessions; never touch the tail
A cagemateOne gecko per enclosure; never group hatchlings
A fallHandle low over something soft; anchor every perch
Trapped in a lid or doorCheck where the tail is before you close anything

Caring for the stump

A dropped tail is not a medical emergency, and the wound closes on its own if it stays clean. Swap any loose substrate for paper towel until the stump has sealed, and change the towel whenever it is soiled. A soft or hard swelling at the stump is the sign of an abscess: if it looks infected rather than drying and closing, that is a vet visit. The tail held fat reserves, so keep a closer eye on weight and hydration from then on.

Life as a "frog butt" A tailless crested gecko, a "frog butt" to keepers, lives a completely normal, healthy life. Losing the tail does not affect breeding, climbing or overall health, and most adult crested geckos in the wild have already lost theirs. There is no treatment and no regrowth, so prevention is the whole strategy, and a dropped tail is a reason to review handling and housing, not a reason to panic.

Stuck shed, respiratory infection & skin trouble

The humidity problems, from both directions: too dry, and too wet for too long.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Almost always a humidity problem. Old skin sticks around the toes, the tail tip or the eyes instead of coming off cleanly, and a ring left on a toe or tail tip constricts it and cuts off the blood supply. Left alone, it can cost the gecko the toe, and the damage builds up across shed cycles, so one slightly imperfect shed matters less than a pattern of them.

  • Fix the cause first: correct the humidity cycle and keep the humid hide damp (page {{P:humidity}}).
  • To loosen shed already stuck: a 30-minute soak in water at enclosure temperature, chin-deep at most, in a shallow dish, supervised the whole time. Then work the softened skin off the toes gently with a damp cotton swab. Never peel dry shed.

Low humidity is the most common cause, but it is one of six: low humidity, skin parasites, nutritional deficiencies, infectious disease, nothing rough to rub against, and reduced thyroid function. Repeated bad sheds in a correctly set up enclosure are a vet conversation, not a humidity problem.

Respiratory infection

Caused by an enclosure that is too cold, too wet, or both, which is the failure that follows misting harder to fix a shedding problem. Look for open-mouth breathing, visible mucus or discharge from the nose, wheezing, and lethargy. It needs a vet promptly; it does not clear up with waiting. Fix the husbandry at the same time: restore the daily dry-back to 45 to 50%, unblock the vents, and make sure the enclosure is not sitting at 72°F (22°C) or below day and night.

Skin and scale trouble, and mold

Chronically high humidity combined with poor enclosure hygiene leads to skin and scale problems. An enclosure that never dries grows mold on the substrate and decor, and with it come bacterial and fungal infections. The signs in the enclosure come first: mold on wood or soil, condensation that never clears between mistings, a musty smell.

The common thread All three are settings rather than bad luck. Stuck shed traces to the humidity cycle and something rough to rub on; respiratory infection to cold and constant damp; skin trouble to damp without cleaning. Any gecko showing one of them should have both humidity readings and the enclosure's daily high checked the same day, because the fix usually lives in those numbers.

Impaction, parasites & overheating

A blockage, the infections a fecal test finds, and the emergency this species has that most reptiles do not.

Impaction

The most common health problem in pet geckos generally, and crested geckos are no exception. It is a blockage in the digestive tract, usually caused by loose substrate such as sand or bark, by prey too large to pass, or by shed skin that was never fully passed.

Watch forNo bowel movement for a week or more, a visibly bloated belly, straining, lethargy, and appetite loss
Prevent itSolid substrate, paper towel or tile, for young geckos, and no insect wider than the space between the gecko's eyes
A mild caseStraining or a missed stool with the gecko otherwise bright: a warm 15-minute soak can sometimes help, then a vet if it is no better in 24 to 48 hours
A vet nowA bloated belly, or no droppings for a week or more. A true blockage is not a home job

Internal parasites and cryptosporidiosis

Internal parasites, and less commonly cryptosporidiosis, are both on the list for this species. Crypto in particular is serious and often difficult to treat, which is the practical reason for a fecal test at the first vet visit rather than waiting for a reason, and again at the yearly exam. Intestinal parasites can show no outward sign at all, so a clean-looking gecko is not a clean fecal. Watery stool more than once in a row is the classic sign of a parasite infection in reptiles, and weight loss on a normal appetite is the other. Bring a fresh sample in a sealed bag.

Overheating

This is the emergency a crested gecko has that most commonly kept reptiles do not. Its home forest never gets as hot as a desert lizard is built to tolerate, prolonged exposure above 85°F (29°C) risks fatal heat stroke, and consistent temperatures above it can cause stress as well as heat stroke. The warning starts lower: more than 80°F (27°C) for long stretches is already too warm. The usual causes are a glass enclosure in the sun, a heat bulb with no thermostat, or a room that got away from you in a heat wave.

If the enclosure has gone past 85°F (29°C) An overheating gecko gapes, tries frantically to get out, or goes limp and unresponsive. Move it somewhere cooler, turn off every heat source in the enclosure, and offer a shallow dish of room-temperature water. Cool it gradually: an ice bath, an ice pack against the body, or cold water can make things worse. A gecko that stays unresponsive or barely responsive, or whose breathing is still labored after 10 to 15 minutes of cooling, goes to a vet right away. Then fix the cause, with the heat-wave plan on page {{P:outage}}.
Mouth rot and the rest Mouth rot (an infection of the gums and mouth), parasites, respiratory infection, impaction and metabolic bone disease are all real medical causes of a gecko going off its food. When appetite loss comes with any sign from the red-flag list, it is a vet visit rather than pickiness.

Reading droppings, urates & hydration

The earliest free health check you get. Look before you clean it up.

A reptile passes its waste in two parts at once, through one opening, the vent. The solid stool comes with a urate: a white to cream, chalky or paste-like portion that is a reptile's version of urine, made from uric acid instead of liquid. Because a crested gecko eats a fruit-based powder as well as insects, its stool runs softer than a strict insect-eater's, so soft is not automatically wrong.

How often

Most adult geckos go every few days to about once a week, and juveniles more often, since they eat more often. The number that matters is your own gecko's baseline. A gap well beyond it, or a sudden jump in either direction, is the signal.

Hydration

A rainforest gecko drinks mostly by licking droplets, so hydration and humidity are the same subject here, and a crested gecko can go only about 3 days without water. Urate texture is the fastest daily hydration read you have: smooth and creamy means well hydrated, dry or gritty means act.

  • The evening mist is the main drink of the day. Coat the leaves, the cork and the glass until they bead.
  • The dish is the backup: shallow, washed and refilled daily, with tap, filtered or spring water, never distilled alone.
  • The humid hide stays damp.
Signs of dehydration Sunken eyes · wrinkled, loose or sunken skin, or skin with a dry, sandpaper texture · repeated stuck sheds · sticky saliva · lethargy, less activity, and a gecko that stops eating. Correct the humidity cycle first and check the hygrometer rather than trusting the routine; then the vet if the signs stay.
