Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Gentle and easy to handle once settled, with one fact to know before you ever pick one up.

The tail does not grow back Unlike a leopard gecko's, a crested gecko's tail never regrows once dropped. Never grab, hold or restrain the tail, under any circumstances. Calm, unhurried handling is what keeps it on (page {{P:fts}}).

Wait, then wait for size

Don't handle a new arrival for about two weeks. Let it settle into the enclosure and start eating normally first. Then wait for size: regular handling begins with a sub-adult or older juvenile, roughly 10 to 15 g, about 4 to 5 in (10 to 13 cm) and 6 months old. A gecko under about 10 g is too fragile for routine handling.

How often and how long

StageSessions
Starting out1 to 2 minutes, every other day
Building upLonger sessions as the gecko stays calm, toward about 15 minutes once it is tame
The ceilingUnder about 20 minutes of handling in total a day, even for a tame adult

Evening works best, since crested geckos are most active at dusk and after dark.

How to do it

  • Let it walk onto your hand rather than grabbing it.
  • Treadmilling: as the gecko reaches the edge of one hand, cup your free hand in front of it. It hops from hand to hand on its own, and you never have to hold it.
  • Stay low, over a bed, a couch or another soft surface. Crested geckos jump readily and without warning, and a fall onto a hard floor can cause real injury.
Put it back when you see Tail-wagging or whipping · squeaking or other calls · erratic jumping · gaping or an open mouth · closed eyes · actively trying to flee. Any one means the session is over. Skittish, rushed handling is exactly what raises the risk of a dropped tail.

Who should hold it

Children under 5 should not handle the gecko or touch its enclosure, because a healthy-looking reptile can still carry Salmonella (page {{P:cleaning}}). Wash hands with soap and running water before and after every session, even a short one. Handling sessions are also when a keeper can check the sex: the gecko has to sit on a flat hand or inside a clear container to be seen from below.

A gecko built for the night

In the wild a crested gecko spends much of its time among thin branches, and there is a reason for it: the much larger New Caledonian giant gecko preys on it, and thin branches help it get away. It grips fine stems well with its toes and with the tip of its tail, which carries the same sticky gripping hairs as the toes. It sleeps by day in secure spots high up, then travels at night, often by long jumps. Almost every good enclosure choice copies one piece of that: height for sleeping, open gaps for jumping, fine perches for moving, and wet leaves for drinking.

Quick differentiator A winter slowdown is a gecko that eats less and moves less while its weight holds. Illness is the same behavior with weight loss, sunken eyes, a weak grip or a soft jaw. The scale is the tell: weigh it, and compare against the log.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Heating it like a bearded dragon: a basking bulb with no thermostat, or an enclosure in a sunny window, past the 85°F (29°C) ceiling.
  • Making insects the main diet, or offering them before the powder is reliably eaten.
  • A tall enclosure with nothing in it. Empty height is space the gecko cannot use.
  • Humidity pinned at 80% around the clock, or vents blocked to hold it in.
  • Trusting a dial gauge instead of a digital hygrometer.
  • Two geckos in one enclosure, or a group of hatchlings.
  • Grabbing the tail, or rushing a skittish gecko.
  • Fruit as a routine rather than an occasional treat.
  • Loose sand or bark chips under a young gecko, or insects wider than the space between its eyes.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after the right temperature, the humidity cycle and a properly sized vertical enclosure, never instead of them. In this order:

1. A real climbing network

The single highest-impact upgrade. Mostly thin branches, bamboo and vines with a few thick limbs, run diagonally so the gecko can go floor to ceiling without touching glass. Leave real gaps. It is hard to overdo; the only limit is stability, so anchor everything.

2. Cover where it sleeps

Cork tubes and hides near the ceiling with dense leaves around them, at least two options at different heights, so the gecko picks a warmer or cooler spot and its own amount of light.

3. Plants that hold water

Live or good artificial leaves give cover, climbing and droplets to drink after the mist (page {{P:substrate}}).

4. Food it has to find

Insects once or twice a week bring out hunting and stalking that a dish cannot. Release a few into the upper branches after dark, and keep the powder dish on a raised magnetic ledge, where the gecko naturally feeds.

5. A bioactive setup

Springtails and isopods working through soil and leaf litter add ongoing low-level stimulation. A long-term option once the basics are dialed in, not a requirement.

6. Small changes, not overhauls

A sudden total rearrangement of a shy gecko's enclosure causes stress rather than enrichment. Change a branch angle or rotate a plant; leave the core climbing structure and the hides where they are.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a gecko, quarantine & the first vet visit

Captive-bred, checked over in good light, and in front of a vet within the first 30 days.

Every crested gecko in the trade is captive-bred. A breeder who hatched it can tell you its hatch date, its food, and when it last ate and shed. Buy only where the enclosures are clean and large enough. A rescue typically charges $50 to $175, and a "free" gecko from a rehoming post often comes with medical bills attached; neither is a reason to refuse it, but both are reasons to run the full quarantine below. A juvenile sold below the weight where males show their bulge, 18 to 25 g, is unsexed, whatever the listing guesses.

Look before you pay

CheckHealthyWalk away or ask more
BehaviorAware of its surroundings, a strong gripLimp, unresponsive, or unable to hold on
BodyRounded, no projecting hips, spine or ribsVisible bones or a sunken tail base
EyesClear and alertSwollen, sunken, cloudy or crusted
Nose, breathingDry nostrils, quiet breathingBubbles, mucus, wheezing, open-mouth breathing
Vent (the opening under the tail)CleanStained, pasted with droppings, or swollen
SkinIntact, no old shed left onWounds, or rings of old shed on toes and tail tip

Ask the seller: the hatch date; whether it was captive-bred; what it eats and when it last ate; and whether it has been treated for anything. A good breeder answers without looking anything up.

Quarantine: 3 to 6 months

If another reptile already lives in the house, keep the new gecko completely apart for 3 to 6 months. With no other reptile, skip the separation but not the vet visit.

  • A separate room. Two enclosures on one shelf share the same air.
  • Paper towel, a hide and a water dish. Paper shows droppings and anything unusual at a glance.
  • Separate tools, and the new gecko last. Its own dishes and tongs, cared for after every other animal, with hands washed in between.
  • Restart the clock if anything flags: a positive fecal test, a regurgitated meal, unexplained weight loss. At the end, disinfect everything that touched it.
The first vet visit, within 30 days Find and book a reptile vet before the gecko comes home (finding one is on page {{P:redflags}}). The first exam records a baseline weight and checks the animal over, and it should include a fecal test. Intestinal parasites can show no outward sign at all, and cryptosporidiosis, a serious, hard-to-treat parasite infection of the gut, is on the list for this species, so bring a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag. That visit is the baseline every later one is measured against.

Sexing & females that lay

Every female owner needs this page, whether or not a male has ever been in the house.

Telling male from female

The only dependable sign is the hemipenal bulge: a male develops a prominent swelling at the base of the tail, just behind the vent, and a female stays flat there. The bulge usually appears between 18 and 25 g, so a gecko under that weight with no bulge is unsexed, not female. Look from below while it sits on a flat hand or inside a clear container, never by holding the tail. Sexing earlier is possible by looking at the femoral pores, a row of tiny pores on the underside of the thighs, through a magnifying glass, but that method is not always accurate, so an early call stays a guess until the bulge settles it. It matters most when juveniles share an enclosure: unsexed youngsters can turn out to include two males, which fight.

Females lay without a male

A mature female kept alone can still develop and lay infertile clutches, and producing an infertile egg is harder on her than a fertile one. A laying female typically drops two eggs about once a month through the season, which can span much of the year. Crested geckos mature at about 15 to 18 months, and a female is not ready to breed until at least 12 months old and 40 g, a male until 10 months and 30 g.

  • Signs she is gravid: a visibly plump body, less activity, and appetite down for a week or more. She may eat less, but she should stay bright and alert.
  • Laid infertile eggs can simply be removed.

The lay box

Any adult female needs one, whether or not she has ever met a male: a container with a few inches of slightly moist coconut fiber and fir bark, roughly half and half. Put it in as soon as she looks gravid. A female with no suitable place to lay is at higher risk of holding her eggs.

Calcium is the other half Each clutch draws on her calcium. Keep calcium in her diet through the season, and look at the two small calcium sacs at the back of her mouth: sacs that shrink noticeably mean her reserves are running down, and a vet should check her. The full calcium picture is on page {{P:supplements}}.

Egg binding

Dystocia: a female who cannot pass the eggs she is carrying. A same-day vet visit.

Laid eggs are not the concern; the egg that does not come out is. A normal gravid female and one in trouble look alike at first, since both eat less. The line is clear once you know it: a normal gravid female stays bright and alert, and a female with egg binding stops eating altogether and turns weak or lethargic.

SignNormal gravid femaleEgg binding
AppetiteEats less, for a week or more before layingStops eating
EnergyBright and alert despite eating lessWeak or lethargic
BellyVisibly plumpSwollen, with straining
TimingLays about once a month through the seasonStill straining after her expected laying date
Call a reptile vet the same day if A gravid female stops eating, turns weak or lethargic, or strains with a swollen belly after her expected laying date. Do not wait it out.

Who it happens to

Most reptiles seen by vets for egg binding are single females kept with no male, so the risk applies to every adult crested gecko female, bred or not.

What causes it

  • Low calcium, the reserve every clutch draws on.
  • Dehydration.
  • Wrong temperatures or humidity.
  • No suitable place to lay: the reason the lay box goes in before it is needed.

What the vet does

A vet confirms it with an exam, gentle feeling of the belly, blood work and X-rays, sometimes ultrasound. Unlike mammals and birds, a reptile can hold eggs for weeks or even months past the normal laying window, which makes the urgency easy to misjudge; it is a reason to get a diagnosis, never a reason to wait at home. With no blockage, treatment can mean fluids, calcium, and a hormone injection to start laying. With a blockage from an oversized or misshapen egg, inducing her is unsafe and surgery is needed instead. The longer it waits, the fewer options remain. A lay box that is always available to an adult female, and calcium kept up through the season, are the cheapest prevention there is.

Shedding & the winter slowdown

Two normal changes that look like trouble the first time you see them.

The shed cycle

Shedding, properly ecdysis, is how a reptile grows and clears old, damaged skin. A juvenile sheds every one to two weeks while it grows, an adult about once a month. Each shed runs in three steps:

StageWhat you see
BeforeColors go dull and the skin looks dry. The gecko may hide more and refuse food: appetite commonly dips for a few days before and after, sometimes for up to about a week in total.
The shedThe gecko rubs against rough surfaces to start the peel, then works its way out of the old skin.
AfterClean, bright skin. Check the toes, the tail tip and the eyes in good light for anything left behind.

Helping it come off clean

  • A damp humid hide lined with sphagnum moss. Higher humidity during the shed helps loose skin come away in one piece, and a humid hide gives that without making the whole enclosure permanently damper.
  • Rough surfaces: cork, branches and textured bark give the gecko something to rub against. A gecko with nothing rough to rub on can fail to shed cleanly even in perfect humidity.
  • The evening mist, on schedule. A ring of old skin left on a toe acts like a tight band and can cost the gecko the toe. What to do about stuck shed is on page {{P:shed}}.
  • No handling while a shed is in progress.

The winter slowdown, not brumation

Brumation is the deliberate winter cooling some temperate reptiles go through. Crested geckos are tropical, do not brumate, and should never be put through a cooling cycle. Even so, many crested geckos eat less and move less through the winter months, even with stable husbandry, on their own internal rhythm. The rule that keeps it safe is the scale: during a suspected slowdown, weigh every few days rather than monthly.

Slowdown or illness? A slowdown with a steady weight is a season. A slowdown with a falling weight is a health problem, and so is one with sunken eyes, a soft jaw, a weak grip, or breathing with the mouth open. Check the heat and humidity readings too: a room that has dropped to 72°F (22°C) or below day and night brings on the same lethargy and loss of appetite.
Section 06 · The Law

Is a crested gecko legal where you live?

Legal without a permit in most of the United States, with seven exceptions.

All 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet crested gecko is legal with no permit in 45, barred in 2, permit-only in 3, and legal on a condition in 2.
