How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

If you haven't set up yet

Read pages 4 through 18 in order before buying anything. The tank and the filter are the two decisions that determine how the next decade goes, and both are far easier to get right the first time than to correct later with a fish already in the water. Budget for the tank before the fish, and expect to spend four to six weeks cycling it before a goldfish goes in. Then use the Budget and Shopping List on page 29 to buy once.

If your goldfish is already set up

Jump to the Setup Checklist and Targets on page 27 to audit what you have, and the Health Red Flags section starting on page 22 if anything seems off. Test the water first, before you change anything else. Most goldfish problems that look like disease are a water reading, and you cannot rule that out by looking at the tank.

If you already have a fish in an uncycled tank

This is the most common situation a goldfish owner is actually in, and it is fixable. Go straight to the fish-in cycle on page 9. Do not tear the tank down, do not replace the filter, and do not start dosing treatments. Daily testing and water changes carry you through it.

Tank size & the bowl myth

A single fancy goldfish needs a minimum of 20 gal (76 L), with 10 gal (38 L) added for each fish after that. Single-tail varieties, the commons and comets sold as feeder fish, reach 10 to 14+ in (25 to 36+ cm) and need at least 55 gal (208 L) for one fish; long term they belong in an outdoor pond, not an indoor tank. Long and wide beats tall. Goldfish are horizontal swimmers, and floor area drives both swimming room and the surface area available for gas exchange.

Goldfish do not grow to fit their container. What actually happens in a small tank is stunting: chronic ammonia exposure and cramped space suppress growth hormone while the skeleton and organs keep developing, and the result is a shortened lifespan and organ failure, not a conveniently small fish. A bowl-kept goldfish often survives months or a couple of years, which is long enough for the setup to look like it works while the fish is slowly declining.

Never Keep a goldfish in a bowl, a vase, or a self-cleaning aquaponics kit. A few plant roots cannot process the ammonia a goldfish produces, and none of those containers hold enough water or surface area for the fish to breathe properly. The marketing changed; the underlying problem did not.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • A full 20 gal tank weighs roughly 225 lb (102 kg) once filled and gravelled. Check the stand and the floor before you fill it, not after.
  • Cover the tank. Goldfish startle and can jump clear of an open rim, and a lid also slows evaporation so the water level stays stable between changes.

Filtration

A goldfish produces a bioload comparable to several tropical fish of the same size combined. That single fact drives almost every filtration decision here, and it is the reason experienced goldfish keepers run two filters as standard practice rather than as backup equipment.

Filtration targetNumber
Minimum turnover4 times the tank's total volume per hour
Preferred turnover5 to 10 times per hour
On a 20 gal (76 L) tank80 gph (300 lph) minimum, 100 to 200 gph (380 to 760 lph) better
On a 40 gal (151 L) tank160 gph (605 lph) minimum, 200 to 400 gph (760 to 1,500 lph) better
On a 55 gal (208 L) tank220 gph (830 lph) minimum, 275 to 550 gph (1,040 to 2,080 lph) better

Manufacturer gallons-per-hour ratings are measured with an empty filter and no head height. Assume the real figure is meaningfully lower once the baskets are full and the hoses are run, and size up accordingly. A filter rated for a tank twice your volume is a reasonable default for goldfish.

Aim the outflow at the surface Oxygen enters the water at the surface, and a goldfish uses a lot of it. Angling the filter return so it visibly ripples or churns the surface does more for oxygenation than anything else in the tank, costs nothing, and matters most in summer, when warmer water carries less dissolved oxygen.

Never Rinse filter media under the tap. Chlorine and a sudden temperature change kill the beneficial bacteria colony that is doing the work, and a tank can crash within days of a well-intentioned deep clean. Rinse media in a bucket of water you just siphoned out of the tank, and replace only one filter's media at a time.

Why two filters, or a filter plus an air pump

Running a second filter, or a sponge filter on an air pump alongside the main unit, does three useful things at once for very little money. It adds biological capacity for a species that badly needs it. It means a failed impeller does not take out your entire bacteria colony overnight. And a battery-powered air pump on that sponge filter is the single most useful thing you can own during a power cut, covered on page 33. A mature sponge filter is also a ready-made instant cycle for a hospital or quarantine tank.

Cycling the tank

Cycling means growing a bacteria colony that converts ammonia to nitrite, then nitrite to nitrate. Until it exists, the tank cannot process waste and a fish put in it is living in its own toxins. This is the step that gets skipped, and skipping it has a name: new tank syndrome, the most common cause of premature goldfish death.

The fishless cycle, four to six weeks

StageWhat you'll read on the test kitWhat to do
Week 1 to 2Ammonia climbing, nitrite 0, nitrate 0Dose ammonia to about 2 ppm, or add a pinch of fish food, and wait. Filter and air pump run continuously.
Week 2 to 4Ammonia falling, nitrite climbing and often spiking hardKeep dosing. The nitrite spike is the longest and most discouraging stage. Do not add fish.
Week 4 to 6Ammonia 0, nitrite 0, nitrate risingCycled. Do a 50 percent water change to bring nitrate down, then add fish.
Speeding it up honestly A handful of used media, a squeeze of sponge, or a few cups of gravel from an established, disease-free tank seeds the colony and can cut weeks off the process. Bottled bacteria products vary widely in whether they help. Either way, the test kit decides when the tank is cycled, not the calendar and not the label.

The fish-in cycle

Plenty of goldfish are bought on the same trip as the tank, and if that is where you are, this is the recovery plan rather than a reason to start over. The goal is keeping ammonia and nitrite low enough not to harm the fish while the colony establishes, which takes roughly two to six weeks.

  • Test daily, or at least every other day, for ammonia and nitrite.
  • Water change at 0.25 ppm. Any reading above roughly 0.25 ppm of ammonia or nitrite calls for a 25 to 30 percent change with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water. Larger changes than that slow the colony you are trying to grow.
  • Feed lightly. Small portions once a day, increasing gradually over the four to six weeks. Every uneaten pellet is more ammonia the tank cannot yet process.
  • Use an ammonia binder. A conditioner that locks up free ammonia protects the gills without stalling the cycle, and is worth having on the shelf regardless.
  • Do not clean the filter, replace media, or deep clean the substrate during a cycle. You would be removing the colony you are waiting for.
How you know it's finished You can feed normal portions for a full week while ammonia and nitrite both hold at 0 ppm and nitrate reads above 0. Nitrate appearing is the proof the cycle is complete: it is the end product, and its presence means both earlier conversions are happening. From then on, nitrate is what your weekly water changes exist to remove, covered on page 11.

Reading the numbers together

Ammonia and nitrite are the emergency pair. Both are acutely toxic and both should read exactly zero in a cycled tank. A reading above zero is not a trend to watch, it is an action today.

Nitrate is the housekeeping number. It is far less toxic and it accumulates continuously, so it measures how well your water change schedule is keeping up with your stocking and feeding. Climbing nitrate between changes means feeding more than the tank can absorb, too many fish, or changes that are too small.

pH and KH work as a pair. KH is the water's buffer, its resistance to pH movement. When KH falls too low, the acids produced by fish respiration and bacterial activity can drop the pH suddenly and dangerously. That is why a tank with soft, low-buffer water is worth watching even when the pH reading itself looks fine, and why chasing a pH number with adjusting chemicals so often makes things worse rather than better.

Liquid kit, not paper strips Strips are convenient and consistently less accurate, and the readings that matter most here are the two that have to read exactly zero. A liquid master kit costs more once and gives you numbers you can act on. Check the expiry date: reagents lose accuracy, and an out-of-date kit reading zero is not the same as a tank reading zero.

Water changes & early warnings

The weekly water change

Change 25 to 50 percent weekly on a stocked goldfish tank. Some general fish care sheets suggest smaller changes at longer intervals; for a goldfish specifically, the waste output justifies the weekly schedule, and nitrate creeping up between changes is the sign to do more rather than less.

  1. Test first, and write the numbers down before you change anything.
  2. Unplug any heater. Leave the filter alone; you are not cleaning it today.
  3. Siphon from the substrate up, working across the floor of the tank. Most of what you are removing has settled into the sand or gravel, so the siphon is doing two jobs at once.
  4. Fill a clean, tank-only bucket with tap water and dose dechlorinator for the whole bucket volume.
  5. Check the new water is within a few degrees of the tank, then pour it back slowly, against the glass or onto a plate, so you do not blast the substrate.
  6. Plug everything back in and confirm the filter has restarted and the surface is moving.
Chloramine does not evaporate Many municipal supplies now use chloramine, a chlorine and ammonia compound, instead of plain chlorine. Unlike chlorine it does not gas off if you leave a bucket standing overnight, so the old advice to age water is not a substitute for a conditioner. Use a dechlorinator rated for chloramine, every time, including for small top-ups.
Never change everything at once A 100 percent change, a full substrate wash, or a scrub of every surface strips out the bacteria colony and restarts the cycle with a fish in the tank. If the water looks bad, the answer is several partial changes across a few days, not one drastic one.

Substrate, plants & decor

Substrate

Fine sand or smooth rounded gravel, 1 to 2 in (2.5 to 5 cm) deep. Research on substrate particle size found that coarse sand supported more natural foraging behavior than larger gravel, and goldfish kept sifting even when they were not hungry. A bare-bottom tank is easier to keep spotless and is a legitimate choice, but it removes a real foraging outlet that costs nothing to provide.

Never Use sharp gravel, crushed glass decor, or any pebble small enough for a goldfish to fit in its mouth but too large to pass. Goldfish sift substrate constantly and a lodged stone is a genuine emergency. Either go finer than the fish can swallow, or larger than it can lift.

Plants

Expect any live planting to be grazed, uprooted, or both. Rhizome plants such as anubias and java fern, tied or glued to wood or rock rather than buried, hold up far better than anything rooted in the substrate. Tough, fast-growing stems and floating plants are the other workable approach, on the understanding that some get eaten. Grazing is not a problem to solve, it is normal feeding behavior, and soft plants like duckweed count as an occasional food rather than decor.

Artificial planting is a legitimate alternative here, and not a compromise. The habitat-preference research that found goldfish spending roughly 90 percent of their time in planted areas found no strong preference for live plants over closely matched artificial ones. It is the structure that matters. If you use artificial plants, choose silk or soft fabric rather than stiff plastic, which tears fancy fins.

Decor checklist

Smooth hardscape only, nothing that can catch a fancy fin
Rhizome plants tied to wood or rock, not buried
An open swim lane running the length of the tank
Planted or structured cover at both ends
Everything anchored so nothing can topple onto a fish
No stiff plastic plants, sharp ornaments, or holes a fish can wedge into
Two decor traps specific to fancy goldfish Ornaments with narrow openings, and anything with a hollow interior, catch a round-bodied fish that swims into them and cannot reverse out. And telescope-eye, bubble-eye and celestial varieties have fragile, protruding eyes with poor vision: for those fish, every surface in the tank needs to be smooth, and sharp hardscape is a real injury risk rather than a theoretical one.

Handling, quarantine & settling in

Handling: net and cup, never hands

A goldfish's slime coat is a living barrier against parasites, bacteria and infection, and bare hands strip it on contact. Losing it also disrupts the fish's ability to regulate salts and water balance in its own body, so it is a mechanical stress on top of the infection risk. The coat takes 24 to 48 hours to regenerate, and during that window the fish is meaningfully more vulnerable than usual. This is why a single unnecessary scoop by hand is not as harmless as it looks.

  • Guide the fish into a container of its own tank water rather than lifting it out. This is the gentlest method and avoids the slime-coat problem almost entirely.
  • If you must use a net, wet it first and choose soft rubber over knotted nylon, which catches fins and scales.
  • If bare hands are genuinely unavoidable in an emergency, wet them first and stay clear of the gills and eyes. Minimize time out of the water in every case.
Aquarium water and your own hands Aquarium water can carry Mycobacterium marinum, which causes a slow, stubborn skin infection through cuts and grazes. Wear waterproof gloves if you have broken skin on your hands, wash with soap afterward, and never start a siphon by mouth. Keep tank gear out of the kitchen sink.

Quarantine, and why it is worth the trouble

Almost every parasite and infection in this package arrives on a new fish, a new plant, or water from a shop tank. A quarantine tank is the one piece of equipment that prevents that, and it does not need to be elaborate: a bare 10 gal (38 L) tub, a sponge filter squeezed from your mature filter so it is instantly cycled, a heater if you need one for treatment, and a lid. Two to four weeks is the practical minimum, and four to six weeks is safer, since different pathogens surface on different timelines.

The same tank doubles as a hospital tank, which is where you want to be medicating rather than dosing your main tank: treatments are cheaper in a small volume, many of them harm your biological filter, and an ill fish is easier to observe without substrate and decor in the way.

First days in a new home

  1. Float the sealed bag in the tank for 15 to 20 minutes so the temperature equalizes.
  2. Open the bag and add a small amount of tank water every 5 minutes for another 20 to 30 minutes, so the fish adjusts gradually to your water chemistry.
  3. Net the fish across and discard the bag water. Never pour shop water into your tank.
  4. Lights off for the rest of the day, no feeding until the next morning, and no visitors at the glass.
  5. Test ammonia and nitrite daily for the first week. A new fish is a new bioload and a small spike is common.

Feeding by age

Goldfish have no true stomach. Food passes more or less continuously through the intestine rather than being held and released, which is why small frequent portions suit them and a single large meal does not. It is also why overfeeding shows up so fast, as constipation, buoyancy trouble, and a waste load your filter was not sized for.

Portion, not schedule, is the thing to get right

Feed what disappears in 30 seconds to 2 minutes and siphon out the rest. A goldfish begs constantly and will keep eating well past the point of usefulness, so its behavior is not a portion guide, and neither is the feeding instruction on the tub. If the water is cloudy or nitrate is climbing between changes, you are feeding more than the tank can process.

The fasting day Many experienced goldfish keepers skip feeding one day a week, particularly for round-bodied fancy varieties. A healthy adult goldfish is entirely comfortable going 3 to 4 days without food, so a weekly fast carries no risk and gives the gut a chance to clear. It is one of the simplest ways to reduce the constipation and buoyancy problems on page 25.
No supplements, and that is normal Unlike a reptile or an invertebrate, a goldfish needs no calcium dusting, no vitamin D3, and no gut-loaded feeders. Nutritional balance comes from genuine variety in the diet itself: a good pellet, vegetables two or three times a week, and protein foods in moderation. Anything sold as a goldfish supplement is doing a job the pellet should already be doing.

Pellets & presentation

Pellets, not flakes

A quality pellet formulated for goldfish is the base diet. Tropical flake is not toxic but its nutrient profile is built for a different fish, and flakes lose a meaningful share of some vitamins within about 30 seconds of hitting the water. Read the label for a named protein source near the front of the ingredient list rather than a generic filler, and buy a bag you will finish within a few months: opened fish food degrades, and a cheap large tub that lasts a year is a false economy.

Sinking, not floating, and why it matters more than it sounds

Sinking food matters especially for fancy varieties. Gulping floating pellets at the surface means swallowing air, and swallowed air is a direct contributor to the buoyancy problems round-bodied goldfish are prone to. Soaking pellets in a spoonful of tank water for a minute before feeding is a simple extra step that reduces both air intake and swelling in the gut, and it costs nothing. Gel foods sidestep the problem entirely: they sink, they carry a lot of moisture, and they suit fancy goldfish particularly well.

Scattering sinking food across the substrate rather than dropping it in one spot turns a 30 second meal into several minutes of foraging, and pairs with the sand or fine gravel on page 12. Same food, same portion, considerably more of the day spent doing something.

Feeding a pond goldfish by temperature

Outdoor fish scale their feeding to the water temperature, because their metabolism does. Feeding a normal ration to a fish whose digestion has slowed to a crawl leaves food sitting in the gut and in the water, and both cause problems.

Water temperatureWhat to feed
Above 50°F (10°C)Feed normally, scaled to how quickly it is taken.
39 to 50°F (4 to 10°C)Switch to a reduced, more digestible wheat-germ formula, fed less often.
Below 39°F (4°C)Stop feeding entirely. The fish drops into a low-metabolism dormancy and uneaten food simply fouls the water.

This applies to outdoor ponds only. An indoor tank at normal room temperature stays in the top row all year.

The rich-food trap Bloodworms and blackworms are the two foods goldfish respond to most dramatically, which is exactly why they get overfed. Keep protein foods to roughly once a week for an adult, and never use them to tempt a fish that has gone off its food: appetite loss is a symptom to investigate, on page 22, not a preference to indulge.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Adding fish to an uncycled tank. New tank syndrome is the single most common cause of premature goldfish death. There is no shortcut that beats testing until ammonia and nitrite both read zero.
  • Under-filtering because the tank looks clean. Clear water and safe water are unrelated. Ammonia and nitrite are invisible, and a goldfish generates far more of them than its size suggests.
  • Overfeeding. Begging is not hunger in this species, and every uneaten pellet becomes ammonia. Portion by the clock, not by the fish's enthusiasm.
  • Buying the fish before the tank. The goldfish costs a few dollars and the setup costs a few hundred. Doing it in that order is what produces the bowl.
  • Cleaning too thoroughly. Scrubbing every surface and rinsing media under the tap strips out the bacteria colony and can crash a stable tank in days.
  • Mixing goldfish with tropical fish. A goldfish needs cool water and a tropical community needs a heater. One of the two is always being kept wrong.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after correct volume, filtration, and a fully cycled tank, never instead of them. Decor cannot rescue a bowl or bad water. In this order:

1. Swimming volume

The single largest welfare factor. A long, wide footprint with a genuinely open lane down the middle beats any amount of decor in a small tank.

2. Structure and cover

Research tracking where goldfish spend their time found roughly 90 percent in planted compartments versus about 10 percent in barren open space, with no strong preference for live plants over closely matched artificial ones. It is the structure that matters.

3. Foraging substrate

Coarse sand supported more natural foraging than larger gravel in substrate research, and goldfish kept sifting even when food was not the only driver.

4. Sinking, scattered food

Scattering sinking pellets across the substrate turns one meal into several minutes of foraging. Same food, same portion, much more of the day spent doing something.

5. Same-type company

Goldfish are social. Match fancy with fancy and single-tail with single-tail so body shape and swim speed stay compatible. Only add fish if volume and filtration genuinely support the combined bioload.

6. Training

Goldfish learn readily and are strongly food-motivated. Target feeding, swimming through a hoop, and following a finger are all achievable, and use the daily ration rather than adding food.

Not enrichment Overstocking labeled as company, plants used as an excuse for weak filtration, and extra food framed as a treat are the same mistake wearing three different names. None of them substitute for getting the tank right.

Why we don't ask you to weigh a goldfish

For most pets a weekly weight is the best early warning available. For a fish it is the wrong tool: weighing means netting the fish out, which damages the slime coat for the sake of a number that moves with how recently it ate. Ammonia poisoning & ich

Ammonia and nitrite poisoning

Cause: An uncycled tank, a tank overstocked for its filtration, overfeeding, or a filter crash after media was rinsed in tap water. It is the most common cause of premature goldfish death and it is entirely preventable. Signs: Gasping at the surface, red or purple streaks in the fins, bloodshot or pale gills, lethargy, clamped fins, refusing food, and dark patches on the body as ammonia burns heal. Diagnosis: A test kit, not the fish's appearance. Any reading above 0 ppm for ammonia or nitrite is the diagnosis. Response: A 50 percent water change immediately with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, then repeat daily until both read 0. Stop feeding for 24 to 48 hours to cut the ammonia source. Increase surface agitation. Do not deep clean the filter, which would remove what little bacteria colony you have. Recovery: Gill damage and fin streaking take days to weeks to resolve once the water is right. Severe exposure can leave permanent gill damage.

The one product worth having on the shelf An ammonia-binding water conditioner buys you hours during a spike by converting free ammonia to a form the fish cannot absorb while the bacteria colony catches up. It is a stopgap for an emergency, not a substitute for a water change or for enough filtration.

Ich, or white spot disease

Cause: The protozoan parasite Ichthyophthirius multifiliis, almost always introduced on a new fish, plant, or unquarantined decor, and taking hold in fish stressed by water quality. Signs: Tiny white spots like grains of salt across the body and fins, flashing (rubbing against surfaces), clamped fins, and rapid gill movement if the gills are affected. Diagnosis: Visually distinctive, though worth distinguishing from the finer, dustier sheen of velvet and from the raised white tubercles on a male in spring, covered on page 20. Response: Correct the water first, then treat the whole tank, never the individual fish, since the parasite is in the water and substrate as well.

Why treatment has to outlast the spots Only the free-swimming stage of ich is vulnerable to treatment. While it is embedded in the fish or encysted on the substrate it is chemically untouchable, so treatment has to run long enough to catch every parasite as it becomes free-swimming. That cycle is measured in days for warmwater fish but in weeks for a coldwater fish like a goldfish. Merck's veterinary guidance is constant chemical exposure for at least 3 weeks; continue for at least 2 weeks after the last visible spot, whichever is longer.

Raising the temperature shortens the parasite's cycle, but goldfish are a coldwater species and warm water carries less oxygen, so this is a smaller lever here than in a tropical tank. If you use it, raise gradually over 24 to 48 hours to no more than about 78°F (26°C), add an air stone, and watch for gasping. Aquarium salt is a common adjunct and goldfish tolerate it well; follow the product's dosing or your vet's, add it dissolved and in stages, and remember it leaves the tank only through water changes.

Fin rot, fungus & dropsy

Three conditions that look unrelated and share one underlying cause.

Fin rot

Frayed, ragged or receding fin edges, often with a white or reddened margin, and usually bacterial. It follows poor water quality, an injury, or fin nipping in a badly matched group. Correcting the water is as much of the treatment as any medication: test, do a substantial water change, and keep up a schedule of them. If the erosion is advancing into the body of the fin after several days of clean water, an antibacterial treatment is warranted, and a vet's input is worth having before you dose an established tank. Fins regrow, though the new tissue often comes back paler or slightly misshapen.

Fungal infections

Cotton-like white or grey tufts, most often on a spot that was already injured or on a fish that has been stressed. Fungus is a secondary problem almost by definition: it takes an opening to get established. That makes prevention mostly a matter of avoiding unnecessary handling, keeping decor smooth, and not letting a minor injury sit in dirty water. Antifungal treatments exist and work; the more important step is finding the injury or stressor that let it in.

Dropsy

A swollen belly with scales standing out from the body in a pinecone pattern, most visible looking down from above. Dropsy is not a disease in itself, it is a visible sign of fluid accumulation, usually from organ or kidney failure, and it frequently carries a poor prognosis. It is not a condition to experiment on with over-the-counter products. Get expert or veterinary help promptly, isolate the fish if you have a cycled hospital tank ready, and keep the water pristine in the meantime.

Pinecone scales are an emergency, not a wait-and-see By the time scales are visibly raised, the underlying problem has usually been developing for a while. Fish caught at the very earliest stage sometimes recover with veterinary treatment. Fish left another week generally do not.
The common thread All three of these are opportunistic. The bacteria and fungi involved are present in every aquarium at all times and cause nothing in a healthy fish in clean, stable, well-oxygenated water. They become infections when something else has already weakened the fish: an ammonia spike, a temperature swing, an injury, crowding, or a badly matched tankmate. This is why the first response to every condition in this section is a test kit rather than a bottle, and why the routine on page 32 does more for your fish's health than anything on a pharmacy shelf.

Swim bladder disorder & minor conditions

Less dramatic than the last page, and far more common.

Swim bladder disorder and constipation

Buoyancy trouble: floating at the surface, sinking to the bottom, or swimming at a persistent angle or upside down. It is much more common in round-bodied fancy varieties, whose compressed body shape crowds the swim bladder and the gut. The usual triggers are diet-related: overfeeding, constipation, and gulping air at the surface while taking floating food. Start with 24 to 48 hours of fasting, then a single cooked, de-shelled pea, and switch permanently to sinking or pre-soaked food. Not every case resolves with diet, and a fish that stays affected for more than a week or two, or that also has swelling or reddening, needs a vet: infection, an internal tumor and physical injury all produce the same outward sign.

Flukes, gill and skin

Microscopic monogenean worms causing flashing, rapid gill movement, excess mucus and irritation with nothing visible on the fish. They are easy to miss and frequently the answer when a fish behaves like it has ich but has no spots. Merck lists praziquantel at 5 mg/L as a prolonged bath as the treatment of choice for ornamental fish, repeated at weekly intervals because the eggs resist treatment.

Anchor worm and fish lice

Visible parasites. Anchor worm (Lernaea) appears as a thread-like projection with a red, inflamed attachment point; fish lice as flat, disc-shaped crawlers. Both usually arrive with pond fish or unquarantined stock. Merck gives diflubenzuron at 0.03 mg/L once as most effective, with a 3 percent (30 g/L) salt dip of under 10 minutes followed by salt at 5 g/L in the tank for 3 weeks as an alternative for freshwater fish. Do not pull an anchor worm out without veterinary guidance; the embedded head tears the tissue and leaves a wound.

Velvet

A fine gold or rust-colored dust over the skin, best seen with a flashlight held at an angle in a darkened room. Finer and more uniform than ich's distinct spots. Treat the whole tank and dim the lights, since the organism is partly photosynthetic.

Popeye and ulcers

A protruding eye is either an injury, usually in one eye, or a systemic infection, often in both. Open red sores or raised reddened patches are bacterial ulcers, and they follow the same water quality story as fin rot. Both warrant a vet rather than a shelf product, particularly when more than one fish is affected.

Medicate in a hospital tank, not the display tank Most of the treatments named on this page are cheaper in a small volume, easier to dose accurately, and safer for your biological filter, several of them kill filter bacteria outright. The exception is ich, which lives in the substrate and water as well as on the fish, so the whole tank has to be treated. Keep a cycled sponge filter running spare, as on page 13, and a hospital tank is twenty minutes of setup rather than a project.

Behavior, spawning & reading waste

Spawning behavior in spring

As temperatures rise in late spring, mature goldfish in a mixed group may spawn. Males chase a female persistently, often pinning her against the glass or plants, and both may go off food for a few days. This is hormone-driven and normal rather than aggression, and it usually resolves in days to a couple of weeks. Watch for a female being pursued to exhaustion or losing scales, which is the point to separate the group. Eggs are scattered over plants and surfaces and will mostly be eaten by the adults, which is the normal outcome unless you deliberately set up to raise fry.

Winter slowdown

Pond goldfish drop into a low-metabolism dormancy as water cools, settling near the bottom and eating little or nothing below about 39°F (4°C). This is normal, and it does not apply to an indoor tank held at room temperature. An indoor goldfish that suddenly becomes sluggish has a water quality or health problem, not a season.

Power outages, travel & transport

For a fish, a power cut is an oxygen problem first and a temperature problem a distant second.

When the pumps stop, the water stops moving and gas exchange stops with it. The fish will use the available oxygen faster than a still surface can replace it, and the filter's bacteria colony begins dying within hours without flow. A goldfish at room temperature is in far less danger from cold than a tropical fish would be, which is why aeration is essentially the whole job here.

Do

  • Run a battery-powered air pump. A single deep-cycle unit can keep a tank aerated for a day or more, and models with a power-save cycle stretch that further.
  • With no air pump, scoop out a cup of water and pour it back from a height every hour or two. Crude, but it genuinely moves oxygen into the water.
  • Stop feeding. Uneaten food and waste consume oxygen and produce ammonia with no filter to process it.
  • If the outage runs past a few hours, take the biological media out of the dead filter and put it in an aerated container or directly in the tank so the bacteria survive.

Don't

  • Don't restart a filter that has been off for many hours without rinsing the media first. Stagnant media dumps a slug of decomposed waste straight into the tank.
  • Don't do a large water change in the dark with untested, unconditioned water.
  • Don't wrap or insulate the tank for a goldfish the way you would for a tropical species. Room temperature is well within range.
  • Don't use candles, a gas stove, or any open flame near the tank.

Leaving town

Trip lengthWhat is actually needed
1 to 3 nightsNothing. A healthy adult goldfish in an established tank is comfortable fasting for 3 to 4 days. Do a water change before you leave and skip the automatic feeder entirely.
4 to 7 nightsA visit or two to check the filter is running and nothing has died. Pre-portion the food into daily containers so a sitter cannot overfeed. Beyond about 5 days without food, condition starts to suffer.
Over a weekA sitter doing a real visit every two or three days, with one water change if the trip runs long. Walk them through the routine in person first.

Transport and vet trips

  • Move the fish in a bag or sealed container of its own tank water, roughly one third water and two thirds air, inside an insulated cooler or box so it stays dark and the temperature stays stable.
  • Do not feed for 24 hours before a move. Less waste in the water means less ammonia during the trip.
  • For a journey over an hour, a battery air pump with an air stone in the container is worth the trouble, and a second bag as backup against a leak costs nothing.
  • On arrival, acclimate as you would a new fish: float, mix, then net across, as on page 13.
  • Never leave the container in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly.

Glossary

AmmoniaThe first waste product of fish waste and decaying food, and highly toxic. The target reading is 0 ppm in a cycled tank.
BioloadThe total waste output a tank's filtration and water volume have to process. Goldfish have an unusually high bioload for their size.
ChloramineA chlorine and ammonia compound used in many municipal water supplies. Unlike chlorine it does not gas off if water is left standing, so a conditioner is required.
CyclingEstablishing the bacteria colony that converts ammonia to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate. Takes roughly four to six weeks in a new tank.
DechlorinatorA water conditioner that neutralizes chlorine and chloramine in tap water before it reaches the fish.
DropsyNot a disease but a sign: fluid swelling that lifts the scales into a pinecone pattern, usually from organ or kidney failure.
Fancy goldfishThe round-bodied ornamental varieties (oranda, ranchu, fantail, ryukin, moor). Slower swimmers, most prone to buoyancy problems.
FlashingRubbing or scraping the body against surfaces. A sign of irritation from parasites or poor water quality.
GH and KHGeneral hardness (dissolved minerals) and carbonate hardness (the water's ability to resist pH swings). Low KH lets pH crash suddenly.
GravidCarrying eggs. A gravid female often looks fuller and asymmetrical viewed from above.
Hospital tankA small, separately cycled tank used for treating a sick fish, so medications do not reach the display tank's bacteria colony.
IchWhite spot disease, caused by Ichthyophthirius multifiliis. Only its free-swimming stage can be treated, which is why treatment must outlast the visible spots.
New tank syndromeAmmonia and nitrite poisoning in a tank stocked before it finished cycling. The most common cause of early goldfish death.
NitrateThe end product of the nitrogen cycle. Far less toxic than ammonia or nitrite, but keep it under about 20 ppm with water changes.
NitriteThe intermediate product of the nitrogen cycle, also highly toxic. The target reading is 0 ppm.
QuarantineHousing a new or sick fish in a separate cycled tank, 2 to 4 weeks minimum and 4 to 6 weeks preferably, before it meets an established group.
Single-tailThe streamlined varieties: common, comet and shubunkin. Larger, faster and much better suited to a pond than a tank.
Slime coatThe living protective layer over a goldfish's scales. It takes 24 to 48 hours to regenerate if damaged by handling.
StuntingSuppressed body growth from cramped space and chronic ammonia exposure while organs and skeleton keep developing. A welfare problem, not a size feature.
Swim bladderThe internal organ controlling buoyancy. Problems with it cause floating, sinking, or swimming at an angle.
TuberclesRough white breeding spots on the gill plates and pectoral fin rays of a mature male in breeding condition. Often mistaken for ich.
TurnoverHow many times per hour a filter processes the tank's total volume. Aim for 4 times minimum, 5 to 10 preferred.
