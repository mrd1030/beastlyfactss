Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a hognose, quarantine & the first vet visit

Captive-bred, eating frozen-thawed mice, and kept apart for months. In that order.

Buy captive-bred. Compared with wild-caught animals, captive-bred reptiles are less likely to bite, more likely to accept the food offered, and less likely to carry parasites. A breeder who hatched the snake can tell you when it hatched, what it eats, and when it last ate and shed. A shop or breeder is worth buying from only if its enclosures are clean and large enough. A rescue snake often arrives with no reliable history, and an expo puts animals from many sellers in one room for a day. Neither is a reason to refuse the animal. Both are reasons to run the full quarantine below.

Ask the seller: the hatch date; whether it was captive-bred; what it eats, when it last ate, and whether it takes plain or scented frozen-thawed mice; whether it has been treated for anything, mites or a respiratory infection above all; and for a receipt naming Heterodon nasicus. A good breeder answers without looking anything up.

Quarantine: 3 to 6 months

Quarantine means keeping a new animal completely apart from any other reptile. The veterinary figure for a new reptile is 3 to 6 months; the hobby often quotes 60 to 90 days as a floor. If this is your only reptile, the separation rules below have nothing to protect, but the first vet visit and its fecal test still apply.

RuleHow
A separate roomA second enclosure on the same shelf shares the same air. A different room is the real separation
Paper towel floorIt shows mites, stool and regurgitated food at a glance. Just a hide and a water dish, things you can disinfect or throw out
Separate toolsTongs, dish, scoop, even the paper towel roll
New animal lastCare for established animals first, every time. Wash your hands, or change gloves, between
Restart the clockIf anything flags, a positive fecal test, a regurgitation, unexplained weight loss, start the window again
Find the vet before the snake comes home Not every practice treats reptiles. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians runs a find-a-vet directory. Ask a clinic how many reptiles it sees, whether it treats snakes routinely, whether it runs fecal tests (stool checks for parasites) in house, and who covers emergencies after hours. Book the first exam within 72 hours of bringing the snake home, and no later than two weeks, with a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag. That visit records a baseline weight and the snake healthy.

Telling the sex, weight & body condition

Sex is a vet's job. Weight and body condition are yours, every week.

Telling male from female

Snakes show no outside sign of sex that an owner can trust. Two hands-on methods work, and both are procedures with real technique behind them:

MethodHow it worksWho should do it
ProbingA lubricated, blunt metal probe slides into the vent and back along the tail. In a male it goes deeper, into the pocket holding a hemipenis (one of a male snake's paired reproductive organs), about 6 to 14 scales; in a female it stops at about 3 to 6A reptile vet or experienced breeder only. The wrong size or angle injures the snake
PoppingGentle pressure at the base of a hatchling's tail turns the hemipenes outward in a maleA reptile vet or experienced breeder only. Too much force injures the tail
UltrasoundImaging that shows the internal organs. Reliable in snakes and needs no handling of the ventA reptile vet

Tail shape is a weak guess on one animal with nothing to compare it against. In an adult hognose, size is a strong hint, because females run so much larger, but it is a hint, not confirmation.

Weigh it every week

Weight, not length, is the number that tells you how a snake is doing. Weigh it once a week on a kitchen scale in grams: set a lidded tub on the scale, zero it, then add the snake. One reading tells you little. Three or four in a row, climbing, holding or falling, tell you a great deal, and it is the direction of that line that should drive a feeding change or a vet call. Use page {{P:species}} for rough adult weights, and your own snake's record for everything else.

The weight line to watch A drop of more than 10% from the snake's normal weight, or a steady fall across several weeks, is a vet visit, whatever the season. A sudden drop is the first thing to show the vet.

Body condition: the cross-section test

Support the snake and look at it end-on, picturing a slice straight across its body.

ShapeWhat you see and feelDo
RightA rounded loaf, full and even, no sharp angles. Muscle over bone along the backKeep going
Too thinTriangular, sloping away from a ridge down the middle of the back where the spine sits close to the surfaceOver several checks, and with falling weight, a vet visit: thinness is as often parasites or illness as underfeeding
Too heavyRound in a different way: skin folds along the sides, or a soft, doughy feel over the ribsFeed smaller or less often, page {{P:feeding}}. Do not stop feeding outright

The 9-point body condition scale used for dogs and cats does not apply to a snake's body. The cross-section check is the one that fits, and it is the one a reptile vet uses.

Females, eggs & eggs that get stuck

For anyone keeping a female that has been with a male, or bought from a breeder who paired her.

This page is not a breeding guide. It is what a keeper of a female needs to recognize, so that a normal clutch is handled calmly and a stuck one gets to a vet in time. A female is gravid when she is carrying eggs.

What a normal clutch looks like

StageWhat happens
MatingIn the wild, June to August. Wait until a female is at least 2 years old, preferably closer to 3, and 150 to 200 g
The pre-lay shedShe sheds, then lays on average 7 to 11 days later. That shed is your signal to put the laying box in
The clutch4 to 23 eggs in a wild female, around 13 on average. Captive clutches have topped 30
Hatching52 to 64 days in the wild. In an incubator at 78 to 82°F (25.6 to 27.8°C), 51 to 60 days

The laying box

A plastic food container with a hole cut in the lid, filled about halfway with loamy soil. Put it in after the pre-lay shed and leave her alone. Solitude and a suitable place to lay are what she needs, and an inadequate nest site is one of the causes of eggs getting stuck.

Egg binding

Egg binding, also called dystocia, is when a female cannot pass her eggs. It is life-threatening.

Egg binding
CausePoor husbandry, an inadequate nest site, an inappropriate diet, and dehydration
SignsA female past her expected laying window who becomes lethargic, weak or unresponsive. It is not usually sudden: reptiles can hold eggs for weeks or even months, so a female that has not laid when expected needs watching closely
DiagnosisThe vet examines her and uses X-ray images and ultrasound to find the eggs
ResponseImmediate vet evaluation. The vet may try medical treatment and better conditions, but medical management often fails and surgery is needed in most cases. Never give any medication at home: the vet chooses the treatment and sets any dose
RecoveryDepends on how long the eggs were held and what the surgery involved. She rests, and the vet decides when feeding restarts
A female kept alone Whether a female hognose kept on her own will produce eggs is not well documented. If your adult female swells, sheds and then turns restless or stops eating with no male in the picture, describe it to your vet rather than guessing.
Section 06 · The Law

Is a hognose legal where you live?

Broadly legal. The exceptions turn on native-species law far more often than on venom, and on which hognose you have.

This section covers all 50 states, Washington, D.C. (the District of Columbia) and New York City, which has its own rules. Of those 52, a pet hognose is legal in 27, conditional in 15, permit-only in 4, and banned in 6. The note beside each says which hognose the rule reaches, because in several states that differs between the western and the eastern. Three of the six bans, Georgia, New York State and West Virginia, reach only their native hognoses, not the western.

LegalThe state's own rules allow a pet hognose with no permitPermitLegal, but a permit or license comes first
ConditionalLegal without a permit only if a stated condition is met, very often which species it isBannedCannot be kept as a pet, and no permit route exists
The venom question, legally The mild venom almost never decides the answer. Several state venomous-reptile laws name hognoses as an exception: Kentucky and Alabama write "except for hognose snakes (genus Heterodon)" into the rule itself. What catches hognoses is native-wildlife law. The eastern and southern hognoses are native and often protected, and a few states protect the western as their own native too. The one place the venom itself decides it is New York City.

Banned

HawaiiEvery snake is banned, with no exception for harmless species
KansasBoth Kansas hognoses, the western (named in the rule as the plains hog-nosed snake) and the eastern, are listed as nongame species in need of conservation, and no rule or permit allows keeping one. An animal held before the rule may be kept only if an affidavit was filed and approved before 1 January 1990
New York StateThe eastern hognose is a protected native snake with no open season and a species of special concern, and a captive-bred animal from another state is treated the same. The only possession license is not issued for pets. The western is not a New York species and the state rule reaches only the eastern, so which hognose decides it. Confirm with the Department of Environmental Conservation
New York CityThe city health code bans every venomous, rear-fanged colubrid (a member of the snake family Colubridae) with a Duvernoy's gland, "even if devenomized." That reaches the western hognose. A prohibited animal can be seized
West VirginiaThe state bars keeping any native reptile not on a short permitted list, which names no snake. The eastern hog-nosed snake is on the state's checklist of native snakes

WhereWhy
IllinoisThe western hognose is listed as a threatened species in Illinois, so keeping one needs a Department of Natural Resources threatened species possession permit, and breeding or selling one needs a separate propagation permit at $150 a year. The eastern is not listed and falls under the ordinary cap of 4 per species and 8 in total. Cities can be stricter

New MexicoAll three hognoses are on the importation list in Group III, a permit with specific conditions attached. The rule covers bringing an animal into the state rather than keeping one already there

States with conditions

Fifteen states where a hognose is legal only on a stated condition. Read the condition, then check which species you have.
WhereThe condition
AlabamaOnly the southern hognose is protected, and keeping one needs a scientific collection permit or a written permit from the Commissioner. The eastern and the western are unrestricted, and the state's venomous-reptile ban names hognoses as an exception
ColoradoA captive-bred western hognose is allowed with documentation and no license, under the rule in effect from 1 May 2026. The eastern is excluded by name. A hognose taken from the wild in Colorado is lawful only if held on or before 1 May 2026 and reported by 30 July 2026; no new wild capture. Check your city or county too
ConnecticutThe eastern is a State Species of Special Concern and needs a permit to import or possess. The western is not a Connecticut species and is unrestricted
DelawareThe eastern is native and not available for private ownership, except a captive-bred animal in an unusual color form held under permit. The western, not a Delaware species, needs an Individual permit from the Department of Agriculture
IndianaThe eastern is an Indiana native: selling, offering or transporting one for sale is barred whatever its origin, and keeping is capped at 4 of a native species. MarylandThe eastern: one per person without a permit. The western is outside the rule and unrestricted. Counties can be stricter, so check the local rule first
MassachusettsThe western is exempt by name. The eastern is a state species of special concern, which puts it in a permit tier the state does not issue for pets, captive-bred or not

MinnesotaA snake from a permitted breeder, or one held before 1 August 2021, may be kept as a pet. Both hognoses are Minnesota natives. The research notes the breeder permit system is not visibly in place yet, so ask the Department of Natural Resources how a buyer meets the condition
NebraskaBoth hognoses are capped at 3 per species for collected animals. One legally bought from outside Nebraska may be kept with no number limit, if you can show proof of legal purchase such as a bill of sale. Keep the receipt
OklahomaBoth hognoses reach Oklahoma. A hunting license is required to take or possess a land reptile, with a limit of 6 per species, and state law exempts a legally obtained pet from a source other than the wild from the possession ban. PennsylvaniaThe eastern is native, with no open season and a limit of zero, and cannot be imported. The western is outside the rule, so a captive-bred western from a shop is lawful
TexasBoth hognoses are on the state nongame list: up to 25 per species without a permit, and no selling, trading or bartering any of them. This applies to captive-bred animals too
VirginiaThe eastern is a Species of Greatest Conservation Need and may not be possessed for private use in any number. The western is a nonnative animal and needs no permit
WisconsinThe eastern is native, capped at 5 until the end of 2026. From 1 January 2027 only captive-bred color variants from a legal source may be kept, and a wild-type eastern already held must be registered with the Department of Natural Resources by 30 June 2027. The western is not a Wisconsin species

Before you buy, wherever you live

  • Know the species. Get Heterodon nasicus and "captive-bred" on the receipt. In most of the conditional states, that line is the condition.
  • Never collect a wild hognose. Wild collection is restricted or banned in many states that allow captive-bred pets, and the eastern and southern hognoses are protected in several.
  • Check local rules. A city or county ordinance can be stricter than the state, and no state list covers it.
  • Moving? Check the new state before you go. A legal pet in one state can be capped, permit-only or banned in the next.
  • Keep the paperwork: the receipt, any permit, and the breeder's details. Nebraska and Colorado both turn on proof of legal origin.
