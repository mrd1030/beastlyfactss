Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & finding a vet

Western hognoses are hardy. Most of what goes wrong traces back to humidity, substrate or feeding, all within your control.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see Open-mouth breathing, wheezing, drooling or mucus · no droppings with bloating and lethargy · belly scales discolored or blistered and getting worse · red spots on the gums, excess saliva or cheesy material in the mouth · mites · a burn · anything protruding from the vent · a meal brought back up twice · weight down more than 10% · limp or unresponsive at normal temperature · an eye cap still on after a shed · a female past her laying window who is weak or listless

What to tell the vet

Basking, cool-side and night temperatures, and what controls the heat
Humidity, and where you measured it
The last meal: date, prey, and whether it stayed down
When the signs started and whether they are getting worse
The last normal dropping, and what it looked like
The weight record from page {{P:ownerlog}}, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag

Why the setup comes up first

Two of the most common problems in this species, respiratory infection and scale rot, share one cause: humidity or dampness running too high. Feeding on loose substrate causes impaction. Overfeeding against the snake's activity causes obesity. So a reptile vet will ask about your setup before anything else, and having the numbers ready shortens the visit. Snakes also hide illness well, which is why a weekly weight and a written record of meals, sheds and behavior catch problems before they show.

Respiratory & belly-scale infections

Two of the most common problems in this species, and the same root cause: too much moisture.

Respiratory infection

CauseHumidity running too high, temperatures too cool, or poor ventilation. This species prefers a dry setup, and dampness is more of a problem for it than for many other snakes. Unsanitary conditions, other illness and poor nutrition also contribute
SignsOpen-mouth breathing, wheezing, drooling or visible mucus, discharge from the nose, difficulty breathing, and lethargy
DiagnosisA reptile vet's examination, together with your temperature and humidity readings
ResponseAlways see a vet: it needs antibiotics and will not resolve on its own. The vet chooses the drug and sets the dose; never give leftover or bought antibiotics. At home, fix the cause at the same time: dry the enclosure, clean it, and hold the warm end correctly, because a sick snake is kept toward the upper end of its normal range. A respiratory infection gets worse in a snake kept too cool
RecoveryDepends on how early it is caught and on fixing the setup. If the humidity stays high, expect it to come back
The best tool for catching it early A digital thermometer and hygrometer, because they let you confirm the enclosure is running dry and warm rather than guessing. Page {{P:humidity}}.

Scale rot (blister disease)

CauseDamp, dirty substrate
SignsDiscolored or blistered belly scales. Blister disease is the early stage of scale rot: the skin develops pustules (small pus-filled bumps) or blisters
DiagnosisA vet's examination of the skin
ResponseMove the snake to a clean, dry setup and keep the substrate dry and clean, which is what this species needs anyway. See a vet if it moves past mild discoloration; any treatment and dose are the vet's call
RecoveryCaught early, the blisters may heal without progressing to open sores. Left alone, they can
One cause, two diseases If you see either of these, read the hygrometer before anything else. A western hognose kept at 30 to 50% humidity, with no misting and a dry, clean bed, is very unlikely to see either one.

Blockages, regurgitated meals & obesity

Three feeding problems, each with a feeding fix.

Impaction

Cause: a gut blocked by swallowed substrate. Hognoses are enthusiastic, somewhat messy eaters and are prone to swallowing substrate along with their food. Signs: a missed stool past its usual gap after a meal, then bloating, lethargy and refused food. The missed stool usually comes first. Diagnosis: a vet's examination, often with a radiograph. Response: there is no fixed number of days to wait, since the gap depends on meal size and temperature. A missed stool together with bloating, lethargy, refused food or regurgitation needs a reptile vet, not more waiting, and severe cases can need surgery. Prevention is feeding in a separate container, page {{P:thawing}}. Recovery: depends on the blockage and how it was cleared; afterward, every meal goes in the feeding container.

Regurgitation

A hognose that brings up a meal undigested or half digested is telling you something went wrong around feeding. Cause: handling within 48 hours of a meal, an enclosure running too cool, stress, a meal that was too big, dehydration, a dirty enclosure, intestinal parasites, contaminated prey, or sharing the enclosure with another snake. Signs: the meal found in the enclosure or feeding tub. Diagnosis: working through that list, and a vet if it repeats, since repeated regurgitation after meals is also the main sign of Cryptosporidium (a stomach parasite, next page).

After a regurgitation Leave the snake completely alone: no handling and no feeding. Check the temperatures. After two weeks of steady improvement, offer a small meal, a quarter to half the usual size, then rest it again until the next feeding. A snake that looks worse after regurgitating, or brings up that small meal too, goes to a reptile vet.

Recovery: most snakes return to normal meals once the cause is fixed. The repeat is what matters, so log every one.

Obesity and fatty liver

Cause: in captivity a hognose gets less activity than in the wild and is sometimes fed too much or too often for its enclosure. The most common version is an adult kept on a hatchling's schedule. It is a real contributor to shortened lifespans in this species, and the serious end of it is fatty liver disease. Signs: a round cross-section with skin folds along the sides or a doughy feel over the ribs (page {{P:growth}}), and a weight that keeps climbing in an adult. Diagnosis: body condition and the weight record; a vet confirms and checks the liver. Response: match feeding frequency and portion to the adult's activity and size, page {{P:feeding}}: smaller meals or longer gaps, not a sudden fast. Recovery: slow and steady over months, tracked on the scale.

Gout, the other cost of overfeeding Diets high in protein may predispose a reptile to uric acid building up, which can cause gout. Gout can be very painful, to the point that a reptile refuses to move, eat or drink. Yellow or gritty urates are an early hint. Diagnosis and any treatment are the vet's.

Mites & internal parasites

Both are treatable, and both are why a new snake is quarantined. The third one here is not.

Snake mites

CauseTiny blood-feeding parasites, Ophionyssus natricis, most under 1.5 mm (millimeters) long. They spread by direct contact and shared equipment, almost always arriving on a new animal, used decor or tools
SignsTiny black, brown or red specks around the eyes, chin and vent, on the shed skin, or moving on the snake or the glass. Rubbing, lethargy, a lost appetite, coarse-looking skin, bad sheds, and long soaking in the water dish, as if trying to drown them
DiagnosisRub the snake gently over a sheet of white paper: mites that fall off show up against it. The vet confirms
ResponseA reptile vet treats the snake and sets the product, dose and schedule. You treat the enclosure the same week: clean it thoroughly, and throw away the substrate, branches and anything disposable. Use paper towel until the vet says it is clear
RecoveryGood when the snake and the enclosure are both treated. Treat only one and the mites come back. A heavy infestation causes anemia (blood loss) and can carry infections
Never treat a hognose for mites on your own Permethrin-based products (a common insecticide), pest strips and some over-the-counter mite sprays can be highly toxic to hognoses. Veterinary medicine does use a permethrin licensed for reptiles, which is exactly why the choice and the dose belong to the vet, not to a pet store shelf.

Internal parasites

Cause: worms and single-celled parasites, more common in wild-caught animals. Signs: weight loss despite eating, repeated watery droppings, regurgitation, or none at all. Diagnosis: a fecal test, which is why every new snake has one during quarantine and why periodic checks are worth it after. Response: a vet-prescribed antiparasitic drug at a dose the vet sets, plus a full enclosure clean. Recovery: usually good; the vet may retest to confirm.

Cryptosporidium

CauseCryptosporidium serpentis, a single-celled parasite of the stomach, and a serious concern in this species
SignsRegurgitation after meals, marked weight loss, slow decline, and often a firm swelling in the stomach region of the body that a vet can feel
DiagnosisA special stain of fresh droppings, or of the coating on a regurgitated meal
ResponseIsolate the snake and its tools, and see a vet. Several treatments have been tried and none has been especially effective
RecoveryThere is no reliable cure. That is the strongest argument on this page for buying captive-bred and running the full quarantine on page {{P:arrival}}. The species found in reptiles is not thought to infect people

Shedding, mouth infections, burns & tissue at the vent

One normal process to support, and three problems that each need a vet.

The shed cycle

Young hognoses shed most often. A shed is coming when the colors fade, the eyes turn cloudy blue, and the snake eats less, hides more and turns more defensive. The eyes stay cloudy for about 4 to 7 days, then clear, and the shed follows about 4 to 7 days after that. Leave it alone for that stretch: no handling and no rearranging. Shedding is called ecdysis.

Helping a dry-country snake shed Keep the rest of the enclosure dry and add moisture in one spot: mix water into the substrate in the humid hide to help a shed start or finish. Rough surfaces such as cork bark give it something to rub against. After the shed, find the skin and check that both eye caps and the tail tip came away with it.

Stuck shed, called dysecdysis, has six common causes: low humidity, skin parasites, nutritional problems, infectious disease, nothing rough to rub against, and low thyroid function. A shed that comes off in pieces now and then is not alarming; a pattern of bad sheds in a correct setup is a vet question. A retained eye cap (the clear scale over each eye, also called the spectacle) that extra moisture does not loosen needs a vet, and you never remove it yourself: left on, it can cause permanent eye damage and blindness.

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

Cause: infection and inflammation of the tissue lining the mouth. Signs: tiny purplish-red spots in the mouth early, then excess saliva and cheesy yellow material along the teeth, and refused meals. Diagnosis: a vet's look inside the mouth. Response: a vet removes dead tissue, cleans with an antiseptic, and prescribes antibiotics and supportive care, at doses the vet sets. Recovery: best when treated early. A snake that is not eating because of it needs the vet's follow-up plan.

Burns

Cause: a snake resting on a spot that is too hot, or staying on a hot spot too long: an exposed lamp or bulb, a heat rock, an unregulated heat source, or a radiator or baseboard heater after an escape. Signs: damaged or discolored skin where the snake lay against the heat. Diagnosis and response: a vet, every time, plus the thermostat and screen fixes on page {{P:heating}}. Recovery: the vet judges it by how deep the burn goes.

Prolapse

Cause: tissue pushed out through the vent, the single rear opening, usually after straining: from impaction or constipation, dehydration, parasites, egg binding or bladder stones. The cloaca (the chamber just inside the vent), a male's hemipenes or part of the colon can be involved. Signs: moist tissue visible outside the vent. Response: an emergency. Keep the tissue clean and moist with a damp paper towel or clean cloth, do not push it back in, and get to a vet immediately: exposed tissue dries and swells fast. Diagnosis and recovery: the vet cleans and replaces the tissue and looks for the cause, because without fixing the cause it tends to come back.

Reading droppings & hydration

The cheapest daily health check there is. Look before you scoop.

A snake does not pass liquid urine the way a mammal does. Its kidneys pull nitrogen waste out of the blood as uric acid, which leaves as urates: a white to cream, chalky or toothpaste-like paste. Because a snake has a single rear opening, the cloaca, the urates come out with the stool in the same pass. A snake generally does not pass a dropping until a meal is mostly digested, so the rhythm follows feeding: weekly for a weekly-fed juvenile, much less often for an adult fed every two weeks. Learn your own snake's normal; a real change from it is the signal.

Keeping a dry-country snake hydrated

Dry air does not mean a thirsty snake. A large water dish, emptied and refilled with fresh water every day, is the hydration plan, and the humid hide is the one damp spot in the enclosure. The urate is your fastest daily hydration read: smooth and creamy generally means a well-hydrated snake, and dry, hard or gritty is the signal to act. Dehydration is also on the list of causes of regurgitation, prolapse and egg binding, so it is worth getting right.

A soaking snake Soaking now and then, especially before a shed, is normal. A snake that suddenly will not leave its water dish is worth a close look at the eyes, chin and vent for mites.
