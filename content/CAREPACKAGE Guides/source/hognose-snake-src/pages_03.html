Section 03 · Feeding

Prey size & feeding schedule

A hatchling can weigh 3 g. At that size the question is not which mouse, but which part of one.

Mice are the staple and the best diet for this species, fed frozen-thawed: frozen, then thawed before feeding (page {{P:thawing}}). Never live: a live rodent can injure a snake that does not subdue it at once, and a 3 g hatchling has no margin for that. Larger individuals sometimes take small rats. Gerbils, hamsters, multimammate mice and chicks are fallbacks for a snake that will take nothing else.

Prey size for hatchlings, by the snake's weight

The chart runs by weight, which is more useful than age for an animal that arrives at wildly different sizes. Weigh the snake on a kitchen scale in grams.

Snake weighsPrey
3 g and underA small pinky head. A pinky is a newborn, hairless mouse
4 to 6 gExtra small pinkies
7 to 9 gSmall to medium pinkies
Larger juvenilesLarge pinkies to peach fuzzies, then peach fuzzies to fuzzies. A fuzzy is a young mouse with its first fur; a peach fuzzy sits between

Past the hatchling stage, size prey by proportion: a prey item slightly thicker than the snake at its thickest point. A 70 g adult male may settle on a hopper (a young, weaned mouse) or a couple of fuzzies. A large adult female works up to considerably bigger prey.

How often

SnakeFeed every
Hatchlings and juveniles5 to 7 days, or simply weekly
Adult malesAbout every two weeks, the default for any adult. Metabolism slows with maturity
Mature females10 to 14 days; some large females 14 to 21
The most common feeding mistake with this species Keeping an adult on a hatchling's schedule. An adult fed weekly gets fat, and obesity is a real contributor to shortened lifespans in hognoses. Match the frequency and the portion to the adult's activity and size, not to the schedule that suited it as a fast-growing juvenile. Diets high in protein may also predispose a reptile to uric acid building up in the body, which can cause gout, a painful disease of the joints and organs. Page {{P:impaction}}.

Keep a feeding record

Write down every meal: the date, the prey, and whether it was taken. Weigh the snake once a week on the same scale. A hognose that is getting sick often shows it on the scale before it shows it anywhere else, and a feeding record is what turns "it seems off" into something a vet can use. The Owner Log on page {{P:ownerlog}} is set up for exactly this.

Section 03 · Feeding

Frozen prey: thawing, storage & where to feed

The part most keepers get sloppy on, and it matters more than the schedule.

Thawing

  • In the refrigerator until fully thawed, the standard method. Or seal it in a bag and submerge it in cool to lukewarm water, changing the water as needed, until there is no frozen core left.
  • Never on a counter at room temperature for hours, and never in a microwave. A microwave heats unevenly, leaving a frozen center beside hot pockets that can burn the snake's mouth or make the prey burst.
  • Offer it at room temperature or a little warmer, never cold from the refrigerator and never hot. Warm it gently, for example by running warm water over the outside of the sealed bag, and check often by touch.
  • Offer it from feeding tongs, never your fingers. Most bites in this species are feeding mistakes, page {{P:venom}}.

Storage

  • Food held at a steady 0°F (−18°C) stays safe indefinitely, but quality declines the longer it sits. Keep prey sealed and airtight, and do not let stock sit for a year or more.
  • Thaw once. A thawed rodent is never refrozen. If the snake will not eat it, it is a loss, not leftovers.
Frozen rodents carry Salmonella too Federal health officials have tied several Salmonella outbreaks in people to frozen feeder rodents. Wash your hands with soap and water right after handling them or anything where they were stored or thawed. Keep them away from where human food is stored, made or eaten, use containers and utensils that never touch human food, never thaw prey in the kitchen sink you use for dishes, and never use a kitchen microwave or kitchen utensils on it.

Where to feed: a separate container

Hognoses are enthusiastic, somewhat messy eaters and are prone to swallowing substrate along with their food. Feed in a separate container, away from the loose substrate this species needs. It is the single most effective way to prevent impaction (page {{P:impaction}}), and it is worth doing as standard practice rather than only after a problem.

StepHow
The containerA plain lidded plastic tub with air holes, lined with paper towel. Plain, lined, boring, easy to clean: the one place the setup should look nothing like the display enclosure
Moving the snake inLift it out of the substrate with a snake hook, never a bare hand that smells of mouse. Most bites happen with a hungry snake, or while it is being moved into a feeding container
Offering the preyFrom tongs, never fingers
Moving it backOnce it has swallowed, move it back gently and briefly, by hook or by letting it crawl, with no handling session. Then leave it alone for 48 hours

If the snake refuses, throw the prey out rather than saving it, since thawed prey is never refrozen, and work through page {{P:refusals}}.

Section 03 · Feeding

The refusing hognose

This species has a reputation for turning down food. A refusal is usually a puzzle, not an emergency.

A hatchling that turns down a meal is not automatically in trouble, and neither is an adult that skips a few. Work through the causes in order: first the enclosure, then the season and the snake's own cycle, then the prey itself. A refusal becomes a vet question when it comes with weight loss or any other sign of illness. A hatchling or juvenile has far less in reserve than an adult, so weigh a young snake every week through any refusal and call the vet sooner.

Scenting

One practical threshold: start scenting if a snake refuses its first three meal offerings. Scent the thawed prey with one of these:

  • Fish: sardine, tuna or salmon.
  • Chicken bone broth.
  • Frog, toad, lizard or gecko scent, or commercial scent strips, the packaged option. The amphibian scents work because they match what the species evolved eating.

Change one thing at a time and keep the feeding record, so you know what worked.

When a refusal becomes a vet visit Weight down more than 10% from the snake's starting weight, or visibly thinner along the spine and tail base. Wheezing, open-mouth breathing, or bubbles or discharge at the nose or mouth. Excess saliva, tiny red spots on the gums, or cheesy yellow material in the mouth. Lethargy well beyond the season's usual quiet. Still refusing by spring, or refusing out of season while losing weight. Any of these ends the wait.
Refused or regurgitated? A refused meal is a snake that never struck. A regurgitated meal was eaten and came back up. The first usually needs patience. The second means something in the setup, the handling or the snake's health is wrong, and it has its own plan on page {{P:impaction}}.
Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Hooding, hissing & playing dead

One of the most dramatic defensive acts of any pet snake, and almost all of it is bluff.

When a hognose feels threatened it puts on a show, usually in this order. It inflates its body with deep breaths and flattens its neck into a cobra-like hood, the source of its "puff adder" nickname. It hisses loudly. It throws closed-mouth mock strikes that never make contact. If none of that works, some hognoses go further and play dead: they roll onto the back, writhe, open the mouth and let the tongue hang out, and sometimes release musk, a foul-smelling fluid, or feces. Flip one back over mid-performance and it will often roll itself belly-up again, as if it has not noticed. Playing dead is called death feigning, or thanatosis.

What it means A hognose putting on the full display is doing everything it can to avoid biting you. Real aggression in this species is close to unheard of. The act is hardwired, not illness and not a personality, and it is not a sign the snake is about to bite. Bites come from a different place entirely: food. Page {{P:venom}}.

What the research adds

A field study of 27 wild western hognoses, published in the journal Ethology by Durso and Mullin, simulated predator attacks and timed the response. Larger snakes played dead for longer than smaller ones, and that link was stronger in females than in males. Snakes found at higher temperatures started playing dead sooner. For a keeper, that means a big female may hold the act a long time, and that is still normal.

What to do when it happens

  • Stay calm and keep your hands steady. Panicking and dropping the snake is the classic first-timer mistake. Calm, steady handling works better.
  • If it rolls over, stop. Put it down somewhere safe, step back, and let it right itself on its own. Do not keep flipping it.
  • Approach from the side next time. A hand or tool dropping from above looks like a predator and sets off the act. Page {{P:handling}}.

Normal, or worth a look

Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

The venom question & bites

Mildly venomous, not dangerous, and worth respecting at feeding time.

The western hognose is rear-fanged and mildly venomous. It has enlarged, ungrooved fangs toward the back of the upper jaw, connected to a gland in front of them called the Duvernoy's gland, which produces a weak venom evolved to help it subdue toads. It is not considered medically significant to a healthy person. A 2026 study calls it the most commonly kept venomous pet snake in the world, which is exactly why the honest answer matters.

What the research says

StudyWhat it found
Weinstein and Keyler, 2009, a case report in the journal ToxiconA keeper was bitten while offering a young mouse to a captive snake, which held on to the inside of the elbow for several minutes. The result was marked local swelling, bruising, swollen lymph nodes (the small glands that fight infection), skin signs of a mild cellulitis (an inflamed skin infection) and blisters. There were no effects anywhere else in the body, and recovery was complete after about five months. The authors concluded that hognoses may uncommonly produce medically significant bites but should not be considered dangerous, and that captive snakes should be handled carefully, particularly when offered food
Damm and colleagues, 2026, a survey of keepers in Frontiers in Amphibian and Reptile Science63 bite reports, 34 of them with symptoms. 24 of the 63 happened during feeding, and most involved a hungry snake or one being moved into a feeding container. Quick strike-and-release bites produced symptoms 37% of the time, gone within 2 days. Bites where the snake held on or chewed produced symptoms 80% of the time, lasting 3 to 6 days. Local swelling was by far the most common symptom. 7 of the 63 sought medical help. The authors judged the species relatively harmless, unable to cause permanent damage or death, and in need of no legal regulation for its venom

How bites happen, and how to avoid them

Real bites are rare and are almost always a feeding response: the snake mistakes a hand for food. So: wash prey scent off your hands before handling; lift the snake out with a snake hook rather than a bare hand, especially on feeding day; offer food from tongs; and leave it alone for 48 hours after a meal. The survey's authors add that proper equipment, leather gloves or a snake hook, and getting the snake off quickly would prevent virtually all envenoming, the term for venom actually entering the body.

If a bite happens and the snake hangs on Get it off quickly, because the longer a hognose holds and chews, the more symptoms follow. But do not yank it off, which can injure both of you. Pour cool water over its face and it will usually let go on its own. Then show a doctor any bite that breaks the skin: tell them it was a rear-fanged hognose. Rare allergic reactions are possible, as with any bite, so get emergency care for any reaction beyond the bite site itself.

None of this puts the western hognose in the category state venomous-reptile laws are written for: several of those laws exempt hognoses by name. The one place the venom does decide the answer is New York City. Pages {{P:legal}} to {{P:legal3}}.

Section 04 · Behavior & Handling

Handling & hygiene

Short, calm, supported sessions, never near a meal, and soap and water every time.

Give a new hognose about two weeks to settle before the first session. After that:

  1. Signal that this is not food. Lift the snake out of the substrate with a small tool such as a snake hook, then move it onto your hands.
  2. Come in from the side. A hand or tool dropping from above looks like a predator and sets off the hood and hiss.
  3. Support as much of the body as you can. Let it rest along your palms and forearm. Never grab the tail and never restrain the head.
  4. Let it move. Keep your hands under it as it crawls, moving one hand ahead of the other.

How long and how often

First sessionsNo more than 5 minutes, and end on a calm snake, not mid-display

Salmonella and the household

Every reptile can carry Salmonella, bacteria that live in the gut of a perfectly healthy snake with no sign of illness and leave in its droppings. A single negative test is not a clean bill of health, so treat every reptile as a carrier. The risk is not the bite. It is hands and surfaces to mouth: the snake, its water, its substrate and its gear all carry it.

  • Wash your hands with soap and running water right after touching the snake, the enclosure, the water or anything that has touched them.
  • Keep the snake and its gear out of the kitchen, and never clean the enclosure, dish or tools in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink or a bathtub people use. A dedicated tub or bin solves this.
  • Don't kiss the snake or hold it close to your face.
  • Children under 5 should not handle reptiles or touch their enclosures at all. Adults over 65, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system are also at higher risk of serious illness.
If someone in the house gets sick Diarrhea, fever and stomach cramps are the ordinary symptoms, which is why the link to a pet gets missed. Mention the snake to the doctor even if nobody remembers touching it, especially for anyone in a higher-risk group.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Researchers let western hognoses choose. They chose enrichment, and chose it more strongly as time went on.

Nagabaskaran, Skinner and Miller ran a preference test, published in the journal Animals in 2022. Each enclosure was divided in two. The enriched half had burrowing substrate, objects to interact with, and a large water dish. The standard half had paper towel and a small dish. Both halves had a shelter, and there was a heat gradient, so the test compared enrichment on top of adequate care, not enrichment against neglect. Over six days of observation:

  • The snakes preferred the enriched side, and the preference grew stronger over time, which rules out simple curiosity about something new.
  • The preference was stronger when the enriched side was the cooler one, which the authors link to this species' burrowing.
  • Boldness made no difference. A shy hognose is not opting out of enrichment.

Priority order

  1. Substrate deep enough to burrow into completely
  2. That deep substrate placed on the cool side
  3. A water dish large enough to soak in
  4. Secure cover at both ends of the gradient
  5. Scent novelty and varied feeding presentation
  6. Rearranging the furnishings, and new objects, last
