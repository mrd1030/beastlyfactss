Section 01 · Quick Profile

Which hognose this is, size & lifespan

"Hognose" is a common name shared by several species. The law and the care both depend on which one you have.

Three hognose species live in the United States, and a fourth, the Mexican hognose, reaches into the southwest. The one sold as a pet is almost always the western hognose, Heterodon nasicus, which some state laws call the plains hog-nosed snake. Its smaller size, milder bluffing and flexible diet made it the hognose the hobby settled on, and most animals sold now are captive-bred rather than collected. This package is written for the western hognose only.

Published ranges vary. The weights in the table come from one long-time breeder's records and are a guide, not a target: your own snake's weight trend matters more.

How long it lives

10 to 15 years is the commonly cited captive range, and 15 to 20 is reported in captivity. In the wild, a western hognose lives about 14 years on average, with a reported range of 9 to 19.

The animal behind the care sheet

The upturned snout is a digging tool. The enlarged scale at its tip, the rostral scale, is shovel-shaped, and the snake uses it to burrow through loose soil after buried toads, its main wild prey. Toads can make up nearly 80% of the diet in some regions. In the wild it spends roughly September to March below ground. Every part of the captive setup follows from that life: dry air, deep loose substrate, and a snake that may slow down in winter.

The enclosure, sized by sex & age

A ground-dwelling burrower measures its home in floor space. Height does almost nothing for it.

The working rule is about one square foot of floor space per foot of snake length. Because females grow so much larger, the minimum depends on sex:

SnakeMinimum enclosure
Adult male20-gallon equivalent, about 30×13×13 in (76×33×33 cm)
Adult female40-gallon equivalent, 36×18×16 in (91×46×41 cm)
Either sex, a solid adult setupA front-opening enclosure of about 2×2×2 ft (61×61×61 cm), or 36×18×18 in (91×46×46 cm)
Hatchling under 6 in (15 cm)A 5-gallon enclosure, about 16×8×10 in (41×20×25 cm)
JuvenileA 10-gallon enclosure until it is about a year old. Starting in the adult enclosure also works, if it has enough hides and cover to feel secure

Front-opening PVC (polyvinyl chloride, a rigid plastic) or glass both work. If you use glass, cover three sides to cut stress. The lid has to latch: a hognose pushes at any gap it finds, and on a baby's enclosure the lid needs clamps or latches. Never use tape, which is notorious for injuring snakes. Keep one snake per enclosure: sharing an enclosure is one of the listed causes of regurgitation, a meal brought back up.

At night the heat goes off and the enclosure can fall to room temperature, as long as it stays at 60°F (16°C) or above.

Why a gradient

A snake cannot make its own body heat. It sets its temperature by moving between warm and cool spots, so the enclosure exists to give it a choice. The hot spot is for digesting; the cool end is where this species digs, and where it goes to cool down. Where a reptile sits in its range has real consequences for how it fights infection, which is why a sick snake is kept toward the upper end of its normal range and why an enclosure should not run at the bottom of the range by default.

Reading it properly

  • Two digital probe thermometers, one probe on the basking surface and one on the cool end. One reading tells you nothing about a gradient.
  • An infrared thermometer, the handheld point-and-read kind, to spot-check the surface itself: the basking stone, the substrate, the wall near the heat. It finds a hot spot the fixed probes are not sitting on.
  • Check both before the snake goes in, and again whenever anything in the setup changes.

Night heat is a different setup

The basking light goes off at night, and a hognose needs no night bulb. A normal room is fine overnight as long as the enclosure stays at 60°F (16°C) or above. Only if your room drops below that, add a heat source that gives off no light, such as a heat mat or a ceramic heat emitter (a screw-in heater with no visible glow), on its own thermostat.

Heat sources, thermostats & probes

Whatever heats the enclosure, a thermostat controls it. That one device is what prevents burns.

The commonly recommended heat source for this species is a low-wattage halogen basking bulb (a bright, focused light bulb that heats the spot under it), mounted over the screen at the warm end and run on a dimming thermostat. A 50-watt halogen spot is a typical choice. Some keepers use an under-tank heat mat instead; the halogen is the current recommendation. Either way, the thermostat is not optional. Put a flat, light-colored stone or piece of wood under the lamp: wild hognoses bask on rocks and logs, and a flat surface warms the snake more efficiently.

Match the thermostat to the heat source

ThermostatHow it controls heatBest matched to
On/offCuts power fully at the set temperature, restores it when the reading dropsHeat mats, which change temperature slowly
Pulse proportionalSends short pulses of power for a steadier averageCeramic heat emitters, which can overshoot on an on/off cycle
DimmingTurns the power up and down like a light dimmerHalogen basking bulbs, with no visible flicker

These pairings are the usual best match, not a hard rule. What matters most is that the heat source has a controller at all.

Where the probe goes

A thermostat only regulates the spot its probe measures. For a heat mat, the probe goes on the floor of the warm hide, the surface the snake lies on, held flat with foil tape. For an overhead halogen, makers disagree: some want the probe on the basking surface under the lamp, others want it in the air a few inches below the bulb. Follow your own thermostat's instructions, keep the probe at the snake's level rather than up near the fixture, and confirm the result with the infrared thermometer.

Never without a thermostat, never within reach An unregulated mat or bulb keeps drawing full power with no ceiling, and a snake pressed against an overheated surface does not always move away in time. That is how a burn happens. Keep any overhead source at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest spot the snake can reach, behind the screen. Skip heat rocks entirely: they burn snakes and heat poorly.

Plan for the thermostat failing

  • The probe slips. The most common failure, and a silent one: the display stays accurate for the wrong spot. Look at the probe at every water change.
  • The thermostat sticks on. Rarer and more dangerous. A second thermometer, away from the thermostat's probe and read daily, is what catches it.
  • Wattage is a starting guess. The number on the box assumes an average room. Set it up, then measure for a day before the snake goes in.
Section 02 · Housing & Environment

Dry air, the humid hide & ultraviolet light

The number that prevents the most illness in this species is a low one.

Keep ambient humidity at 30 to 50%, dry by the standard of most pet snakes. Do not mist the enclosure to raise it. Running the whole enclosure damp is a direct path to respiratory infection and scale rot, two illnesses that both trace back to the same cause. A western hognose comes from prairie and sandy ground, and that makes its setup drier than a ball python's or a corn snake's.

The one humid spot

Give the snake one humid hide for shedding: a hide whose substrate is mixed with a little water, or packed with moist sphagnum moss, while the rest of the enclosure stays dry. Add moisture there only as needed, mostly when a shed is coming (page {{P:shedding}}). Set it between the warm and cool ends so the snake can use it at a comfortable temperature.

Measure it, don't guess it

A digital hygrometer (a humidity meter) is core equipment here, not an extra, because this species' whole setup depends on humidity being confirmed low rather than guessed at. Read it at snake level, away from the water dish and the humid hide.

A day and a night

Unlike most pet snakes, a hognose is active by day, so it gets a normal day and night: lights on a timer, 12 hours on and 12 off. An optional seasonal schedule runs longer days in summer and shorter ones in winter.

Ultraviolet light

UVB is not strictly required by every source, and a hognose will not die without it. But a low-output UVB tube is increasingly recommended for this day-active species. The specification that fits:

Deep bedding, digging & cleaning

For a burrower the substrate is not decoration. It is the part of the enclosure the snake lives in.

Provide 3 to 6 in (8 to 15 cm) of loose, dry, diggable substrate. A soil and sand mix works well: roughly 70% soil-based substrate to 30% reptile-safe sand is a common ratio, and aspen or coconut fiber works as the soil-based part. Depth is the variable that matters. Give the snake enough to disappear into completely, not just to push around, and choose a mix that holds a tunnel rather than collapsing. A depth a hognose can vanish into takes more than one small bag. Keep it dry: damp substrate plus this species' preference for arid conditions is a fast route to skin and respiratory problems.

Put the digging on the cool end In the preference study on page {{P:enrichment}}, western hognoses chose the enriched side more strongly when it was also the cooler side. So: deepest substrate at the cool end, basking surface at the warm end, cover at both. The snake never has to choose between the temperature it wants and the behavior it wants.

A bioactive setup, a planted substrate with a cleanup crew of tiny invertebrates, is the exception and can run for years without a full change. To disinfect: scrub first, then household bleach at 1:32, which is half a cup per gallon of water, kept wet on the surface for 10 minutes, rinsed until no bleach smell remains, and aired for a full day before the snake goes back. Do it in a utility sink, a dedicated tub or outdoors, never the kitchen sink or a bathtub people use.
