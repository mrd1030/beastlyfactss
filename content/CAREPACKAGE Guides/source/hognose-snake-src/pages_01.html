How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

Units used throughout Every temperature and size is given twice. Temperatures appear in degrees Fahrenheit (°F) with degrees Celsius (°C) beside them. Sizes appear in inches (in) and feet (ft), with centimeters (cm) beside them. Snakes and their food are weighed in grams (g), the unit a kitchen scale shows. Humidity is a percentage (%) of how much moisture the air could hold. Prices are in United States dollars.

If you haven't bought a hognose yet

Read the law section first, starting on page {{P:legal}}. In most states a western hognose raises no legal question at all, but in a handful it is banned, capped or permit-only, and in several more the answer depends on which of the three hognose species you buy. Then read pages {{P:profile}} through {{P:substrate}} before buying any equipment. Two decisions are expensive to get wrong: the enclosure size on page {{P:enclosure}}, which depends on whether you buy a male or a female, and the heat source on page {{P:heating}}, which has to run through a thermostat, a controller that cuts the heat at a set temperature. The Budget and Shopping List on page {{P:budget}} then lets you buy once instead of twice.

If your hognose is already home

Start with the Setup Checklist on page {{P:checklist}} to audit what you have. Then read page {{P:humidity}}: this species wants dry air, and an enclosure kept damp is the root of the two most common illnesses in it. If your snake hoods, hisses or rolls over to play dead, page {{P:display}} explains why that is normal. If it is refusing food, page {{P:refusals}} comes before any worry. If anything already looks wrong, go straight to Health Red Flags on page {{P:redflags}}.

Why this package exists The western hognose has a reputation built on two things: a theatrical defensive act, and the word "venomous." The act makes owners think a calm snake is an aggressive one, and the word makes owners think a mildly venomous snake is a dangerous one. Neither is true, and both are easier to live with once you know what is going on. The care itself is simple once three things are right: dry air, deep digging substrate (the loose material on the enclosure floor), and feeding an adult less often than a growing juvenile. Every figure here comes from the Beastly Facts hognose guides or from veterinary and scientific sources, listed on page {{P:sources}}, and every temperature and dimension is given in both imperial and metric units.
Not a substitute for A veterinarian who sees reptiles. This is a husbandry reference for day-to-day care, and anything urgent is a professional call, not a page in this book. Find a reptile vet before you need one, and write the number on page {{P:emergency}}. Nothing in the law section is legal advice either.
Section 01 · Quick Profile

Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Species coveredThe western hognose, Heterodon nasicus, also called the plains hognose. It is the hognose the pet trade sells. The eastern and southern hognoses are different species, and the difference matters for the law. Page {{P:species}}
Native rangeThe North American plains, from southern Canada to northern Mexico: shortgrass prairie, dry rocky land, and sandy soil it can dig in
Adult sizeFemales run much larger than males. A typical adult male is 16 to 24 in (41 to 61 cm); a typical adult female 21 to 30 in (53 to 76 cm). Page {{P:species}}
Captive lifespan10 to 15 years is the commonly cited range, with 15 to 20 reported in captivity
ActivityActive by day, most of all in the morning and late afternoon, and a dedicated burrower
DietToads in the wild. In captivity, frozen-thawed mice, fed every 5 to 7 days while young and about every two weeks as an adult. Page {{P:feeding}}
Enclosure, one adultMale: 20-gallon equivalent, about 30×13×13 in (76×33×33 cm). Female: 40-gallon equivalent, 36×18×16 in (91×46×41 cm). Floor space over height. Page {{P:enclosure}}
TemperatureBasking 90 to 95°F (32 to 35°C), cool side 70 to 75°F (21 to 24°C), nights 75 to 78°F (24 to 26°C). Page {{P:temperature}}
Humidity30 to 50%: dry. One humid hide for shedding, and no misting the enclosure. Page {{P:humidity}}
LightingA 12-hour day. UVB (ultraviolet B, the part of sunlight a reptile's skin uses to make vitamin D3) is not required by every source, but a low-output tube is increasingly recommended for this day-active snake. Page {{P:humidity}}
Substrate3 to 6 in (8 to 15 cm) of loose, dry, diggable substrate, about 70% soil-based to 30% reptile-safe sand. Page {{P:substrate}}
VenomRear-fanged and mildly venomous. Not considered dangerous to a healthy person. Page {{P:venom}}
Conservation statusLeast Concern, the lowest-risk category on the global Red List of threatened species, but listed as threatened in some states, Illinois among them. Buy captive-bred

Cost overview

Initial setup

The snake is $50 to $100 for a normal, wild-type animal (natural color and pattern), and far more for a rare color form. Females typically cost $50 to $200 more than males. Itemized, the equipment comes to $258 to $441, and the enclosure is the biggest single line. With the snake, a first exam and a fecal test (a stool check for parasites), plan on $383 to $691. Page {{P:budget}} has every line.

Ongoing

Frozen mice, substrate, electricity and a yearly wellness exam come to $180 to $330 a year. A respiratory infection or an impaction can run into the hundreds to treat, and an X-ray image, called a radiograph, is $100 to $250 a view.

The number that should drive the decision Not the price. The lifespan. A hognose bought for a ten-year-old can still be in the house when that child leaves for college, and some live to 20. The purchase is the cheap part. The routine on page {{P:routine}}, and a reptile vet for every one of those years, is what you are signing up for.
