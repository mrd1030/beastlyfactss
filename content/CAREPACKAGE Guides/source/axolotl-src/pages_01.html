How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

Units used throughout Every temperature and size is given twice. Temperatures appear in degrees Fahrenheit (°F) with degrees Celsius (°C) beside them. Sizes appear in inches (in) with centimeters (cm) beside them. Tanks are measured in US gallons (gal), with liters (L) where a number matters. Water test readings are in parts per million (ppm): how many parts of a substance sit in a million parts of water. Small amounts are in milliliters (mL) and grams (g), and a salt bath's strength is in grams per liter (g/L). Prices are in United States dollars.

If you haven't set up yet

Check the law first, on page {{P:legal}}: an axolotl is legal without a permit in most of the United States, but it is banned, permit-only, conditional or unclear in fourteen places. Then read pages {{P:profile}} to {{P:cleaning}} in order before buying anything. Cooling is the decision that shapes the whole budget, and a new tank needs weeks of cycling before an axolotl goes in it. The Budget & Shopping List on page {{P:budget}} lets you buy once.

If your axolotl is already home

Read the thermometer and test the water before you change anything else. Most axolotl problems that look like disease turn out to be a temperature or a water reading, and you cannot rule either out by looking at the animal. If anything seems off, go to the Red Flags page (page {{P:redflags}}). If the water is already too warm, the steps that work in the next hour are on page {{P:heat}}.

Why this package exists Axolotl advice contradicts itself most on the two things that matter most: how cold the water has to be, and what is safe to put on the tank floor. This package gives numbers you can test against, cross-checked against the veterinary and research sources listed on page {{P:sources}}.
Not a substitute for An amphibian or aquatic veterinarian. This is a husbandry reference for day-to-day care. Anything urgent or health-related is always a vet call, not a page in this book. Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameAmbystoma mexicanum, a mole salamander and a close relative of the tiger salamander
Adult size9 to 12 in (23 to 30 cm)
Captive lifespan10 to 15 years with consistent care; 5 to 6 years in the wild
Native rangeThe lake and canal system of Xochimilco, on the southern edge of Mexico City
Water temperature60 to 64°F (15.6 to 17.8°C). Chronic stress above 72°F (22°C), and possibly fatal from about 75°F (24°C). Never a heater
Tank minimumA 20 gal long tank for one adult; a 40 gal breeder if you keep two
Water targetsAmmonia 0 ppm, nitrite 0 ppm, nitrate under 10 ppm, pH 7.4 to 7.6, in a fully cycled tank (page {{P:water}})
Diet typeCarnivore: earthworms or nightcrawlers as the staple, with a sinking pellet made for axolotls
FeedingJuveniles about daily, adults every 2 to 3 days (page {{P:diet}})
Sexual maturityMales at about 10 months, females at 12 to 18 months
HandlingOnly when necessary, and with a container rather than hands. A pet to watch, not to hold
Conservation statusCritically Endangered on the Red List kept by the IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature). Pet axolotls are captive-bred

Cost overview

Initial setup

$30 to $100 for a standard axolotl. $185 to $530 for the tank and equipment, or $335 to $1,180 with a chiller. Every line is on page {{P:budget}}.

Monthly ongoing

$15 to $45 for food, pellet and water conditioner top-ups, filter media and the electricity a chiller draws.

Budget for the bad month too An exam for an aquatic animal can cost more than one for a reptile or a bird, and tests, medication or surgery for a swallowed stone come on top. Keep a few hundred dollars set aside from the first week.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Axolotls regrow whole limbs, the tail, and parts of the eye, brain, spinal cord and heart, and the wound closes without a scar.
  • They breathe four ways at once: through the skin, the lining of the mouth, the feathery gills, and simple lungs filled by gulping air at the surface.
  • They have no eyelids, which is why dim, indirect light suits them.

The species, morphs, size & lifespan

What you are buying, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

A salamander that keeps its larval body

The axolotl is a salamander from the lake and canal system of Xochimilco, in Mexico City, that keeps its larval body for life: feathery external gills, a finned tail and a fully aquatic habit. The word for this is paedomorphosis, and it is not the same as staying a baby. An axolotl reaches full adult size and breeds normally. Nor is it strictly permanent: the tissues still respond to thyroid hormone, and a high enough dose in a laboratory can push one through metamorphosis into a land-dwelling adult. What is underactive is the hormone system that would trigger it. In a home tank that never happens, which is why a correct setup has no land area and no basking spot.

Wild and captive

In the wild the species is close to gone. Surveys of the Xochimilco canals counted about 6,000 axolotls per square kilometer in 1998 and 36 in 2014, a fall of 99 percent, driven by pollution and by introduced carp and tilapia that eat eggs and young. In laboratories and home tanks it is common: most laboratory axolotls descend from six of the 34 animals shipped from Mexico City to Paris in 1864.

Morphs, in plain terms

A morph is a color variety. Every morph needs exactly the same cold water, food and care.

MorphWhat it is
Wild typeDark olive and speckled, the color of every wild axolotl.
LeucisticMostly white with dark eyes: fewer pigment cells develop, and most fail to reach the skin. The pale pet-shop axolotl.
AlbinoNo black pigment at all, because the enzyme that makes it is broken. The gene came from a tiger salamander crossed into the laboratory colony.
MelanoidSolid dark, with many black pigment cells and none of the reflective ones. Sometimes sold as melanistic.
GFP (green fluorescent protein)Carries a jellyfish gene for a glowing protein, first put into axolotls in 2006 for research. Glows green under ultraviolet light, and can be combined with any color.
Mosaic and chimeraBuilt from two genetically different cell lines, so one body carries patches of two morphs. A mosaic does not breed more mosaics, which is the whole price premium.

The common colors come from single genes, mostly recessive, so two wild-type parents can both carry a hidden color. A dark morph hides early redness and fungus, so look closer under good light.

Size and lifespan

Most adults finish at 9 to 12 in (23 to 30 cm). An axolotl commonly lives 10 to 15 years in captivity and 5 to 6 in the wild. Plan for house moves, summers away and a decade or more of hot Augusts.
