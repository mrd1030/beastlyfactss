Section 06 · The Law

Is an axolotl legal where you live?

Legal without a permit in most of the country, and banned or restricted in fourteen places.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet axolotl is legal with no permit in 38, banned in 6, permit-only in 4, legal on a condition in 3, and unclear in 1.

Section 06 · The Law

The federal rule, Virginia & why the bans exist

One federal change that moved two states, and one ban that ended in 2021.

The 2025 federal rule

A federal rule effective January 10, 2025 added 16 more salamander genera to the list of injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act, the federal law that lets the government restrict harmful wildlife, bringing the total to 36 genera. All of them are tied to the risk of Bsal, the salamander-killing fungus, and genus Ambystoma, which includes the axolotl and the tiger salamanders, is among the new ones.

What the listing restrictsWhat it does not restrict
Importing axolotls into the United States, and shipping them to or from Hawaii, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico or the US territories.Owning, breeding or selling an axolotl within the 49 continental states. The US Fish and Wildlife Service states that distinction directly in its own answers on the rule.

The twist is in two states. Wyoming and Alabama do not write their own lists for this; they bar whatever is on the federal injurious list. So for owners there, the January 2025 rule was the moment the animal became illegal to keep. And Hawaii's conditional yes sits beside a federal limit on shipping one there.

Virginia is legal, and has been since 2021

Virginia still shows up on ban lists across a surprising number of hobby sites. Since August 1, 2021 it has been legal to import, export, sell and possess axolotls there with no permit, after an amendment to the state's administrative code carved axolotls out of its general ban on selling salamanders. A list that shows Virginia as banned predates the change.

Why the bans exist

None of the restrictions protect axolotls. They protect native salamanders. The axolotl sits in genus Ambystoma with North America's mole and tiger salamanders, including California's own tiger salamander, which is federally threatened. Regulators weigh three risks: hybridization with native salamanders, an escaped population establishing itself, and disease, the chytrid fungi above all. California's label for the genus, a detrimental species, says so outright. New Jersey adds an enforcement reason: a gilled adult axolotl looks like the larva of a protected native salamander.

Never release one An axolotl, its tank water or its plants never go into any pond, stream or drain. A captive amphibian carrying Bd or Bsal becomes a wild exposure the moment it is loose, which is the whole reason for the federal listing.

Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Red flags & finding a vet

What to watch for, when to call, and what to have ready when you do.
See an aquatic or exotic-amphibian vet promptly for Fungal growth that has not improved within 48 hours of correcting the water · suspected impaction: bloating, floating instead of resting on the bottom, no waste · red streaking, reddened skin or open wounds · a swollen, fluid-filled body · uncontrollable floating · appetite loss lasting 5 days or more.

Test before you treat

A large share of what goes wrong traces back to water that is too warm, and most of the rest to water quality. So the first two actions for any symptom are a thermometer reading and a full test of ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH. An axolotl often shows a water problem before a test does, and medication into a tank running at 75°F (24°C) fixes nothing. Correcting the water is not a delay in treatment: it is usually the largest part of it. Overfeeding and the wrong food are common triggers too.

Finding a vet before you need one

General small-animal clinics often are not equipped for this species, and warm water or a swallowed stone rarely waits for business hours. Use the find-a-vet directory of the Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians to find candidates, ask each practice directly whether it sees axolotls, and write the number on the emergency card. Some exotic hospitals book aquatic animals as a longer, two-part appointment, which costs more than a reptile or bird exam.

What to have ready when you call

The water temperature, and how long it has been out of range
Your latest ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH readings, with the date
A sample of tank water in a clean container, to bring along
Tank size, filter and floor type, and how long the tank has run
What it eats, how often, and when it last ate
When the signs started, and the owner log
Getting it there A small, clear, watertight container of its own tank water, kept cool on the way. The vet can examine it through the side without taking it out.
Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Heat stress & reading the gills

The condition most axolotl health problems trace back to, and the early warning on the animal's own head.

Cause: water above 72°F (22°C) for any length of time suppresses the immune system; above about 75°F (24°C) the heat itself can be fatal. Signs: gill tips curled forward, appetite loss, floating, and pale patches on the skin. Above about 75°F, axolotls turn sluggish, float uncontrollably, develop ascites and pick up bacterial and fungal infections. Response: bring the temperature down, steadily, and test the water while you do.

The water is too warm, right now

  1. Lights off, lid cracked open, and a fan blowing across the water surface.
  2. Close the blinds, and run the room as cool as you can.
  3. Float sealed bottles of frozen dechlorinated water, swapping them as they melt. Never loose ice: it shocks the water around each cube.
  4. Lower the temperature no faster than about 1°F an hour, never in a drastic jump.
  5. Check the filter output too: gills curled forward can mean too much current rather than heat.
  6. Test ammonia and nitrite, since warm water makes the same ammonia reading more toxic.
An axolotl already in crisis For an animal in outright heat crisis, from long exposure above 75°F (24°C), placing it in a dish in the refrigerator is a recognized stopgap while the main tank is cooled. It is a short emergency measure, not routine care.

Reading the gills

What the gills look likeWhat it usually means
Full and feathery, held out to the sidesHealthy. Note what yours look like on a good day, as a baseline.
Curled or angled forward toward the faceStress: heat first, then water quality or current. Often well before appetite loss.
Red, inflamed patchesAmmonia or nitrite burn. Test now (page {{P:burns}}).
White, cotton-like tuftsFungal infection taking hold on stressed tissue.
Why the gills are such a good early warning They curl or angle forward under heat stress well before more obvious signs like appetite loss show up. A look every day, not just when something already seems wrong, buys days of notice.
Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Fungal & bacterial infection

The two infections that follow warm water and dirty water, and how to tell them apart.

Fungal infection

Signs: a white or gray, cotton-like growth on the gills or body, the water mold that is the most common fungal infection in the species. Cause: warm water, poor water quality, stress, or an existing injury that gave the fungus an opening. Response: correct the water quality and cool the tank. Salt baths can help, but the one published figure is a ceiling, not a dose, and the working dose belongs to an exotics vet (page {{P:tubbing}}). Severe cases need a vet-prescribed antifungal, and so does any case not improving within 48 hours of the water being right.

Bacterial infection

Signs: red streaks, reddened skin, open sores, and a swollen, fluid-filled body (ascites). Cause: most often Aeromonas or Pseudomonas bacteria, which take hold when the water runs warm or dirty. Response: a vet and prescription treatment. Water changes alone will not clear it. Caught early, it is treated with systemic antibiotics such as enrofloxacin or a trimethoprim-sulfonamide, and once advanced the outlook is poor, which is the reason to act on the first signs.

Telling them apartFungalBacterial
What you seeFluffy white or gray tufts standing off the skin or gillsRedness, streaks, sores, swelling, no fluff
Usual triggerWarm or poor water, stress, an injuryWarm or dirty water
First responseFix the water and cool; vet if no better in 48 hoursFix the water and call a vet the same day
What a vet may do During an antibiotic course for a bacterial infection, a vet may hold the axolotl at 41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C) for several weeks, because cooler water appears to help a sick axolotl. That is part of a treatment a vet is managing, not something to try alone. This is here so you can follow the plan, not so you can medicate at home.
Prevention really is the treatment Both are opportunists. An axolotl in cold, clean, cycled water, with no injuries and no tankmate biting it, rarely gets either.

Impaction, floating & waste

The preventable emergency, and how to tell it from the harmless version of the same symptom.

Cause: swallowed gravel or small decor, straight from the way an axolotl feeds: it gulps whatever sits next to its food. Signs: bloating, floating instead of resting on the bottom, appetite loss, and no waste. Response: a vet. Mild cases sometimes resolve with fasting and cooler water under a vet's guidance, but a blockage low in the gut can need surgery.

Floating: four presentations that look alike

What you seeLikely causeWhat to do
Rises for a gulp of air, then swims back downNormalNothing. Axolotls breathe partly through simple lungs.
Floats, with curled gills, in a warm tankHeat stressCool the water gradually and reassess (page {{P:heat}}).
Floats, bloated, with no wasteImpactionStop feeding and call a vet.
Floats with a swollen, fluid-filled bodyAscites: heat or a bacterial infectionVet, promptly.

Any floating it cannot correct, whatever the cause, is a call to the vet rather than a wait-and-see.

Waste as a signal

A healthy axolotl that is eating passes waste. No waste after meals, especially with bloating, is one of the named signs of impaction. Waste and leftover food on the floor are also the main source of ammonia, so siphon them out at every water change.

Preventing it entirely

  • Bare bottom for any axolotl under 5 in (13 cm), and fine sand under 1 mm at most for the rest. Never gravel or small rocks.
  • Feed from tongs, on a bare patch or in a dish, so the mouthful is food and nothing else.
  • Worm in head-sized pieces, and no hard-shelled feeders.
  • Nothing small enough to swallow anywhere in the tank.
Do not wait it out If you know the axolotl swallowed a stone or a piece of decor and it has stopped eating, that is a vet call today. Fine sand or a bare floor prevents almost all of it.

Ammonia burns, parasites, eyes & obesity

The rest of the list, most of which is water chemistry or feeding wearing a different face.

Ammonia and nitrite burns

Signs: red, inflamed patches on the skin or gills. Cause: an uncycled or poorly maintained tank. Response: test, then change at least a third of the water with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, 50 percent or more if a reading is clearly climbing, and repeat until ammonia and nitrite both read 0 ppm. The real fix is a properly cycled tank, checked with a test kit before you rely on it.

ConditionSignsCause and response
Gut parasitesFound with a fecal testPicked up from food or tankmates. Hexamita, Opalina, Trichodina and Ichthyobodo are among them. A fecal test at the vet, ideally during quarantine, and vet treatment if found. One more reason never to use feeder fish.
Eye problemsChanges in the eyesLinked to prolonged high nitrate. Keep nitrate under 10 ppm with weekly changes, and see a vet if it persists.
ObesityA heavy, overfed bodyFeeding an adult on a juvenile's schedule. Adults eat every 2 to 3 days, in head-sized portions.

Bite woundsMissing toes, gill filaments or limb tipsFrom a tankmate. Separate them: a tub isolates an injured animal without disturbing the tank. Watch the wound for white fluff or redness.

Regeneration is not a reason to relax

Axolotls regrow lost limbs and the tail, and the wound closes without a scar. That is remarkable, and it still happens in water that carries bacteria and fungus. Remove the cause, keep the water clean and cool, and watch the site daily for the signs on the infection page.

Do not medicate a tank on a hunch Amphibian skin absorbs what is in the water, and medicating the display tank can kill the filter's bacteria colony too. Correct the water first, and get a diagnosis before any medication goes in.

Tubbing, cooling & salt baths

Three interventions worth knowing before you need them, and where each one stops.

Tubbing

Tubbing means moving the axolotl out of its tank into a bare, unfiltered container of dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, changed completely rather than filtered. Research colonies house many adults this way as routine, in bowls with no circulating water, because water replaced regularly cannot build up waste. A tub is easy to watch against a plain background, isolates a sick or injured animal from a tankmate, and gives somewhere to go while a tank is put right.

  • A clean plastic tote, bin or bucket the axolotl can turn around in without bumping the walls. No gravel and no sharp decor; one simple hide such as a length of PVC pipe.
  • A full water change once daily as the baseline, twice for a genuinely sick animal, with fresh dechlorinated water checked by thermometer before the axolotl goes back in. A partial change defeats the point.
  • Move the axolotl in a submerged container, never by hand.
  • Keep testing the main tank in parallel: a tub fixes one animal's exposure, not whatever made the tank a problem.

Cooling as treatment

A sick axolotl on antibiotics for a bacterial infection may be held at 41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C) for several weeks, because cooler water appears to help. That belongs to a treatment a vet is running. Cooling a tub within the normal range as general support for a stressed animal is reasonable on your own, done gradually.

Salt baths

ElementThe published figures
Strength10 to 15 g/L

What these figures areThe ceiling before salt itself starts damaging skin and gills, given as a caution against overdoing it. Not a prescription.

If a vet prescribes a bath, expect the strength, the time and the type of salt to be set for that animal and that condition.

Where home care stops A tub does not fix a bacterial infection, a spreading fungus, an impaction or an infected wound. Open sores or red streaking, a fungus not improving within a couple of days, uncontrollable floating, bloating, or appetite loss running several days all mean a vet. A tub can also hide subtle behavior changes, so it is a tool for a purpose, not a place to keep an axolotl just in case.
