Choosing an axolotl, morphs & sexing

What to look at before you pay, what the colors actually are, and how to tell males from females.

What to check before you buy

Good signs

  • Full, feathery gills fanning outward and backward, with long filaments.
  • Clear skin with no fuzzy patches, red streaks, sores or missing toes.
  • A rounded body roughly as wide as the head, and a straight, unkinked tail.
  • An alert response to a passing shadow, and a seller willing to feed one in front of you.
  • A cool tank at the shop, and a known hatch date and current diet.

Walk away from

  • Warm water, gravel substrate, or fish sharing the tank.
  • Curled-forward gills, or gills worn down to stubs.
  • White cottony growth anywhere on the body or gills.
  • A floating animal, or one lying on its side.
  • Mixed larvae with visibly different head shapes. Tiger salamander larvae look similar young, then metamorphose onto land.

Morphs, in plain terms

Morphs are color varieties, not different species or different care. Every one of them needs exactly the same cold water. The common ones are wild type (mottled brown-green with gold flecking), leucistic (pale pink body with dark eyes, the classic pet-shop axolotl), white albino (pale with clear or pink eyes), golden albino (yellow-gold), melanoid (uniformly dark, no gold flecking) and copper (soft tan). Rarer combinations such as mosaic, chimera and the fluorescent GFP line cost more and are cosmetic only. One practical note: a dark morph hides early redness, fungus and gill color change, so a melanoid needs a closer look under decent light.

Sexing

FeatureMaleFemale
Cloaca (the vent behind the back legs)Noticeably swollen and bulgingFlat, or only slightly raised
Body shapeRelatively straight and slimRounder and plumper, particularly when carrying eggs
NailsOften dark or black on the toe tipsUsually pale
TailTends to be longerTends to be shorter

None of this is reliable before maturity at about a year, so a juvenile sold as sexed is a guess.

A pair will breed, and that is a bigger commitment than it sounds A mature male and female housed together will produce clutches of several hundred eggs, repeatedly. The larvae are cannibalistic, need separating and need live food several times a day. If you do not want to raise hundreds of animals or dispose of eggs, keep a single axolotl or two of the same sex.

Housing together & tankmates

The short version: one axolotl per tank is the safe default, and no fish at any size.

CombinationVerdictWhy
One adult aloneBestNo competition for food, no nipped gills, and every water reading is down to one animal. Axolotls are not social and lose nothing by living alone.
Two adults of similar sizeWorkablePossible with a large water volume, several hides and both animals well fed. Feed them separately, watch for nipping, and be genuinely ready to split them up.
Juveniles togetherNoYoung axolotls are cannibalistic and will take each other's limbs and gills. Raise them separately.
Adult with a much smaller axolotlNoAnything that fits in the mouth gets swallowed, and that includes a smaller tank mate.
Any fishNoSmall fish get eaten. Larger or nippy fish pick at exposed gills, and many carry parasites an axolotl has no defense against. Fish also want warmer water than this animal can survive.
Snails, shrimp, other amphibiansNoSnails and shrimp are food, or a shell hazard when swallowed. Mixing amphibian species risks passing disease between them.

If you do keep two

  • Match them by size, not by age. A size gap is the thing that turns cohabiting into predation.
  • Give each animal at least one hide it can occupy alone, and enough floor length that they are not forced into contact.
  • Target-feed each one with tongs so neither goes hungry and neither has to compete at the same spot.
  • Keep a second cycled tank or a tub ready before you house them together, not after the first bite.
  • Recheck the water more often. Two animals in a 40 gallon (150 L) tank produce a lot more waste than one.
Nipped gills and limbs regrow An axolotl that loses a gill filament, a toe or even a whole limb to a tank mate usually regenerates it, which is genuinely remarkable and is not a reason to tolerate the injury. Every bite is an open wound in water that carries bacteria and fungus, so separate the animals, keep the water clean, and watch the site for white growth or redness.
Never introduce a new axolotl straight into an occupied tank A new animal goes into its own quarantine setup first, every time. See page {{P:arrival}}.

Bringing one home, quarantine & handling

The first week, and the reason this animal stays in the water.

Acclimation, step by step

  1. Get the animal home fast and dark. Keep the container out of the sun, and never leave it in a parked car.
  2. Float the sealed container in the tank for 15 to 20 minutes so the temperatures equalize. Temperature is the shock that matters most here.
  3. Add tank water to the container a little at a time over 30 to 60 minutes, until the volume has roughly doubled, so pH and hardness shift gradually.
  4. Move the axolotl across by guiding it into a submerged cup or container. Do not tip the shop's water into your tank, and do not use a net.
  5. Leave it alone. Dim the lights, skip the first day's meal, and let it find a hide. Most axolotls eat within a day or two of arriving.

Quarantine

If you already keep an axolotl, a new one never goes straight into the established tank. Set it up in its own small tank or tub, cycled or with daily water changes, for a few weeks while you watch it eat, move, breathe and pass waste normally. This is also the window for a first veterinary check, including a fecal test for parasites. Quarantine protects the animal you already have, and it protects the new one from an infection it picks up while still stressed from the move.

Handling: why the answer is almost always no

An axolotl has thin, scaleless, permeable skin, exposed external gills doing the actual work of breathing, and a skeleton that is largely cartilage rather than bone. There is no robust slime layer to fall back on the way a fish has. Handling risks skin damage, gill damage, stress and an infection at any point of contact, and it offers the animal nothing in return. This is a pet you watch, not one you hold.

When a move is genuinely necessaryHow to do it
Tank maintenance or a rehouseGuide the animal into a submerged container while it stays underwater, then lift the container. Never lift the animal itself out by hand.
A vet visit or a health checkSame method, into a lidded container of its own tank water, insulated and kept cool for the trip. See page {{P:outage}}.
A treatment bathSame method again, into the prepared bath at a matched temperature. See page {{P:tubbing}}.
NeverNets. The fine gill filaments and toes snag in mesh and tear. Wash your hands before and after any contact, with no soap residue left on them.
The relationship is real, it is just not tactile Axolotls learn who brings food, and most will come to the front of the tank and take a worm from tongs. That is the interaction this species has to offer, and no amount of good care turns it into a pet you can hold.

Diet & feeding by age

A carnivore that hunts by smell, swallows whole, and slows right down as it grows.

Axolotls eat invertebrate prey and need no plant matter at all. Earthworms and nightcrawlers are the staple: nutritionally complete on their own, strongly scented, and accepted by almost every axolotl. Everything else in the diet is a supplement to that or a treat alongside it.

AgeFrequencyWhat and how much
Hatchling, 0 to 3 months2 to 3 times dailyLive food, typically baby brine shrimp, then chopped blackworms. Hatchlings need moving prey.
Juvenile, 3 to 6 monthsAbout dailyChopped nightcrawler and small soft sinking pellets. Bare-bottom tank at this size.
Sub-adult, 6 to 12 monthsEvery 1 to 3 daysCut worm and pellets, sized to the animal rather than to the packet.
Adult, 12 months and overEvery 2 to 3 daysWhole or halved nightcrawler, or roughly five soft pellets. Adults digest slowly in cold water and genuinely need the gap.

Portion size, and how to tell

  • Offer only what the animal finishes in two to three minutes, and no piece wider than its own head.
  • Remove anything uneaten within 15 to 30 minutes. Leftover worm is the fastest route to an ammonia spike.
  • Target-feed with soft tongs, held just in front of the animal. It hunts by scent and water movement, not sight, so food dropped elsewhere in the tank is often food wasted and fouling the water.
  • Feed each animal separately if you keep two, so neither goes short and neither strikes at the other in the confusion.
The most common feeding mistake is not what you feed It is feeding an adult on a juvenile's schedule. Daily food for a grown axolotl produces an obese animal and a tank fighting its own waste. Obesity is a recognized problem in this species, and unlike most axolotl problems it builds so slowly that nobody notices until the body is visibly wider than the head.
Appetite is your best daily health signal A steady feeding response, every 2 to 3 days for an adult, is the simplest vital sign you have and it takes no equipment. Log it on page {{P:ownerlog}}. Refusal is rarely the first thing to go wrong, but it is very often the first thing you notice.

Staples, treats & the never-feed list

A short shopping list, and a shorter list of things that cause real harm.

FoodTierNotes
Earthworms and nightcrawlersStapleNutritionally complete and readily taken. Bait-shop or pesticide-free garden sourced. Cut to size for juveniles. Rinse before offering.
Soft sinking pellets for axolotls or carnivorous amphibiansStapleA practical primary or supplementary diet. Salmon-based and similar soft pellets are standard in research colonies. About five per adult feeding.
Blackworms, live or frozenOccasionalExcellent for juveniles and for tempting a fussy eater. Rinse frozen portions and thaw before offering.
Frozen bloodworms, brine shrimp, daphniaOccasionalGood variety and good for hatchlings. Too thin nutritionally to be the whole diet for an adult.
Feeder fish, live or frozenNeverReal parasite risk, and many common feeder species carry an enzyme that causes vitamin B1 deficiency over time.
Beef heart, chicken and other mammal meatNeverAxolotls cannot properly digest mammalian fat and protein.
Adult mealworms, crickets, crayfish and other hard-shelled preyNeverExoskeletons are hard to digest and can injure the gut on the way through.
Anything wider than the animal's headNeverIt gets swallowed anyway. Oversized food is one of the two routes to an impaction, the other being the tank floor.

Supplements

None are needed. A diet of worms plus a formulated pellet covers this species' requirements without added vitamins or minerals, which is unusual among exotic pets and one less thing to get wrong. There is no calcium dusting step here, no D3 schedule, and no multivitamin.

Store food properly, and buy small Keep nightcrawlers refrigerated in their bedding and check them before every feed, discarding any that are dead or smell sour. Keep frozen foods frozen, thaw only the portion you are about to use, and never refreeze. Pellets go stale: buy a small tub, keep it sealed and dry, and replace it every few months rather than working through a bulk bag for a year.
The worm objection Plenty of people would rather not keep a tub of live worms in the fridge. That is a fair preference and it is worth knowing before you buy the animal, because the alternative is a pellet-only diet, which works but is a narrower one. If worms are a hard no, at least buy the best pellet you can and add frozen blackworms for variety.

Enrichment: hides, foraging & novelty

Not toys. Choice: somewhere to rest, cover that feels secure, and food that takes some finding.

The bar for "safe" is higher here No eyelids, delicate external gills and a real impaction risk from anything loose mean every item in this tank has to be smooth, stable and either larger than the head or fine enough to pass. That rules out a lot of what an aquarium shop sells as decor.

Structure and cover

Two hides per adult is the floor, three or four of different shapes and heights is better, because a hide is how an axolotl controls its own light level and its distance from anything alarming. Sideways terracotta pots with the drainage hole sealed, wide sanded PVC sections and smooth resin caves all work. Live or silk plants add cover: java fern and anubias tied to decor rather than buried, moss balls kept larger than the animal's head. A gentle current on one side of the tank paired with genuinely still water on the other lets the animal choose its own water movement instead of living in whatever you picked.

Foraging

Dropping food in the same spot and walking away skips the hunting behavior entirely. Tong-feeding, or scattering pieces of nightcrawler so the animal has to locate them by scent, is a real upgrade and costs nothing. A sand saucer, a shallow dish of sub-1 mm sand with small food items hidden in it, adds searching behavior in a contained way without loose sand across the whole floor. Rotate where you feed every few days.

Sensory novelty

A mild monthly rearrange of the non-critical decor, the plants, the extra tunnel, the spare stone, adds spatial novelty without taking away the hides the animal actually relies on. Introducing one new safe item for five to seven days and then rotating it out works the same way. Floating plants or a dimmed light create the dappled shade this species is drawn to. Strong current and bright light are not enrichment for an axolotl, they are just stress with a different name.

EnrichmentPriorityWhy
Two or more hidesEssentialSecurity and light control, for an animal that cannot close its eyes.
Dim, indirect lightingEssentialA genuine stress reducer for this species, not a comfort preference.
A still-water zoneEssentialLets the animal opt out of the current entirely.

Health red flags

What to watch for, when to call, and what to have ready when you do.

Call an amphibian or aquatic vet promptly if you see White or grey cottony growth on skin or gills that has not improved within 48 hours of correcting the water · suspected impaction, especially bloating, floating or no waste passed · red streaking, open wounds or ulcers · appetite loss lasting 5 days or more · bulging eyes · sudden severe floating or loss of balance · any injury with visible tissue damage

Test before you treat

Almost every condition in this section traces back to temperature or water quality, so the first two actions for any symptom are a thermometer reading and a full test. A vet will ask for both before anything else, and a course of medication into a tank that is running at 75°F (24°C) fixes nothing. Correcting the water is not a delay in treatment, it is usually the largest part of the treatment.

What to have ready when you call

Current water temperature, and how long it has been out of range
Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH readings, with the date tested
Tank volume, substrate type, filter type and how long the tank has been running
What you feed, how much and how often, and the date of the last meal
When symptoms started and whether they are worsening

Heat stress & reading the gills

The condition behind most of this section, and the early warning system on the animal's own head.

Cause: water above 72°F (22°C) for any sustained period, and acutely above 75°F (24°C). Signs: gill tips curling forward toward the face, appetite loss, restlessness or the opposite, uncontrolled floating, pale patches, and a general fragility that shows up as a fungal or bacterial infection a week later. Response: bring the temperature down, steadily rather than instantly, and test the water while you do it.

Cool it down, but not all at once Aim to lose no more than 1 to 2°F (about 1°C) an hour. Move the tank out of sun, get a fan across the surface, float sealed bottles of frozen dechlorinated water and swap them as they melt, cut the lights, and stop feeding until the temperature is back in range. Never tip loose ice into the tank, and never chase a fast drop with a large cold water change.

Reading the gills

What the gills look likeWhat it usually means
Long filaments, fanned outward and back, deep pink or redHealthy and well oxygenated. This is the baseline to photograph so you have something to compare against.
Curled forward toward the faceStress, most often heat or water quality. The earliest visible sign there is, and often days ahead of appetite loss.
Pale or washed outPoor oxygenation or poor condition. Check temperature and surface movement.
Very dark red and flaredWorking hard for oxygen. Warm water holds less of it, so check the thermometer before anything else.
Filaments short, stubby or thinning outLonger-running water quality problems, excessive flow, or nipping from a tank mate. They regrow once the cause is removed.
White cottony growth on the filamentsFungal infection taking hold on stressed tissue. Page {{P:fungus}}.
Why gills are such a good early warning They are external, well supplied with blood, and the first tissue affected when oxygen drops or the water turns hostile. That means they change before appetite does and long before anything visibly fails. Look at them for five seconds every day, in the same light, and you get days of notice on almost every problem in this section.
Cold is not the mirror image of heat A tank that drifts down to the low 50s°F (around 11 to 12°C) makes an axolotl sluggish and puts it off food, and that is genuinely less dangerous than the same drift upward. Vets deliberately hold sick axolotls in cool water during treatment. Correct a cold tank, but do not panic about it the way you would about a warm one.
Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Fungal & bacterial infection

The two conditions that follow warm water and dirty water around, and how to tell them apart.

Fungal infection

Signs: white or grey cotton-like tufts growing from the skin or gill filaments, most often starting at a site that was already damaged. Cause: almost always secondary. Warm water, poor water quality, stress or an existing injury opens the door, and the fungus, usually a Saprolegnia species, walks through it. Response: correct the water first, cool the tank into the lower half of the range, and remove whatever caused the original injury. Salt baths at the dose on page {{P:tubbing}} are the standard next step. A case that has not improved within 48 hours of the water being right, or one spreading over the body, needs a vet and a prescribed antifungal.

Bacterial infection

Signs: red inflamed patches, red streaking through the skin or into the limbs, open sores or ulcers, swelling, and an animal that is lethargic and off its food. Advanced septicemia may show as bleeding under the skin or a body-wide redness. Cause: commonly Aeromonas and Pseudomonas species, which are present in any tank and become a problem when water quality slips or the animal is already weakened. Response: this is a vet case, not a home one. Caught early, systemic antibiotics are usually curative. Left to advance, the prognosis is poor.

Telling them apartFungalBacterial
What you seeFluffy white or grey tufts standing off the surfaceRedness, streaking, ulcers, swelling, no fluff
Where it startsGills or an existing woundLimbs, belly and vent, or a wound
SpeedDays, and visible from the outsetCan move fast, and the animal often looks unwell before the skin does

What a vet may reach for Published veterinary guidance for axolotls uses enrofloxacin, gentamicin or amikacin at 5 mg per kg by injection, sometimes with the animal held in cool water at 41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C) for several weeks during treatment, and notes that tetracyclines are unsafe in this species. This is here so you can discuss it with a vet, not so you can dose an animal at home. Antibiotic dosing in amphibians is weight-based and route-specific, and getting it wrong causes its own harm.
Prevention really is the treatment Both of these are opportunists. An axolotl in cold, clean, cycled water with no injuries and no tank mate biting it rarely gets either, which is why the highest-leverage page in this whole health section is the thermometer on page {{P:cooling}}.

Impaction, floating & gas

The preventable emergency, and how to tell it from the harmless version of the same symptom.

Cause: a swallowed object that cannot pass. Gravel is the classic, oversized food is the other, and both come straight from the way this animal feeds: it inhales whatever is in front of its mouth. Signs: bloating, floating or an inability to sink, appetite loss, no waste passed for days, and lethargy. Response: stop feeding and call a vet. A gastric foreign body can sometimes be retrieved from the stomach by a vet with lubricated forceps, and one that has moved further along needs surgery.

Do not wait to see whether it resolves An impaction that is going to pass usually does so within a couple of days. Every day past that is lost time. If you know the animal ingested substrate or an oversized item and it has stopped eating, that is a call today, not a wait-and-see.

Floating: three different problems that look alike

PresentationLikely causeWhat to do
Rises to the surface, gulps air, swims back downNormal behaviorNothing. Axolotls take surface gulps.
Floats tail-up but can still swim down with effort, eating normallyTrapped gas, often swallowed air or a large mealUsually resolves in a day or two. Fast the animal, check the temperature, and reduce anything blowing bubbles into the water. A brief spell in a cool, shallow tub can help.
Cannot sink at all, or lists to one side, with bloatingImpaction or serious gas buildupVet, promptly. Do not feed.
Floating with heat signs, curled gills, in a warm tankHeat stressCool the water gradually and reassess. Page {{P:heat}}.

Preventing it entirely

  • Bare bottom for anything under about 5 in (13 cm), and fine sand under 1 mm at most for adults.
  • No food wider than the animal's head, ever.
  • Target-feed with tongs so the animal strikes at the food rather than at the floor around it.
  • Keep sand shallow, and siphon it at every water change so there is less loose material to inhale.
  • Remove small decor, and apply the head test on page {{P:substrate}} to anything new.
Why this page exists at all Impaction is almost entirely a husbandry outcome. Bare bottom or fine sand, correctly sized food, and it more or less stops being a risk. Gravel in an axolotl tank is the one decor choice in this package that has a body count.
Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Burns, injuries & other conditions

The rest of the list, most of which is water chemistry wearing a different face.

Ammonia and nitrite burns

Signs: red, inflamed patches on the skin or gills, gills held curled, appetite loss, and an animal that looks generally irritated. Cause: an uncycled tank, a crashed filter, or a missed run of water changes. Response: test, then change 25 to 50 percent of the water with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, and repeat daily until both readings hold at 0. A conditioner that temporarily detoxifies ammonia and nitrite buys time between changes. Damaged tissue recovers once the water is right, but broken skin is also an entry point for the infections on page {{P:fungus}}, so keep watching the site.

Injuries and regeneration

Nipped gill filaments, lost toes and even whole limbs regrow, which is genuinely extraordinary and is not a reason to be relaxed about the wound. Regeneration takes weeks and it happens in water that carries bacteria and fungus, so the job is keeping the site clean while the animal does the rest: remove the cause first, whether that is a tank mate or a sharp edge, keep water quality immaculate, keep the tank in the cooler half of the range, and watch daily for white growth or spreading redness. A wound that looks worse rather than better after a few days is a vet call.

Tubbing, cooling & salt baths

Three interventions worth knowing before you need them, and where each one stops.

Tubbing

Tubbing means housing the axolotl in a plain container of dechlorinated, temperature-matched water changed completely every day, instead of in the tank. No filter and no cycle to maintain, because the daily change does that job. It is the standard answer while a tank cycles with the animal already in it, while an injured animal heals away from a tank mate, during quarantine, and whenever water quality has gone wrong faster than water changes can fix it.

  • A clean, food-safe plastic tub with a lid, big enough to turn around in and deep enough to cover the animal.
  • Change 100 percent of the water daily, matching temperature. Keep the tub cool and dark.
  • Never use soap or detergent on it, and keep the tub for this purpose only.
  • Feed as normal, removing uneaten food within 15 to 30 minutes.

Cooling as treatment

Cooler water slows bacterial growth and lowers the animal's metabolic demand, which is why published veterinary guidance for treating bacterial infection in axolotls holds them at 41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C) for several weeks during the course. Hobbyists call the home version fridging. Be precise about what it is: a therapeutic measure, under veterinary direction, in daily-changed tubbed water, brought down gradually rather than by moving an animal from a warm tank into a cold fridge. It is not routine husbandry and not a substitute for fixing the tank.

Dissolve the salt fully before the animal goes in, never over it. Stay for the full ten minutes, end early if it shows real distress, then return it to clean, cool tubbed water. Excessive salt treatment does harm, so more and longer is not better, and salt does not belong in the display tank as a preventive.

Where home care stops These three are supportive. They buy time and close the door on whatever caused the problem. They do not treat a bacterial septicemia, a blockage or an ulcer. If a fungal case has not turned around within 48 hours of the water being right, or you see red streaking, open wounds, bulging eyes or a refusal to eat past five days, stop managing it at home.
