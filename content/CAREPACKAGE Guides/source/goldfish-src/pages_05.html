Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Red flags & finding a vet

Goldfish are hardier than their reputation when kept right. Most trouble starts in the water.

Test the water first, then act, if you see Gasping at the surface or laboring gills. Scales standing out in a pinecone pattern. Floating, sinking or swimming at a persistent angle. Gray-white cottony growths. Open sores or red inflamed patches. White spots like grains of salt. Clamped fins with lethargy. A protruding eye. Refusing food for 24 to 48 hours alongside any other sign here.

Why the water comes first

Ammonia and nitrite are invisible, and a very large share of sudden sick-fish cases trace back to them, or to low oxygen, rather than to disease. Unionized ammonia above about 0.05 mg/L already damages gills. A fish in a tank less than a month old that looks sick points to the nitrogen cycle first. Ich, fin rot, fungus and ulcers all take hold in fish already stressed by poor water, crowding or a temperature swing, which is why treating the symptom without fixing the water buys little. Medicating before you rule the water out adds a stressor to a fish that already has one, and some medications also kill the filter's bacteria.

When it is a vet, not a home fix

  • Illness spreading across more than one fish.
  • An open ulcer, significant bloating or a badly protruding eye.
  • Fin erosion or a white-spot outbreak advancing day by day despite clean water tests.
  • A fish listing to one side, or resting on the bottom unresponsive.
  • Persistent loss of buoyancy, severe fin damage, ongoing lethargy or appetite refusal, or a treatment that is not working.
  • Any time the honest answer to "what caused this" is "I don't know, and the water tested clean". A vet can look at a skin or fecal sample under a microscope, which is the only reliable way to name a nonspecific sign.

When you call, have your recent water test numbers, the tank volume and filter, how long the signs have been going on, and anything you have already added to the tank.

Finding a vet who treats fish

Most small-animal clinics do not see fish, and a general search will not say which ones do. The American Association of Fish Veterinarians keeps a Find a Fish Vet map, which covers North America only. If it shows no one within reach, there are three next moves:

  • Ask a local vet, ideally one who already sees your other pets, whether they would take the case with guidance from a fish vet. The association will connect the two.
  • Contact the association directly. It will email its members to look for someone near you.
  • Ask an aquarium shop or a local aquarium society, which usually know who in the area treats fish.

Ammonia poisoning & ich

Ammonia and nitrite poisoning

Cause: an uncycled tank, a tank overstocked for its filtration, overfeeding, or a filter crash after media was rinsed in tap water. It is entirely preventable. Signs: gill damage, lethargy, poor appetite and gasping, and in acute poisoning spinning and disorientation that can pass for an exotic disease. Diagnosis: a test kit, not the fish's appearance. Any reading above 0 for ammonia or nitrite is the diagnosis. Response: a water change of at least a third at 0.1 ppm, and 50 percent or more once a reading is clearly climbing toward toxic, with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water. Add surface movement. Do not deep-clean the filter, which removes the little colony you have.

One product worth having on the shelf An ammonia-binding water conditioner buys a margin between changes while the bacteria catch up. It is a bridge, not a substitute for a water change or for enough filtration.

Ich, or white spot disease

Cause: the parasite Ichthyophthirius multifiliis, almost always introduced on a new fish, plant or unquarantined decor, and taking hold in stressed fish. Signs: tiny white spots like grains of salt, evenly spread over the body and fins, with flashing, clamped fins, lethargy, dark coloration and rapid breathing if the gills are affected.

Why timing decides everything. The white spot is a stage armored by the fish's own skin. When it matures it drops to the bottom and forms a cyst, inside which it divides into as many as 1,024 offspring, which burst out as free-swimming parasites that must find a fish within about a day. Only that free-swimming stage can be killed, so a treatment has to keep the water lethal long enough to catch every wave. At 75 to 79°F (24 to 26°C) the cycle runs 3 to 6 days, and in cold water it stretches to weeks.

For a goldfishWhat to do
HeatThe 86°F (30°C) that clears ich in tropical fish is far above a goldfish's ceiling. Warm only as far as 75°F (24°C), with an air stone, to speed the cycle.
SaltAbout one tablespoon of aquarium salt per 5 gallons, added with time to acclimate. A goldfish is scaled, so salt suits it.
MedicationMalachite green at full strength, 5 ml per 10 gallons every 24 hours with a one-third water change before each dose. Never halve the dose. No improvement in 5 days means the diagnosis was probably wrong.
How longUntil every spot has been gone for days and no fish has died. The spots vanishing only means the parasites left to encyst. Expect a longer course than in a tropical tank.
  • Treat the whole tank the fish are in, since the cysts sit on the gravel. Vacuum the gravel at each change, and take out snails and shrimp first.
  • Pull the carbon and run an air stone through the course.
  • Quarantine infected fish for at least 30 days before introducing any new fish to their tank.

Fin rot, fungus, ulcers, popeye & dropsy

Conditions that look unrelated and share one underlying cause: something has weakened the fish.

Fin rot

Frayed or disintegrating fin edges, usually bacterial and tied to stress and water quality. Test the water and correct it first, and keep up the water changes. There is no home antibiotic dose to reach for: antibiotics are rarely used for fin rot, because the drug struggles to reach the fin tips and medication in the tank harms the filter bacteria. Clean water, a good diet and a stable tank let the fish clear most cases, and the fins regrow. A case that keeps spreading past clean water tests is a vet case, where treatment can mean antibiotic injections and cleaning or trimming the infected tissue.

Fungal infections

Gray-white, cottony patches that develop after an injury or a period of stress. They typically appear on damaged skin or a healing wound, not on intact skin. Keep handling to a minimum and decor smooth, and find the injury or stressor that let it in.

Ulcers

An open sore, sometimes shallow, sometimes deep enough to expose muscle. They are typically bacterial, with Aeromonas a common culprit, tied to poor water, organic buildup, handling stress or an injury that gave the bacteria an opening. Advanced lesions can become deep, crater-like abscesses. This is not a shelf-medication problem: treatment is antimicrobial therapy based on sensitivity testing, which means a vet identifies which antibiotic will actually work.

Popeye

Swelling that pushes one or both eyes outward. One eye points more often to an injury or a local infection, both eyes more often to something systemic, commonly kidney or gill dysfunction that lets fluid build up behind the eyes and is frequently traced to water quality. Trauma is the most common cause overall. Some fancy varieties, telescope eye especially, are bred with naturally prominent eyes, so compare with that fish's normal look. Test the water. If it is clean and there is no obvious injury, it is a vet case.

Dropsy

A swollen belly with scales standing out in a pinecone pattern, best seen from above, or severe bloating. It is a visible sign of fluid building up in the body, usually from organ or kidney failure, and it frequently carries a poor prognosis. There is no safe home dose: treatment depends on the cause and usually combines antibiotics, corrected water and raised salinity, with the salt level set by a vet for that fish. Over-the-counter medication can harm the tank's bacteria and make the fish worse. Some fish caught early improve within days to weeks.

Pinecone scales are an emergency Get a fish vet promptly. A goldfish that has stopped eating or can no longer swim properly is the point where humane euthanasia becomes the kinder choice (page {{P:waste}}).

Flukes, anchor worm & velvet

Three parasite problems that do not look like the spots of ich, and are easy to miss early.

Gill and skin flukes

Flatworms (Dactylogyrus and Gyrodactylus) roughly 0.1 to 0.8 mm long, too small to see on the fish. You see the reaction instead: flashing against decor and substrate, gasping or rapid breathing at the surface, and gill covers held open over gills that look swollen and pale. Confirming flukes takes a skin or gill scrape examined under a microscope, not a look at the fish. Praziquantel is the standard treatment, as a prolonged bath at 5 mg/L, and it belongs in a vet's hands.

Anchor worm

Lernaea is a thin, thread-like parasite anchored in the flesh, with a red, inflamed, sometimes ulcerated point where it burrows in to feed. It is especially common in wild-caught and pond-raised fish, so a new pond addition or a fish from a crowded holding tank deserves a closer look before it joins your stock. Pulling the thread out with forceps is often incomplete and does not touch the parasite's other life stages, so more tend to appear later. The treatment is diflubenzuron at 0.066 mg/L, which is classed as a restricted-use pesticide in some regulatory settings. Source it and apply it on the guidance of a vet or an aquatic animal health professional.

Velvet

Piscinoodinium shows as a fine gold or rust-colored dust across the body, and it is harder to catch than ich. Regular tank lighting washes it out, so angle a flashlight across the fish from the side and the dust shows as a fine sheen. It is not cosmetic: infections in freshwater fish can cause high mortality once established.

Flashing with no spots Unexplained flashing, gasping or irritation with no visible spots is worth checking with a scrape under magnification, or a flashlight held at an angle, before you assume it is nothing. Test the water first, since a water problem looks the same.
Where to treat Treat in a hospital tank, not the display, except for ich, which lives in the whole tank. A new fish quarantined for 30 days and a clean water record prevent most of what is on this page.

Swim bladder disorder & constipation

Less dramatic than the last pages, and far more common in fancy varieties.

What it looks like

Buoyancy trouble: floating at the surface, sinking to the bottom, or swimming at a persistent angle. It is much more common in round-bodied fancy varieties, whose swim bladder is connected directly to the digestive tract. Air swallowed at the surface can end up in it, and gas buildup from excess or low-fiber food presses on it.

The usual triggers

  • Overfeeding.
  • Constipation.
  • Gulping air at the surface while taking floating food.

The first response

  1. Measure portions, and switch to sinking pellets instead of floating flakes.
  2. A short fast.
  3. Then a single cooked, de-shelled pea as a short-term aid.

Diet is always the first thing to check, but not every case clears with peas alone. Abnormal buoyancy that persists points toward something internal: a swim bladder problem, bloating, or something systemic. Persistent loss of buoyancy is a reason to see a fish vet.

Why a pea works The fix is almost comically simple because swim bladder problems are so often tied to constipation and diet in a round-bodied fish rather than to anything more serious. Peas are a short-term aid and should not become a staple.
Feeding to prevent it Sinking pellets, gel food, measured portions and no extra meals are the prevention. Adults are fine going 3 to 4 days without food.

Reading waste & when euthanasia is kindest

Reading waste

Once the water checks out, waste is one of the few internal health signals you can see without a microscope or a vet visit. Normal fish waste is a solid, somewhat compact strand that roughly matches the color of the food and does not linger behind the fish for more than a few seconds. The signal to watch is a shift toward pale, clear or white waste trailing in a long, stringy thread.

It looks likeUsually meansDo
A compact strand that matches the food and drops awayNormalNothing
Pale, stringy, trailing, a single episode in an eating, active fishNonspecificNote it and recheck. Do not panic and do not treat
The same over several days, with weight loss or a pinched, sunken bellyA pattern that fits internal worms, or a fish that has stopped eatingIsolate it in a hospital tank and call a vet

It is nonspecific because the same sign fits several causes. Weight loss with a pot-bellied look fits internal worms, weight loss with refusing food fits an intestinal parasite, and a fish that simply stopped eating for any reason, including bad water, often passes mostly mucus that looks identical.

When euthanasia is the kindest option

Some fish will not recover, and a fish vet is the one to judge whether the case is past treatment. The first choice is a vet who treats fish: an overdose of anesthetic is the humane method. Where no vet can be reached, the method for aquarium fish is a clove oil overdose, since clove oil contains the sedative eugenol.

  1. Use about 0.4 ml of clove oil per liter of water, in a container of water taken from the fish's own tank.
  2. Mix the oil with a little warm water first, and add it slowly over about five minutes.
  3. The fish loses consciousness, stops breathing and dies. Leave it in the solution for at least 30 minutes after the last gill movement.
  4. Only then, as a final check, can it go in the freezer until frozen.
Methods to rule out Flushing puts a live fish into chlorinated water. Freezing a live fish, boiling, cutting off the head without first stunning the fish, and leaving it out of water to suffocate all cause the suffering the exercise is meant to end.
