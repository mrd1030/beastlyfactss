Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling: net and cup, never hands

A goldfish is not a pet you interact with by touch, and that is how the species works.

Why the slime coat matters

A goldfish's skin is covered in a slime coat that is its first barrier against parasites, bacteria and infection, and it protects the scales. Bare hands, especially dry ones, wipe it off on contact. Contact like that decreases the fish's chemical protection, disrupts the regulation of salts and water balance in its body, and removes the physical barrier against invaders. The coat can take 24 to 48 hours to regenerate, and the fish is more open to infection for that whole window. Some of the fungal and bacterial problems goldfish pick up trace back to exactly this opening.

The right way to move a goldfish

  1. Guide it into a container of tank water instead of lifting it out. It is the gentlest option and avoids the slime-coat problem almost entirely.
  2. If you need a net, wet it first and choose soft rubber over knotted nylon, which catches on fins and scales. Use the net as a guide: steer the fish toward the container, then lift the container.
  3. If bare hands are unavoidable in an emergency, wet them first and keep well clear of the gills and eyes, both especially sensitive.
  4. Keep the time out of water as short as you can, whichever method you use.
What handling looks like day to day Container transfers for water changes, gentle net use when moving a fish to a new tank, and otherwise leaving it in its own water. The relationship is built through water quality, feeding and observation, which is plenty for a fish that can live well over a decade.

Carrying a goldfish any distance

A trip to a vet or a new home is a matter of oxygen and temperature in the travel container. The bagging steps, the air gap and the battery air pump are on page {{P:outage}}.

Your own hands and the tank Tank water can carry Mycobacterium marinum, which infects people through cuts. Wear gloves for contact with the fish, the water or the equipment, and wash your hands as soon as the gloves come off.
Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Normal behavior & body language

Behavior changes show up before anything visible does. Learn the normal first.

What a goldfish is like

Goldfish are social and generally do better with their own kind. They form associative memories that last months, they can be trained to navigate mazes and push levers for food, and they appear to learn the routines of the person who feeds them. That is not a three-second memory.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Adding fish to an uncycled tank. There is no shortcut that beats testing until ammonia and nitrite both read zero.
  • Under-filtering because the tank looks clean. Ammonia and nitrite are invisible, and a goldfish produces far more of them than its size suggests.
  • Overfeeding. Adding meals without shrinking them is how most goldfish get overfed, and every uneaten pellet becomes ammonia.
  • Buying the fish before the tank. The fish costs a few dollars and the setup costs a few hundred. Doing it in that order is what produces the bowl.
  • Cleaning too thoroughly. Rinsing media under the tap or replacing all of it strips out the bacteria colony.
  • Mixing goldfish with tropical fish. A goldfish needs cool water and a tropical community needs a heater, and goldfish will go after the fins of slow, long-finned fish such as a betta.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after correct volume, filtration and a fully cycled tank, never instead of them. Decor cannot rescue a bowl or chronically poor water. In this order:

1. Swimming volume

A long, wide footprint with a deliberately open lane down the middle matters as much as any structure.

2. Structure and cover

Fish spent about 90 percent of their time in planted areas and 10 percent in barren space, live or artificial.

3. Foraging floor

Sand supported more natural foraging than larger gravel, and goldfish kept foraging even when not hungry.

4. Sinking food

Sinking pellets as the staple encourage the same foraging, which also helps cut air gulping in fancy varieties.

5. Same-type company

Fancy with fancy, single-tail with single-tail, only if the volume and filter carry the combined waste.

6. Occasional greens

Blanched vegetables add variety, within the same daily portion.

Not enrichment Overstocking called company, plants used as an excuse for weak filtration, and extra food called a treat are the same mistake wearing three different names. Sharp decor and fin-ripping plastic plants cause real injury. A bare, sterile box with no structure and no foraging outlet is under-enriched even with perfect water.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a healthy goldfish & bringing it home

Feeder or not

A feeder goldfish is the same species as a pet goldfish. The label describes what the fish was sold for, food for a larger animal, and a fish held on those terms is bought in bulk and kept crowded. The fish can still make a pet. The tank it comes from decides how risky it is, so judge the tank before the fish.

  • Walk away from any tank holding a dead or visibly sick fish, whatever the price.
  • A healthy goldfish has clear eyes, a normal body condition, intact skin and fins, no wounds, and breathes at the same pace as its tankmates. Fast gill movement next to calm neighbors is a warning.
  • Visit the shop a few times before buying, to see whether its animals look healthy in general or only on a good day.
A fish that passes every check can still carry something The water a goldfish comes home in can carry fish pathogens, among them Aeromonas and Flavobacterium columnare. Heavy intestinal infection with the parasite Goussia has been tied to high death rates in comet goldfish, with the fish lethargic, lying on their sides and passing pale feces.

Bringing it home

  1. Float the sealed bag on the tank surface for 20 to 30 minutes so the water inside comes to the tank's temperature.
  2. Net the fish across, or pour the bag through a net held over a bucket, and release only the fish. Bag water never goes into the tank, because it carries the shop's ammonia and whatever the shop's tanks were carrying.
  3. Go slow when the gap is big. When the bag and the tank differ by more than 10°F (about 6°C), acclimate slowly with an airline running into the bag and tank water added gradually, and past a gap of about 18°F (10°C) stretch it over two hours or more. The same applies when the shop's water and yours sit at very different pH or hardness.
Temperature first Goldfish are often sold from warm shop tanks and taken home to an unheated tank or a garden pond. A fish that would shrug off cold water in December can be stressed by the same drop when it comes all at once. Match the temperature first, then keep the newcomer apart for its first weeks.
Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Quarantine & the hospital tank

A fish that looks fine on arrival is the normal state of a fish whose symptoms have not shown yet. The value of a quarantine tank is that if something surfaces, it surfaces in an empty tank you can treat without risking every other fish you own.

PieceWhat it needs
How longAt least 30 days. Thirty is the floor, not the target, and 30 to 60 days is the range for a fish you especially do not want to lose or to introduce something from.
The tankA bare 10 to 20 gal (38 to 76 L) tank sized to what you quarantine: no substrate, wood or rock to hide a parasite or its eggs.
FilterA sponge filter and a small air pump, seeded from a mature filter, either from a spare sponge kept cycling in the main filter or borrowed for a few days, so it works on day one.
HeaterMatched to what the species and treatment call for.
GearIts own nets and siphon hoses that never touch another tank. Disinfect the tank and gear between uses and store them dry.

What to watch for

Feed normally, do routine partial water changes, and check daily for anything not there on arrival: clamped fins, unusual spots or patches, labored or rapid gill movement, flashing, appetite loss, cloudy eyes, or a fish hanging listlessly. A fish that arrives thin or stressed may take a few days to show its real baseline, so do not read the first day or two too literally. The hospital filter is small and new, so test ammonia and nitrite partway through.

Treat there, not in the display

  • Several medications kill the display's filter bacteria along with the pathogen, which stacks a poisoning risk on the illness.
  • Copper is extremely toxic to invertebrates and plants, which must come out before it is used.
  • Pull the carbon first and keep it out until the course ends, because carbon removes medication from the water.
  • Turn up the aeration, since several treatments cut the oxygen available and warm water holds less.
  • Dose to the label or a vet, never to memory.
Aquarium salt Salt is for scaled fish, which a goldfish is. The starting dose for ich is about one tablespoon of aquarium salt per 5 gallons, added gradually. There is no fixed number of days: continue until the infection is gone, then remove it with water changes. Salt does not evaporate, so top off with plain conditioned water. Ich is on page {{P:ammonia}}.
Find the vet during quarantine Most small-animal clinics do not see fish, so use the directory on the red flags page ({{P:redflags}}) to find one now, while nothing is wrong.
Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Sexing, spawning & fry

Goldfish are mature at about one year and in their prime in the third.

SignWhat to look for
Male, ready to spawnTubercles: small white pimples about the size of a pinhead, on the pectoral fins and the gill covers.
Female, ready to spawnHeavy at the rear, with a swelling on the left side of the body. She can lay roughly 500 to 1,000 eggs in one spawn.
TimingGoldfish usually spawn in warmer water, in the morning. In a mixed tank the chasing starts in late spring.
Tubercles are not ich Tubercles sit only on the pectoral fins and gill covers. The white spots of ich are small and spread evenly over the body and fins, and they come with flashing, clamped fins and lethargy (page {{P:ammonia}}).

Spring spawning behavior

As temperatures rise in late spring, mature goldfish in a mixed group may spawn. Males chase persistently, and some of the fish go off food for a while. That is hormone-driven and normal. Other fish prey on the eggs, which is the usual outcome unless you deliberately set up to raise fry.

Fry in brief

Do not feed for the first couple of days after hatching, because the fry are still absorbing the yolk sac. Once free-swimming, offer infusoria or baby brine shrimp nauplii, 3 to 6 times a day for the first month. The full schedule is on page {{P:diet}}.

A swollen belly is not always spawning A belly that is swollen with scales standing out in a pinecone pattern is not a fish ready to spawn. It is dropsy, and it is an emergency (page {{P:bacterial}}). Raising a full spawn of fry is beyond this package.

Heat waves, pond winters & moving outdoors

A hot tank, step by step

Warm water holds less oxygen while a goldfish uses more, so heat is a double bind. These methods work, ranked from the best:

MethodWhere it fits
Room air conditioningThe single most effective option: the tank follows the room down.
A fan across the surfaceThe best cheap active method. It cools by evaporation and needs an open top or a mesh lid instead of glass.
Lights off, lid cracked openFree and immediate. Lighting runs warm and a sealed lid traps warm, humid air.
Lower canister flowA modest assist only. A canister motor adds heat, but do not cut below the turnover the bioload needs.
Shade and insulationPrevents a slow daytime climb. Does little to cool water that is already hot.
Frozen bottlesEmergency stopgap for a single hot afternoon. They chill hard and then do nothing, which swings the temperature.
A chillerThe real fix when the problem recurs or the room runs warm all year.

Run extra aeration the whole time. Fish gulping at the surface of a pond are showing oxygen depletion, and more aeration is the best response. Do not change the temperature faster than 1°F (0.5°C) an hour.

Pond winters

Goldfish and koi are cold-hardy and can overwinter in an outdoor pond in most temperate climates, slowing their metabolism and settling near the bottom as the water cools. Feed by temperature, with the table on page {{P:pellets}}, and stop below 39°F (4°C).

Moving between a tank and a pond

The same tolerance rule applies. A fish acclimated to a warm room is not ready for spring pond water, even though goldfish overwinter outdoors. Move it when the pond and the tank are close in temperature, and float it exactly as you would a new arrival. A pond goldfish that wintered outside has its limits set for cold water, and dropping it straight into a heated indoor tank is the same shock in reverse.

Spring Expect chasing in a mature group and a dip in appetite as the water warms.

This is not a survey of all fifty states These two states are the ones this package has a source for. Goldfish rules in your state are set by its wildlife agency, so check there. Nothing on this page is legal advice.

Why release is a problem

Goldfish released into lakes, ponds and rivers are an invasive species. In the wild they do not stay small: wild populations can reach close to 2 ft (60 cm) long and weigh up to 6 lb. Common and comet goldfish are the ones that outgrow a tank, which is why the 55 gal (208 L) minimum and a pond plan matter before you buy.

If you cannot keep it

  • Ask the retailer about a return.
  • Find a surrender event to rehome the fish.
  • Give or trade the fish with other hobbyists or a local society.
  • Ask a veterinarian or a pet retailer about humane disposal. The method for a fish that will not recover is on page {{P:waste}}.
