Tank size, type & the bowl myth

A single fancy goldfish needs at least 20 gal (76 L), with 10 gal (38 L) added for each fish after that. A shubunkin needs 40 gal (151 L). A common or comet needs at least 55 gal (208 L) for one fish and belongs in an outdoor pond in the long run. Long and wide beats tall, because goldfish are horizontal swimmers. A 20-gallon long suits a fancy: it is sized for a 30 in (76 cm) length and a 12 in (30 cm) turning radius.

A bowl-kept goldfish often survives months or a couple of years, which is long enough for the setup to look like it works while the fish slowly declines. Without a filter, ammonia builds up within a day or two even in a large bowl.

Never Keep a goldfish in a bowl, a vase or a self-cleaning kit with a plant growing from the top. A handful of plant roots cannot process the ammonia a goldfish produces, and none of those containers hold enough water or filtration. The marketing changed; the underlying problem did not.

Two fish, and how to tell the tank is too small

  • Two fancy goldfish need at least 30 gal (114 L) with strong filtration. Two commons or comets need a pond or a tank well over 60 gal (227 L).
  • The test is the water, not the fish. If ammonia or nitrite ever reads above zero between water changes, or you are changing water more than twice a week just to keep the fish alive, the tank is too small for the waste it carries.
  • A few days in a bowl in an emergency, such as a move, is not housing. Even then it needs partial water changes every day.

Water temperature, heater & lid

A coldwater fish, so the equipment list is shorter than for a tropical tank.

The target

Keep the water at 65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C). At normal room temperature a goldfish needs no heater, and a heater running too warm stresses it. A heater is only for a room that falls below 65°F. A thermometer in the tank tells you which case you are in.

Where the tank goes

Away from a sunny window and away from anything that radiates heat, because the water slowly follows the room. A tank placed well solves much of the summer problem before the water goes in.

Tolerant, but only after adjusting

A goldfish survives a huge range, but its limits move with the water it has been living in. The coldest temperature goldfish survived ranged from 0.3 to 12.6°C (33 to 55°F) and the hottest from 30.8 to 43.6°C (87 to 110°F), and the temperature they had been acclimated to explained about 90% of that difference. So the rule is slowness. Change the temperature by no more than 1°F (about 0.5°C) an hour, and match the water before moving a fish between a warm shop, a room, a tub or a pond.

Steady beats perfect A tank held steady at a slightly too warm temperature is likely a better outcome than the same tank swinging between too cold and too warm in a day. Rapid temperature changes sit on the list of conditions that come before disease.

Why warm water is the danger

Warm water holds less oxygen while a warmer fish uses more. Water at 90°F (32°C) holds about 7.4 mg/L of dissolved oxygen when full, and water at 45°F (7°C) about 11.9 mg/L. Goldfish warmed from 21 to 30°C (70 to 86°F) used steadily more oxygen, roughly tripling for every 10°C (18°F) of warming. That is why a hot tank can crash with nothing else visibly wrong. The steps for a heat wave are on page {{P:seasons}}.

Dissolved oxygenWhat it means

The lid

Secure the tank with a tightly fitting lid to prevent escape. A mesh top does the same job and lets heat and humid air out, which matters in summer.

Filtration: sizing the filter

A single goldfish produces a waste load, its bioload, comparable to several tropical fish of the same size combined. That one fact drives almost every filtration decision here, and it is why experienced keepers run two filters as standard practice rather than as backup equipment.

Filtration targetNumber
Turnover to aim forThe tank's whole volume 10 times an hour or more. That is the heavy column for a heavily stocked, messy tank
Lightly stocked or planted tankAbout 4 times an hour
Typical community tank6 to 8 times an hour
On a 20 gal (76 L) tank200 gph, about 760 liters an hour
On a 40 gal (151 L) tank400 gph, about 1,500 liters an hour
On a 55 gal (208 L) tank550 gph, about 2,080 liters an hour

The box number is a best case

The flow rating printed on a filter is measured in clean water, with no media loaded and no height for the pump to lift against. Your filter never runs that way. Media clogs between cleanings and every foot of lift costs flow. Treat the rating as a ceiling you will rarely reach, size from the multiplication above rather than the tank-size label, and buy toward the higher end.

Filter types for a goldfish tank

TypeBest forTrade-off
Hang-on-backStandard tanks, the easiest to maintainLess room for media than a canister
CanisterLarge or heavily stocked tanks that need serious turnoverMost expensive, most involved to open and clean
Sponge, on an air pumpGentle low-flow filtering, fry, hospital tanksLittle chemical filtration, needs a separate air pump

Running two filters on one goldfish tank is normal. A sponge filter on an air pump can run beside the main unit, and a spare sponge kept cycling in the main filter can seed a bare quarantine tank.

Aim the outflow at the surface Oxygen enters the water at the surface, and a moving surface does it fastest. Pointing the filter outflow so it breaks the surface adds oxygen at no cost, and it matters most in summer, when warm water holds less. A battery-powered air pump is the one item worth owning before an outage (page {{P:outage}}).
Pick the filter by the order of the work Mechanical media catches solids first, biological media turns ammonia into nitrite and then nitrate, and chemical media polishes last. The biological stage is the one that keeps fish alive day to day.

Filter media, air pump & maintenance

The dirtiest-looking part is the forgiving one. The cleanest-looking part is the one that keeps the fish alive.

MediaWhat it removesHow to maintain it
Mechanical
floss, foam, sponge		Uneaten food, waste particles, visible debrisRinse freely, even weekly. It holds no colony worth protecting.
Biological
ceramic rings, sponge		Ammonia and nitrite, through the bacteria living on its surfaceBarely ever. Rinse gently in old tank water only, and never all at once.
Chemical
activated carbon		Dissolved toxins, discoloration, odor, residual chlorineConsumable: replace it on a schedule. Take it out before any medication and keep it out until the course ends.
Never Rinse biological media under the tap. Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine to kill microbes, and the media is covered in the microbes you want to keep. A few seconds under the tap is a small version of the disinfection that kills a whole colony. Fill a bucket with water siphoned out at a water change, swish the media in that, and put it straight back.

Replace biological media in portions

Swapping every piece of biological media in one pass removes the surface the colony lives on and forces a partial recycle. Replace it in portions, staggered weeks apart, so the untouched media keeps the colony running while the new media fills in.

After a medication

Antimicrobial treatments tend to kill the filter's bacteria along with their target, and copper is harder on it still. Expect ammonia and nitrite to rise for weeks to months after a copper course, and keep testing long after the course ends.

When the filter stops

The colony needs a steady flow of oxygenated water, and it becomes inefficient once dissolved oxygen in the filter drops below about 2 ppm. After an outage, a filter that sat dead for hours holds stagnant water and decomposing waste. Unplug it deliberately rather than leaving it dead in the water, and rinse the media in old tank water, or at least open the housing and look, before switching it back on.

The air pump

An air pump runs a sponge filter and adds surface movement. It pays for itself in an outage and again in a heat wave, when extra aeration is the cheapest margin you can buy.

Cycling, with or without a fish

Cycling means growing two groups of bacteria: one converts ammonia, which is toxic, into nitrite, which is also toxic, and the second converts nitrite into nitrate, which is far less toxic and is what routine water changes remove. Until the colony exists, a fish in the tank is living in its own waste.

The fishless cycle, four to six weeks

StepWhat to do
1. DoseAdd pure ammonia, with no added surfactants or dyes, to a target of 3 ppm, inside the usual 2 to 4 ppm range.
2. Test and redoseTest daily or every other day, and redose back up to 3 ppm once it drops.
3. WaitMost cycles take four to six weeks in warm, stable water. Real tanks have taken from about three weeks to two months. Overdosing slows it, because high ammonia inhibits the nitrite-converting bacteria.
4. ConfirmDone when a full dose of ammonia reads zero within 24 hours, nitrite reads zero, and nitrate has begun to build. All three must be true before a fish goes in.

Speeding it up. Media, gravel or filter floss from an established, healthy tank is the most reliable way to seed a new one. Bottled bacteria products are inconsistent, so do not assume one replaces the weeks of dosing and testing. Cold water and an inconsistent dose push a cycle toward the slow end.

The fish-in cycle

Many goldfish are bought on the same trip as the tank. This is the recovery plan, not a reason to start over.

  1. Test daily for ammonia and nitrite with a liquid reagent kit.
  2. Act at 0.1 ppm. A reading of 0.1 ppm of either calls for a water change of at least a third of the tank. A reading clearly climbing toward toxic calls for 50 percent or more, done without hesitation: a large change that briefly interrupts the cycle is a far smaller risk than leaving a fish in rising ammonia. Most fish-in cycles are managed day to day between 0.1 and 0.25 ppm.
  3. Use an ammonia-binding conditioner. It buys a margin between changes without killing the bacteria you are growing. It is a bridge, not a substitute for testing and changes.
  4. Leave the filter alone. No tap rinsing, no replacing all the media at once.
How you know it is finished Ammonia and nitrite hold at zero and nitrate builds. Nitrate is the proof, because it is the end product of both conversions. From then on, nitrate is what your weekly water changes remove (page {{P:changes}}).
If a cycled tank reads ammonia again Work backward through the usual causes before blaming the fish: overdosed ammonia, a pH or KH (carbonate hardness, the buffer that holds pH steady) crash, untreated tap water, a medication, tap-rinsed media, or a cold drop. A change of 1°C (about 2°F) can cut the bacteria's work by around 30 percent.

Reading the numbers together

Ammonia and nitrite are the emergency pair, both toxic, both exactly zero in a cycled tank. Unionized ammonia above about 0.05 mg/L already damages gill tissue, and chronic exposure brings lethargy and poor appetite. Nitrate is the housekeeping number: most fish tolerate up to about 20 mg/L, and 40 is the ceiling below which most are considered safe. It rises between changes, so it measures whether your water-change schedule keeps up with your stocking and feeding.

KH is the buffer that holds pH still. When it is used up, a tank can crash in pH suddenly, and a bottle of pH-up or pH-down against low KH can overshoot badly. Reach for a plain water change, or crushed coral or aragonite for a slow safe lift in KH and GH, before a bottled adjuster. Goldfish have far more room than a tropical fish, which is why the crash surprises people after years of neglect.

Use a liquid reagent kit A kit that reads ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH, in liquid form, is the only reliable way to know the numbers rather than guess from how the fish look. A clear tank is not a safe tank: ammonia, nitrite and low oxygen are all invisible. Test first whenever a fish looks off (page {{P:ammonia}}).
Chloramine does not evaporate Many water supplies use chloramine, chlorine bonded to ammonia, so standing a bucket overnight does not make the water safe. Use a conditioner labeled for both chlorine and chloramine, every time.

Water changes, cleaning & hygiene

A filter never removes nitrate. Only taking water out does.

How much, and how often

Change 25 to 50 percent of the water weekly on a stocked goldfish tank, because a goldfish is the heaviest waste producer for its size in the hobby. Test nitrate before each change and let the reading set the size.

Nitrate before the changeWhat it meansWhat to do
Under 20 ppmThe schedule matches the wasteKeep the weekly change
20 to 40 ppmNitrate is winning slowlyChange 40 to 50 percent weekly, or add a midweek change, and feed less
Over 40 ppmPast the safe lineA 50 percent change now with matched water, retest in two days, and find the cause

Small changes run against you: a 25 percent change on a tank that adds 20 ppm a week settles near 80 ppm, while 50 percent settles near 40.

The steps

  1. Test first and write the numbers down. Leave the filter alone: you are not cleaning it today.
  2. Siphon from the substrate up, since most of the waste settles into it, and most nitrate is made there.
  3. Match three things before the water goes back: no chlorine or chloramine, a temperature within a couple of degrees of the tank, and roughly the same chemistry.
  4. Dose the conditioner as the water goes in, not after, because chlorine damages gills within minutes.
  5. Rinse any media you must touch in the bucket of removed water, never under the tap.
Never Swap most of the water in one go on a tank that has gone months without a change. The water has drifted far from the tap, and a catch-up change of 75 percent has killed half a tank overnight. That tank gets small daily changes: 10 to 15 percent a day for a week, every other day in the second week, then 20 to 25 percent twice a week for two more weeks.

Hygiene

Tank water can carry Mycobacterium marinum, a bacterium that infects people through cuts. Wear gloves whenever your hands are in the fish, the water or the equipment, and wash your hands as soon as the gloves come off. Keep separate nets and siphons for the hospital tank. A hospital tank and its gear are disinfected and stored dry between uses (page {{P:quarantine}}).

Substrate, plants & decor

Substrate: bare, sand, or big gravel

Goldfish forage by sucking sand and gravel into the mouth and spitting out what is not food. Occasionally a piece lodges in the mouth, and a lodged stone can need sedation and forceps at a fish vet to remove it without tearing the lining. Swallowed gravel is worse, because a goldfish cannot digest it and it can block the gut. That leaves three safe floors.

FloorWhy
Bare bottomRemoves the risk entirely and is the easiest to keep clean
Fine sandLets the fish sift the way it wants to without swallowing harmful amounts. Coarse sand supports more natural foraging than larger gravel
Gravel at least 1/2 in (1.3 cm) acrossSafe only when every piece is too big for the mouth. Small pea gravel, the default in many starter kits, is the size that causes trouble

Plants

Goldfish eat and uproot most soft aquarium plants, so a planted goldfish tank is built from the tough ones. Anubias, especially the larger Anubias barteri, java fern and bolbitis fern have thick leaves and rhizomes that survive being bumped and chewed. All three grow attached to rock or driftwood instead of rooted in the substrate: tie or wedge them on and never bury the rhizome. Crinum calamistratum, a tough bulb plant that prefers cooler water, and marimo moss balls round out the list. Expect some damage whatever you pick.

Structure matters more than whether it is alive. Goldfish spend roughly 90 percent of their time in planted areas and about 10 percent in barren open space, with no strong preference for live over closely matched artificial plants.

Decor checklist

  • Smooth hardscape only: fancy goldfish have delicate fins and some have prominent eyes, so sharp ornaments and rough rock are injury risks.
  • Plants tied to wood or rock, not buried.
  • An open swim lane running the length of the tank, with planted or structured cover at both ends.
  • Everything secured so nothing can topple onto a fish.
  • No tight plastic plants or fin-trapping clutter, which can tear delicate fancy fins.
Enrichment starts here Space, structure and a foraging-friendly floor are the biggest enrichment items a goldfish gets. The ranked list is on page {{P:enrichment}}.
