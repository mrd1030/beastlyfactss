Glossary, A to H

Air stoneA porous block on an air pump's tube that releases fine bubbles. It moves water and breaks the surface, which adds oxygen. Page {{P:media}}.
AmmoniaThe first and most toxic waste product, from fish waste and decaying food. The target is 0 ppm in a cycled tank. Page {{P:water}}.
Ammonia-binding conditionerA water conditioner that binds or neutralizes ammonia, buying a margin of safety between water changes while the bacteria catch up. Page {{P:cycling}}.
Anchor wormA thread-like parasite (Lernaea) anchored in the skin, with a red, inflamed point where it burrows in. Page {{P:parasites}}.
BioloadThe total waste a tank's filter and water volume have to process. A goldfish's is unusually high for its size. Page {{P:filtration}}.
Biological mediaCeramic rings or sponge in a filter that hold the bacteria that turn ammonia into nitrite and nitrite into nitrate. Rinse gently in old tank water, never under the tap. Page {{P:media}}.
BlanchingPutting a vegetable in boiling water for a minute or two, then cooling it, so a goldfish can break it apart and eat it. Page {{P:foods}}.
ChloramineChlorine bonded to ammonia, used in many water supplies. Unlike chlorine it does not gas off when water stands, so a conditioner is always needed. Page {{P:water}}.
Clamped finsFins held tight against the body instead of spread. One of the more universal signs of stress or illness. Page {{P:behavior}}.
CyclingGrowing the bacteria that convert ammonia to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate. It takes roughly four to six weeks in a new tank. Page {{P:cycling}}.
DechlorinatorA water conditioner that removes chlorine, and for chloramine a product labeled for both, from tap water before it reaches the fish. Page {{P:changes}}.
DiflubenzuronA medicine that kills anchor worm, used at 0.066 mg/L and only on a vet's guidance. Page {{P:parasites}}.
Dissolved oxygenThe oxygen in the water, in mg/L. Above 5 supports optimum health, 2 to 4 is distress, and below 2 is deadly. Page {{P:temperature}}.
DropsyNot a disease but a sign: fluid swelling that lifts the scales into a pinecone pattern, usually from organ or kidney failure. Page {{P:bacterial}}.
Fancy goldfishThe round-bodied ornamental varieties, such as oranda and ranchu. Slower swimmers, most prone to buoyancy trouble. Page {{P:species}}.
Fin rotFrayed or disintegrating fin edges, usually bacterial and tied to poor water. Page {{P:bacterial}}.
FlashingRubbing or scraping the body against surfaces, a sign of irritation from parasites or poor water. Page {{P:behavior}}.
FlukesMicroscopic flatworms on the gills and skin. They cause flashing and fast breathing with nothing visible. Page {{P:parasites}}.
GHGeneral hardness: the dissolved calcium and magnesium in the water. Moderately hard to hard water (61 to 180 mg/L) suits goldfish. Page {{P:water}}.
gphGallons per hour: how much water a filter pumps. Ten times the tank volume an hour or more is the goldfish target. Page {{P:filtration}}.
Hospital tankA bare, separately cycled tank for quarantine and treatment, so medication does not reach the display tank's bacteria. Page {{P:quarantine}}.
Reference

Glossary, I to Z

IchWhite spot disease, caused by Ichthyophthirius multifiliis. Only its free-swimming stage can be killed, so treatment must outlast the visible spots. Page {{P:ammonia}}.
InfusoriaTiny living creatures, the first food for free-swimming fry. Page {{P:diet}}.
KHCarbonate hardness: the water's buffer against pH change. Above 100 mg/L is recommended, and at or below 50 mg/L the filter bacteria suffer. Page {{P:water}}.
Malachite greenA dye-based medicine used against ich, at full strength, 5 ml per 10 gallons, never halved. Page {{P:ammonia}}.
mg/LMilligrams per liter, a measure of how much of a substance is dissolved in water. For most water tests it reads the same as ppm. Page {{P:water}}.
NaupliiNewly hatched baby brine shrimp, the standard first food for free-swimming fry. Page {{P:diet}}.
NitrateThe end product of the nitrogen cycle. Far less toxic than ammonia or nitrite, but kept under 20 ppm with water changes. Page {{P:changes}}.
NitriteThe middle product of the nitrogen cycle, also toxic. The target is 0 ppm. Page {{P:water}}.
Nitrogen cycleThe chain in which bacteria turn ammonia into nitrite and nitrite into nitrate. Page {{P:cycling}}.
NYCRRThe New York Codes, Rules and Regulations, the state's rulebook. Part 575 lists the regulated invasive species that include the goldfish. Page {{P:legal}}.
pHHow acidic or alkaline the water is. A goldfish does best at 6.5 to 7.5, and a steady reading matters more than the number. Page {{P:water}}.
PipingGulping air at the surface. It is usually low oxygen, not infection. Page {{P:behavior}}.
PopeyeOne or both eyes pushed outward, from injury, infection or fluid buildup behind the eye. Page {{P:bacterial}}.
ppmParts per million, a measure of how much of a substance is dissolved in water. For most water tests it reads the same as mg/L. Page {{P:water}}.
PraziquantelA medicine for flukes, given as a prolonged bath at 5 mg/L under a vet's guidance. Page {{P:parasites}}.
QuarantineKeeping a new or sick fish in a separate tank for at least 30 days before it meets other fish. Page {{P:quarantine}}.
Single-tailThe streamlined varieties: common, comet and shubunkin. Larger and faster, and much better suited to a pond than a tank. Page {{P:species}}.
Slime coatThe living protective layer over a goldfish's skin. It takes 24 to 48 hours to regenerate if handling strips it. Page {{P:handling}}.
StuntingSuppressed outward growth from cramped space and chronic ammonia while the organs keep developing. A welfare problem, not a size feature. Page {{P:species}}.
Swim bladderThe internal organ that controls buoyancy. Problems with it cause floating, sinking or swimming at an angle. Page {{P:buoyancy}}.
TuberclesSmall white pimples on the gill covers and pectoral fins of a male ready to spawn. Not disease. Page {{P:spawning}}.
TurnoverHow many times an hour a filter processes the tank's total volume. Page {{P:filtration}}.
UlcerAn open sore in the skin, usually bacterial, needing a vet. Page {{P:bacterial}}.
Units°F and °C: degrees Fahrenheit and Celsius. in, cm, mm, ft: inches, centimeters, millimeters, feet. gal and L: gallons and liters. ml: milliliters. lb: pounds.
VelvetA fine gold or rust-colored dust on the skin from the parasite Piscinoodinium, best seen with a flashlight. Page {{P:parasites}}.
