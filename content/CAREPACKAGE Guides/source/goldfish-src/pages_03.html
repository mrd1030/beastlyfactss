Diet by age

Goldfish have no true stomach. Food passes along the gut instead of being held and released, which is why small portions suit them, a single large meal does not, and overfeeding shows up fast as a waste load the filter was not sized for.

AgeFoodFeedingsHow much
Fry, first couple of daysNothing: still absorbing the yolk sacNoneFeeding now only fouls the water
Fry, free-swimming to about 1 monthInfusoria or baby brine shrimp nauplii (newly hatched shrimp)3 to 6 a daySmall amounts
1 to about 4 monthsSame foodsAbout 3 a dayTapering from 3 to 6 a day as growth slows
JuvenileSinking goldfish pellet, with blanched vegetables 2 to 3 times a week2 to 3 small feedings a dayHigher caloric needs for growth
AdultSinking goldfish pellet, with blanched vegetables and protein foods 2 to 3 times a weekOnce a dayA portion it finishes in under 2 minutes

The adult day's ration can be split into two smaller meals, which suits a fish with no stomach to hold a large one. The total stays the same. Adding meals without shrinking them is how most goldfish get overfed.

Portion, not schedule Feed only what is eaten in about 30 seconds to 2 minutes, and remove anything left. If the water clouds or nitrate climbs between changes, the fish is getting more than the tank can process. What to do about a high nitrate reading is on page {{P:changes}}.

Fasting is safe in a healthy adult

A healthy adult in a mature, well-kept tank can go 3 to 4 days without food with no real concern. That is why a short trip needs no automatic feeder. The limits and the warning signs are on page {{P:appetite}}.

No supplements, and that is normal Unlike a reptile or an invertebrate, a goldfish needs no calcium dusting and no gut-loaded feeders. Nutritional balance comes from variety in the diet itself: a staple pellet, vegetables a couple of times a week, and protein foods in moderation.

Pellets, gel, flake & presentation

All three sit on the same shelf. They are not interchangeable.

FormatVerdictNotes
Sinking goldfish pelletStapleCooked, more nutritionally uniform per particle than flake, formulated for a coldwater omnivore. The simplest, best-established staple.
Gel foodReasonableHolds together in water far better than flake, so less protein and fat is lost into the tank. Good for picky eaters and for working medication into food. It has not replaced the sinking pellet as the default.
Goldfish flakeOccasionalWorks for smaller or younger fish. It loses a share of certain vitamins within about 30 seconds of hitting the water and is easy to overfeed.
Tropical fish flakeAvoid as stapleNot toxic, but the nutrient profile is built for a different fish.

Why sinking matters

Fancy goldfish have a swim bladder connected directly to the digestive tract. Air swallowed while chasing floating food at the surface can end up in it and contribute to buoyancy problems. A sinking or neutrally buoyant diet cuts that air intake, which is why sinking pellets are generally the better call for round-bodied varieties (page {{P:buoyancy}}). Sinking pellets also encourage the same natural foraging as a sand floor.

Keeping food fresh

Ascorbic acid, a vitamin goldfish need for normal growth, breaks down quickly once a container is open. Buy food in small amounts, ideally about once a month, store it cool and dry, and discard it after roughly three months whatever is left.

Feeding a pond goldfish by temperature

Outdoor fish scale their feeding to the water temperature, because their metabolism does.

Water temperatureWhat to feed
Above 50°F (10°C)Feed normally, scaled to how quickly it is taken
39 to 50°F (4 to 10°C)A reduced, more digestible wheat-germ formula, fed less often
Below 39°F (4°C)Stop feeding. The fish enters a low-metabolism dormancy and uneaten food only fouls the water

This applies to outdoor ponds. An indoor tank at room temperature stays in the top row all year, and a goldfish that turns sluggish indoors has a water or health problem, not a season.

Vegetables, protein foods & treats

A staple every meal, supplements a couple of times a week, treats rarely.

TierFoodsWhy
Staple every mealA sinking pellet formulated for goldfishComplete, balanced nutrition for a coldwater omnivore
2 to 3 times a weekBlanched peas, spinach, kale, lettuce, zucchini, cucumber; bloodworms, brine shrimp, daphnia; algae wafersAdds variety and fiber without crowding out the pellet base
Rare treatA small bit of orange, watermelon or squashFine now and then, not complete or balanced on its own

Blanching vegetables

Leafy and fibrous vegetables are good goldfish food, but not straight from the fridge. A goldfish has no teeth built for grinding raw fiber, so it mouths a chunk and spits it back. A minute or two in boiling water, then cooled, softens the structure enough to break apart. Cut the pieces small and offer only what will be eaten in a few minutes. Pull out whatever is left, because uneaten vegetable matter breaks down and fouls the water quickly.

Peas

Shelled peas, mashed or whole, are the genuinely useful short-term fix for constipation (page {{P:buoyancy}}). They should not become a long-term staple.

Protein foods

Bloodworms, brine shrimp and daphnia are high-protein treats, fed in moderation as variety rather than as a second staple. Baby brine shrimp are the first food for free-swimming fry.

If a food is not on this page Treat that as a reason to skip it, not a reason to try it. The lists here are the foods with a reason behind them.

The never-feed list, in one place Bread and other refined human starches, raw meat, cheese and onions. A food that is not on the safe list is a food to skip.

Avocado and chocolate

Keep both away from any pet. The toxicity behind them is documented in birds and mammals rather than in fish, so the reason to avoid them here is that neither is a goldfish food, not a measured fish poison.

Whatever is left, pull it out

Uneaten food of any kind is one of the more common drivers of the ammonia spikes that stall a tank. Overfeeding with vegetables counts too. Remove leftovers after a couple of minutes and check the water if the glass clouds.

When a goldfish stops eating

Appetite loss is a symptom. Start with the water.

The usual reasons, in order

  1. Water quality. Ammonia, nitrite or nitrate spikes, especially in a newer tank still cycling, are the most commonly cited cause.
  2. Overfeeding, constipation or swim bladder trouble. Excess or low-fiber food causes gas buildup that presses on the swim bladder, so appetite loss comes with abnormal floating or sinking.
  3. Stress. A new tank, incompatible tankmates, bullying or a tank that is too small. Appetite loss is often the first visible sign.
  4. Disease. Ich, internal or external parasites and fungal infections often cut appetite before anything else shows.
  5. Cold. Below roughly 50°F (10°C) a pond goldfish slows into a natural low-metabolism dormancy and eats little to nothing until spring. That is normal for a pond fish, not illness.
  6. Breeding season. As temperatures rise in late spring, goldfish getting ready to spawn, especially chasing males, may go off food for a while. It is hormone-driven and normal.
  7. Diet monotony. A repetitive diet, or too many treats crowding out the pellets, can reduce interest.

How long is too long

Time without foodWhat it means
Up to 3 to 4 daysFine for a healthy adult in an established tank, with no special arrangement
Beyond about 5 daysEffects on health may start to show

1 to 2 weeksThe commonly cited survival limit in a cold-water metabolism. It is an upper bound, never a target
The trigger to get help 24 to 48 hours of refusing food combined with any of: pronounced lethargy, scales standing out in a pinecone pattern, clamped fins, floating sideways or sinking, gasping at the surface, visible wounds or white spots, cotton-like growths, or a gaunt, thinning body. That combination, not the fasting alone, is what calls for a fish vet. Test the water first (page {{P:redflags}}).
