Setup checklist & targets

Print this one. Audit an existing setup against it, or buy against it.

The cage

At least 20×20×30 in (51×51×76 cm); 24×24×30 in (61×61×76 cm) for easier movement; wider rather than taller
Bars half an inch (1.3 cm) apart or closer, running vertically
Stainless or powder-coated; nothing galvanized (page {{P:hazards}})
Against a wall, off drafts, out of direct sun, away from windows that flash at night
Not in or near the kitchen, and not in your bedroom
Night light in the room, or a cage cover (page {{P:climate}})
Plain paper on the tray, with a grate

Inside the cage

Perches of varied diameter and texture, about 5/8 to 1.5 in (1.6 to 3.8 cm)
Dishes placed where droppings cannot fall in
Cuttlebone or mineral block, always available
Foraging and shreddable toys, rotating
No nest-like hideaway, no mirror, no sandpaper perch cover
No grit, and no frayed rope

Diet and equipment

Pellets about 75 to 80% of the diet; vegetables and fruit no more than 20 to 25%
Seed a small minority; treats under about 10% of the week
Fresh water, dishes washed daily
Gram scale reading in single grams; weigh daily
Travel carrier and first aid kit ready
Avian vet found; number on the emergency card
Nonstick cookware replaced; aerosols and scented products out of the bird's air

First aid kit

Styptic powder (cornstarch or flour as a backup)
Nail clippers sized for a small bird
Clean gauze squares
A small hand towel kept for this
A small flashlight or phone light
Avian vet and emergency clinic numbers

Targets at a glance

Room temperatureAbout 70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C); no drafts, no direct sun
Darkness10 to 12 hours, quiet and uninterrupted, at the same clock time every night

Emergency & quick targets

Call an avian vet now if The bird is on the cage floor, or fluffed for long stretches · tail bobbing with each breath, labored breathing or clicking · discharge from the eyes, nose or mouth · bleeding that does not stop in two to three minutes · a hen straining, sitting low, or no egg passed in 48 hours · any mouth contact from a cat or dog · collapse or a suspected fume exposure (fresh air first) · a sudden change in appetite or behavior.

Plan for, do not count on avoiding Avian exams come on top of every figure above: one when the bird comes home and at least one a year after that, with many avian vets recommending two, and fecal testing or blood work adds to each visit. Reproductive emergencies like egg binding arrive with no warning. Pet insurance for birds is uncommon, so most owners cover vet costs directly: keep a dedicated emergency fund.
Not priced in the tables The avian vet exams, a gram scale, a travel carrier, a night light or cage cover, a cuttlebone or mineral block, and a first aid kit are all worth buying and none is in the setup range above. A pellet-based diet with fresh food costs more than seed alone, and it is the healthier choice.
Where not to cut corners The cage. It is often the biggest single line, more than the bird, and a too-small cage becomes a welfare problem, not just an inconvenience. The bird is the smallest number here; the cage and the vet care decide the lifetime cost.

First 30 days

In order. Most of the first two weeks is doing less than you want to.

Before the bird arrives

Cage built, stocked and sited before pickup day (page {{P:enclosure}})
Avian vet found and the number written down (page {{P:quarantine}})
Quarantine room ready if you already keep birds
Nonstick cookware replaced, aerosols and candles moved out (page {{P:air}})
Night light in the bird's room (page {{P:climate}})
Gram scale, carrier, pellets and a cuttlebone in the house

Days 1 to 7: let it settle

Eating a little less for a day or two after arrival is common; the daily weigh-in and the 12-hour no-food limit (page {{P:convert}}) are the lines
No handling. Food, water, cleaning, and sitting near the cage talking softly (page {{P:handling}})
Look at the droppings every morning; they are the first thing to go odd (page {{P:droppings}})
10 to 12 hours of dark from night one, at the same time nightly
Weigh before breakfast every day and write the number down (page {{P:weight}})

Days 8 to 14

First avian exam, within one to two weeks of arrival, with a fecal check
Millet through the bars once the bird has settled
Offer the same vegetable daily for 3 to 5 days before giving up on it (page {{P:veg}})
A foraging toy with food in plain sight on top (page {{P:enrichment}})

Days 15 to 30

If the bird came on seed, begin the pellet conversion, weighing daily (page {{P:convert}})
Hand in the cage with millet, then the flat-palm step-up, 10 to 15 minutes at a time
Check one room for hazards and take the first out-of-cage session there (page {{P:hazards}})
Daily bath offered in the morning
End of quarantine at 30 to 45 days, if you keep other birds; then the introduction stages (page {{P:flock}})
What normal looks like by day 30 Eating and drinking, droppings consistent, weight steady, preening and chirping, sleeping on one foot, and grinding its beak softly at bedtime, which is contentment.

Power outages, heat, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in a blackout is usually what people light, not the cold.
The opposite of the reptile plan A cockatiel is warm-blooded and carries almost no energy reserve between meals. During an outage, keep food and water in front of it the whole time, checked and refilled by hand if anything depends on power. A bird that goes off food during a stressful outage is a reason to call the vet, not to pull the bowl.

During an outage

  • No candles, no kerosene or propane heater, no gas stove or oven used for heat, in any room the bird is in. Flashlights and battery candles beat open flame, and a battery light covers the night light too.
  • A generator runs outside, at least 20 feet (about 6 meters) from doors, windows and vents, and never in or near a garage, house or shed. An open window is not enough.
  • Keep a working carbon monoxide detector in any room with gas heat, a gas range or a nearby generator. People need levels above about 70 parts per million before they feel anything, and a bird has no such buffer.
  • For warmth, cover most of the cage with a thick towel or blanket, leaving a gap for airflow, and move it to the warmest interior room. A wrapped hot water bottle goes near the cage, never touching it. A sick bird does best at 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C).

Hot weather

A bird indoors should spend under 20 minutes in direct sunlight, with shade it can reach. An overheating bird pants with its beak open, feathers sleek and flat, wings held away from its body. Fluffed feathers are the opposite signal: cold or sick. Move it to shade or air conditioning, mist it with room-temperature water, never cold, and call an avian vet on the way.

What to do
