Safe vegetables, greens & herbs

The 20 to 25% of the diet that is not pellets, and the part that fixes vitamin A.

Vegetables are where a cockatiel gets the vitamin A its ancestors got from fresh growing seed heads, and vitamin A deficiency is the commonest nutritional disease in pet birds. The rule that does the work is simple: dark green and dark orange. Pale, watery vegetables are not harmful, they are just close to empty, and they take up room in a small bird's day.

VegetableTierNotes
Kale, chard, dandelion greensStapleThe best of the greens. Wash well, chop small, offer daily
Carrot, grated or in coinsStapleDense in vitamin A precursors. Raw is fine and holds up better in the cage
Cooked sweet potatoStapleCooked and cooled, never raw. A reliable favorite with birds refusing everything else
Bell peppers, any colorStapleSeeds and all. The capsaicin question does not arise; birds do not register heat the way mammals do, but sweet peppers are the ones to use
Broccoli, florets and leafStapleRaw or lightly steamed. Good for shredding as well as eating
Pumpkin and winter squashStapleCooked. Seeds are fine in small amounts and make good foraging
Zucchini, snap peas, green beansRegularWell accepted, lower in vitamin A than the list above. Good variety rather than good nutrition
Romaine and other leaf lettuceRegularFine, and mostly water. Do not let it crowd out the dark greens
SweetcornSparingEagerly eaten, high in sugar and starch. A treat, not a vegetable serving
Iceberg lettuce, celerySkipNot toxic, close to nutritionally empty, and iceberg can loosen droppings enough to worry you unnecessarily
Onion, garlic, leek, chiveNeverOnion and garlic are linked to a serious blood disorder in birds. Page {{P:fruit}}

Getting a refuser to eat them

Many cockatiels reject vegetables on sight for weeks. Nothing about that is unusual and none of it means the bird dislikes the food; a cockatiel decides what is food by watching its flock eat it. What works, in rough order:

  • Eat it in front of the bird, obviously and with some enthusiasm, from your own plate. This is the single most effective trick with this species
  • Change the format, not the food. A refused chunk of carrot gets eaten grated, or woven through the cage bars, or clipped to a skewer
  • Offer it first thing, before the pellet bowl goes in
  • Chop it into the size of the pellets the bird already accepts, and mix
  • Keep offering. Twenty refusals then acceptance is a normal pattern, not a failure
Herbs count, and they are cheap Parsley, cilantro, basil, dill and mint are all safe and all get shredded with enthusiasm. A supermarket herb pot on the windowsill gives fresh greens for weeks and doubles as destructible enrichment. Rinse anything you did not grow, and never use plants sprayed for pests.
Two hours, then out Fresh food is a bacterial growth medium at room temperature. Put it in, let the bird work on it, and take the remains out after about two hours. A dish of yesterday's vegetables is a health risk, not a leftover.

Fruit, treats & the never-feed list

The smaller half of the fresh share, and the short list that is genuinely toxic.

Fruit sits inside the same 20 to 25% as the vegetables, not on top of it, and it should be the smaller part. It is mostly sugar and water, cockatiels love it, and a bird filling up on banana is a bird not eating kale. Wash everything, cut it small, and remove pits and apple seeds.

FruitTierNotes
Berries: blueberry, strawberry, raspberryBestLower sugar, easy to portion, and they colour the droppings red or purple for a day, which is normal. Page {{P:droppings}}
Papaya, mango, melon, apricotBestThe vitamin A end of the fruit list
Apple, pear, peachRegularSeeds and pits out. Apple seeds and stone-fruit pits release cyanide-related compounds. The flesh is fine
Banana, grapeSparingHigh sugar, universally popular. A treat-sized piece, not a serving
Dried fruitSparingConcentrated sugar. Check for sulphite preservatives and use a piece the size of the bird's eye
Cooked egg, lean meat, fish, cheeseSparingSmall amounts are fine and useful for a molting or laying bird. Nothing processed, salted or seasoned
Millet sprayTraining onlyThe most effective training reward you have. Off the cage, in your hand, for a reason
Never, at any quantity Avocado, which is the food flagged most consistently across veterinary sources and is genuinely fatal to birds. Chocolate. Caffeine. Coffee, tea, energy drinks. Alcohol. Onion and garlic, linked to a serious blood disorder. Fruit pits and apple seeds. Salt, and salted human snacks, at 90 g of bird. And anything sugar-free containing xylitol, which has no established safe dose in birds and is not worth finding out about.

Treats, and the portion problem

Treats should stay under about 10% of the weekly diet, and the practical failure is not what people give but how much. A cockatiel weighs about 90 g. A level teaspoon of anything is a substantial meal, a whole millet spray is a day's calories, and half a grape is a portion. Break treats down before they go in, and give them by hand where possible, so the treat buys you training as well as calories. Page {{P:handling}}.

The seed dish is a treat dish now Once the bird is converted, the way to keep seed in the picture without undoing the work is to stop putting it in a bowl. Use it as the reward in a foraging toy, or as the payment in a training session on page {{P:mistakes}}. Same seed, a tenth of the quantity, and the bird has to work for it, which is the point.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Cockatiels finally have research of their own, and it points at the food bowl.

Two studies now exist on cockatiel enrichment specifically, a 2020 paper on cockatiel behavior in aviaries and a 2025 paper concluding that environmental enrichment improves behavior and welfare in captive cockatiels, with reduced feather-damaging behavior singled out as the outcome. Most parrot enrichment research has gone to larger or endangered species, so a bird kept in millions of homes was studied late.

The structural finding across parrots is the one to act on. Food offered in a bowl produces foraging times of well under an hour a day, and abnormal repetitive behavior fills the rest. That is not a small change to the routine, it is most of the bird's waking behavior missing.

Priority order, highest value first

  1. Move the food out of the bowl. Deliver part of the daily ration through things that have to be worked at. Start easy: a bird that has only ever been bowl-fed does not know food is inside a device, so stage one is food sitting visibly on top of a toy, not hidden inside it
  2. Company, whether a compatible bird or a real daily routine with you. Page {{P:flock}}
  3. Varied perch diameters and materials. A foot health intervention and a choice intervention at once. Page {{P:furnishings}}
  4. Shreddable, destructible material, rotated. Paper, softwood, palm, untreated natural fiber. Material that survives the week has not done its job
  5. A bathing opportunity, two or three times a week
  6. Out-of-cage time in a proofed room. Page {{P:hazards}}
  7. Novel objects, last. They are the least important thing on this list and the thing most often bought first

The mistakes that actually cost the bird

MistakeWhat it leads to
A seed bowl, left as the base dietVitamin A deficiency, fatty liver, a shorter life. Page {{P:nutrition}}
No night lightAn avoidable injury from a night fright. Page {{P:light}}
A cage in a spare roomChronic stress in a flock animal, then screaming and plucking. Page {{P:plucking}}
A happy hut, nest box or dark cavityA hormonal hen and a laying cycle you then have to break. Page {{P:eggs}}
A tall narrow cageUnused height, a worn tail, and no flight line. Page {{P:cage}}
Treating a solved puzzle as enrichmentA toy the bird finished in March is furniture by April. Rotate
Buying toys instead of giving timeNothing in a cage substitutes for the flock. Enrichment is partly you
Assuming plucking is behavioralIt is a vet visit first, every time. Page {{P:plucking}}
Training is enrichment, and it is free Five minutes twice a day with a millet reward: target training, step up, a recall to the hand, and stepping onto a portable perch. It costs nothing, it is mentally harder work for the bird than any toy, and it produces a cockatiel you can get back into a cage on a bad evening. Keep the sessions short and stop while the bird still wants more.

Handling, taming & reading the crest

Trust first, technique second. The crest tells you which one you are on.

The first two weeks: do nothing

A new cockatiel needs 7 to 14 days to settle before any handling attempt. Feed it, change the water, talk to it, sit near the cage and read out loud, and otherwise leave it alone. Rushing this is the commonest reason a bird takes months to tame instead of weeks, because everything you do in week one teaches the bird whether hands are safe.

The progression

  1. Sit beside the cage at the bird's level and talk softly. No hands
  2. Offer millet through the bars until the bird takes it without backing off
  3. Rest a still hand inside the cage, holding the millet, and wait. Do not follow the bird
  4. Move the millet closer to your hand each session until the bird has to stand on you to reach it
  5. Introduce the step-up cue, then practise it away from the cage where the bird is less territorial

Sessions of 10 to 15 minutes are plenty, and the bird's comfort sets the pace, not a schedule. Going back a step after a bad session costs one day. Pushing through a bad session costs a week.

Offer a flat palm, not a finger Fingers pointing down, thumb tucked in, so the bird steps onto a flat surface, or use a handheld perch. A single upright finger is an awkward thing to grip and most birds bite it rather than stand on it. And never chase a loose cockatiel to catch it: a panicked bird can fly into a window hard enough to injure itself or exhaust itself into collapse, and forcing the moment costs you weeks of trust for one evening's convenience.

Reading the crest

The crest is close to a mood dial, and reading it before a bite is one of the more useful skills with an unfamiliar bird.

CrestWhat it means
Upright and relaxed, slightly curvedCalm curiosity. Carry on
Held back, flat but loose, body softContent. Often paired with beak grinding, which is a sleepy contentment sound
Flattened tight to the head, body rigidFear or aggression. Back off now
Straight up and stiff, held forwardStartled alertness. Something changed. Find out what before you reach in

Stress signals, stop and wait

Hissing. A flattened or agitated crest. Lunging. Tail flicking. Fluffed feathers with rapid breathing. Scrambling to the far side of the cage. Neck stretching to keep you in view.

Comfort signals, carry on

Soft chirping. Preening while you are there. Beak grinding. A relaxed upright crest. Stretching one wing and leg together. Sleeping on one foot in your presence.

Where not to touch a cockatiel Head and neck only. Stroking the back, under the wings, or around the vent mimics a mate and is a direct hormonal trigger, which is how an affectionate handling routine turns into the egg problem on page {{P:eggs}}. This applies to cocks as well as hens. Scratch the head, leave the body alone.

Wing clipping & flight

You will be asked about this in week one, usually by whoever is selling the trim. Here is both sides.

Wing clipping means shortening some of the primary flight feathers so the bird cannot gain height. It is not permanent: the cut feathers are dead structures and they are replaced at the next molt, so a clip lasts one molt cycle, roughly six to twelve months. It is also not a behavior tool. A clipped cockatiel is not tamer, calmer, quieter or less hormonal than a flighted one. It is a bird that cannot fly.

The case for clippingThe case against
Removes the escape risk from an open door or windowThe same risk is removed by closing the door. A clipped bird startled outdoors can still catch a gust and go, and it cannot fly back
Removes the collision risk from windows and mirrors in a new homeFlight is the bird's main exercise. Clipped birds gain weight and lose muscle, and both feed the pages in section 03
Makes handling an untamed bird easier in the first weeksHandling built on a bird that cannot leave is compliance, not trust. Page {{P:handling}} builds the real thing
Reduces the risk in a home with a hob, a fish tank or a dogA clipped bird lands hard. Keel and leg injuries from bad landings are the common consequence, especially on hard floors
Blood feathers, and why a trim is not a haircut A growing feather has a live blood supply in its shaft and looks dark and full near the base. Cut one and it bleeds, sometimes badly enough to matter in a 90 g bird. Only fully mature feathers are ever trimmed, and knowing the difference is the whole skill. If you clip, an avian vet or an experienced groomer does it, and you watch the first time. Do not attempt a first clip yourself off a video.

If it is done, what a correct clip leaves

A correct trim leaves the bird able to glide down to the floor under control from a standing perch, symmetrically on both wings so it does not spiral. It does not leave a bird that drops like a stone, and it never removes every primary. Ask for a light, symmetrical trim and check the landing before you leave.

The honest recommendation

For most households, a flighted cockatiel in a bird-proofed room is the better outcome, and the effort goes into the pre-flight check on page {{P:hazards}} rather than into the bird. Clipping is defensible in a specific and temporary situation, a first month with an untamed bird, a house with young children, an owner who genuinely cannot secure the doors, and in those cases it is a decision to revisit at the next molt rather than a permanent policy.

Train a recall either way A cockatiel that flies to your hand on cue is safer than a clipped one, because you can retrieve it from anywhere in the room without a chase. Millet, a short distance, a consistent cue, twice a day. It is the single most useful behavior in this book and it takes a fortnight.

Sexing, weight & body condition

One of the few parrots you can sex by looking, and the one number you should track weekly.

Sexing the normal grey

Cockatiels are visibly dimorphic in the wild-type grey once the bird has been through its first molt, which is why a young bird of either sex looks like a hen. After it:

CockHen
FaceBright yellow forehead, throat, crest and cheeks, with vivid orange ear patchesMuch less yellow, a duller face, and paler orange cheeks
Under the wingSolid, unmarked plumageA barred pattern underneath the wings
TailPlain undersideBarred or spotted underside
VoiceWhistles, learns tunes, sometimes wordsRarely whistles tunes to any degree

This does not transfer to the mutations. Lutino, pied, whiteface, albino and pearl birds can be impossible to sex on appearance, and a pied bird may retain hen-type barring for life. Where it matters, and it matters for anything on pages {{P:eggs}} and {{P:eggbinding}}, a DNA test on a blood spot or a plucked feather is the actual answer and costs about the same as a bag of pellets.

Why you need to know Because a hen can lay without a mate, and chronic laying is the commonest serious problem in this species. An owner who thinks their bird is a cock is an owner who does not notice a laying cycle starting.

Weight is this species' vital sign

A bird hides illness until it cannot, but it cannot hide a falling weight. A digital kitchen scale reading in 1 g steps costs less than a bag of pellets and is the single most useful piece of equipment you will buy. Glue or tape a short perch to the pan, weigh the bird at the same time each day, before breakfast, and write it on page {{P:ownerlog}}.

ReadingWhat it means
80 to 125 g, average about 90 gThe published range. Your own bird's stable number is what matters, not where it sits in the range
Day-to-day variation of 2 to 3 gNormal. Food in the crop, a dropping just passed
A steady fall over a weekInvestigate. In a converting bird it means the conversion is failing. In a settled bird it is a vet call
Roughly 10% below baselineCall the vet. In a 90 g bird that is 9 g, which is nothing on a bathroom scale and everything to the bird
Roughly 20% above idealMerck's working definition of obesity in a bird. Page {{P:nutrition}}

Feeling the keel

The keel is the ridge of breastbone running down the front of the bird. Rest a finger either side of it during a normal head scratch.

  • Sharp and prominent, muscle falling away either side: underweight. Common in a bird that has been converting too fast, or is sick
  • Ridge easily felt, muscle rounded and full to each side: what you want
  • Keel hard to find, chest convex, a groove down the middle: overweight. Diet and flight, not a crash diet
Establish the baseline in week one Weigh the bird every day for the first fortnight and record it. That fortnight of numbers is what makes every future reading meaningful, and it is the thing owners wish they had when something looks off in year three.

Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

VCA notes reproductive problems are even more common in cockatiels than in budgies. This is the page that prevents them.

A cockatiel hen will lay eggs with no male present and no intention on your part. She does it because the environment told her to, and every clutch costs her calcium she pulls out of her own skeleton. Chronic egg laying is laying clutch after clutch, out of season, and it ends in metabolic bone disease, weakness, and the emergency on page {{P:eggbinding}}. It is close to entirely preventable, and the levers are environmental rather than medical.

The trigger list, and what to do about each

TriggerRemove it
Long daysThe main lever. 10 to 12 hours of darkness normally; to break a cycle, cut to 8 hours of light a day under vet advice. Page {{P:light}}
Any dark enclosed cavityNest box, happy hut, tent, a cardboard box, the space behind the sofa, a kitchen drawer she found. All of it goes
Petting the back, wings or ventHead and neck only, by everyone in the house. Page {{P:handling}}
A mirror, or a bonded toyOut. To a lone hen a mirror is a mate
Shredding material and soft nesting substrateRestrict paper shredding during a hormonal period, and keep the tray liner out of reach
Rich, abundant, constant foodPlenty reads as a good breeding season. Keep portions measured, keep treats rare, and move food into foraging toys
Rearranging nothingMoving the cage to a different wall and rearranging perches disrupts a territory she has decided is a nest site
Do not remove the eggs This is the counterintuitive one and it is the one owners get wrong. Pulling eggs as they appear tells the hen the clutch has failed and she lays a replacement, which is exactly how chronic laying starts. Leave the clutch alone and let her sit. A cockatiel clutch is 3 to 8 eggs, average 5, and incubation runs 19 to 21 days, so a hen allowed to sit typically gives up after roughly three weeks and stops. If you must, swap real eggs for dummy eggs so nothing rots. Sitting on a clutch costs her far less than laying four more.

Support a laying hen

  • Calcium, permanently available. Cuttlebone and a mineral block in the cage, not offered occasionally. A hen on a seed diet is a hen in calcium debt
  • Vitamin D3, which she needs to use the calcium. This is another reason the diet on page {{P:diet}} is a pellet diet
  • Weigh her daily through a laying period. Page {{P:sexing}}
  • Extra protein while she is laying: a little cooked egg is traditional and effective
When the environment is not enough A hen who keeps laying after everything above has been done needs an avian vet, not another month of adjustments. Hormone therapy exists, in the form of injections or implants, and it is used exactly for this. Ask for it before she is depleted rather than after the first egg-binding episode.

Egg binding

A decision you make in ten minutes, not a condition you monitor. Read this before it happens.

Egg binding is a hen unable to pass an egg. VCA names cockatiels among the small birds most often affected, and states plainly that small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg-bound, from compromised circulation and pressure on the airways. There is no wait-and-see version of this page.

Signs, roughly in the order they appear Weakness, and sitting low or on the cage floor rather than perching. Straining, as if trying to pass a dropping, often repeatedly and with nothing produced. A visibly swollen or firm lower abdomen. Fluffed feathers, wide stance, laboured breathing. Sometimes an egg visible at the vent, or tissue prolapsing out of it. In late cases, leg weakness or paralysis on one or both sides, from the egg pressing on the nerves.

Any of these is a same-day emergency call. Not tomorrow morning.

Why it happens

CauseDetail
Low calciumThe commonest reason. Calcium is what makes the oviduct contract, so a depleted hen cannot push. Seed diets are deficient in calcium, selenium, and vitamins D and E
Obesity and lack of exerciseBoth from the same high-fat seed diet. Page {{P:nutrition}}
Chronic layingEach clutch depletes her further, so the risk rises with every one. Page {{P:eggs}}
A first clutch, or an old henBoth carry higher risk
An oversized or misshapen eggOften itself a nutritional problem: soft-shelled and shell-less eggs point straight back at calcium
Cold, or no suitable place to layAn environmental contribution rather than a cause on its own

What the vet will do

Supplemental heat, injectable fluids, calcium and vitamin D3 are the first-line supportive treatment, and many hens pass the egg once those are on board. Oxytocin may be given to stimulate contraction. If that fails, the egg may be lubricated and eased out, or collapsed by aspirating its contents through a needle so the shell can be removed in pieces. Surgery, a salpingohysterectomy, is the last resort and is also the permanent solution for a hen who binds repeatedly.

What you can do in the car Keep her warm and quiet in a small carrier, ideally around 85 to 90°F (29 to 32°C) with a covered heat source she cannot touch directly, and get moving. Warmth and humidity are genuinely supportive. Do not try to pull, squeeze or manipulate the egg: the shell breaks inside her, and that turns a treatable emergency into a surgical one. Do not give calcium orally to a collapsed bird.
Prevention is four things A pellet diet, a permanent cuttlebone, 10 to 12 hours of darkness, and no cavities. That is it. Every hen this happens to is a hen who was missing at least one of them, and the four together cost roughly nothing.
