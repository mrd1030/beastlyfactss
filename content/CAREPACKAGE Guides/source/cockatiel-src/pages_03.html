Section 03 · Feeding

Diet: pellets, seed & how much

The single biggest lever on how long a cockatiel lives.

Commercial seed mixes are high in fat and carbohydrate but short on protein, vitamins and minerals, calcium and vitamin A among them. Worse, a bird picks its favorite seeds, sunflower and millet, and skips the more balanced parts, so what it eats is narrower than the label suggests. All-seed diets are a well-documented cause of the malnutrition commonly seen in pet cockatiels, and they are linked to fatty liver disease.

How the day works

Cockatiels are free-choice eaters, not meal eaters. Their fast metabolism means most choose to eat through the day, so refresh the pellet bowl in the morning, when they are hungriest, and top it up if it empties before evening: it should never sit empty for long. Fresh food goes in daily and comes out within a couple of hours, before it spoils. Portions are smaller than they look: a teaspoon of food for a cockatiel is roughly a dinner plate for a person.

How much a day

A cockatiel fed seed gets about 1.5 to 2 level measuring teaspoons a day, in a shallow dish. Two birds sharing a cage get a dish each, so the lower-ranked bird still eats. Seed left over at the end of the day means too much went in, and the bird has been picking out its favorites.

On pellets, the bowl stays topped up and the bird sets its own intake. To put a number on one particular bird, use a gram scale: weigh the food going in, weigh what is left 24 hours later, and average the difference over seven days. That average is the full day's food, and it is the amount to keep offering while seed is traded out for pellets.

Hens, molts and youngsters Laying hens need enough calcium in the diet, since a shortfall is a direct route to egg binding (page {{P:binding}}). A molting bird's need for protein, calcium and iron rises sharply. A weaned youngster eats the same pellet-based diet as an adult.

Converting a seed eater to pellets

A few weeks of mild inconvenience, if it is done slowly. A real emergency risk, if it is rushed.
Never starve a bird onto pellets A bird raised on seed may not recognize a pellet as food, and if the seed disappears before it does, some birds simply stop eating. The firm limits are 12 hours without food for a small bird like a budgerigar and 24 hours for an African grey. A cockatiel is a small bird with a fast metabolism: hold it to the shorter limit.

Two schedules

Both substitute gradually while the bird keeps guaranteed access to the old food. They were written for conures, but the mechanism is the same for any seed-imprinted parrot, cockatiels included.

MethodSteps
Stepped75% seed and 25% pellets for three days, then 50/50 for three days, then 25% seed and 75% pellets until the bird is fully converted.
Tapered90% seed and 10% pellets on day one, 80/20 on day two, and so on, down to 0% seed on day 10. A bird that balks partway holds at 20% seed and 80% pellets until it converts.

If the bird stalls, hold at that ratio and let the timeline stretch. In a mature bird the change can take days, weeks or months.

What helps with a cockatiel

  • Pellets first thing in the morning, before any other food, when the bird is hungriest and most willing to try something new.
  • Play to the ground forager. Spread pellets on a flat surface for the bird to walk through and peck at, or tap single pellets on a table to mimic natural pecking. That can work where a bowl swap does nothing.
  • Use a moist food as a bridge. Grind pellets to powder and mix it into a little cooked egg, mashed vegetable or soft fruit, then coarsen the pellet and reduce the moist food over time.
  • Pull the seed only once the bird is reliably eating pellets alongside fresh vegetables and fruit.

A bird whose conversion stalls completely, whose weight keeps dropping, or who develops breathing sounds along the way needs a vet exam rather than more pushing.

Vegetables, fruit & fresh food

The 20 to 25% that pellets do not cover, weighted toward vegetables rather than fruit.

Fresh food fills no more than about 20 to 25% of the daily diet by volume. Wash it and cut it small, take the seeds out of apples, and pull anything uneaten within a couple of hours to prevent spoilage and bacterial growth.

GroupSafe choices
Vegetables and greensBroccoli, carrots, leafy greens, bell peppers, pumpkin, squash, sweet potato, zucchini
Fruit, in small piecesApple with the seeds removed, apricot, banana, blueberries, melon, mango, papaya, peach, pear, strawberries
Table food, small amountsCooked lean meat, fish, egg, or a little cheese. Nothing processed.
Choose by color The bright yellow, red and orange foods carry the most vitamin A: bell peppers, broccoli, carrots, sweet potatoes, squash, mango, papaya and cantaloupe. A seed diet is short on vitamin A, which makes these the foods worth offering most often.

Little nutrition

Iceberg lettuce and celery are not toxic, but they are close to empty calories: mostly water with few nutrients. They displace better food rather than adding anything, so skip them in favor of the denser choices above.

Washing

Birds are very sensitive to pesticides, chemicals and sprays. Wash all produce well and buy organic where you can. Anything from a garden that has been sprayed stays out.

Getting a suspicious bird to try something new

A cockatiel that refuses a new vegetable has not decided anything yet. Offer the same one every day for 3 to 5 days before giving up on it. Spreading food on a flat surface, as in the pellet conversion, works for fresh food too, because it matches how a ground-foraging cockatiel feeds.

The clock

Fresh food goes in when you get up and comes out a couple of hours later. Water stays available all the time, and the dish is washed daily, because a dish that has held vegetables and droppings for a day is not somewhere a bird should drink.

Section 03 · Feeding

Treats, supplements & the never-feed list

The smallest part of the diet, and the part where the real dangers are.

Treats

Treats and table food stay small: no more than about 10% of the weekly diet. Millet is the most useful taming reward there is, offered through the bars and then from your hand (page {{P:handling}}), so keep it for that job rather than the food dish, and break it into small pieces. A teaspoon of anything is a big serving for a bird of about 90 g.

Cuttlebone and supplements

A cuttlebone or mineral block belongs in the cage at all times, for calcium and for the beak. A bird eating 75 to 80% of its diet as pellets does not need vitamin or mineral supplements on top. A bird still on seed, or a laying hen, is the one whose diet falls short, and the answer is the conversion and a talk with the vet rather than a jar off a shelf.

Never feed Avocado. The food most consistently flagged as toxic. Every part of the plant contains persin, a compound with documented cardiac toxicity in birds. Breathing distress typically shows about 12 hours after eating it, and death is possible within one to two days.
Chocolate. Theobromine and caffeine cause hyperactivity, tremors and potentially seizures. Darker, more bitter chocolate carries more risk.
Caffeine and alcohol, in any amount: coffee, tea and soda included.
Onion and garlic. They contain chemicals that can harm a bird's blood cells, especially in concentrated forms like garlic powder or dry soup mix.
Apple seeds, and processed food.
No dramatic accident needed A countertop within flight range of an out-of-cage bird is enough exposure to any of the foods above. Clear the counter before the cage door opens.

The seed dish trap

A cockatiel hulls its seed and leaves the husks behind, so a seed dish can look full while holding very little. Empty the husks out before deciding the bird has food. A bird still eating mostly seed needs the conversion on page {{P:convert}}, not a bigger dish.

Weight, body condition & the daily scale

A cockatiel hides illness under its feathers. Weight is the vital sign that does not.

A gram scale catches what your eyes and hands cannot. Weight loss shows on a scale days to weeks before a bird looks thin, sick or fluffed up, because feathers hide body condition extremely well. A bird that eats normally can still be quietly losing mass, which is exactly how avian gastric yeast works (page {{P:gut}}).

WhatTarget
Typical adult weight80 to 125 g, about 90 g on average. Your bird's own settled weight is what matters.
How oftenEvery day, at the same time, before breakfast, on the same scale. Write the number down.
ScaleA kitchen scale that reads in single grams. A perch fitted to it makes the habit easier.
During a diet changeDaily without fail. A loss of more than 1 to 2% in a week means the change is too fast; more than 10% below the starting weight means call the vet.
ObesityRoughly 20% over the bird's ideal weight, a body condition score of 4 out of 5. In a 90 g bird that is about 18 g, invisible without the scale.

A trend, not a number

A single reading tells you almost nothing. A logged trend, taken at the same time of day on the same scale, tells you a lot. A vet asking what the bird normally weighs is not making conversation: a few grams over a month in a bird this size is a finding, and a bird with no history is a guess. The Owner Log on page {{P:ownerlog}} is built for it.

Feeling the keel

Weight alone does not say whether it is muscle, fat or a full crop. Body condition scoring in birds is built around the keel: the blade-like ridge of breastbone down the center of the chest, with the flight muscles attached on either side. It is assessed by feel, not by eye, on a scale from 1 (emaciated) to 5 (obese).

What the finger findsWhat it points to
A subtle ridge, with muscle rounded out on either sideGood condition
A sharp, prominent keel with little paddingUnderweight
A keel you can barely find, cushioned in fatOverweight
Do not chase a number The value of the keel check is tracking the same bird over time. A keel that has gotten sharper since last month is the signal. The nine-point scale used for dogs and cats does not transfer to birds.
