Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Trust first, technique second. It goes faster when the bird sets the pace.

Let it settle first

A new cockatiel needs a genuine adjustment period: give it 7 to 14 days with no handling attempts. Feed it, change the water, sit near the cage and talk softly. If other birds already live in the house, those days overlap quarantine (page {{P:quarantine}}), and the new bird is handled last each day.

The progression

Move to the next step only once the bird is calm at the current one.

  1. Treats through the bars. Sit near the cage, talk softly, and offer millet through the bars.
  2. Hand in the cage. Once that is comfortable, offer the treat with your hand inside the cage, held still.
  3. Step-up. Offer a flat palm with your fingers pointing down and your thumb tucked in, or a handheld perch, as the surface to step onto. Avoid a single upright finger: most birds find it awkward to grip and are more likely to bite it than step onto it.

Keep sessions short, 10 to 15 minutes is plenty, and let the bird's comfort decide how quickly you move forward rather than a fixed timeline.

Later rungWhat it buys you
TargetThe bird touches a stick with its beak on cue: a way to move it anywhere without grabbing it
Step up and step downHandling without a chase, in and out of the cage
RecallA bird that comes back to your hand on cue, which is what makes a flighted bird manageable
Carrier and towelA calm bird at every vet visit, and an easy nail trim or exam

Each rung is built the same way: a marker, a small reward such as a flake of pellet or a sunflower seed, and a short session ended while the bird is still interested.

Never chase a loose cockatiel A panicked, exhausted bird can collapse, or fly into a window hard enough to injure itself. Forcing the moment also sets back the trust you have been building, which matters more than winning any one evening.

Where to touch

Head and neck. A bird can read stroking along the back, rump or hindquarters as mating behavior, which is one of the triggers for chronic egg laying in a hen (page {{P:laying}}). A trusting cockatiel is also simply easier and safer to handle for routine jobs like cage cleaning, nail trims or a vet visit.

One cockatiel or two

A decision that shapes every other one.

Cockatiels are flock birds and need regular interaction to stay well-adjusted. A single bird with excellent toys and no company is still a single bird, and the daily time commitment for a solo cockatiel is substantial: the number to plan around is an hour or more out of the cage every day. A solo bird can live happily without another cockatiel on one condition: daily attention from its people, with toys and a radio or television for the hours in between.

The middle path

A second bird in its own cage gives company without taking over the bond you have with the first. No choice of sex guarantees the two will get along, because wild cockatiels pick their own mates. The companion that works is another cockatiel: a bird of a different species gets its own cage.

Adding a second cockatiel

  1. A vet exam for the new bird, then the full quarantine in a separate room, where the resident can hear the newcomer but not see it.
  2. Both cages in one room, close enough to see each other but not to reach. Move them closer over the following days while both stay calm. Two birds that choose the perches nearest each other and interact through the bars are ready for the next step.
  3. A neutral cage that neither bird has claimed, with the first days together closely supervised.

Beak sparring and squabbling over perches are normal. Pulled feathers or any blood mean the birds are separated for good. Through all of it, give the resident extra attention with the newcomer in view, so the new arrival reads as a bonus and not a replacement. Full acceptance takes weeks to months.

Answer this honestly first Will this bird get an hour or more out of the cage with you every day, on the busy weeks as well as the easy ones? If yes, one bird handled daily is a full life. If the honest answer is "some days," plan for two.

Body language & reading the crest

The crest is real information, and it is one cue among several.

The crest

Cockatiels, cockatoos and hawk-headed parrots raise and lower their crest feathers to show their emotional state. Cockatiels erect the crest to show excitement, in response to good and bad things alike. The crest tracks mood only in a general way, so read it alongside posture and feather fluff before deciding what it means.

CrestWhat it usually goes with
Relaxed and uprightA calm, comfortable bird
Held back and relaxedContentment
Raised erect, or swept sharply forwardExcitement or startled alertness: something changed
Flattened tight to the head, body rigidFear or aggression. Back off now.

Stress: stop and wait

Hissing. A flattened or agitated crest. Lunging. Tail-flicking. Fluffed feathers with rapid breathing. Scrambling to the far side of the cage. Neck-stretching.

Comfort: carry on

Soft chirps. Preening while you are there. Beak grinding. A relaxed, upright crest.

Cues worth knowing

  • Beak grinding is a soft scraping sound a comfortable bird makes, often just before sleep.
  • Eye pinning, the pupils flashing wide and narrow, marks arousal, good or bad. Watch the rest of the bird before a hand goes near its face.
  • Tail bobbing timed to each breath is a bird working harder to breathe: a same-day vet visit, not a mood.
  • Regurgitating food onto a favorite person, mirror or toy is a bonding behavior, distinct from vomiting, which sticks to the face and chest feathers.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Cockatiels finally have research of their own, and it points at the food bowl.

Two studies now look at enrichment in cockatiels specifically. A 2020 study examined the effects of environmental enrichment on cockatiels in aviaries, and a 2025 study concludes that environmental enrichment improves behavior and welfare in captive cockatiels, with reduced feather-damaging behavior singled out. Across parrots, food offered in a regular bowl produces foraging times of less than an hour a day, which leaves most of the day unoccupied.

Priority order

  1. Food delivered through foraging rather than a bowl. Start easy: a bird that has only ever been bowl-fed does not know food is inside a device, so the first stage is food left in plain sight on top of a toy. Increase difficulty as it learns, and keep an eye on intake.
  2. Company, whether a compatible bird or a real daily routine with you.
  3. Varied perch diameters and materials, at different heights and distances.
  4. Shreddable material, rotated: paper, softwood and palm. Material that survives the week has not done its job.
  5. A bath offered daily, in the morning so the feathers dry before night: a shallow dish of lukewarm water or a gentle mist, with a spritz once or twice a week for a bird that will not bathe on its own. Regular bathing also keeps less powder down in the air.
  6. An hour or more out of the cage daily, supervised, in a room checked for hazards.
  7. Novel objects, last.

Flight & wing clipping

You will be asked about this in week one, usually by whoever would do the trim.

The facts are mostly settled: a clip is feathers, not amputation; it grows back at the molt; a correct trim leaves controlled descent; and an incorrect one is dangerous. What is genuinely unsettled is a values call, and it belongs to you and your vet.

What a clip is

A clip shortens the primary flight feathers along the outer edge of the wing, so the bird cannot gain or hold altitude. A correct trim removes four to seven of the outermost primaries on both wings, cut below the covert feathers that overlay them. The secondary feathers closer to the body are left alone, because they let the bird slow down and control a landing. Both wings always get the same trim. Only mature feathers are cut: a growing blood feather has a live blood supply in its shaft, which is the main reason a vet does the trim.

Cockatiels fly fast and land badly A cockatiel clipped too short cannot slow itself down and crash-lands on hard floors, which injures the keel or the skin just behind the vent. A correct trim still leaves the bird able to flutter down under control. Check the landing before you leave the clinic.
The case for clippingThe case against
Reduces the risk of flying into windows, mirrors or ceiling fansFlight is a bird's main exercise, and a safe flighted home can make trimming unnecessary
Lowers the odds of an escape through an open door in a busy householdLoses a major route to heart and muscle conditioning
Can make early taming manageable for a new or nervous ownerA poor trim risks falls that injure the keel or beak
Some clipped birds become more accepting of hand trainingA bird more accepting because it is less able to leave has not been trained

What clipping does not do

  • It does not guarantee a bird cannot fly. A tailwind, a draft or feathers that have grown back can give enough lift to reach an open door.
  • It does not treat behavior. Biting, screaming and hormonal behavior have their own causes.
  • It is not permanent. Every clipped feather regrows at the next molt, and molt is not on a fixed calendar. Treat a clip as a decision revisited each molt.
Train a recall either way A bird that reliably returns to your hand on cue can be out of its cage without every open door being a live risk, which is what makes keeping a cockatiel flighted a realistic option (page {{P:handling}}).

Nails, beak, blood feathers & first aid

A cockatiel weighs about 90 g, and that changes how much blood it can spare.

Nails

How often nails need trimming depends more on what the bird stands on all day than on a calendar, so check the bird in front of you. Every toenail has a blood vessel and nerve running partway down it, the quick, and the longer the nail, the further it extends. On a light nail it shows as a pink area; on a dark nail, a flashlight held behind it helps. Take a little at a time. If you cut the quick, keep the bird safely restrained, pinch the toe from side to side just above the cut, press styptic powder to the nail end, and keep the bird away from its water dish for several hours so the clot is not disturbed.

Beak

An overgrown beak is a symptom A healthy bird with enough to chew rarely needs a beak trim. An abnormally long beak is most commonly caused by chronic malnutrition or liver disease, and overgrown beak and nails together can point to the liver. A beak that keeps coming back means a vet workup, not another trim.

Broken blood feathers

A blood feather is one still growing in, with an active blood supply through the shaft. A bird carries very little blood for its size, and one that has snapped a blood feather can lose a lot in a short time.

  • Do not pull it at home. It leaves the shaft open and can cause more blood loss; even a vet pulls one only as a last resort.
  • Shaft snapped, base still seated: blot the broken tip and press styptic powder, cornstarch or plain flour onto the exposed end. Never pack powder into an open follicle.
  • Feather torn out completely: that is an open wound. Use firm, steady pressure with clean gauze and get the bird to a vet.
  • If it has not stopped in two to three minutes, call an avian vet at once.

A new feather forms in the same follicle and matures in roughly four to six weeks.

The towel

Teach the towel before you need it, in short, reward-paired sessions, so the bird steps into a toweled hand rather than being chased with one. Keep a session short and stop the moment the bird shows real distress rather than mild annoyance. If its breathing becomes labored or it weakens, return it to the cage at once.

First aid kit

The six items for a small bird's kit are on the setup checklist (page {{P:checklist}}). Check the kit twice a year. The most useful item in it is your avian vet's number.
