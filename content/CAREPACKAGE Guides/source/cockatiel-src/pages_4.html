The line nobody budgets for A reproductive emergency, the likeliest one in this species, runs $300 to $800 or more, and diagnostics sit on top of the exam fee. Bird insurance barely exists, so almost every owner self-insures, and that only works if the money is somewhere. Put $20 a month aside from the day the bird arrives.
Where to save, and where not to Not the cage, because a small one gets replaced within a year. Not the bar spacing, because it is a safety limit. Not the pellets, because the whole of section 03 runs through the food bowl. Do save on toys, which are paper, cardboard and untreated wood, and on perches, which grow on unsprayed apple and willow for free.

First 30 days checklist

In order. The first fortnight is mostly about doing less than you want to.

Days 1 to 3: leave it alone

Cage set up and standing before the bird came home, in its permanent spot
Whatever food the bird was already on, in the dish. Change the diet later, not now
Night light on from the first night
Sit in the room and talk or read aloud. No hands in the cage
Weigh daily and start the log on page {{P:ownerlog}}

Week 1

Well-bird exam booked and attended, with a PCR panel if there is another bird in the house
Quarantine room set up if you have other birds. Page {{P:quarantine}}
Baseline weight established across seven days
Droppings photographed once so you know what normal looks like
Millet offered through the bars, no hands inside yet
Sleep routine fixed: same lights-out time every night

Week 2

Hand resting inside the cage holding millet, still and patient
First vegetables offered, in several formats. Expect refusal. Page {{P:veg}}
Pellet conversion started, one method chosen. Page {{P:diet}}
Weight watched daily through the conversion
Foraging toy introduced with food visible on top, not hidden inside

Week 3

Step-up cue introduced, in 10 to 15 minute sessions. Page {{P:handling}}
Bathing offered: a shallow dish or a light misting
Room bird-proofed and the pre-flight check practised. Page {{P:hazards}}
Pellet share visibly increasing, seed share falling

Week 4

First supervised out-of-cage session, doors and windows shut, fan off
Recall training started with millet. Page {{P:clipping}}
Sex confirmed if the bird is a normal grey, or a DNA test ordered. Page {{P:sexing}}
Toys rotated for the first time, not added to
Emergency card on page {{P:emergency}} filled in and on the fridge
Next wellness exam pencilled into the planner on page {{P:equiplog}}
What normal settling looks like A new cockatiel eats less than usual for a few days, sits still and watchful, and may be silent for a week before it whistles at all. That is a bird assessing a new environment, not a sick bird, and the difference is the scale. A quiet, watchful bird holding its weight is settling. A quiet bird losing weight is ill. Power outages, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in an outage is not the cold. It is what people light to fix the cold.
The rule that inverts from every other pet A reptile in a power cut is a temperature problem. A cockatiel in a power cut is a fumes problem. Candles, kerosene and propane heaters, generators running near a window, a gas hob left on for warmth, and a wood stove that smokes are all far more dangerous to a 90 g bird than a cold night is. If the power goes out, the first thing you decide is what you are not going to light in the bird's air.

If the power goes out

  • Keep feeding. A cockatiel has almost no energy reserve and eats through the day. Food and water stay available whatever else is happening
  • Move the cage to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors, and cover three sides with blankets to hold warmth while leaving airflow at the front
  • Body heat beats fire. A carrier under a blanket in an occupied room, or a covered hot water bottle the bird cannot touch directly, is safer than any flame
  • Ventilate anything you do burn. If a heater has to run, it runs in a different room with the door closed and a window cracked, and the bird is not in that room
  • Keep the night light plan. A battery night light in the emergency box means an outage does not also become a night fright. Page {{P:light}}

The go bag

Travel carrier with a low fixed perch
Three days of pellets in a sealed bag
Bottled water and a clip-on dish
Millet, for a bird refusing to eat
Battery night light and a torch
Towel, paper liner, first-aid box
A copy of pages {{P:emergency}} and {{P:sitter}}
Photo of the bird, on your phone and printed

In the car

RuleWhy
A carrier, not the cageA cage is a box of perches and toys to be thrown around in. A small carrier with one low perch is safer, and the reduced space calms most birds rather than distressing them
Cover itLess visual input, much less stress. Leave one side open for airflow
Belt it in, on the floor or the seatNever the boot, never a lap, never the front seat with an airbag
Water on the road, not in the carrierA full dish soaks the bird and a soaked bird chills fast. Offer water at stops instead
Never leave the bird in a parked carNot for five minutes. A closed car in the sun kills a bird faster than it kills a dog, and cold does it almost as quickly
No air freshener, no smoking, no vapingA car is a very small volume of air. Page {{P:hazards}}
Practise before you need to Leave the carrier open in the room for a week, put millet in it, and let the bird go in and out on its own. A bird that has already chosen to sit in the carrier is a bird you can move in ninety seconds during an evacuation, and one that has never seen it is a chase in a smoke-filled house.

Owner log

Weight is this species' vital sign. A trend beats a single reading every time.
DateWeight (g)EatingDroppingsNotes: behavior, molt, eggs, out-of-cage time
How to use this page Weigh weekly once the baseline is set, at the same time of day, before the first feed, and daily through a diet conversion, a molt, or a laying period. Write the number even when it is boring, because the boring numbers are what make one interesting number readable. Act on a drop of more than about 10% from the bird's own baseline, or any steady decline across three weighings. Take this page to the vet.

Glossary

Terms used in this book, in the sense they are used here.
Blood featherA growing feather with a live blood supply in the shaft. Dark and full at the base, and it bleeds freely if broken or cut. Page {{P:minor}}
CereThe fleshy area above the beak carrying the nostrils. In a cockatiel it is small and feathered, unlike a budgie's, so it is not a sexing feature here
ChlamydiosisInfection with Chlamydia psittaci. Called psittacosis in people. Page {{P:respiratory}}
Chronic egg layingRepeated clutches out of season, driven by the environment rather than by a mate. Page {{P:eggs}}
Contact callA loud call used to locate flock members out of sight. Most cockatiel screaming is this. Page {{P:plucking}}
CropA storage pouch at the base of the neck where food sits before digestion. Visibly full after a meal
DimorphicHaving visibly different males and females. Cockatiels are, in the normal grey, after the first molt. Page {{P:sexing}}
Egg bindingA hen unable to pass an egg. A same-day emergency. Page {{P:eggbinding}}
ForagingWorking for food rather than eating it from a bowl. The single highest-value enrichment change. Page {{P:mistakes}}
Hepatic lipidosisFatty liver disease. Fat deposited in the liver, usually from a seed diet. Page {{P:nutrition}}
Hypovitaminosis AVitamin A deficiency. The commonest nutritional disease in pet birds. Page {{P:nutrition}}
KeelThe ridge of breastbone down the front of the bird, used to judge body condition by feel. Page {{P:sexing}}
MoltOrderly, symmetrical replacement of the plumage. Page {{P:molt}}
Night frightA panicked thrashing episode in the dark, which cockatiels are unusually prone to. Page {{P:light}}
PBFDPsittacine beak and feather disease. An incurable circovirus. Page {{P:viral}}
PhotoperiodThe length of the light part of the day. The main lever on hormonal behavior. Page {{P:light}}
Pin featherA new feather still in its waxy keratin sheath. Normal during a molt, sore to touch. Page {{P:molt}}
PolyuriaExcess urine, with the fecal part still formed. Not the same as diarrhea. Page {{P:droppings}}
Powder downSpecialized feathers that break down into a fine dust used in grooming. Cockatiels produce it, which is why they dust a room
PTFEPolytetrafluoroethylene, the nonstick coating whose fumes kill birds when overheated. Page {{P:hazards}}
RegurgitationDeliberate bringing up of food as courtship, aimed at a mate, a toy or a hand. Not vomiting. Page {{P:molt}}
UratesThe chalky white part of a dropping, uric acid rather than urine. Page {{P:droppings}}
ZoonoticPassing from animals to people. Psittacosis is. Page {{P:respiratory}}

Where the sources disagree

Five figures where credible references do not match, and what this book prints instead.
FigureThe disagreement, and the choice
Bar spacingLafeberVet gives 0.5 to 0.75 in; pet care guidance gives half an inch or smaller. This book prints 1/2 in (13 mm), the stricter figure, because the risk it guards against is head entrapment and the cost of being wrong is fatal
Cage sizeSources range from 20×20×30 in to 30×24×36 in. Neither end was picked: page {{P:cage}} prints 20×20 in as the floor and 24×24×30 in as the target, and says why
Captive lifespanLafeberVet gives 12 to 15 years with up to 25 reported. Other references give 15 to 20 and 15 to 25. This book prints 12 to 15 typically, up to 25, which is the range the veterinary source supports
Diet splitSome care references give 60 to 70% pellets. VCA's cockatiel feeding page gives 75 to 80%, and that is what is printed here
CostPublished setup figures of $320 to $860 omit a gram scale, a carrier, a sleep cage and a real first veterinary visit. Page {{P:budget}} itemizes all of them and lands at $392 to $1,027, with the lines summing to the totals
