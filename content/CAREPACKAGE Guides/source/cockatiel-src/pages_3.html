Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & what to tell the vet

A cockatiel that looks sick has been sick for a while. That is the whole problem.

Wild cockatiels live in flocks where a predator picks the bird that looks weakest, so masking illness is hardwired rather than chosen. By the time a pet cockatiel visibly stops coping, the illness is usually well advanced. This is why the weight log on page {{P:sexing}} and the droppings check on page {{P:droppings}} matter: they see the problem before the bird lets you.

Same-day call, no waiting Any difficulty breathing: tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing, a clicking or wheezing sound, or a change in voice. Bleeding from anywhere. Sitting on the cage floor instead of perching. Discharge from eyes, nostrils or mouth. Straining, in a hen especially: page {{P:eggbinding}}. Fluffed and unresponsive with both feet down. A fall, a cat contact of any kind, or contact with a hot surface. Nothing on this list improves overnight.

Call within 24 hours

  • A sustained drop in appetite, especially alongside anything else here
  • Any change in droppings that persists beyond about 24 hours. Page {{P:droppings}}
  • Weight down roughly 10% from the bird's own baseline
  • Vomiting, which sticks to the face and chest feathers and is not the same as the deliberate courtship regurgitation onto a toy or a mirror
  • New feather damage, bald patches, or obsessive preening of one spot. Page {{P:plucking}}
  • Lameness or an unwillingness to bear weight on one leg
  • Sleeping far more than usual, or sleeping fluffed on two feet rather than resting on one
  • Sneezing more than occasionally, or a wet or stained cere

The normal numbers, for reference

MeasureNormal in a cockatiel
Body temperatureAbout 107.1°F (41.8°C). Much higher than a mammal, and not something to take at home
Resting heart rateAbout 206 beats per minute
Respiratory rate40 to 50 breaths a minute at rest, with no tail movement
Weight80 to 125 g, average about 90. Your bird's own baseline is the real number
DroppingsRoughly 25 to 50 a day in a small parrot, formed, with distinct white urates

What to tell the vet, written down before you call

An avian consultation is short and expensive, and the history does most of the diagnostic work. Have these ready:

Today's weight, and the baseline from page {{P:ownerlog}}
Exactly what the bird eats, by share, not by brand
When the signs started, and what changed that week
Hours of darkness it actually gets
Whether it is a hen, and whether it has ever laid
Any new bird, new house, or new person
Nonstick cookware, candles, aerosols, or a new floor
A photo of the droppings on the paper liner
Find the vet before you need one Avian practice is a specialty and the nearest one may be an hour away. Call in week one, ask whether they see cockatiels, ask what their emergency arrangement is after hours, and write both numbers on page {{P:emergency}}. Doing this on the night it matters is how birds are lost.

Vitamin A, calcium & fatty liver

One cause, three diseases. This page is what a seed bowl actually buys.

Hypovitaminosis A

The commonest nutritional disease in pet birds, and Merck is specific that it is not only all-seed diets that cause it: even a diet of half seed and half pellets leaves a bird deficient. Vitamin A maintains the lining of the airways, mouth and eyes, so a deficient bird loses its first line of defence against infection, which is why so many respiratory cases on page {{P:respiratory}} are nutritional underneath.

SignWhat you see
Airway and sinusNasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eye, conjunctivitis, laboured breathing
MouthWhite plaques inside the mouth and around the eyes, and loss of the normal papillae on the roof of the mouth. Your vet looks for this specifically
KidneysExcessive drinking and excessive urination, which shows on the paper as a wet ring
FeathersPoor feather quality, and feather picking. Page {{P:plucking}}
AppetiteAnorexia, often the last sign rather than the first

Treatment is injectable vitamin A given by a vet in acute cases, alongside treating any secondary infection, but the actual fix is the diet conversion on page {{P:diet}} and the dark orange and dark green vegetables on page {{P:veg}}.

Calcium deficiency

Calcium runs through this species by a different route than in most parrots: not through a hypocalcemia syndrome, which Merck highlights in African greys rather than cockatiels, but through eggs. A laying hen mobilizes calcium out of her own skeleton to build shells, and on a seed diet she never replaces it. Signs of depletion are weakness, incoordination, tremors, depression, seizures, and fractures that happen from nothing. In a breeding hen it shows first as thin-shelled, soft or shell-less eggs, which is the warning that egg binding on page {{P:eggbinding}} is next.

Prevention is a permanent cuttlebone and mineral block, a pellet diet that carries vitamin D3 so the calcium can be used, and controlling laying at all. Page {{P:eggs}}.

Obesity and hepatic lipidosis

A high-fat seed diet plus a cage-bound bird produces obesity, and obesity in a bird means fat deposited in and around the liver. Merck's working figure for obesity is roughly 20% over ideal weight, which in a 90 g cockatiel is about 18 g and is invisible without the scale on page {{P:sexing}}. Fat accumulation in the abdomen presses on the air sacs, so the first thing many owners notice is that the bird gets breathless crossing the room, or goes lame.

Fatty liver disease is quiet until it is not A bird with hepatic lipidosis may show nothing for a long time, then present with a distended abdomen, laboured breathing, poor feather quality, or overgrown, soft or oddly coloured beak and nail keratin, which is a genuinely useful early sign because you can see it during a head scratch. It is managed by a vet with a controlled diet change. Do not crash-diet a fat bird yourself. Rapid weight loss in a bird mobilizes fat into a liver that is already struggling and can kill it.
The whole prevention plan, in one line Pellets at 75 to 80%, dark vegetables daily, seed as a training reward rather than a meal, a cuttlebone in the cage, and enough flight to keep the chest muscle on. Everything on this page is downstream of that.

Respiratory disease & psittacosis

Breathing trouble in a bird is always an emergency, and this species is named first on the psittacosis list.

A bird's respiratory system has very little reserve. There is no mild version of avian breathing difficulty, and a cockatiel that is visibly working to breathe is a bird in trouble now. Learn what normal looks like while it is healthy: 40 to 50 breaths a minute at rest, with the tail perfectly still.

Same-day signs Tail bobbing with each breath, which is the bird recruiting its whole body to move air. Open-mouth breathing. A clicking, wheezing or rasping sound. A change or loss of voice, which means the problem is at the syrinx. Discharge or bubbles at the nostrils, or a stained, matted cere. Sitting fluffed with the neck stretched up. Any of these is a phone call, not a wait.

Psittacosis, and the part that concerns you

Psittacosis, or avian chlamydiosis, is caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. The public health compendium on this disease names cockatiels first among the pet birds most often behind human cases, alongside parakeets and conures, so this is not a footnote for this species.

In the birdDetail
Often nothing at allA cockatiel can carry it with no signs whatsoever, shedding it in droppings and respiratory secretions to other birds. This is why a new bird is quarantined and tested rather than trusted. Page {{P:quarantine}}
When it does showRespiratory signs, diarrhea, weakness, and an enlarged liver. Lime-green droppings are a classic pointer. Page {{P:droppings}}
DiagnosisTesting at the vet. Shedding is intermittent, so one clear sample does not clear a bird
TreatmentDoxycycline. 45 days has historically been the recommendation, and shorter courses of 21 to 30 days may also be effective. Finish the course: a partial course produces a carrier
It passes to people In humans, illness typically begins 5 to 14 days after exposure, with an abrupt fever, chills, headache, malaise and muscle aches, and a dry cough that can come with breathlessness or chest tightness. It responds to antibiotics and is not something to be frightened of, but it is something to mention. If anyone in the house develops a flu-like illness with a dry cough and there is a sick bird at home, tell the doctor there is a bird. They will not think of it, and that one sentence is what gets it treated properly.

The other causes, in rough order

  • Vitamin A deficiency, which weakens the airway lining and lets everything else in. Page {{P:nutrition}}
  • Bacterial and fungal infection, including aspergillosis, which is favoured by damp, dirty bedding and poor ventilation
  • Inhaled irritants: aerosols, smoke, scented candles, dusty substrate. Page {{P:hazards}}
  • PTFE exposure, where the usual sign is a dead bird rather than a sick one. Page {{P:hazards}}
  • Goiter, an iodine deficiency of all-seed birds, which presses on the airway and sounds exactly like a respiratory infection. Named in budgerigars specifically, worth asking about in any seed-fed bird with an unexplained breathing noise
Warmth on the way in A bird struggling to breathe should travel warm, dark and quiet in a small covered carrier, with as little handling as possible. Stress alone kills birds in respiratory distress. Do not open the carrier to check on it every two minutes.

PBFD, polyomavirus & other viral disease

The reason a new bird is quarantined and tested, rather than introduced.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

PBFD is a circovirus affecting over 50 species of bird, concentrated in parrots from Africa, Australia, New Zealand and Indonesia, which puts the cockatiel squarely in the susceptible group. It is rare in New World parrots such as macaws, amazons and conures. Infection can happen at any age but is most common in birds under three years old.

FormWhoWhat happens
PeracuteNewly hatched chicksDeath, often before any feather sign
AcuteYoung birds growing their first feathersRapid, severe feather abnormality during that development
ChronicOlder birdsProgressive worsening at each successive molt, which is how most owners meet it

Signs. Feathers come in stunted, club-shaped, or pinched off at the shaft, with premature molting and eventually bald areas. In cockatoos, and the cockatiel is one, the beak becomes elongated, excessively flaky or unusually shiny, and breaks easily, because the powder down that normally dusts and dulls the beak has failed. A glossy beak on a cockatiel is worth a mention to your vet.

Spread and diagnosis. The virus enters through the mouth, nose or cloaca and is shed heavily in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust, which is what makes shared air a route. Incubation is anything from weeks to years. Diagnosis is a blood test or an oral swab for the virus DNA, or a skin and feather biopsy.

There is no treatment and no cure Most affected birds die of secondary infections within months to years. That is the entire reason page {{P:quarantine}} exists as a page: a 30-day quarantine and a PCR panel before a new bird joins an existing one is the only control you have, and it is cheap compared with what it prevents.

Avian polyomavirus

A second virus quarantine is designed to catch, and one that behaves very differently. It is most associated with budgerigars, where it is known as budgerigar fledgling disease, but it infects other psittacines including cockatiels. The dangerous form is in unweaned chicks, where it causes crop stasis, haemorrhage under the skin, and death within a day or two, and it is largely a breeder and pet-shop problem rather than a risk to a settled adult bird. A vaccine exists and is used in aviaries. Its practical relevance to a pet owner is the same as PBFD's: it is on the PCR panel, and it is a reason not to buy a bird from a shop with sick birds in the next cage.

What to ask for before you buy

  • Whether the bird has been tested for PBFD, polyomavirus and chlamydia, and to see the results
  • Whether any bird in the building has been ill in the last month
  • Whether birds from different sources share air, dishes, or a cleaning cloth
  • Whether you can return the bird if a vet check within a week finds something. A good breeder says yes without hesitating
Book the well-bird exam for week one Not week four. A baseline exam on a bird that looks fine gives your vet a healthy reference to compare against later, catches the things you cannot see, and starts the relationship you will want at 10pm in year six. Budget it as part of the purchase, on page {{P:budget}}.

Foreign bodies, heavy metal, mites & injuries

The shorter conditions, including two the cockatiel is specifically known for.

Swallowed rope and fabric

Cockatiels are noted as frequent offenders for ingesting rope fibers, and the combination of curiosity and a beak built for exploring makes it a real risk in an under-proofed home. A swallowed length of fiber can obstruct the gut or, worse, act as a linear foreign body that saws through it. The same behavior wraps loose threads around a toe and cuts off the blood supply.

Prevention: inspect rope perches and fabric toys weekly, retire anything frayed rather than trimming it, and keep the bird off blankets, towels and knitted things during out-of-cage time. Signs of an obstruction are regurgitation, straining, a sudden stop in droppings, and a bird that goes quiet. Same-day vet.

Heavy metal poisoning

The other thing this species is known for swallowing. Sources and signs are on page {{P:hazards}}; what matters here is that both lead and zinc are treatable with chelation if caught, and that the signs, weight loss, regurgitation, diarrhea, weakness, excessive drinking, incoordination and in bad cases seizures, look like a dozen other things. A bird with unexplained neurological signs gets a metal level checked. Zinc chelates faster than lead, because zinc is not stored in bone.

Giardia, and the itch nobody expects

The cockatiel differential worth knowing Giardia is an intestinal protozoal parasite that cockatiels are specifically noted as susceptible to, and its effect is not what you would guess. VCA describes infected birds as having loose stools or being intensely itchy, attacking themselves violently, especially under the wings, and that itch can drive a feather-destructive habit that then looks entirely behavioral. A cockatiel that plucks under the wings deserves a fecal test before anyone starts rearranging the cage. Page {{P:plucking}}.

The rest, briefly

ConditionWhat to know
CandidaA yeast overgrowth, common after antibiotics or in a bird on a poor diet. Regurgitation, weight loss, diarrhea, reduced appetite. Treatable
Scaly face miteCrusty, honeycombed growth at the cere, beak edges and around the eyes, sometimes on the legs. More classic in budgies but seen in cockatiels. Treated by a vet with ivermectin-class drugs, and the whole cage is treated with the bird
Broken blood featherA growing feather with a live blood supply, which bleeds freely when snapped. Apply gentle pressure with clean gauze and cornstarch. If it does not stop within a few minutes the feather must be pulled, which is a vet job. In a 90 g bird, a few millilitres of blood matters
Overgrown beak or nailsNails wear on varied perches. An overgrown or misshapen beak is different: it is a sign of liver disease, mites or trauma, not a grooming failure. Page {{P:nutrition}}
BumblefootPressure sores on the underside of the foot, from identical perches, obesity, or a dirty cage. Page {{P:furnishings}} prevents it
Cat or dog contactA cat's mouth carries bacteria that kill birds within about 48 hours from a wound too small to find. Any cat contact is a same-day vet visit for antibiotics, even if the bird seems fine
The first-aid kit, in a labelled box Cornstarch or styptic powder, sterile gauze, blunt-tipped scissors, a small towel, a travel carrier, a heat source you can control, saline, and the vet's number taped to the lid. Assemble it in week one and put it where anyone in the house can find it in the dark.

Feather plucking & behavioral health

Easier to prevent than to reverse, and it is a medical question before it is a behavioral one.

Feather-destructive behavior is the bird damaging or removing its own feathers, most often on the chest, legs, and under the wings, where it can reach. The head is the one place a bird cannot pluck itself, which is a useful diagnostic: feather loss including the head is a disease, not a habit. Everything else needs working through.

A vet visit comes first, every time Assuming plucking is behavioral before a vet has ruled out a medical cause is the commonest and costliest mistake with this species. The medical list is long and several of its entries are specifically cockatiel problems: Giardia and its violent under-wing itch (page {{P:minor}}), vitamin A deficiency (page {{P:nutrition}}), PBFD (page {{P:viral}}), bacterial skin infection, and pain from an internal problem. Fix a real cause and the behavior often stops. Rearrange the cage instead and you lose months.

The behavioral drivers, in rough order

DriverWhat to change
Boredom and an empty dayFood out of the bowl and into foraging. The single highest-value change. Page {{P:mistakes}}
Insufficient companyA flock bird alone all day is chronically stressed. Page {{P:flock}}
Sleep debtCount the actual hours of dark. It is very often nine. Page {{P:light}}
Hormonal frustrationA hen with a cavity and long days, or a bird bonded to a mirror. Page {{P:eggs}}
A change in routineA new work schedule, a move, a new person or pet. Plucking that starts within weeks of a change usually names its own cause
Dry air and no bathingRegular bathing or misting helps more than owners expect. Two or three times a week
Attention learningIf plucking reliably brings you across the room, it now has a second function. Reward calm, ignore the act itself

Catching it early

The window matters. Established plucking becomes self-soothing and habitual, and can continue after the original cause is gone, which is why prevention is worth so much more than treatment here. Watch for time spent preening one specific area beyond normal grooming, small bald patches on the chest or under the wings appearing gradually, and any increase in agitation that coincides with a change at home. Act at the first two, not at the bald patch.

Screaming is the same conversation Persistent screaming in a cockatiel is almost always a contact call from a bird that has lost its flock, and it responds to the same list: more company, more to do, more sleep, a predictable routine. What makes it worse is reacting to it, because any reliable response, including an annoyed one, teaches the bird that the call worked. Answer the quiet chirps and the whistles instead. That is the whole technique.
When to bring in a professional If plucking escalates after several weeks of consistent change, or if the bird has broken skin or drawn blood, do two things in parallel: an avian vet visit to rule out a medical cause, and a referral to a certified avian behaviorist if the bird is cleared. Waiting makes the behavior more entrenched, and this is one of the few cases where the specialist is genuinely better than more effort.

Molt & seasonal behavior

The normal process people mistake for illness, and the abnormal one people mistake for molt.

What a normal molt looks like

Molting is the orderly replacement of the whole plumage, usually once or twice a year, most often after the breeding season. Feathers are shed and regrown in a symmetrical, staggered pattern, matching feathers going on each wing so the bird can still fly, and new feathers push up before the old ones drop. You should never see a bald bird. What you will see is loose feathers on the cage floor and pin feathers: new feathers still encased in a waxy keratin sheath, which look like short spines, particularly around the head and neck.

During a moltWhat is happening
Quieter, touchier, sleeps moreGrowing a full set of feathers is metabolically expensive. A molting bird is genuinely tired and its head is full of sore pin feathers. Handle less, scratch gently
Eats moreThe demand for protein and calcium rises sharply. This is the time for a little cooked egg, and for making sure the pellets are actually being eaten
Wants more bathingMisting or a shallow dish softens the sheaths. Offer more often than usual
Off food, brieflyHeavy molt is a recognized reason a cockatiel goes off its food. Watch the scale. A drop is still a drop
Head scratches become a favorOnly the flock can open the sheaths on the head. Gentle rolling of a sheath between finger and thumb is welcomed by a tame bird, but never touch a sheath that is still blue at the base
The three-way differential owners get wrong Molt: symmetrical, new feathers visibly coming through, feathers found intact on the cage floor, head included, bird otherwise well.
Plucking: asymmetrical, confined to where the bird can reach, head feathers untouched, chewed or broken shafts left behind. Page {{P:plucking}}.
PBFD: feathers coming in wrong rather than falling out, club-shaped, pinched or stunted, worsening at each molt, often with a shiny or flaking beak. Page {{P:viral}}.
If you cannot tell, photograph the bird and the floor and take both to the vet.

The hormonal season

Cockatiels are triggered into breeding condition by lengthening days and abundant food, and in the northern hemisphere that usually means spring, though housed birds under artificial light lose the seasonality and can go hormonal at any time, including autumn. What you will see: more regurgitating onto a toy, mirror or hand, more territorial defence of the cage, a hen shredding paper and hunting for cavities, a cock whistling incessantly and displaying with wings held out and heart-shaped, and a general increase in biting.

None of it is bad behavior and none of it responds to discipline. It responds to the levers on page {{P:eggs}}: shorten the day, remove the cavities, stop the body petting, and move the food into foraging.

Support the molt, do not medicate it A molting bird needs food, sleep, water to bathe in, and to be left alone a bit. It does not need a molt supplement, and a bird eating 75 to 80% pellets is already getting what those products sell. If a molt drags on for months, goes bald in patches, or the bird stays unwell after it, that is a vet visit rather than a longer molt.

Reading droppings & hydration

The cheapest daily health check you own, and the reason the tray is lined with plain paper.

A bird dropping has three components, and telling them apart is the whole skill. The feces is the solid, formed, coiled part. The urates are the chalky white part, uric acid crystals that let a bird excrete nitrogen with almost no water. The urine is the clear liquid halo around the outside. Any of the three can change independently, and which one changed is what tells you where the problem is.

What you seeWhat it usually means
Green feces, formed, in a seed-fed birdNormal. Diet colours the fecal part: seed-eating birds pass green feces
Brown feces, formed, in a pellet-fed birdNormal. Expect the colour to change when you convert the diet, and do not panic at it
Red, pink or purpleAlmost always berries or beetroot, and it lasts a day. If the bird has eaten nothing coloured, it is blood, and it is a same-day call
Lime green or yellow-greenA liver pointer, and a classic sign of psittacosis. Page {{P:respiratory}}. Vet
Black or tarryDigested blood from higher in the gut. Vet, today
Yellow or green uratesThe white part should be white. Coloured urates point at the liver. Vet
Watery, but the feces still formedPolyuria, excess urine, not diarrhea. Usually a watery meal, sometimes stress. Persisting with no fruit in the last day raises kidney disease
Unformed feces, no solid partTrue diarrhea, which is much less common than owners think and much more serious. Vet
Undigested seed in the droppingFood passing through unabsorbed. Always abnormal. Vet
Far fewer droppings than yesterdayThe bird is not eating, or is obstructed. Both matter. Page {{P:redflags}}
The 24-hour rule Get familiar with your own bird's normal droppings, because once you know them, anything else is abnormal by definition. A single odd dropping is not an emergency; you changed the food, or it ate a blueberry. Droppings that stay abnormal for more than about 24 hours are a vet visit. Photograph the paper before you throw it away, and take the photo with you.

Hydration

A dehydrated bird has dry, tacky mouth tissue, dull sunken eyes, skin over the keel that stays tented when gently lifted, and urates that go thick and yellow. Cockatiels drink relatively little and get a real share of their water from fresh food, which is one more reason page {{P:veg}} matters. Change water daily, and again after a bath: a cockatiel will bathe in its drinking bowl and then be reluctant to drink from it.

Line the tray so you can read it Plain paper, changed daily, no grate in the way if you can manage it. Corncob, sand and wood chip all hide droppings, absorb them, and grow mold underneath, which turns the most informative surface in the cage into the least. Page {{P:furnishings}}.

Quarantine & adding a second bird

The one page that protects a bird you already own. Skipping it is how a healthy flock ends.

A new bird can carry PBFD, polyomavirus or chlamydia with no visible sign at all, shedding all three in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust. Quarantine is not about whether the new bird looks well. It looks well precisely because that is how these diseases work.

30 days minimum, and test The public health guidance for birds exposed to other birds at shows, sales or events is to quarantine for at least 30 days and test before adding them to a group. Take that as the floor. In a household with an existing bird, 45 to 60 days is the safer figure, because it covers a slow shedder and gives time to repeat a test. There is no version of this that takes a weekend.

How to actually run it

RuleWhy
A separate room, ideally not on the same air handlingFeather dust carries PBFD, and it travels further than droppings do. A different floor is better than a different corner
Separate dishes, tools, and cleaning clothsCross-contamination is usually a bucket or a sponge, not the birds
Established birds first, new bird lastDo the whole daily routine for the resident bird before you go near the new one, every day, without exceptions
Wash hands and change your shirtFeather dust sits on clothing. This is the step people skip
A vet exam and a PCR panel in week onePBFD, polyomavirus and chlamydia. This is what the 30 days is for; quarantine without testing is just waiting
Weigh the new bird dailyYou are looking for the thing it is hiding. Page {{P:sexing}}

Then the introduction

  1. Cages side by side, a few feet apart, for several days. Let them see and call to each other with no contact
  2. Move the cages closer over a week, watching for interest rather than lunging at the bars
  3. Neutral ground first. The first physical meeting happens on a play stand or a table, never inside either cage, and never inside the resident bird's cage. That is the resident's territory and it will be defended
  4. Short supervised sessions, extended as they get boring. Normal negotiation is beak fencing, some chasing and a lot of noise; a real fight is one bird pinning another or grabbing a foot and not letting go. Separate for that, not for the noise
  5. A shared cage last, and only if it is genuinely big enough for two, with two of every resource so nothing is worth guarding
They may simply not like each other Cockatiels are social but they are not indiscriminate, and a pairing can fail. Have a plan for two cages permanently before you buy the second bird, not after. Two birds who live beside each other and call back and forth are still much better company than one bird alone.
Quarantine even if it is your only bird A 30-day settling period in a quiet room, with a vet exam at the start of it, is good for a single new bird too. It catches problems while your expectations are still forming, it gives the bird the low-stimulus start page {{P:handling}} asks for, and it means you find out about a health problem before you have fallen in love with the bird.
