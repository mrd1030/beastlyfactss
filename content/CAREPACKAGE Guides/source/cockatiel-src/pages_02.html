Section 02 · Housing & Environment

Cage size, bar spacing & placement

The purchase that is hardest to undo, and the one number that is a safety limit.

Size and shape

Reference tables put the minimum for a single cockatiel at about 20×20×30 in (51×51×76 cm), and many keepers aim for 24×24×30 in (61×61×76 cm) for more comfortable movement. Cage minimums are quoted per bird, so a cage shared by two has to be larger than one sized for a single cockatiel.

Prioritize width over height. Cockatiels fly in short, fast, horizontal bursts between perches rather than climbing, so a tall, narrow cage wastes exactly the space the bird would use. A floor-to-ceiling cage can look impressive in a store and still give a cockatiel none of the flight space it needs. A wide flight-style cage is worth the extra footprint.

Bar spacing and bar direction

A safety limit, not a preference Bars must be half an inch (1.3 cm) apart or closer. Wider spacing is a genuine and avoidable risk of head entrapment.

Choose vertical bars. When a wing catches between horizontal bars during a flapping bout, the bird can damage it as it struggles free. Check the finish too: galvanized wire is a zinc source, and stainless steel or powder-coated hardware avoids the problem (page {{P:hazards}}).

Perches, dishes & what to leave out

The inside of the cage does more for the bird than the outside dimensions.

Perches

Provide several perches of varying diameter and texture rather than a uniform set, which supports better foot health over time. Diameters from about 5/8 in to 1.5 in (1.6 to 3.8 cm) suit a cockatiel's foot. A cage of identical dowels puts the same pressure on the same points of the foot all day and gives the bird no reason to move. Natural branch beats plastic.

Place perches at different heights and distances, so crossing the cage takes more than one hop. A bird that stands on rough, varied perches wears its nails down through ordinary use; one confined to smooth dowels needs trims more often (page {{P:grooming}}).

TypeNotes

Dishes and water

Position food and water dishes so droppings cannot fall into them. Fresh water goes in every day, in a dish washed daily, and stays available all the time. A cockatiel fed seed in a shared cage needs a dish of its own (page {{P:diet}}).

Cuttlebone or mineral block

Keep a cuttlebone or mineral block in the cage at all times. It is a calcium source, which matters most for a hen, and something for the beak to work against. Cockatiels are noted as especially prone to calcium deficiency (page {{P:nutrition}}).

Leave these out Grit. Cockatiels hull their seed before swallowing it, so grit has no job to do, and a bird that eats too much of it, a sick bird especially, can end up with a dangerous gut blockage.
Nest-like spaces. Any dark, enclosed hideaway reads as a nest site and is a trigger for chronic egg laying (page {{P:laying}}).
Mirrors. A bird can bond to a mirror or a favorite toy as it would to a mate.
Galvanized metal, and frayed rope or loose threads.
Line the tray with plain paper Plain paper, changed as part of the daily clean, shows the droppings clearly, and the droppings are the cheapest daily health check you have (page {{P:droppings}}). A grate over the tray keeps the bird off its own droppings between changes.

Temperature, light, sleep & night frights

A steady room, the right light, real darkness, and the one injury this species is known for.
SettingTarget
Room temperatureAbout 70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C). No drafts, no direct sun on the cage.
Darkness10 to 12 hours of quiet, dark, uninterrupted sleep every night, at about the same clock time
UV light10 to 12 hours a day of UV light exposure, or safe supervised time outdoors, supports healthy vitamin D levels
Sick or recovering bird75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C); birds recover and eat better at the upper end

Why the dark hours matter

A bird reads day length as a season and uses it to decide when to go into breeding condition. A long photoperiod is one of the major breeding triggers, and cockatiels, lovebirds and budgies are the small parrots most often seen as emergencies for reproductive disease. A household that keeps the lamps and the television on until late hands the bird an endless summer.

"Uninterrupted" does real work in that sentence. A bird that gets 11 dark hours but has a hallway light flick on twice is not getting the same rest as one with 11 genuinely dark, quiet hours. A cover on the cage blocks light, but it does nothing about a television's sound or people walking through the room. A smaller sleep cage in a quiet room, used at the same time every night, solves both.

Night frights

Cockatiels are prone to night frights: sudden, panicked flapping in the dark, where a startled bird thrashes inside the cage and can injure itself on the bars. Avian vets in earthquake-prone California have noticed that cockatiels, more than other species, turn up injured the morning after a tremor, and a bird clinic in Melbourne saw cockatiels come in with minor injuries after two small tremors there.

The fix costs almost nothing A small night light in the room, or a cage cover, helps prevent injuries during these episodes. Place the cage away from windows where headlights and outside movement reach it at night. Solve this before adding anything else to the cage.
Heat is the bigger risk than cold A bird is overheating if it pants with its beak open, holds its feathers sleek and flat, and holds its wings away from its body. Move it to shade or air conditioning, mist it with room-temperature water, never cold water, and call an avian vet. A bird that lives indoors should spend under 20 minutes in direct sunlight, with shade it can reach.

Air quality: fumes & feather dust

A bird's lungs make ordinary household air dangerous, and a cockatiel's own dust is a slow risk to you.

Air moves through a bird's lungs in one direction, pushed along by air sacs that keep oxygen flowing even on the exhale. That efficiency is the problem indoors: a bird pulls far more of whatever is in the air through its lungs than a person in the same room does. The rule of thumb: if you can smell it, it may harm your bird.

Nonstick cookware

PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) is the coating on most nonstick pans. Heated above 280°C (536°F) it releases odorless, colorless particles and acidic gases, and a pot boiling dry on the stove is the classic way it happens. Documented cases describe multiple birds dying within 30 minutes of the fumes from a single overheated pan, and the danger is not limited to birds in the same room. PTFE also turns up in drip pans, waffle irons, clothing irons, ironing board covers and some heat lamps. Cook with stainless steel or cast iron, and keep the cage out of the kitchen entirely.

Powder down and your own lungs

A cockatiel is one of the few pets with true powder down: feathers that grow continuously and flake into a fine keratin dust the bird spreads through its plumage. A white or gray film on the bars and the furniture beside the cage is normal and comes back daily. Breathing avian proteins in that dust and in dried droppings over years can cause bird fancier's lung, a form of hypersensitivity pneumonitis, in a small number of owners. Pet birds in the home raise the risk, and it tends to be diagnosed between 50 and 70. Untreated, it can scar the lungs permanently.

Cut the dust, and tell your doctor Run a HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air) purifier in the bird's room, bathe or mist the bird, damp-wipe rather than dry-dust, prefer hard floors to carpet, and keep the cage out of the bedroom. If you develop a cough, breathlessness or a flu-like illness that lingers or keeps coming back, tell your doctor you keep a parrot.

Metals, pets, plants & everyday traps

The hazards that are not fumes, including two this species is known for swallowing.

Zinc and lead

Cockatiels are among the species avian vets see most often with heavy metal poisoning, so scan the cage for anything metal the bird has worked loose. Zinc poisoning, once called "new wire disease," comes mainly from galvanized cage hardware: zinc can leach off hot-dipped galvanized coatings without the bird swallowing a visible flake. Electroplated galvanized metal is considered safe; hot-dipped, the more common coating, is not. Early signs are stomach upset and appetite loss, then weakness, pale gums and discolored droppings.

Lead is just as dangerous. Curtain weights, stained glass, costume jewelry and paint in homes built before 1978 are common sources, and lead tastes sweet, so a bird that finds it keeps chewing. Signs run from weakness and an upset stomach to circling, tremors and leg paralysis. Both metals respond to treatment if caught early.

Rope and fibers

Cockatiels are noted as frequent offenders for swallowing rope fibers. Check rope perches and toys often and retire them at the first frayed strand.

A cat or dog bite is a same-day emergency Any mouth contact between a cat or dog and a bird needs a vet the same day, even with no visible wound. Cat saliva carries Pasteurella bacteria, which can cause fatal blood poisoning within hours of a bite too small to see through the feathers.

Open water, fans, windows and other animals

  • Water. An open toilet, a full sink or a pot of hot water is a drowning or scalding risk.
  • Ceiling fans. A flying bird cannot see spinning blades as an obstacle. Fans go off before the cage door opens.
  • Glass. Birds do not perceive windows and mirrors as barriers and can fly into them hard enough to be hurt. Open doors and windows are an escape risk.
  • Other pets. Cats, dogs and ferrets keep a hunting instinct toward a bird however calm they seem. Out-of-cage time is supervised, with other animals out of the room. A larger parrot should never be loose around a cockatiel.

Foods and plants within reach

A countertop within flight range is enough exposure to avocado, chocolate, onion or garlic (the full list is on page {{P:never}}). Check any houseplant against a current toxic-plant list before it goes into a room the bird can reach.

Cleaning & hygiene

The daily health check hides inside the daily chore.

Every day

  • Spot-clean the cage, taking out soiled paper and dropped food. Look at the droppings before they go in the bin.
  • Wash the food and water bowls.
  • Fresh produce comes out within a couple of hours, before it spoils in the dish.
  • Wipe away the powder down on the bars and the furniture next to the cage.

At least weekly

Replace the paper or liner in the tray, more often when two or more cockatiels share the cage. Check rope toys for frayed strands and the cage for any metal the bird has worked loose.

The full clean

Move the cockatiel to a travel cage in a different room first, so it is in a separate air space while anything is sprayed or scrubbed. Strip out the old paper and every accessory. Every dropping and scrap of food comes off first with detergent, because disinfectant does little against organic debris. Then the disinfectant goes on, and most need 5 to 10 minutes of wet contact to work. Rinse until no trace of the cleaner or its smell is left, and let everything dry completely before the paper, perches and toys go back and the bird returns.

DisinfectantMix
Freshly mixed household bleach1 part bleach to 32 parts water, about half a cup per gallon
Quaternary ammonium disinfectantDiluted 1 to 1,000, following the label

Fumes reach the bird fast A bird's respiratory system reacts to aerosolized fumes, which is why it leaves the room before anything is sprayed. Keep the room being cleaned ventilated, and never mix a disinfectant with another product.

Dust and the people in the house

Dried droppings and feather dust lift into the air when swept, so spray the floor under the cage with water before sweeping. Psittacosis can spread from birds to people (page {{P:respiratory}}). Wash your hands with soap and water after handling the bird or its equipment.
