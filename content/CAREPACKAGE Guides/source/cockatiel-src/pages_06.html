Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Red flags & finding a vet

A cockatiel hides illness until it cannot. By the time it looks sick, it has been sick a while.

Wild cockatiels live in flocks where predators single out the weakest-looking bird, so hiding illness is hardwired, not a choice. A cockatiel can mask sickness well enough that owners often notice nothing until the illness has progressed. This page exists to move the moment you notice it earlier, and the daily weighing and droppings check are how.

Same day, not a few days of watching Discharge from the eyes, nose or mouth, or bubbles from the beak.
Tail-bobbing with each breath, labored breathing, or clicking breathing sounds.
Sitting on the floor of the cage instead of perching.
Feathers fluffed for long stretches, or sleeping fluffed on two feet.
A hen straining, sitting low, or with no egg passed in 48 hours.
Bleeding that has not stopped after two to three minutes of pressure.
Any mouth contact from a cat or dog, even with no wound.
A noticeable, sustained drop in eating alongside any other sign here.

Call the vet soon

  • A change in the droppings that lasts more than 24 hours: color, consistency, number or amount.
  • Weight trending down over several weighings, or more than 10% below the bird's own baseline.
  • Vomiting, which sticks to the face and chest feathers. Regurgitating onto a toy or a person is courtship, not illness.
  • New feather damage, bald patches, or unkempt, ratty-looking plumage from neglected preening.
  • Listlessness, or any sudden change in appetite or behavior.

Normal numbers worth knowing

Body temperatureAbout 107°F (41.8°C). Far higher than a mammal's, and not measured at home.
Heart rateAbout 200 beats a minute at rest
Breathing40 to 50 breaths a minute at rest
Weight80 to 125 g, about 90 g on average; stable against the bird's own baseline
DroppingsOne about every 20 to 30 minutes while awake

Using the vet well

Avian vets are a specialized field, and the nearest one may be some distance away, so locate one before an emergency forces the search. Annual wellness exams are worth doing even when nothing seems wrong. When something is wrong, have ready today's weight and the usual weight, what the bird eats, how long the sign has lasted, and a phone photo of the droppings. Write the vet's number and an after-hours number on the emergency card today.

Vitamin A, calcium, obesity & fatty liver

The most common preventable illness in pet birds, and it starts in the food dish.

Nutritional disease from a seed-heavy diet is the most common preventable illness in pet birds. Birds that eat only seeds, especially sunflower seeds and peanuts, are the most prone, and diet is the common thread running through most of the conditions in this section.

Vitamin A deficiency

Hypovitaminosis A is a vitamin A deficiency that shows across several body systems. Standard seed mixes provide vitamin A well below what a cockatiel needs.

Where it showsSigns
Nose, eyes and airwayNasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eyes, conjunctivitis, labored breathing
KidneysDrinking and urinating more
FeathersPoor feather quality, feather picking
AppetiteLoss of appetite

The fix is the diet: pellets, and the bright yellow, red and orange vegetables (page {{P:veg}}).

Calcium deficiency

Calcium deficiency, sometimes leading to a condition called nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, is common, and cockatiels are especially prone to it. It shows as weakness, unsteadiness, tremors, depression, seizures, and bones that break with no real injury. A laying hen spends calcium on every eggshell, which is why she runs short first (page {{P:laying}}). Keep a cuttlebone or mineral block in the cage at all times.

Obesity and fatty liver disease

A high-fat, seed-heavy diet combined with limited activity leads to obesity, and an all-seed diet is linked to hepatic lipidosis, fatty liver disease. Obesity means roughly 20% over the bird's ideal weight, a body condition score of 4 out of 5 on the keel: in a 90 g cockatiel that is about 18 g, which only the scale shows (page {{P:weight}}).

The beak and nails can be the clue An abnormally long beak is most commonly caused by chronic malnutrition or liver disease, and an overgrown beak and overgrown nails together can point to the liver. A beak that keeps growing back needs a vet workup, not another trim.
The whole prevention plan Pellets at 75 to 80% of the diet, vegetables daily, seed as a small minority or a training reward, a cuttlebone in the cage, and an hour or more out of the cage every day.

Breathing problems & psittacosis

Fast-moving, and one of them can pass to the people in the house.

Any breathing sign in a cockatiel is urgent. Learn the normal while the bird is healthy: 40 to 50 breaths a minute at rest, with the tail still. Tail-bobbing with each breath, labored or open-mouth breathing, clicking sounds, bubbles from the beak and nasal discharge all carry the same message: a same-day vet visit.

CauseWhat points to it
An inhaled toxinSudden onset with nothing wrong beforehand. Nonstick fumes, aerosols, smoke (page {{P:air}}).
Vitamin A deficiencyNasal discharge, sneezing and swelling around the eyes in a seed-fed bird
Egg bindingLabored breathing in a hen, because the trapped egg presses on the air sacs (page {{P:binding}})
PsittacosisBelow

Psittacosis

Psittacosis, also called parrot fever or avian chlamydiosis, is caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. It is zoonotic, meaning it can pass to people in the household. In a cockatiel it tends to show as breathing trouble or conjunctivitis, and in some birds gray feathers turn black. Lime-green droppings, in both the stool and the urates, are another pointer, because it is a common cause of liver disease in birds.

  • A healthy-looking bird can carry it. Some infected birds look healthy and shed the bacterium only on and off, which is why a bird that looks clean on day 10 of quarantine can still test positive later.
  • Treatment is the antibiotic doxycycline, for a confirmed or strongly suspected case, on the course your vet sets. It is never a preventive shortcut.
  • For people, cleaning the cage is the exposure. Spray the floor with water before sweeping so dried droppings do not lift into the air, and wash your hands after handling the bird or its equipment.
While you get to the vet Keep the bird warm, around 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), in a covered carrier, with as little handling as possible. Stop any spraying, cooking or cleaning in the house. If a fume exposure is suspected, get the bird into fresh air first and call from outside.
A cough in the house If a person in the house develops a cough, breathlessness or a flu-like illness that lingers, tell the doctor there is a parrot at home. Psittacosis is one reason, and bird fancier's lung from the feather dust is the other.

Beak and feather disease & polyomavirus

The two viruses quarantine and testing are largely built around.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

PBFD is caused by a circovirus that attacks the fast-growing skin cells behind the beak and the feather follicles, so both grow visibly wrong. It occurs mainly in parrots native to Africa, Australia, New Zealand and Indonesia, which puts the Australian cockatiel in its range, and it is rare in macaws, Amazons and conures.

  • Feathers look stunted, club-shaped or pinched off, changing symmetrically across the body, with feathers lost even where the bird cannot reach, such as the top of the head. Powder down can fail too.
  • The beak can grow long, flaky or oddly shiny, and break with ordinary handling.
  • Outlook: there is no cure, and most affected birds eventually die of secondary infections.
  • Spread: direct contact, feather dust, dander and dried droppings. A young bird can carry it for a long time with no signs, which is why a PCR test belongs in quarantine.

Avian polyomavirus

This virus is two diseases under one name, and the gap between them is mostly the bird's age at infection.

WhoWhat happens
Unweaned chicksSigns appear 7 to 10 days after exposure: lethargy, and a crop that stops emptying. Death follows within 24 to 48 hours. Mortality can reach 100% in birds under 15 days old, and many die with no signs at all.
Older survivorsOutside budgerigars, birds past the youngest age may show no obvious signs, or show bleeding and clotting problems instead.
AdultsTypically resistant to the disease, but can shed the virus on and off for up to 90 days, and a recovered bird can remain a carrier with no signs.

The virus travels mainly in feather dust and droppings, and also in urine, urates and secretions from the airway and crop. A licensed vaccine exists for selected parrots. In its efficacy study in cockatiels, the first dose went in at 35 days of age, with a second 2 to 3 weeks later; breeding birds get two doses two weeks apart in the off-season. Which schedule fits a given bird is a vet's decision, and a fair question to ask a breeder.

Do not guess from a photo Feather changes from either virus can look like a molt or like plucking. An avian vet tests for both directly, with a PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test on blood, swabs or feather dander.

Giardia, gastric yeast, rope & metal

Two gut infections this species is known for, and two things it is known for swallowing.

Giardia and other gut flagellates

Giardia and its relatives are motile parasites living in the lower intestine, linked to diarrhea in cockatiels. They do not show in the droppings to the naked eye: the vet finds them by looking at the droppings under a microscope, and treats with an antiparasitic drug. The first exam is the natural time to ask for that check.

The itch that looks like a habit An infected cockatiel may have loose stools, or may instead be intensely itchy and attack itself, especially under the wings. Plenty show only the itch, with a normal-looking tray the whole time, and from across the room that looks exactly like a stress or boredom habit. A cockatiel plucking or digging at its skin, particularly under the wings, gets a fecal test before anyone rearranges the cage.

Avian gastric yeast

Avian gastric yeast, once called megabacteriosis, is a yeast, Macrorhabdus ornithogaster, not a bacterium. Budgerigars, parrotlets, lovebirds, cockatiels and finches are the small pet birds most often affected, and it turns up more often in cockatiels than in budgerigars.

  • The pattern: a bird that keeps eating on schedule, keeps its feathers smooth, and still loses weight by the gram. Whole or partly digested seed in soft droppings is another flag. The only reliable early catch is the daily scale.
  • Diagnosis is a fresh dropping under the microscope. The yeast sheds on and off, so one negative result does not clear a bird.
  • Outlook varies widely: 10 to 80% of affected birds die, depending on the bird and the strain.
  • Treatment is a 30-day course of an antifungal drug from the vet. Failures are common when the course runs shorter, most often because the bird looked better and the owner stopped.

Rope fibers and heavy metal

Cockatiels swallow rope fibers and heavy metal objects more than most parrots: curiosity plus a beak built for exploring. Matted fibers can block the gut, so rope toys come out at the first frayed strand. Metal is the other risk, and the zinc and lead sources and signs are on page {{P:hazards}}. Both metals respond to treatment if caught early, so a cockatiel with weakness, an upset stomach or tremors and no obvious cause deserves a vet's look for metal.

Feather plucking & feather loss

A vet visit and environmental changes, in that order. Most owners work this list backwards.

A bird missing feathers sends most owners straight to the scariest answer. Compare what you see against a normal molt first (page {{P:molt}}), then use the one built-in limit of plucking: a bird can only chew, pull or over-preen feathers its own beak can reach.

What the feathers look likeWhereFirst move
Normal moltWhole feathers shed gradually, new pin feathers coming inScattered across the bodyBathe or mist; let it run
PluckingChewed, ragged or pulled outOnly where the bird can reach; the head is sparedVet visit, then environment and behavior
GiardiaDamaged by self-attackCommonly under the wingsFecal test (page {{P:gut}})
PBFDStunted, club-shaped or pinched, changing symmetricallyIncludes places the bird cannot reachAvian vet diagnosis; no cure (page {{P:viral}})
The head tells you Bald or damaged feathers on the chest, back or under the wings with a full set on top of the head is very likely self-inflicted. Feather loss on top of the head while everything else looks fine is not plucking, because the bird cannot reach that spot.

Work the list in this order

  1. Is it plucking at all? Use the table above.
  2. Medical. The underlying cause is not always obvious from outside, and for a cockatiel the fecal test for Giardia comes before anything else. Environmental fixes help only once medical causes are ruled out.
  3. Boredom, loneliness and stress. These are the causes plucking is most often traced back to. Move food into foraging, increase time out of the cage, and look honestly at company (page {{P:enrichment}}).
  4. Sleep and hormones. Protect the 10 to 12 hours of dark (page {{P:climate}}), and remove mirrors and nest-like spaces.
  5. Handling. Consistent, patient handling goes a long way toward preventing the boredom and stress behind it.
The cockatiel evidence Environmental enrichment reduces feather-damaging behavior in captive cockatiels. The changes that help are the ordinary ones: foraging, company, varied perches, things to shred and a daily bath.

Reading droppings & water

The one health check that costs nothing and happens by itself every 20 to 30 minutes.

A normal dropping is three outputs mixed together, because a bird's digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts all empty through one shared opening, the vent. The feces are the solid, formed part, green or brown. The urates are the white or cream, chalky part: uric acid, which lets a bird excrete nitrogen while conserving water. The urine is the clear liquid ringing the rest.

PartNormalGenuine red flag
FecesGreen to brown, shifting with diet, sometimes red or orange on colored pelletsBlack or tarry, bright red or blood-tinged, or watery with no formed stool
UratesWhite to creamBright yellow or lime green, especially if the stool turns lime green too
UrineClear, a small ringLarge volumes on every dropping, or a marked drop in how often the bird goes
TextureSolid, curled, holds its shapeUndigested whole seed, pea-soup consistency, or bubbles

What the red flags can mean

  • Lime-green urates and stool together point to psittacosis (page {{P:respiratory}}).
  • Undigested seed in soft droppings can mean avian gastric yeast (page {{P:gut}}).
  • Red or black droppings can mean lead poisoning, which causes bloody urine.

Polyuria is not diarrhea

Polyuria is extra clear liquid around an otherwise normal, formed stool: plain excess urine, often from stress, a diet change or a lot of fresh fruit or vegetables, and on its own usually not an emergency. True diarrhea is the fecal part itself turning mushy or semi-liquid, and it shows up alongside several serious conditions. One wrinkle: polyuria with no obvious dietary cause can be the first sign of kidney disease from zinc poisoning.

The 24-hour rule Once you know your bird's normal, any deviation counts, and if it lasts longer than 24 hours it is a same-day call to an avian vet. Call sooner for blood in a dropping, black or tarry droppings, a bird that has stopped producing droppings near its normal frequency, or any change paired with a fluffed-up, quiet or off-food bird.

The habit, and water

Line the tray with plain paper and look at it at the same time each day. Note anything unusual with a date, even as a phone photo. Water must be there, clean, all the time, in a dish washed daily.
