Choosing a cockatiel

Most of what makes or breaks a bird-owning household is decided before the bird comes home.

The commitment

A cockatiel typically lives 12 to 15 years, and up to about 25 has been reported. Match that span to your own life stage and your household's long-term stability, not just to how appealing the bird looks in the store. Be honest about daily time too: a cockatiel needs an hour or more out of the cage every day, not just a full dish. The bird itself is the cheap part of owning one (page {{P:budget}}).

Hand-raised or parent-raised

A hand-raised bird is pulled from the nest partway through development and fed by a person. A parent-raised bird stays with its parents through weaning and meets people afterward. Hand-raised birds usually step up more readily and bond with people faster. That tameness has a cost: Schmid, Doherr and Steiger (2006), studying African grey parrots, linked hand-rearing to more frustration-related behavior disorders, including aggression and feather picking, and to more dependence on people. Most hand-raised birds live fine lives, and the study was in greys, not cockatiels. A parent-raised bird takes longer to tame but starts from a more typical baseline.

Where to get one

SourceAsk
BreederTo see the parent birds; whether the chick was hand-raised or parent-raised, how old it was when it left the nest, and whether it is weaned; about diet and health issues in their line. Visit in person rather than having a bird shipped.
Pet shopA shop's birds usually come from several breeders, which mixes health histories. Ask how long the bird has been in the store, whether a vet has examined it, and whether new arrivals are quarantined away from the birds already on the floor.
RescueA first option, not a fallback. Ask about the bird's history and any known behavior problems before taking it home.

Assessing the bird in front of you

Watch the whole cage before you focus on one bird. A healthy cockatiel is lively, alert, interactive and attentive: it tracks movement around the room, responds to sound, and interacts with its cage mates or surroundings. Its feathers lie smooth and it moves around rather than sitting in one spot the whole time you watch.

Do not take this one home A bird that stays fluffed and still, sits with its eyes half closed during the day, or does not react when you approach the cage is not the bird to take home, even if it is the only one left. If one bird looks off compared with the others, ask the seller what they have seen.
The vet visit is part of the purchase A bird can look fine in a cage on a single visit and still carry something a shop, a breeder or your own eye missed. The first exam also gives you a baseline to compare against later.

Quarantine & the first vet visit

A new bird can look completely normal for weeks while carrying something that would spread through a flock in days.

How long, and where

Thirty days is the floor. It applies to every newly acquired bird, including one that has been to a show, and to a household with no other bird yet, because the habit matters most on the day there is a second bird to protect. Plan on 30 to 45 days in a separate room while the bird is screened. For a multi-bird household or an aviary, run nearer 90 days, because an infected adult can shed polyomavirus on and off for up to 90 days.

RuleWhy
A separate room with its own door, kept shutIsolation means separate air space, not a second cage in the same room
Its own dishes, perches, toys and cleaning toolsA shared dish, sponge or scoop can carry bacteria or a virus across the gap
Established birds first, the new bird lastHealthy before isolated, every day, so nothing on your hands reaches the birds you are protecting
Wash your hands between birdsWith soap and water, or change gloves. Feathers, dander and dried droppings travel on clothing too.
Medicine is not a shortcut Routine preventive antibiotic treatment is discouraged: it can harm the bird and encourage resistant bacteria. Antibiotics are for a confirmed case of psittacosis (page {{P:respiratory}}), not for skipping quarantine.

The first exam

It belongs inside quarantine, within one to two weeks of bringing the bird home, so results are back before the new bird meets anyone. It starts with a physical exam: the bird is weighed, its mouth checked, and its heart and breathing listened to. The tests are chosen from there:

  • Blood work: a complete blood count and a chemistry panel covering calcium, protein and organ function.
  • A fecal exam for intestinal parasites, and swabs of the mouth and vent for abnormal bacteria or yeast. Ask for the fecal check specifically: cockatiels carry Giardia, which does not show to the naked eye.
  • Screens for psittacosis, polyomavirus and PBFD (psittacine beak and feather disease), as the bird's history calls for. A PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test can detect infection in a bird that still looks healthy, and because shedding comes and goes, one negative result is not a clean bill of health.
  • Whole-body X-rays, which need sedation or gas anesthesia, sometimes.

Finding the vet

At a minimum, choose a member in good standing of the Association of Avian Veterinarians, whose find-a-vet search lists members by location. Find the vet before the bird comes home. After the first visit, one checkup a year is the floor, and many avian vets recommend two.

Sexing by plumage & the color exceptions

One of the few parrots you can sex by looking, in one color, after one age.

Cockatiels are a real exception among parrots: in the right bird, looking works. There are two things to read, not one.

CockHen
FaceA bright, clean yellow forehead, throat, crest and cheeks, with vivid orange ear patchesMuch less yellow, a duller, grayer face
Under the wings and tailSolid blackA barred pattern

The underwing and tail are the more reliable half, because they are a pattern rather than a judgment of brightness. If the face and the wing disagree, trust the wing.

Limit one: age

Until maturity at about 6 months, every young cockatiel is colored like a hen, bars under the wings included. A bird under six months tells you nothing, and a "hen" bought as a chick can turn out to be a cock after its first molt.

Limit two: color

The cue is clearest in the standard gray, the wild-type bird with the yellow face and orange cheeks, and most pet cockatiels are not that bird. The method works by contrasting the yellow and orange against the gray and black, so any mutation that removes or scrambles either pigment removes the cue with it.

ColorWhy the visual method fails
LutinoNo dark pigment left to make the barring or the black underwing readable
WhitefaceNo yellow or orange to brighten
AlbinoA whiteface lutino: neither pigment
PiedBoth pigments scattered at random; the hardest of all to call
PearlA pearl hen keeps her pearling while a cock usually molts his away: a clue, not an answer

DNA testing is the actual answer

For any of those birds, and for confirming a visual guess before it matters, a DNA test is the answer. A blood sample or a plucked feather is sent to a lab, directly or through your avian vet. A 2023 study found blood the more accurate of the two, because it carries more DNA than a feather or a mouth swab, though a feather is the easier sample to collect at home.

Why it matters A hen can lay with no male anywhere in the house, and repeated laying is the most common serious problem in this species (page {{P:binding}}). An owner who believes the bird is a cock does not notice a laying cycle starting.

Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

A single hen with no male in the house can lay, and repeated laying is what harms her.

Chronic egg laying, egg binding, soft or shell-less eggs and oviduct prolapse are all common in cockatiels, even in a single hen with no male present. The likely reason is where the species comes from: wild cockatiels live in arid Australia and come into breeding condition only when food and water are plentiful, and a pet with a full bowl lives in permanently good times. Of all the small parrots, the cockatiel is the one this comes up in most.

Maturity6 to 12 months
Clutch3 to 8 eggs, 5 on average
Incubation19 to 21 days

What chronic laying costs her

Each clutch drains the calcium used to build eggshells. The low blood calcium that follows, hypocalcemia, shows as weakness, lethargy, brittle bones that can fracture during normal handling, egg binding, seizures and death.

The triggers: take away as many as you can

  • A long day. A long photoperiod is one of the major triggers. Protect the 10 to 12 hours of dark first (page {{P:climate}}).
  • Abundant, unlimited rich food. A steady surplus mimics the conditions for raising a wild clutch.
  • A nest-like space: a dark enclosed hideaway, a nest box, a corner of the cage floor.
  • A mate, or something treated as one: a mirror or a favorite toy.
  • Petting. Stroking the back, rump or hind end reads as mating. Keep contact to the head and neck while she is laying.
Do not pull the clutch Removing each egg as it appears prompts her to lay more to replace it. Leave her to sit on a completed clutch, real or dummy eggs, for a full incubation, which for a cockatiel is 19 to 21 days. Then remove the eggs gradually, roughly one every one to two days, rather than all at once. Take away the nest-like furnishings, mirrors and favorite toys at the same time.

Light, diet and the vet

Cutting daylight or room light to 8 hours a day, or 8 to 10 as a short-term measure, can reduce the stimulus to lay. It is one tool among several, to run with an avian vet. Seed-based diets are short on calcium, selenium and vitamins D and E, so a pelleted diet with proper calcium and a cuttlebone are standard advice for a chronic layer. For a hen that keeps cycling anyway, an avian vet has hormonal options: an injection that stops laying for three to four weeks and is repeated on a schedule, or an implant under the skin that lasts three to six months or longer. Neither replaces removing the triggers.

Egg binding

A hen who cannot pass an egg has hours, not days. This is the page to know by heart.

Egg binding is an egg that forms but fails to pass and lodges in the oviduct, the tube an egg travels down. It is where chronic laying stops being a management problem and becomes a same-day emergency. Small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg-bound, as the trapped egg compresses circulation and presses on the air sacs. A hen kept alone can bind, so the risk exists whether or not you breed her.

StageWhat to watch for
EarlyDepression, closed eyes, sitting low or on the cage bottom, and no egg passed in 48 hours by a hen that may be laying
ProgressingWeak and not perching, straining as if to lay or pass a dropping, noticeable abdominal swelling
Advanced: act nowA bobbing tail and labored breathing, an egg bulging at the vent or a prolapse, or a hen unable to stand or grip the perch, because the egg presses on the nerves to her legs
What to do, in order 1. Call an avian vet now, the same day and ideally the same hour. There is no home remedy that safely dislodges a bound egg, and waiting for tomorrow's appointment is never the right call.
2. Keep her warm on the way. A sick bird recovers best at 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C): a warm room and a covered carrier, with no open flame.
3. Do not try to squeeze or work the egg out yourself.

What the vet will do

A mildly affected hen may respond to supplemental heat, injectable fluids, calcium, vitamin D3 and oxytocin, a hormone that makes the oviduct contract and helps push the egg out. A severe case can need the egg's contents drawn out with a needle passed through the vent, or surgery.

Why it happens

Without enough calcium, the oviduct cannot contract hard enough to push the egg out, and cockatiels are prone to low dietary calcium. Obesity from a high-fat, all-seed diet is another cause, alongside hereditary factors, old age and an unsuitable place to lay.

Preventing the next one

  • A pelleted diet with proper calcium, and a cuttlebone or mineral block always available.
  • Light cut back when a hen is cycling, and a quiet, dark sleep every night.
  • No nest-like furnishings, no mirror, and no petting down the back.
  • Weight kept in check, and a vet visit for a hen who is laying heavily, thin and slow to recover between clutches, before anything gets stuck.

Molt & seasonal changes

Normal, uncomfortable, and mistaken for illness often enough to learn.

What a normal molt looks like

Birds normally lose and replace most of their feathers at least once a year, and many also run a smaller partial molt about six months after the main one. Timing is not fixed: nutrition, sunlight, day length and humidity all move it. A normal molt is slow and gradual, and the feathers that come out are whole and undamaged, not shredded or snapped off partway.

New feathers start as pin feathers, growing feathers in a waxy sheath that stays soft while the feather is supplied with blood, then dries and flakes away as it matures. Most birds work the dry sheaths loose through ordinary preening. Molting is demanding, and the pin feathers can be uncomfortable, so an easygoing cockatiel can turn cranky for a stretch. A bird that suddenly does not want to be handled is usually just having a grumpy molt. A heavy molt is also one of the reasons a cockatiel goes off its food, because growing a full set of feathers raises the need for protein, calcium and iron sharply. Keep weighing: a drop is still a drop.

A normal moltPast a normal molt
Slow, with whole, undamaged feathers shed graduallyBald skin appearing quickly, or feathers that look chewed, pinched or stunted
New pin feathers coming in as old ones dropMissing patches, or loss from a spot the bird cannot reach, such as the top of its head
Scattered across the bodyLoss only where the beak reaches
A quieter, touchier birdA bird fluffed on the cage floor, off its food, or unwell

Helping through a molt

  • Keep the daily bath going. Bathing or misting softens the sheaths and encourages the preening that works them off.
  • Do not pull a pin feather. A growing one carries a blood supply (page {{P:grooming}}).
  • Expect grumpiness and handle less while it lasts.

Seasonal changes

Day length is the seasonal signal a cockatiel reads, and an indoor bird under household lighting can be pushed into breeding condition at any time of year. It becomes a problem when the environment holds a hen in that state, which is why the light schedule comes before anything else (page {{P:laying}}).

Heat is the other season to plan for. A parked car can climb 20°F in as little as 10 minutes and 50°F in an hour, and a bird is never left in one (page {{P:outage}}).

Wing feathers grow back at the molt A clipped flight feather regrows the next time that feather is molted out, so the molt is also when a trim stops working. Which month that is cannot be predicted from the calendar.
