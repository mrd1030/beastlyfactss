Section 03 · Feeding

Diet & feeding by age

Leopard geckos are obligate insectivores: live insects make up the diet, with no fruit or vegetable purees. Their nutrition is decided less by which insect you pick than by what that insect ate first (page {{P:gutload}}) and what you dusted it with. Feed only when the warm side is at temperature: a gecko whose warm hide floor runs cool cannot digest properly, and a cool warm side is one of the most common reasons a gecko goes off food.

AgePrey sizeHow oftenHow much
Hatchlings and young geckosAbout 1/4 in (6 mm)DailyA few insects at a time, or what it clears in about 15 minutes
JuvenilesWorking up to slightly larger, never wider than the space between the eyesDaily, or every 1 to 2 daysThe same 15 minute rule
Adults, once the tail is as thick as the neckNo wider than the space between the eyesEvery 2 to 4 daysA few insects, adjusted to body condition (page {{P:condition}})

The adult interval is not settled: anything from every other day to every 5 days once the tail has filled out is in use. Feed the gecko in front of you rather than the calendar.

Sizing and presentation

Prey too large to pass safely is one of the two usual causes of impaction, alongside loose substrate. Hatchlings take insects around 1/4 in (6 mm); after that, measure against the gecko rather than the calendar. Feeding tongs control the portion and keep insects from burrowing into the substrate.

When and how

  • Feed in the evening. Leopard geckos hunt at dusk, so an evening feeding matches their natural activity better than a morning one.
  • Commercially raised feeders only. Never wild-caught, whatever the garden looks like.
  • Gut-load, then dust. Every feeder spends 24 to 72 hours on a calcium-rich diet, then gets a light coat of powder right before it goes in (page {{P:supplements}}).
  • Don't leave insects loose. Feeder insects are not left unsupervised in the enclosure long-term; remove what isn't eaten.
Feed the gecko, not the calendar The tail is the gecko's fat store and the best gauge of whether the schedule is right. A full tail and a steady weight mean it is working. A tail that thins over a couple of weeks means something is wrong, whatever the calendar says.

Why the calcium numbers matter

A gecko needs more calcium than phosphorus in its diet, roughly 2 parts to 1, or at the very least 1 to 1. Phosphorus competes with calcium for absorption in the gut, so a meal carrying plenty of calcium alongside far more phosphorus still leaves the gecko short. Analyses by Finke put plain crickets near 1 to 9 and mealworms near 1 to 14. A gecko fed nothing but plain feeder insects runs a calcium deficit at every meal, and sustained, that is the road to metabolic bone disease. Gut-loading and dusting close that gap between them.

Buying and keeping feeders

  • Keep feeders in a ventilated container with room to move. Crowded crickets die fast and eat each other.
  • Keep the gut-load diet and a calcium-fortified water gel in with them all the time, since insects drown in an open dish.
  • Offer more than one staple. Some geckos lose interest in a repeated insect and pick back up on something different.
  • Buying in bulk, or keeping a small dubia roach colony, brings the monthly feeder cost down significantly.
Section 03 · Feeding

Gut-loading: what to feed the feeders

A gecko's nutrition is mostly decided a day or two before the insect is ever offered.

An insect is a container: whatever is in its gut when the gecko eats it gets eaten too. The effect is measured, not folklore: Klasing and colleagues (2000) found that crickets fed a diet of about 8 percent calcium for 48 hours had their whole-body calcium raised enough to bring the calcium to phosphorus balance into acceptable territory, and Allen and Oftedal (1989) showed that changing a cricket's diet changes what it delivers. The catch is that the diet has to be genuinely high in calcium, and that is where most gut-loading fails.

Gut-load foodTierNotes
Dry commercial gut-load sold on its calcium contentStaplePick one sold on its calcium figure, not one that just says "gut-load" on the front. Labels vary a lot, one reason to dust as well.
Collard greensStapleCalcium-dense and low in oxalates, the combination that actually moves the balance.
Mustard greens, turnip greensStapleThe same profile as collards.
Dandelion greensStapleCalcium-dense and low in oxalates.
Butternut squashStapleCalcium-dense, and insects take it readily.
Calcium-fortified water gelStapleWater and calcium in one. Insects drink even when they won't eat a dry diet. Never the only calcium source.
Carrot, potato, appleOccasionalKeep insects alive and hydrated, which is worth doing, but as a calcium boost they do close to nothing. Not gut-loading on their own.
Spinach, beet greens, Swiss chardNeverThey contain calcium, but also oxalic acid, which binds that calcium into a form nothing can absorb. Self-defeating as a gut-load.

The timing window

24 to 72 hours on the diet before feeding, with 48 hours the most often cited. Too short and the insect has not eaten enough for its gut contents to matter. Longer stops helping, because gut-loading does not build up: an insect only carries what is in its gut right now. Insects start emptying their gut as soon as they come off the food, so feed them out within a few hours. With a standing colony, the simple approach is to leave the gut-load in with them all the time rather than treating it as a two-day event before each feeding.

Fresh food without the mold

Offer fresh produce in small amounts and remove it before it molds: mold in a warm feeder bin can kill a colony quickly. A gel cube diet handles food and moisture in one item without rotting, but treat it as what keeps the colony alive, not as the calcium source, and run a high-calcium dry diet alongside.

Gut-load and dust, not one or the other A properly formulated high-calcium gut-load can, in principle, get an insect to an acceptable balance on its own. In practice, dust as well: you cannot see how much of the diet a given cricket actually ate, and powder on the outside does not empty out the way gut contents do.

Why a leopard gecko stops eating

Most refusals are normal. Work through the list before assuming illness:

  1. A temperature too low. One of the most common fixable causes. Read the warm hide floor first; it should be 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C), with the cool side at 70 to 77°F (21 to 25°C).
  2. A shed. Many geckos go off food for a day or two before, and sometimes after, a shed. Appetite returns once it is done.
  3. Brumation. A natural winter slowdown cued by cooler temperatures and shorter days, with less activity and appetite and brief waking to drink (page {{P:brumation}}).
  4. Stress. A new enclosure, too much handling, or another gecko in the enclosure. It usually resolves once things settle.
  5. Breeding season. Both sexes may eat less during courtship, and gravid females often eat less as they near laying.
  6. Prey boredom. Some geckos lose interest in a repeated feeder and pick back up on something different.
  7. Illness or impaction. Refusal alongside bloating, straining, loose stool, discoloration, poor shedding or sunken eyes points to a medical cause, not a cycle.
When a refusal becomes a vet visit A healthy adult with a plump tail can typically go a couple of weeks without much concern, and the outer edge may be closer to a month. Rather than counting days, watch the tail: a noticeable thinning over a couple of weeks is the clearest warning sign, whatever day you're on. Go sooner for a refusal with lethargy beyond normal brumation, bloating, straining, discolored skin, poor or incomplete shedding, sunken eyes, or wrinkled, dry-looking skin.
The never-feed list, in one place Fireflies and lightning bugs · wild hornworms, the bright green ones · any wild-caught insect · fruit, vegetables and purees · powdered fruit diets made for crested geckos · anything wider than the space between the gecko's eyes · superworms for a juvenile · spinach, beet greens and Swiss chard as a gut-load.

Multivitamin (reptile-specific)About once a weekAbout once a weekA reptile multivitamin, not a general one.

The calcium dish

A shallow dish of plain calcium can stay in the enclosure at all times as an optional backup. It does not replace dusting: the schedule above is the plan, and the dish is a top-up the gecko can take on its own.

Dusting and gut-loading are two jobs

Gut-loading improves what is inside the insect; the powder adds calcium on the outside. Neither replaces the other. There is also a job gut-loading cannot do at all: vitamin D3. A gecko needs D3 to use calcium, from UVB on the skin or from its food, and gut-loading is a poor route for it. That is why, in a setup without UVB, the D3 dust is doing work nothing else covers.

  • Dust right before feeding. Powder falls off, and an insect left dusted in a container for an hour has groomed much of it away.
  • Read the label. The everyday dust is plain calcium with no vitamins added. Keep a D3 formula for its own slot.
  • Date the jars. Replace opened powders by the date on the jar, and log the date on page {{P:equiplog}}.
If the signs say calcium, check the UVB and heat A gecko that is gut-loaded and dusted properly and still shows signs of calcium trouble, such as a soft jaw or tremors, usually has a light or heat problem rather than a powder problem. Leopard geckos appear to rely heavily on UVB and a correct warm side to process calcium, not on supplements alone.
Females need calcium whether or not a male is around A female can make eggs with no male present, and making eggs draws on her calcium. A diet low in calcium is one of the causes of egg binding. Keep up the dusting for every adult female, with plain calcium at most feedings while she is breeding or laying.

Growth, weight & the tail

The scale and the tail say whether the feeding schedule is working.

The numbers

Adult length6 to 9 in (15 to 23 cm) nose to tail tip
Adult weightMales 60 to 90 g; females lighter, some as light as 45 g
Adult tailAs thick as the neck, the point it moves onto the adult feeding schedule
Sexual maturity9 to 18 months, so a female can start making eggs while she is still growing (page {{P:eggs}})

How fast they grow

Growth is fast for the first year, then levels off. A 2025 University of Manitoba study weighed seven captive leopard geckos through their whole lives:

AgeAverage weight
Hatching4.4 g
300 days (about 10 months)38 g
400 days (about 13 months)52 g
Adult60 to 75 g, with growth slowing after 400 days

Those geckos were fed every day, more often than the adult schedule on page {{P:diet}}, and a healthy gecko can sit either side of them. What matters is the trend: steady gain for a young gecko, a stable weight for an adult.

The tail is the fuel gauge

A leopard gecko stores its fat in its tail and lives off that reserve in lean times, through a brumation, or after a refused meal. A plump, thick tail means good condition. A thin one is the first sign of wasting, and a tail thinning toward a stick while the gecko still eats is the signature of cryptosporidiosis (page {{P:crypto}}). Look at the tail every day; it shows trouble before almost anything else.

The weekly weigh-in

  • Weigh once a week, at the same time of day and before a feeding, so a full stomach doesn't skew the reading.
  • Use a kitchen scale that reads to single grams. In an animal this small, a slide of a few grams is invisible by eye and obvious on a scale. Write every reading on page {{P:ownerlog}}.
  • Watch the line, not the number. Through a suspected brumation, keep weighing at least every couple of weeks: a brumating gecko eats less but should not be losing significant body mass.
Section 03 · Feeding

Body condition: thin, ideal & overweight

Four shapes to recognize, and which ones are a vet visit.

A healthy adult keeps its reserve in the tail, and that tail is nearly as wide as the rest of the body. Too little food, parasites or cryptosporidiosis draw the reserve down; too much food pushes fat past the tail and onto the body. Both ends show on the outside before they show anywhere else.

Underweight

Hips, spine or ribs standing out, a sunken or narrow tail base, a body smaller than it should be for its age. Check the warm hide floor first, then see a vet rather than simply feeding more: parasites and cryptosporidiosis both look like this.

Ideal

A rounded body with no projecting hips, spine or ribs, and a full tail as thick as the neck at its base and nearly as wide as the body. Weight stable week to week in an adult, climbing steadily in a young gecko.

Overweight

A tail wider than the body. A thick layer of fat over the back and sides, so the spine and ribs can no longer be felt. Fat under the neck that hangs like jowls, and a pear-shaped torso instead of a streamlined one.

Wasting

A tail thinning to a stick, weight falling, often with undigested food or foul-smelling stool, sometimes while the appetite stays normal. Keep it away from any other gecko and see a reptile vet promptly.

Bringing an overweight gecko down

The fix is in the food bowl. Space an adult's meals out to the adult schedule, every 2 to 4 days once the tail is full, and cut the fatty treats first: waxworms and superworms. Keep weighing weekly and log every reading, so the drop shows on paper.

When a weight change is a vet visit A tail that visibly thins over a couple of weeks. A thinning tail together with weight loss, at any time of year. Real weight loss during a suspected brumation. Any steady loss across the weekly log. Bring the log; a trend line tells a vet more than a single reading.
