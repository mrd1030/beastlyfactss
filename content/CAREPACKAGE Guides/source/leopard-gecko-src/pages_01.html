How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

Units used throughout Every temperature and size is given twice. Temperatures appear in degrees Fahrenheit (°F) with degrees Celsius (°C) beside them. Sizes appear in inches (in) with centimeters (cm) beside them, millimeters (mm) for the smallest insects, and tank sizes in gallons, the way they are sold. A gecko and its weight log are measured in grams (g), the unit a kitchen scale shows. Humidity is a percentage (%) of the moisture the air could hold. Prices are in United States dollars.

If you haven't set up yet

Check the law first, on page {{P:legal}}: a leopard gecko is legal without a permit in most of the United States, but not everywhere. Then read pages {{P:profile}} to {{P:cleaning}} in order before buying anything. The enclosure size and the three-hide layout decide most of what goes in the cart, and the thermostat is never optional. Use the Budget & Shopping List on page {{P:budget}} to buy once.

If your gecko is already home

Audit what you have against the Setup Checklist in the print list below, and go to the Red Flags page (page {{P:redflags}}) if anything seems off. Read the warm hide floor with an infrared thermometer before you change anything else: a warm side running too cool explains more refused meals than any other fixable cause, and the feeding pages starting on page {{P:diet}} show why.

Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific nameEublepharis macularius
Adult size6 to 9 in (15 to 23 cm) nose to tail; males 60 to 90 g, females as light as 45 g
Captive lifespan10 to 20 years with proper care, and some reach 25 to 30
Native rangeRocky desert, arid grassland and dry scrub in Pakistan, Afghanistan, Iran, India and Nepal
Diet typeObligate insectivore: live insects, no fruit or vegetables (page {{P:diet}})
ActiveAt dusk and dawn (crepuscular), so evening is feeding and handling time
Enclosure minimum (adult)36×18×18 in (91×46×46 cm), front-opening preferred; floor space matters more than height
HeatAn under-tank heat mat on a thermostat, 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C) on the warm hide floor
UVBOptional but beneficial, kept low: a UVI (ultraviolet index, the strength of the UVB) of 0.6 to 1.4 at the basking spot
Handling difficultyBeginner; tolerant once settled, and never picked up by the tail
Conservation statusLeast Concern, the lowest-risk grade on the global list of threatened species

Cost overview

Initial setup

$20 to $45 for a pet-store gecko and $175 to $495 for the equipment, plus $150 to $215 for the first vet exam and fecal test (a stool check for parasites): $325 to $710 before the gecko. Every line is on page {{P:budget}}.

Monthly ongoing

$20 to $82: feeder insects, supplements, substrate, electricity and UVB tube replacement. The yearly wellness exam, $85 to $105, comes on top.

Budget for the worst case too A sick or emergency visit runs $100 to $800 or more, and diagnostics or surgery push it higher again. Treat a small emergency fund as part of the setup cost, not an optional extra.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Unlike most geckos, leopard geckos have movable eyelids and can blink. They also lack the sticky toe pads other geckos climb glass with.
  • The fat tail is the gecko's energy store. A plump tail means good condition, and a thinning one is often the first sign that something is wrong.
  • The temperature an egg is incubated at decides the sex: cooler eggs hatch mostly females, warmer eggs mostly males, and the hottest eggs, about 93°F (34°C) and up, mostly females again.

The species, morphs, size & lifespan

What you are buying, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

One species

The pet leopard gecko is Eublepharis macularius. In the wild it lives across arid country from Pakistan to northwestern India, sheltering by day in rocky outcrops and dry grassland and coming out at dusk to hunt insects, scorpions, small lizards and worms. It is a ground dweller: no toe pads, no climbing glass, and a life spent on and under the floor, which is why floor space matters more than height in its enclosure. It is not a social species and does not do well sharing an enclosure, so every leopard gecko is housed alone.

Morphs

A morph is a captive-bred line that looks different from the natural, wild-type gecko: a different color or pattern. Over 100 are recognized. A standard or wild-type gecko costs $20 to $45 at a pet store; breeders often charge $50 to $150 even for a basic morph, popular morphs sit around $75 to $300, and rare designer morphs run $500 to $3,500 or more. The care in this package applies to every morph with one exception. Albino and other pale morphs burn more easily under UVB, so they get a UVI of 0.5 to 0.7 or lower instead of 0.6 to 1.4 (page {{P:uvb}}).

Size

Adult length6 to 9 in (15 to 23 cm) nose to tail tip.
Adult weightMales 60 to 90 g. Females are lighter, some as light as 45 g. A gecko that drops its tail loses a large share of its reserves at once, which is why the tail matters so much in an animal this small.
Young geckosA gecko under about 3 in (8 cm) long is fragile and easily injured. It waits until it reaches roughly 6 in (15 cm) before regular handling.
Counted as adultOnce the tail has thickened to about the width of the neck. That is the point it moves onto the adult feeding schedule (page {{P:diet}}).

Lifespan

10 to 20 years is typical in captivity with proper care, and some leopard geckos reach 25 to 30. Wild geckos manage 6 to 8 years, because predation, parasites and harsh conditions cut them short. At $240 to $984 a year in running costs, 10 to 20 years adds up to $2,400 to $19,680 before a single sick visit. A hatchling bought today may outlast its enclosure, its heat mat and several UVB tubes, so plan for the whole span: the routine and a reptile vet for every one of those years.

Captive-bred is the only kind on sale Leopard geckos were imported into the United States and Europe from Pakistan, Afghanistan and India through the 1960s and 1970s, and were being bred in the United States by the early 1970s. Large-scale breeding started in the late 1980s, and the color morphs followed in the 1990s. Essentially every leopard gecko sold today is captive-bred, with no wild-collected supply behind the pet trade. What to look for in the animal itself is on page {{P:arrival}}.
