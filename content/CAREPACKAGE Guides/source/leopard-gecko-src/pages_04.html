Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Let it settle first

Wait one to two weeks after a new gecko comes home, and treat that as a benchmark rather than a countdown: don't start until it stops fleeing or hiding when you approach and is eating normally. A gecko under about 3 in (8 cm) long waits until it reaches roughly 6 in (15 cm) before regular handling, since hatchlings are fragile and easily injured. Settling is separate from quarantine, which runs for months if another reptile lives in the house (page {{P:arrival}}).

Use the settling weeks to introduce your hand without touching: rest it flat in the enclosure for a few minutes each evening, when the gecko is up, and let it approach or tongue-flick your fingers on its own terms. A gecko that walks over to investigate a still hand is ready for the first pickup.

How to pick one up

Use a two-handed scoop: one hand goes under the body to support the hips and the base of the tail, and the gecko walks onto your hand rather than being grabbed from above. Support the feet, body and tail together in an open palm, and let it walk hand to hand rather than holding it still. Stay low to the ground: leopard geckos jump and dart without warning, and a fall from height is a real injury for an animal this small.

Never by the tail A leopard gecko can drop its tail, called caudal autotomy, when it is grabbed by the tail, badly frightened, handled roughly or attacked by another gecko, and it can come off with very little pressure. The tail is its fat store, so losing it has a real cost. It regrows in about 30 days, but the new one is built on cartilage instead of bone and comes back shorter, thicker and different-looking (page {{P:shedding}}).

How often and how long

Once settled, start with 5-minute sessions every other day and build gradually toward 10 to 20 minutes. Cap a young gecko at a few short sessions a week. Evening works best, close to its natural active time, but not right after it wakes. It doesn't need daily handling. Never handle during a shed, and skip handling for about 48 hours after a large meal: jostling a full gecko can make it bring the meal back up.

Put it back when you see Chirping or squeaking · screaming, more common in juveniles · a slow side-to-side tail swish, usually with an arched back and the gecko up on its toes · fast retreats or attempts to hide · refusing food afterward. A bark alone isn't necessarily distress and can just mean hunger; the others end the session.

Who should hold it

Children under 5 should not handle the gecko or touch its enclosure, because a healthy-looking gecko can still carry Salmonella (page {{P:cleaning}}). Older children handle it with an adult supervising. Wash hands with soap before and after every session.

Too cold looks quiet

Too hot is loud. Too cold is quiet: it shows slowly, as a gecko that eats less and moves less, long before anything shows on the animal. That is why a refused meal sends you to the infrared thermometer before anything else. Open-mouth breathing, discharge from the nose or labored breathing in a cool enclosure is a respiratory problem, not a mood (page {{P:respiratory}}).

Quick differentiator Brumation looks like a gecko that is quiet, hiding and off food but holding its weight and its tail. Illness looks like the same behavior with a thinning tail, weight loss, sunken eyes or real lethargy. The scale is what separates them.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • One hide instead of three. It is not enrichment with a gap; it is a missing foundation.
  • Skipping the humid hide, or raising the humidity of the whole enclosure instead of using one.
  • A heat mat with no thermostat, or a probe anywhere but the warm hide floor.
  • The old 20 gallon long tank in place of the 36×18×18 in (91×46×46 cm) minimum.
  • Calcium sand, including sand sold as an enrichment substrate.
  • Lifting a gecko by the tail during "play," or treating a shed as handling time.
  • Wild-caught insects or fireflies as a "natural" treat.
  • Assuming a wheel or a toy substitutes for hides and hunting.

What the research says

Leopard geckos are unusual in having real studies behind their enrichment. Bashaw and colleagues (2016) tested five types of enrichment: thermal and feeding enrichment produced the strongest responses, and mirror-based visual enrichment the weakest. Rickman and colleagues (2025) found better welfare in enriched housing than in sparse setups, and geckos given the choice consistently picked the enriched side. Enrichment comes after correct belly heat and three hides, never instead of them. In this order:

1. Belly heat and the three hides

The foundation. A warm dry hide, a cool dry hide and a maintained humid hide let a gecko choose its microclimate while staying hidden. That choice only exists if the gradient under the hides is right.

2. Feeding and hunting

The highest-value enrichment after heat. Tong-feeding, short supervised insect hunts inside the enclosure, a rolling dispenser that drops insects as it is nudged, and simple timed-release prey containers all beat a static bowl. Offering small insects in a more natural way has been linked to lasting increases in activity. Feed in the evening, when it hunts.

3. Low climbing and cover

Low sturdy branches, cork flats or tubes and stacked slate give somewhere to climb and rest off the ground, plus cover that still lets it move through the space. Height matters less than floor space.

4. A dig box

A single box to dig in satisfies the digging some geckos do, especially some females, without turning the whole floor into loose substrate. Paper towel or tile stays the main floor. Never fill it with calcium sand.

5. Light novelty

A new piece of cork, a mild rearrangement. Some value, genuinely last on the list, and no substitute for anything above it.

What barely registers

Mirrors and other visual novelty were the weakest result in the research, and a wheel or a toy does not stand in for hides and hunting. The answer is more of items 1 to 4, not novelty for its own sake.

Choosing a gecko, quarantine & the first vet visit

Captive-bred, checked over in good light, and in front of a vet within 72 hours.

A breeder who hatched the gecko can tell you its hatch date, what it eats, and when it last ate and shed. A pet store or breeder is worth buying from only if its enclosures are clean and large enough. A rescue or an expo animal often comes with no reliable history: not a reason to refuse it, but a reason to run the full quarantine below. A seller who cannot say whether an animal was bred or caught has answered the question.

Ask the seller: the hatch date; whether it was captive-bred; what it eats and when it last ate; whether it has been treated for anything; and whether it has had a fecal test (a stool check for parasites). A good breeder answers without looking anything up.

Quarantine: 3 to 6 months

If another reptile already lives in the house, keep the new gecko completely apart for 3 to 6 months. 60 to 90 days is a floor, not a target: apparently healthy geckos can carry cryptosporidiosis, which has no cure, without showing a sign. With no other reptile, skip the separation but not the vet visit.

  • A separate room. Two enclosures on one shelf share the same air.
  • Paper towel, a hide and a water dish. Paper shows stool, mites and a regurgitated meal at a glance.
  • Separate tools, and the new gecko last. Its own tongs, dishes and feeder tub, cared for after every other animal, with hands washed in between.
  • Restart the clock if anything flags: a positive fecal test, a regurgitated meal, unexplained weight loss. At the end, disinfect everything that touched it.
The first vet visit Book a reptile vet before the gecko comes home (finding one is on page {{P:redflags}}), for within 72 hours of arrival and no later than two weeks. The first exam records a weight and a look over the whole animal, and checks a fresh stool sample for parasites, so bring one. Ask for the cryptosporidiosis PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test by name: a lab test that finds a germ's genetic material in the sample. That visit is the baseline every later one is measured against.

Sexing & females that lay

Every female owner needs this page, whether or not a male has ever been in the house.

Telling male from female

Look at the underside, near the vent. It only works on a maturing gecko, so treat a hatchling sold as male or female as a guess and check again as it grows. A male shows a V-shaped row of enlarged preanal pores (waxy pores just in front of the vent) and a more bulbous tail base where the hemipenes, the male's paired reproductive organs, sit as two hemipenal bulges. A female lacks the enlarged pore row and has a flatter tail base. Never hold the gecko by the tail to look.

Sex is set in the egg. Rhen and colleagues (2011) bred leopard geckos over 13 generations: eggs incubated at 86°F (30°C) hatched mostly female and eggs at 90.5°F (32.5°C) mostly male, so a breeder who records incubation temperatures can say which way a clutch was likely to go. A temperature is a probability, not a sexing.

Females lay without a male

A female leopard gecko matures at 9 to 18 months and can then produce eggs with no male in the enclosure. Those eggs are infertile, but making them is the same physical work, and most reptiles brought to a vet for egg binding are single females that have never met a male. So every adult female needs a place to lay, and calcium in her diet whether or not she is kept with a male (page {{P:supplements}}).

Signs she is gravid

  • A bulging belly and weight gain.
  • Restlessness and more digging.
  • A smaller appetite.

Clutches

A clutch is two eggs. A mature female can lay four to five clutches in a season, about a month apart, so a female that has just laid is often gravid again a few weeks later. Keep the lay box in for the whole season, not just for the first clutch.

The lay box

A covered container large enough for her to turn around in but small enough to feel enclosed, filled with damp sphagnum moss she can dig into. Damp but not waterlogged, holding about 70 to 80% humidity inside the box. Place it in a quiet corner where she feels secure. A female with nowhere suitable to lay may hold her eggs, which is how a normal clutch turns into egg binding.

The cheapest prevention there is A lay box ready for any adult female, correct temperatures, humidity and UVB, and a calcium-rich diet cover most of what causes egg binding. The page after this one is the one to know before you ever need it.

Egg binding

Dystocia: a female who cannot pass the eggs she is carrying. A same-day emergency.

Egg binding can be fatal without intervention. A gravid female and one in trouble share some signs at first: both are restless and both eat less. The difference is what comes next, and the clock: laying itself should be over in less than 48 hours. A female in trouble strains with nothing produced, turns weak and depressed, and stops eating altogether with a distended belly and no eggs laid.

Call a reptile vet the same day if She is straining and producing nothing · she has been digging or straining for more than 48 hours without finishing · she is weak, depressed or restless without laying · her belly is distended and she has stopped eating without having laid · any tissue is protruding from her vent, which is an emergency on its own.

What causes it

  • Nowhere to lay: no lay box, or one she doesn't feel secure in.
  • Husbandry: temperatures too hot or too cold, the wrong humidity, missing UVB.
  • Diet: too little calcium or protein, and dehydration.

What the vet does

A vet may start with fluids, calcium and hormone injections to help her lay, and surgery follows if those fail. Because it can be fatal, it is a same-day visit, not a wait-and-see.

Prevention, in one line Every adult female gets a lay box, correct heat and humidity, and calcium dusting, whether or not a male has ever been near her. Most egg binding starts with one of those missing.

The shed cycle & brumation

Two normal cycles that look like illness to a new owner, and how to tell them apart from the real thing.

The shed cycle

Reptiles shed their skin, a process called ecdysis, to grow and to clear parasites and old, damaged skin. Before a shed, the gecko's colors go dull and its skin looks dry, and it often hides more and refuses food for a day or two. Then it rubs against rough surfaces to start the peel and works its way out of the old skin.

  • Humidity, in the right place. The humid hide is what lets old skin come away in one piece. Keep it damp, especially when a shed is due, rather than making the whole enclosure damper.
  • Something rough. Cork bark, slate and branches give it something to rub against to start the peel.
  • Hands off. No handling during a shed. Expect a refused meal or two; appetite returns once the shed is done.
  • Check afterward. The toes, the tail tip and around the eyes, in good light, after every shed. Retained skin collects in those three places, and a ring of it can cut off the blood supply (page {{P:shedding}}).
Shed week routine Top up the humid hide. Leave the gecko alone. Don't worry about a refused feeding. The day after, check toes, tail tip and eyes. Anything still attached gets a soak, never a fingernail.

Brumation

Brumation is a reptile's natural winter slowdown, cued by cooler temperatures and shorter days. You don't have to induce it. A brumating gecko is less active, eats less or stops eating, and wakes briefly to drink. It lives off the fat in its tail meanwhile, which is exactly why the tail and the scale tell you whether it is going well.

Weigh itAt least every couple of weeks, in grams. A brumating gecko eats less but should not be losing significant body mass.
Watch the tailIt should stay full. A tail that visibly thins is not brumation.
Keep water outFresh water in the dish every day: it wakes briefly to drink.
Not brumation: call the vet if The tail is visibly thinning · weight is falling · it is lethargic beyond normal brumation behavior · there is bloating, straining, discolored skin, sunken eyes or wrinkled, dry-looking skin. A slowdown with any of these is illness until a vet says otherwise.

Legal without a permit almost everywhere in the United States, with a short list of exceptions.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet leopard gecko is legal with no permit in 46, barred in 2, permit-only in 2, and legal on a condition in 2.
