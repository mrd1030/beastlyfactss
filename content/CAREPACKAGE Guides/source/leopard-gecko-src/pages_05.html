Red flags & finding a vet

What to watch for, what to tell the vet, and how to find one before you need one.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see A tail thinning to a "stick," especially with weight loss · a soft or deformed jaw · tremors · any breathing difficulty, or open-mouth breathing · drooling or pus around the mouth · appetite loss that comes with a visibly thinning tail, or with any other sign here · a bloated belly with no normal bowel movements · straining with nothing produced · blood in the stool · any tissue protruding from the vent · retained shed that is swollen, darkened, or won't clear from the eyes or toes · darkened skin, wobbliness or limpness in the heat · a female straining or weak without laying, or still digging past 48 hours.

What to tell the vet

The warm hide floor reading from an infrared thermometer, not the thermostat's setting, and the cool side
Substrate, and whether loose substrate has ever been used
Which powders, how often, and whether UVB is running
When symptoms started and whether they're getting worse
Last normal bowel movement, and what it looked like
Weight trend from the Owner Log, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag if you can

Why the heat comes up first

A leopard gecko cannot make its own body heat, so the warm hide floor is not a comfort setting: it powers digestion and the immune response. A floor a few degrees low brings refused meals, slow stools, a gecko that hides all day, and in time a real infection, each looking like a different problem. That is why a vet asks for the number before anything else.

Finding a reptile vet

Many dog and cat practices do not see reptiles, so find one early. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory of its members, and a member is the place to start. Ask a clinic how many reptiles it sees, whether it treats leopard geckos routinely, whether it runs fecal tests in house, and who covers emergencies after hours, then write the number on page {{P:emergency}}. Book a wellness exam once a year even when the gecko seems perfectly healthy: several of the conditions in this section, cryptosporidiosis above all, can be present without obvious signs until they are advanced.

Cryptosporidiosis: stick tail disease

The one condition every leopard gecko owner should be able to recognize. It is severe, highly contagious, and has no cure.

What it is: an infection with the parasite Cryptosporidium. In leopard geckos it commonly causes clinical disease and wasting, though not every carrier shows signs right away, and apparently healthy carriers spread it. One study gives a sense of scale: Nipithakul and colleagues (2025) screened the geckos at one Thai breeding facility with a PCR test and found Cryptosporidium varanii in 51% of them. It earned its common name from the sign that gives it away: a healthy tail is thick with stored fat, and this disease melts it down to a stick.

Signs

  • A tail that thins to a "stick," with no fat reserve left.
  • Weight loss and an emaciated look overall.
  • Loss of appetite.
  • Undigested food in the droppings, and foul-smelling stool.
  • A bloated or distended belly.

Diagnosis and treatment

A reptile vet diagnoses it with a fecal PCR test. False negatives happen, so one clear test does not fully rule it out. The main treatment drug, paromomycin, manages the condition rather than curing it. Mortality for geckos with symptoms runs around 50%, and a gecko that hasn't responded well to treatment within a month generally has a poor outlook.

A thinning tail is cryptosporidiosis until a test says otherwise See a reptile vet promptly for any tail thinning toward a stick, especially with weight loss. Keep that gecko away from every other reptile, with its own tools, cared for last, with hands washed in between, until the vet has answered the question.

Prevention is the only real control

With no cure and carriers that look healthy, prevention is about keeping it out. Quarantine any new gecko for 3 to 6 months in a separate room before it shares a room, a tool or a hand with an existing animal (page {{P:arrival}}). Ask a breeder whether the gecko has had a fecal test before you buy. Bring a fresh stool sample to the first vet visit and ask for the cryptosporidiosis PCR test by name, then keep up the yearly wellness exam.

Why the scale matters most here A gecko losing weight while it still eats is the pattern to catch. The weekly weigh-in shows it weeks before the tail looks obviously thin, and it is the difference between this disease and a normal winter slowdown.

Metabolic bone disease

Common in leopard geckos, and largely preventable once heat and calcium are handled correctly.

What it is: metabolic bone disease (MBD) is a weakening of the skeleton from too little usable calcium. Why it is common in this species: leopard geckos are so often kept without UVB, without proper calcium supplementation, or without enough heat, and all three contribute. This species appears to rely heavily on UVB and a correct warm side to process calcium, not on supplements alone, which is part of why low-level UVB is worth providing even though it is technically optional.

Signs

WhereSigns
JawA soft or deformed jaw, sometimes called "rubber jaw"; difficulty eating
Limbs and bonesTwisted or bent limbs; fractures from minor knocks or falls
Nerves and musclesTremors
Whole bodyLethargy and weight loss

Response: see a reptile vet promptly. Any softness in the jaw, any tremor, or any new difficulty getting around is an appointment, not a wait-and-see.

While you wait for the appointment Handle the gecko extremely gently, and as little as possible. Take out any climbing decor: weakened bones can fracture in a fall that would not normally matter.
Prevention beats treatment A warm hide floor at 88 to 92°F (31 to 33°C) on a thermostat, gut-loaded and dusted insects on the schedule (page {{P:supplements}}), and low-output UVB replaced on time (page {{P:uvb}}) prevent nearly all of it. A cool warm side is one of the usual ways it starts, and the easiest one to miss.

Why the three legs

Calcium from the food is only useful if the body can absorb it, and that takes vitamin D3, which a leopard gecko makes in its skin under UVB or takes in from a D3 dust. Digesting the meal in the first place takes heat from the floor. Take away any one of the three, calcium, D3 or heat, and the bones pay for it.

Impaction, dehydration & prolapse

Three problems that often travel together: a dry, cold or blocked gut, and the straining that follows.

Impaction

What it is: a blockage in the digestive tract. Cause: usually swallowed loose substrate, most often sand, or prey too large to pass safely. A warm side too cool to digest, dehydration and parasites all make it likelier. Signs: a bloated belly, loss of appetite, weight loss, and no normal bowel movements. Response: warm baths and gentle belly massage help mild cases; a true blockage may need surgery. That is the reason paper towel and tile are the safer floor, and the reason prey is measured against the space between the eyes rather than guessed.

When it is a vet visit A bloated belly with no normal droppings, straining with nothing produced, or a gap in droppings well beyond your gecko's own normal rhythm. Know that rhythm from the log, so a gap means something.

Dehydration

A desert species still drinks, and dehydration builds quietly. The urates usually show it first.

Signs of dehydration Wrinkled, dry-looking skin outside a shed, sunken eyes, and urates that are yellow, orange or gritty instead of smooth and white. Give it a bigger, easier-to-reach water dish and a properly damp humid hide. A gecko that stays sunken and wrinkled, or that is also off food and losing weight, is a vet visit.

Prolapse

Pink or red tissue protruding from the vent. It is always an emergency: get to a vet immediately. On the way, keep the tissue moist with clean water and never try to push it back in. It is a sign of an underlying problem rather than a condition on its own, and straining, from a blockage or from a female trying to lay, is one of the usual routes to it.

Respiratory infection, parasites & mites

One that comes from the enclosure, and two that usually come in with a new animal.

Respiratory infection

What it is: a bacterial infection of the airways. Cause: in a leopard gecko it almost always starts with an enclosure that is too cool, too damp, or both, and more than a day or two well below the gecko's comfortable range is the textbook setup for it. Signs: open-mouth breathing, bubbly saliva, clogged nostrils, lethargy, loss of appetite and weight loss. Response: it does not clear on its own. A reptile vet prescribes antibiotics, usually with a probiotic, and a sick gecko may need extra fluids and help with feeding while it recovers. At home, fix what caused it: bring the warm side back up to temperature, and find the source of excess moisture, such as a humid hide that has soaked the substrate around it. That is also why raising the humidity of the whole enclosure to fix a shedding problem is the wrong move.

Internal parasites

Gut parasites, coccidia (single-celled gut parasites), other protozoa and worms, show no outward sign in many geckos, which is why a microscope check of a fresh stool sample is part of every first visit and every yearly one. When they do show, repeated watery stool is the classic sign, often with weight loss and poor condition. Bring a fresh sample in a sealed bag. Treatment depends on which parasite the fecal test finds, which is why a test comes before any medicine.

Mites

Tiny black, brown, reddish or orange flecks on the head, neck or belly, especially around the eyes. A reptile with mites often sits in its water dish for unusually long spells, as if trying to drown them. To check, gently rub the gecko while it stands over a sheet of white paper and look for anything that falls off. Mites arrive with a new animal or on shared equipment, spread fast, and can carry other infections, so a confirmed case is a vet visit and a reason to check every reptile in the house.

The common thread A correctly heated enclosure that is dry everywhere except inside the humid hide prevents most respiratory infections. A full quarantine on paper towel, separate tools and a fecal test catch most parasites and mites before they reach anything else you keep (page {{P:arrival}}).

Stuck shed, tail loss & burns

Skin and tail problems a look after every shed catches early.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Old skin that stays on after a shed collects in the same three places: around the eyes, on the toes, and at the tail tip. At the eyes it can cover them and stop the gecko from seeing. A ring of it can tighten around a toe or tail tip and cut off its blood supply badly enough to cause tissue death and lost toes, and the damage builds over several sheds. In leopard geckos the usual causes are low humidity, which a humid hide fixes, or too little vitamin A. Six things in all can cause a bad shed: low humidity, skin parasites, poor nutrition, infection, nothing rough to rub against, and low thyroid function. Repeated bad sheds in a correct setup are a vet conversation, not a humidity problem.

  • Never peel dry, stuck shed. Soak first.
  • The soak: shallow water at enclosure temperature, chin-deep at most, for 30 minutes, supervised throughout. Then ease the loosened skin off a toe or tail tip with a damp cotton swab.
  • Eyes are a vet job. Retained shed around the eyes that won't clear, or any shed that is swollen or darkened, goes to a reptile vet.

Tail loss

A dropped tail is a stress response: a gecko grabbed by the tail, badly frightened, handled roughly or attacked by another gecko. If it happens:

  • Move the gecko onto clean paper towel straight away, and keep the enclosure especially clean while the stump heals.
  • No handling for 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Watch the stump for swelling, discharge, discoloration or a foul smell. Any of those means a vet.

The tail regrows in about 30 days, built on cartilage instead of bone, shorter, thicker and different-looking. The real cost is the fat it carried, so keep a closer eye on the weight log for the next few months.

Burns

Unregulated heat is one of the most common causes of thermal burns in captive leopard geckos: a heat mat with no thermostat, a thermostat whose probe has moved, a hot rock, or an overhead lamp it can reach. Burns show as blistered, blackened or peeling skin. Turn the heat source off, fix the regulation, and get anything past a small red patch seen by a vet, because reptile burns often look worse over the following days rather than better.

Reading poop, urates & hydration

Stool is the earliest free health check you get. Look at it before you clean it up.

Urates are the white part of a reptile's droppings. Instead of liquid urine, the body turns nitrogen waste into uric acid, made in the liver from protein digestion, and passes it as a white to cream, chalky or paste-like portion alongside the stool. Its color and texture are the fastest daily read on hydration you have.

How often is normal

Most adult geckos go every few days to about once a week, and young ones more often, since they eat more often. The number that matters is your own gecko's baseline. A real change from it, in how often, how it looks, or how it smells, is the signal, not any fixed count.

Hydration

  • A shallow dish of fresh dechlorinated water, changed daily, that the gecko can climb in and out of easily.
  • The humid hide is hydration equipment as well as shedding equipment. Keep it damp all year.
  • Dry, hard or gritty urate is the signal to act: more water access for a ground-dwelling species like this one, not more misting of the enclosure.
