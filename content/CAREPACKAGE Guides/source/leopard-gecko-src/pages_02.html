Enclosure size, type & where it goes

36×18×18 in (91×46×46 cm) is the current minimum for one adult, and bigger is always fine. The 20 gallon long tank, roughly 30×12×12 in (76×30×30 cm), is the old floor and no longer the standard. A hatchling can start in something as small as a 10 gallon while it is small, then move up. Leopard geckos are terrestrial and cannot climb smooth glass, so floor space matters far more than height.

Choose a front-opening, well-ventilated enclosure with an escape-proof screen top. A PVC (polyvinyl chloride, a rigid plastic) or wood enclosure keeps heat that a glass tank loses, so a heat setting carried over from glass lands hotter: read the warm hide floor again after any change of enclosure (page {{P:thermostats}}).

Never Run a heat mat without a thermostat. An unregulated mat is a real burn and fire risk, and unregulated heat is one of the most common causes of thermal burns in captive leopard geckos. Skip hot rocks entirely: they heat unevenly and burn.

Where it goes

  • Out of the kitchen and away from anywhere food is made or served.
  • In a room that stays at 65°F (18°C) or warmer at night if you can, so no night heat is needed.
  • One gecko per enclosure. This is not a social species, and it does not do well sharing.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Hides should be snug and cave-like, never oversized and open. A roomy hide gives no sense of cover.
  • Add rock or wood for nail filing and light climbing.
  • The humid hide sits on the cool or middle side, never over the mat (page {{P:substrate}}).

The warm hide floor is the number to build everything else around. A floor surface runs above the air around it, which is why the floor target sits above the ambient one.

Why the floor number matters

A leopard gecko cannot make its own heat. Everything it does, from digesting a cricket to fighting off an infection, runs on warmth borrowed from the enclosure floor, and it digests on its belly. A gecko kept a few degrees too cool goes off food long before it looks sick. It moves between the warm and cool zones through the day to manage digestion and activity, so a tank warm from end to end takes that choice away and stresses it over time.

Measuring

  • Read the warm hide floor with an infrared thermometer, a handheld gun aimed at the surface the gecko lies on. A thermostat set to 90 does not mean the floor is 90; the infrared reading is how you find out.
  • Use digital thermometers with probes for the air in each zone, and a hygrometer (humidity gauge). A stick-on dial reads the air at the glass, not the floor.
  • Check at different times of day. Leopard geckos are active at dusk and dawn, so night temperatures matter too.

Signs the temperatures are wrong

Heat mats, thermostats, probes & timers

Belly heat first

An under-tank heat mat under the warm hide, on a thermostat, is the standard here. A mat makes a small, steady warm patch, uses little power, and can run all night without light. Overhead heat, a halogen bulb or a deep heat projector (a lightless heater that sends its heat down in a focused beam), warms a wider area but dries the air and can burn a gecko that climbs too close. Many setups run both: overhead by day for the gradient, the mat for the floor. Belly heat is preferred; skip an overhead lamp as the only source.

The thermostat is the safety device

A thermostat measures the temperature at one point and cuts or lowers the power once that point reaches the setting. Without one, a mat or bulb draws full power with no ceiling, and a gecko pressed against a hot surface cannot always move away. Every heater gets one.

Heat sourceController
Under-tank heat matOn/off thermostat with a probe. A mat changes temperature slowly enough that a full on and off cycle doesn't make a harsh swing.
Halogen bulb or deep heat projectorDimming (proportional) thermostat. It eases the power down before the surface overshoots.
Ceramic heat emitter (night heat)Pulse proportional or dimming thermostat. It reacts faster than a mat, so a plain on/off unit can overshoot before it cuts.

Where the probe goes

On the floor inside the warm hide, directly over the mat, on the surface the gecko lies on. Hold it with a probe clip or a suction cup, never tape: tape over a probe throws off its reading, and a gecko can stick to exposed adhesive and tear its skin. A probe stuck to the glass, hanging in the air, or sitting on the cool side holds the wrong number all year.

How heating fails

  • The probe moves. The most common failure, and a silent one: the display keeps showing an accurate reading for the wrong spot. Check the probe's position at every spot clean.
  • The mat makes a hot spot away from the probe. A thermostat will not catch it. Scan the whole warm end with the infrared thermometer rather than trusting the display.
  • The thermostat sticks on. Rarer and more dangerous. Keep a second probe, from a digital thermometer, on the warm hide floor beside the thermostat probe, and read it daily. The two should agree within a degree or two; a gap that widens, or a reading that keeps climbing, is the warning.
  • Check before the gecko goes in, with both the probe thermometer and the infrared gun, and again after any change of enclosure, substrate or hide.

Clearance, timers and night heat

Any overhead heat source or lamp sits at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest point the gecko can reach, behind a guard. Put the lights on a plug-in timer, 14 hours in summer and 12 in winter, so day length never depends on memory. Skip colored night bulbs, red and blue included: they disrupt sleep. A light bulb cannot be night heat. If the room drops below 65°F (18°C), use a ceramic heat emitter, which gives heat with no light, on its own thermostat; a room that holds 65°F needs no night heat at all.

UVB: low output, optional & worth it

Not required the way it is for a bearded dragon. A real benefit anyway.

Why bother

Leopard geckos were kept without UVB for decades on the assumption that a crepuscular species gets all the vitamin D3 it needs from its food. Controlled work has since found otherwise: Gould and colleagues (2018) and Oonincx and colleagues found that even brief, low-level UVB, as little as two hours a day, measurably raises vitamin D3 made in a leopard gecko's skin. Vitamin D3 is what lets the body absorb calcium. This species appears to rely heavily on UVB and correct temperature to process calcium, not on supplements alone, which is part of why metabolic bone disease turns up in geckos kept without it.

UVI targets

Leopard geckos are Ferguson Zone 1, the crepuscular and shade-dwelling group. Aim for a UVI of 0 to 0.7 across most of the enclosure, peaking at 0.6 to 1.4 at the basking spot, or 0.5 to 0.7 or lower for albino and other pale morphs, which burn more easily. Deep shade has to be available, and the hides provide it.

Which light

A low-output linear T5 tube (a fluorescent tube 5/8 inch across) about two-thirds the enclosure length: on the 36 in minimum, a 24 in (61 cm) 2.5% standard-output tube. A tube lays a gradient across the floor. A high-output tube (T5 HO) uses its maker's chart, not the table below. Compact coil bulbs give a less even spread and have been linked to eye and skin irritation.

Distance, mesh and glass

Distance is part of the dose: UVB falls off sharply as the tube goes higher, so the same tube is strong close up and weak far away. Glass or plastic between tube and gecko blocks most UVB, and a screen top blocks about 30%, so a tube above mesh sits closer than one inside. Like any lamp, keep it at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest point the gecko can reach. Heights above the basking spot for the 24 in 2.5% standard-output T5 tube:

GeckoTube on top of a screen lidTube inside, no mesh
Normally colored8 to 14 in (20 to 35 cm)10 to 18 in (25 to 45 cm)
Albino and other pale morphs12 to 14 in (30 to 35 cm)14 to 18 in (35 to 45 cm)

Add 1 to 2 in (3 to 5 cm) for the gecko's height. Any other tube: use its maker's distance chart.

Don't be fooled by the 2.5%. The percentage on the box is the share of the tube's light that is UVB, not how strong the tube is. Length and wattage count too: the maker's (Arcadia's) chart puts this 24 in, 14 W tube at a higher UV index than a short 12 in 7% tube at the same distance (1.0 against 0.65 at 12 in). Set it by its own heights, never by a chart written for another tube.

UVB: meter & schedule

Measure, don't assume A handheld UV index (UVI) meter reads what actually reaches the basking spot rather than what the box claims: it is the only way to know the tube is at the right height and when to replace it; UV cards show total UV, not the UVB that makes vitamin D3. It is the most expensive item in a reptile kit and highly recommended.

Schedule and replacement

  • On the same timer as the rest of the lighting: 14 hours a day in summer, 12 in winter.
  • Replace every 6 to 12 months depending on the model, by the calendar and the maker's rated life. Output fades long before the light dims, so a year-old tube that still lights may be giving almost no UVB. Write the date on page {{P:equiplog}}.
  • No UVB at all is a choice some keepers make. If you make it, the calcium with vitamin D3 dust becomes the backup the gecko depends on (page {{P:supplements}}).

Substrate, furnishings & the three hides

Substrate

Paper towel or tile are the safest choices, and the right default for anyone newer to reptiles: they remove the impaction risk and make a daily stool check easy. More experienced keepers sometimes move to a bioactive sand or soil mix, a planted floor with tiny invertebrates that break down waste, but that has more to get right and suits healthy adults only. A dig box (page {{P:enrichment}}) gives a gecko on paper or tile somewhere to dig.

Never Calcium sand, or any fine loose sand. It carries a real impaction risk (page {{P:impaction}}), whatever the label says. Corncob and crushed walnut shell bedding too: both get swallowed and can block the gut, and walnut shell scratches the skin.

The three hides are the husbandry, not the decoration

A warm dry hide over the mat, a cool dry hide at the far end, and a humid hide on the cool or middle side are the minimum. Together they let a gecko move between temperatures and moisture levels without ever leaving cover, which is a real choice rather than a forced trade between feeling safe and being the right temperature. Keep all three snug and cave-like.

The humid hide

A closed hide lined with damp sphagnum moss, running 70 to 80% humidity inside while the rest of the enclosure stays at 30 to 40%. It prevents the majority of shedding problems in this species. Keep the lining damp, not swampy and not bone dry, and replace it often, before it breaks down or molds. Don't raise the humidity of the whole enclosure to reach 70% either: that just adds a respiratory infection risk.

Furnishings checklist

Warm dry hide directly over the mat, probe inside it
Cool dry hide at the far end
Humid hide with damp sphagnum moss, cool or middle side
Shallow water dish the gecko can climb in and out of, refreshed daily
Rock, slate or wood for nail filing and light climbing
Cork flats or low sturdy branches to rest off the floor
A small dish of plain calcium, an optional backup (page {{P:supplements}})
Digital thermometer and hygrometer
Humidity, in one line Put the moisture in one box, not in the air. Glass fogging or a damp smell means the enclosure itself is too humid: fix the ventilation and look for the source, such as a humid hide that has soaked the substrate around it.

Cleaning & hygiene

Cleaning removes the waste. Disinfecting kills what is left. Hygiene keeps it off your hands.

Salmonella Healthy reptiles shed Salmonella bacteria in their droppings without being ill, on and off, and one negative test clears nothing. It travels from the gecko, its enclosure and anything it walks on to hands, then to mouths or food, so wash with soap and running water after every contact. Do not kiss the gecko or hold it to your face. Children under 5 should not handle the gecko or touch anything in its enclosure, and adults over 65, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system are at higher risk. Geckos are not exempt: an outbreak linked to pet geckos, closed in January 2026, made 113 people sick across 36 states and put 31 in the hospital.

Three clocks

WhenWhat to do
DailyRemove droppings, urates (the white part of the waste), uneaten insects and shed skin as soon as you see them. Empty, wash and refill the water dish with fresh dechlorinated water (tap water treated with a reptile water conditioner).
WeeklyWipe down glass, walls, hides and decor with a reptile-safe cleaner.
MonthlyMove the gecko to a secure temporary tub. Take everything out, scrub it with detergent and hot water, disinfect the enclosure, hides and dishes, rinse, dry, and put in fresh substrate.

Paper towel is replaced whenever it is soiled. A bioactive enclosure skips the monthly strip-down: pick up what you can see and let the cleanup crew handle the rest.

Disinfecting, step by step

  • Scrub first. A film of waste shields bacteria from disinfectant, so it only works on a surface already cleaned.
  • Dilute household bleach 1:32, which is half a cup of bleach per gallon of water. It is inexpensive and works against bacteria and many viruses.
  • Keep it wet for 10 minutes. Bleach needs that long in contact to work, so don't wipe it straight off.
  • Rinse until no bleach smell remains, then let the enclosure and every item dry completely and air out before the gecko goes back in. Plan on a full day of airing.
Never the kitchen sink Never wash the gecko, its dishes, hides or enclosure in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink, or a bath people use, or anywhere food is prepared. Keep a plastic tub for the job, or work in a utility sink or outdoors. If a bathtub does get used, clean it thoroughly afterward. Wash hides and decor separately from anything used for food, and keep the gecko out of the kitchen entirely.

If someone in the house gets sick

The signs are ordinary ones: diarrhea, fever, stomach cramps. That is why the link gets missed. Tell the doctor there is a reptile in the house, even if nobody remembers touching it, since enclosure surfaces are enough on their own. It matters most for a child under 5, an adult over 65, someone pregnant, or someone with a weakened immune system, in whom the infection can spread beyond the gut.
