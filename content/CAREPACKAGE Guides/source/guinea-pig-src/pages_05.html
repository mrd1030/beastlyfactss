Red flags, the 8 to 12 hour rule & finding a vet

For most pets, a skipped meal is nothing. For this one it is the alarm.

Guinea pigs hide illness well, and their digestion depends on food moving through continuously. The point to call a vet is 8 to 12 hours without eating or producing droppings, and once the gut has stopped, it can become life-threatening within 24 to 48 hours.

Same day, no waiting Not eating, or eating noticeably less · no droppings, or droppings that are small, dry or few · labored, noisy or open-mouth breathing, wheezing or clicking · a hunched, tense posture or audible teeth grinding · a bloated or firm abdomen · drooling · diarrhea · straining or crying out to urinate, or blood in the urine · panting, drooling or weakness in warm weather · lethargy · a pregnant sow that stops eating, or one not delivered by day 70
Within a few days Steady weight loss · hair loss, scabs or intense scratching · a new lump anywhere, particularly the neck · limping or stiffness · discharge from the eyes or nose, or repeated sneezing · red, swollen or sore soles · a wet chin · hair loss down both flanks of a sow

Finding a vet before you need one

  • Look for a vet who sees guinea pigs, often called cavy-savvy. General small-animal clinics are not always equipped for guinea pig problems, and a vet who mostly sees dogs and cats can get fasting, drug choices and recovery priorities backwards without meaning to.
  • Ask how many rabbit or guinea pig procedures the clinic handles in a typical month. An answer that is confident and specific tells you something before an emergency does.
  • Book an annual wellness exam even when everything looks fine, because guinea pigs hide illness well.
  • Write down a backup 24-hour emergency option too, somewhere you will find it at 11 at night. Questions to ask any clinic are on page {{P:vetvisit}}.

Normal numbers worth knowing

MeasureNormal
Weight, adult boar900 to 1,200 g (2 to 2.6 lb)
Weight, adult sow700 to 900 g (1.5 to 2 lb)
Breathing40 to 100 breaths a minute, quiet and through the nose

Have ready when you call

Its current and usual weight, when it last ate and what, when you last saw a normal dropping and what the droppings look like now, whether it is drinking, and any change in the last week such as a new food, new bedding, a new cage mate or a hot day.

Gastrointestinal stasis & a pig that stops eating

The emergency this species is most likely to have, and the one with the shortest fuse.

Gastrointestinal (GI) stasis means the gut, the stomach and intestines, slows or stops moving food through. It usually comes from inadequate fiber, dehydration, pain or stress. It is both a cause and an effect of not eating, which is what makes it a genuine emergency.

Signs, and the line

  • Reduced or absent droppings, or droppings gone small or dry.
  • Lethargy or reluctance to move, and a hunched, tense posture.
  • Audible teeth grinding, which signals pain.
  • A bloated or firm abdomen, and noticeable weight loss.
  • Reduced water intake, and labored breathing.
The clock Call a vet at 8 to 12 hours without eating or producing droppings. Once stasis sets in, it can become life-threatening within 24 to 48 hours. A low body temperature alongside these signs makes it more urgent still.

Why a guinea pig stops eating

 CauseNotes
1Dental diseaseThe most commonly cited cause. Overgrown back molars are painful and easy to miss.
2GI stasis itselfSlowed motility from too little fiber, dehydration, pain or stress.
3Illness or pain elsewhereRespiratory infection, bladder stones, arthritis or injury commonly suppress appetite.
4Environmental stressA new home, a moved cage, new cage mates or loud noise.
5Fear of new foodA new hay type or pellet brand turned down for being unfamiliar. Introduce changes gradually.
6Pregnancy toxemiaA risk in overweight or late-term pregnant sows.
7Vitamin C deficiencyDecreased appetite is one of the earliest signs, appearing within 2 to 3 weeks of a deficient diet.

What the vet does

Treatment is supportive: fluids for dehydration, assisted feeding with a critical care diet, pain relief and drugs that get the gut moving again, alongside a search for the underlying cause. A vet may send you home to give food and medicine by syringe, placed into the side of the mouth and past the loose tissue behind the front teeth. With prompt care many guinea pigs improve within 24 to 72 hours, though full recovery can take several days to a week.

Prevention is unlimited hay, fresh vegetables, constant water, supervised floor time, regular checkups, and a daily look at appetite and droppings (page {{P:droppings}}).

Dental disease

Every tooth grows for life, and the ones that go wrong are the ones you cannot see.

All of a guinea pig's teeth, the front incisors and the cheek teeth, grow throughout life. The cheek teeth at the back are where trouble usually starts: the molars can overgrow even when the front teeth look completely normal, which is part of why the condition catches owners off guard. When an adult's incisors suddenly look overgrown, the problem is often at the back of the mouth.

Signs, which are vague and that is the problem

EatingFrom mild pickiness to refusing food. Reluctance to eat despite interest in food. Difficulty chewing or swallowing.
Mouth and chinDrooling, or a wet, matted chin and neck (the "slobbers").
BodyWeight loss and reduced activity.
DroppingsUnusually small, because less is going in.

Why it happens

Teeth stay the right length by wearing against each other, and the wear comes from chewing hay. A diet where hay is an afterthought lets the teeth drift out of alignment, and uneven wear makes the misalignment worse. Prevention is a proper hay-based diet with unlimited hay, plus chew items. Low-quality diets, vitamin C deficiency, infection, trauma and inherited jaw faults are other causes.

Treatment, and what to expect

  • A vet has to examine the back teeth with special instruments, because the mouth is a small, narrow opening.
  • Treatment is reducing the crowns, the visible tooth height, with a dental drill under general anesthesia, and sometimes pulling a severely diseased tooth.
  • Severe or chronic cases can need the procedure repeated as often as every 4 to 6 weeks. Chronic cases mean repeat procedures, which is part of why prevention matters.
  • Ask whether the clinic does this routinely and has the equipment, since a general small-animal clinic may not.
Do not try this at home Reducing the teeth is done by a vet with a dental drill under general anesthesia. The prevention at home is unlimited hay.

Scurvy: stages, treatment & recovery

One of the more common preventable illnesses in pet guinea pigs, and easy to miss early.

Scurvy is vitamin C deficiency. Vitamin C is essential for making collagen, the structural protein that holds together skin, blood vessels, joints and connective tissue, so without a reliable daily source collagen production breaks down over days to weeks. The effects show up wherever collagen matters most: joints, gums, blood vessel walls and wound healing. It also weakens the immune system and disrupts normal bone and tooth development.

Recognizing it by stage

StageWhat you will see
EarlyA rough or puffed coat, mild lethargy, a slightly reduced appetite, subtle stiffness when moving. Appetite loss appears within 2 to 3 weeks of a deficient diet.
ProgressingSwollen, painful joints, reluctance to move or bear weight, weight loss and slow wound healing.
AdvancedBleeding gums, spontaneous bruising, joint hemorrhage and diarrhea. A genuine emergency at this stage.

The early signs are easy to miss or blame on something else. A guinea pig that seems slower or less interested in food than usual is worth a closer look at the diet before assuming it is nothing.

Treatment

A guinea pig showing signs of scurvy needs a vet visit, not a wait-and-see approach, especially once joint pain or gum bleeding is visible. Treatment combines vet-directed vitamin C by mouth or by injection, depending on severity, with an immediate correction of the diet.

Recovery

  • Early and moderate cases often improve within one to two weeks of proper treatment.
  • Advanced cases, with real joint damage or significant weight loss, take longer and can leave some lingering stiffness. That is the strongest argument for catching it early.

Why it happens in households that think they are feeding well

Most pellets are fortified with vitamin C when milled, but vitamin C is unstable, and much of it is gone within weeks even in an unopened bag. Relying on the pellet bag's label as the only source is one of the most common ways owners end up with a scurvy case despite feeding a complete diet.

Prevention is simple, just not optional Daily fresh vegetables, led by bell pepper, alongside a quality hay-based diet. With that, a guinea pig never has to rely on its pellets' vitamin C at all.

Respiratory infection & pneumonia

Urgent because of one anatomical fact.

Respiratory infections are caused by bacteria such as Bordetella bronchiseptica, Streptococcus and Staphylococcus species, and they are made more likely by poor husbandry, vitamin C deficiency and a young age. Pneumonia can end in sudden death.

Obligate nasal breathers Guinea pigs cannot breathe through the mouth as a fallback when the nose blocks. A guinea pig breathing with its mouth open needs a vet immediately.

Same-day signs

  • Sneezing, nasal or eye discharge.
  • Noisy or labored breathing, including wheezing and clicking.
  • Lethargy and a drop in appetite.

All of these warrant a same-day vet visit rather than wait-and-see.

Where it comes from

SourceWhat to do
Drafts and damp beddingKeep the enclosure out of drafts and away from cold windows, and keep bedding dry. Draft and damp are the realistic road to a cold-stressed, respiratory-compromised animal.
Cedar, raw pine and sawdustTheir oils and dust irritate the respiratory system over time. Use paper, fleece or kiln-dried pine.
Glass tanks and plastic binsPoor ventilation. Use a well-ventilated enclosure.
A rabbit in the same cageRabbits can carry Bordetella without looking ill and pass it on. Never house the two together.
Vitamin C deficiencyWeakens the immune system. Fresh vegetables daily.

Treatment

Antibiotics are the treatment, and the choice matters, because several common ones are dangerous to guinea pigs. The antibiotic page (page {{P:antibiotics}}) is the one to bring to the appointment. Treatment is not something to improvise at home.

A sneeze is not always an infection One sneeze after a dusty bedding change is not a call. Repeated sneezing, discharge, noisy breathing or any change in appetite or activity is.

Bladder stones & urinary problems

Common, painful, and the one area where the vegetable list has consequences.

Roughly 90 percent of stones in guinea pigs are calcium carbonate, which sets them apart from the struvite or oxalate stones common in dogs and cats. That difference matters: there is no prescription diet or medication that dissolves a calcium carbonate stone once it has formed. Surgical removal, a cystotomy, is generally the only way to resolve one. Small stones sometimes cause few or no symptoms at first, so by the time a stone is caught it may already need surgery. Males face the higher risk of a life-threatening blockage of the urethra.

Signs: same-day vet

  • Squeaking or crying out while urinating.
  • Straining or repeated attempts to urinate, and a hunched posture.
  • Blood in the urine, and urine staining or matted fur around the hindquarters.
  • Not eating. A stone can also cause a partial or complete blockage, which is a true emergency.

What raises the risk

FactorWhat to do
Alfalfa hay or alfalfa pellets in an adultThe biggest dietary lever. Grass hay for healthy adults, since alfalfa is only for young, pregnant or nursing guinea pigs.
The same high-calcium greens every dayKale, spinach and parsley add to the calcium load in the urine. Rotate them.
Low water intakeConcentrated urine makes crystals more likely. A bottle tends to increase daily intake over a bowl.
RhubarbHigh in oxalic acid and linked to bladder stones. Never feed it.

After a stone

Increasing water intake and reducing calcium are the cornerstones of managing a guinea pig prone to stones. Neither is a guarantee. Medical management on its own is often unrewarding and recurrence after surgery is common, so diet and water intake stay worth watching well after a stone has been removed.

If a secondary bladder infection is part of the picture, a vet may prescribe antibiotics. Guinea pigs tolerate only a narrow list of them safely, so ask which drug it is before the first dose (page {{P:antibiotics}}).

A reading on urine Urine runs from pale to reddish-brown depending on the diet, so color alone does not mean blood. Straining, crying out while urinating, and a stained rear end do mean a bladder problem.

Ovarian cysts, pregnancy & spaying

Very common in sows, and easy to mistake for a skin problem.

Ovarian cysts are extremely common in unspayed female guinea pigs, and the risk climbs with age. They are found in more than half of female guinea pigs. The most visible sign is hair loss, which gets mistaken for a skin problem rather than a reproductive one.

SignOvarian cystsSkin mites
Hair loss patternSymmetrical, both flanksPatchy, wherever scratching is worst
ItchingNoneIntense, constant
Skin underneathLooks normalRed, irritated, scabbed or raw
Other signsCrusty or enlarged nipples, abdominal swelling, lethargyFlaky skin, possible secondary infection

An older intact female losing hair evenly on both sides without scratching points toward cysts before mites. Functional cysts secrete hormones and are the ones behind the hair loss, nipple changes and sometimes aggressive or mounting behavior. Non-functional cysts do not, but can still cause discomfort by growing large enough to press on nearby organs, with a firm or swollen abdomen, lethargy or a drop in appetite.

Diagnosis and treatment

A vet may feel an enlarged ovary by palpating the abdomen, but ultrasound is the more reliable way to confirm a cyst. Spaying, an ovariohysterectomy, is the definitive treatment. It resolves the cysts and stops them recurring on remaining ovarian tissue. Surgery carries more risk in an older or already-debilitated guinea pig, so catching it early makes a real difference.

Should a healthy female be spayed?

That is the case for a preventive spay, and it is one exotics vets make for young, healthy sows. At that age the ovaries alone can be removed through a small incision in each flank, a shorter surgery than the traditional belly approach. It keeps the surgeon clear of the large cecum, the pouch of the gut where fiber ferments, and lowers the risk of pain, appetite loss and GI stasis afterward. An older female with cysts usually needs the full ovariohysterectomy, because the uterus is often diseased too. The time to raise it is while she is young and healthy. A sow kept only with other females needs no spay for contraception, so for her the decision rests on cyst risk alone.

Pregnancy and pregnancy toxemia

Pregnancy lasts 59 to 72 days, and a sow that has not delivered by day 70 needs a vet immediately. Exhaustion, persistent unproductive contractions or heavy bleeding during labor are also reasons to go straight to one. Pregnancy toxemia is the other serious risk, most likely in overweight sows or those not eating well, which is why a pregnant guinea pig that stops eating is an emergency and not a 12-hour wait.

Mites, lice, ringworm, lumps, bumblefoot & boar butt

The outside of the animal, and two of these can transfer to you.

Hair loss and itching

All of these cause hair loss, crusting or flaky skin, and each needs a vet to tell it apart and treat it.

CauseWhat it looks like
Mange mitesIntense itching, so severe that the scratching can look like a seizure. A guinea pig that suddenly thrashes or runs while scratching hard may have mites rather than a neurological problem. The skin underneath ends up crusty, raw and prone to infection.
LiceHair loss and crusty lesions, typically around the face, behind the ears or over the shoulders. A vet confirms them under a microscope.
RingwormA fungus, not a worm. Circular patches of hair loss ringed by inflamed, crusted skin, most often on the face, head and ears.
Catchable by people Ringworm and some mite infestations can spread to people in the household. Wear gloves and wash your hands when treating a guinea pig, and treat promptly rather than letting these run their course.

Bumblefoot (pododermatitis)

A painful foot infection linked to wire flooring or unsanitary bedding, and also to obesity, arthritis, overgrown nails and vitamin C deficiency. Mild cases are treated with topical care, bandages, pain relief and vitamin C while the cause is fixed. A deep infection leaves the animal lame and in pain. Check the soles during the weekly look-over: redness, swelling or a sore calls for a vet. Solid-floor housing with clean bedding goes a long way toward preventing it.

Lumps in the neck

Often an infected lymph node (cervical lymphadenitis) that follows a scrape inside the mouth. It shows as a large swelling on one side of the neck and is treated with surgery and antibiotics. Any new lump needs a vet, since abscesses, cysts and benign skin tumors all look alike from the outside.

Boar butt: an impacted rectal pouch

Males, older intact ones especially, have a pouch behind the anus where droppings can pack in. Left alone it causes smell, irritation and infection. Check older boars for it and ask the vet to show you how to clean it. Castration prevents it.

Antibiotics & enterotoxemia

Print this page. It is the one to hand over at a clinic you have not used before.

Guinea pigs ferment fiber in an enlarged cecum packed with bacteria, and those bacteria are overwhelmingly gram-positive, a broad group of bacteria. Certain antibiotics are built to target gram-positive bacteria. Given by mouth, they wipe out the gut flora, and a toxin-producing bacterium, Clostridioides difficile, overgrows and floods the body with toxin. The result is enterotoxemia, and it can be rapidly fatal. A dog-safe antibiotic can kill a guinea pig.

Ask before the first dose, by any route
Penicillins: penicillin, ampicillin, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanateShould not be given to guinea pigs
Lincosamides: clindamycin, lincomycinShould not be given to guinea pigs
Macrolides: erythromycin, tylosinUnsafe for guinea pigs
CephalosporinsSame drug family as the penicillins, with the same risk
Bacitracin, including topical ointment if lickedUnsafe for rodents
StreptomycinOn the do-not-give list for rodents
Injectables containing procaineGuinea pigs are sensitive to procaine penicillin

What a vet can usually use instead

Options generally usable in guinea pigs, because they have less effect on gram-positive bacteria, include trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, chloramphenicol and fluoroquinolones such as enrofloxacin. That is the shape of the conversation with your vet, not a prescription to self-select. The route matters too: oral dosing carries more risk than an injectable form of the same drug, because it passes directly through the gut where the flora lives.

What it looks like

Diarrhea, often watery and sometimes bloody or greenish-brown, loss of appetite, lethargy and a drop in body temperature. A guinea pig typically goes limp, dehydrated and cold, with death one to two days after the diarrhea begins. Any diarrhea, appetite loss or lethargy that starts during or shortly after an antibiotic course is a call-the-vet-now situation, not something to monitor overnight, and stopping the antibiotic is usually the first step the vet will want to take. Do not wait for the next scheduled dose.

Say this at the clinic

The question "Is this antibiotic one of the ones known to cause fatal diarrhea in this species, and have you prescribed it to a guinea pig before?" A vet experienced with exotics will have an immediate answer. One who does not may need to look it up, and that is a reasonable thing to ask before the prescription is filled. Ask which drug it is, and whether it works against gram-positive bacteria.

Record the name of every drug given, not just "antibiotic", on the vet log.

Anesthesia, travel & the vet visit

A guinea pig's gut and a dog's gut ask for opposite things around an operation.

Questions to ask any clinic

How many rabbit or guinea pig procedures do you handle in a typical month?
What is your fasting policy before anesthesia?
What is your plan if my guinea pig is not eating within a couple of hours of waking?
Can the cage mate stay alongside during a recovery or an overnight stay?
Who covers out of hours, and do they see guinea pigs?
Do you check every antibiotic against this species' risks?

Two things that run opposite to a dog or cat

Fasting. Guinea pigs cannot vomit, so the reason for fasting a dog does not apply. A long fast leaves a gut that runs on constant food sitting idle for no benefit. What vets do ask for is a short fast, a couple of hours at most, for a narrower reason: to make sure the mouth is clear of food before a mask or tube goes in. If a clinic tells you to withhold food overnight, ask them to confirm it for this species.

Appetite is the finish line. A guinea pig recovering from anything eats less than it should, and eating is the whole game. If it is not eating within a couple of hours of waking, the plan is hand feeding, a change in pain control or a referral to a vet who sees guinea pigs more often. The warning signs in the days after any procedure are the same appetite and droppings changes as on the red flags page.

The carrier and the trip

  • A hard-sided, ventilated carrier with room to lie down and turn around, not a wire cage. Wire bars flex under braking, and the open sides let a prey animal watch every passing car and person.
  • A hide spot built in: a towel over the carrier, or a cardboard box or small tunnel inside, plus a toy or clean bedding from home and a layer of hay to nibble on the way.
  • Strap it in with a seatbelt or wedge it in the footwell, with its side facing the direction of travel, so a hard brake pushes the animal against a wall.
  • Keep the car cool and ventilated, the carrier out of direct sun and never in a closed trunk, and never leave the animal in a parked car, even briefly.
  • Keep the trip short where you have a choice. A vet close to home can beat a highly rated clinic forty-five minutes away.
  • Take the cage mate when the visit allows it.

Reading droppings, urine & hydration

Two minutes a day, and the fastest read you get on how the gut is doing.

Healthy droppings are small, firm, well-formed teardrop or oval pellets in dark brown, and plentiful. Count them roughly every morning, and look at them before you clean up.

What you seeWhat it usually means
Fewer droppings, or noneThe gut is slowing. Call at 8 to 12 hours with none.
Small or dry droppingsNot enough is going in, often the first sign of dental disease or too little water.
Watery, unformed or foul-smelling stool, or a soiled rear endDiarrhea, not just a few soft droppings. A same-day vet call, especially with lethargy, a loss of appetite or a recent antibiotic.
Droppings that change after a new foodTrace it to the food. Add new foods one at a time, in small amounts, so you can.

Guinea pigs also eat some of their own droppings straight from the body, and that is normal.

Urine

Urine runs from pale to reddish-brown depending on the diet, so color alone does not mean blood. Straining, crying out while urinating, or a stained rear end do mean a bladder problem (page {{P:urinary}}).

Hydration

  • Fresh water must be available all day, in a bottle or a heavy bowl. A bottle tends to raise daily intake, which helps the bladder.
  • Check the nozzle runs, because a clogged bottle looks full.
  • Reduced water intake is a sign of stasis, and dehydration is part of what makes it worse.
  • In hot weather, offer water-rich foods such as cucumber and keep water in more than one container.
  • Nothing goes in the water: vitamin C breaks down quickly there and can make a guinea pig drink less.
What to tell the vet When a guinea pig seems off, the questions are when it last ate, when you last saw a normal dropping and what the droppings look like now. A weekly weight and a daily look at the droppings make those answers easy to give.
