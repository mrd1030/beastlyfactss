Dental disease & malocclusion

Every tooth grows for life, and the ones that go wrong are the ones you cannot see.

A guinea pig's incisors, premolars and molars all grow continuously. The front teeth are easy to look at and are rarely the whole story; the cheek teeth at the back are where malocclusion usually starts, and in this species the lower cheek teeth characteristically overgrow inward and bridge over the tongue, trapping it so the animal cannot swallow properly. An owner sees a pig that has gone off its food and looks otherwise normal.

Signs, which are nonspecific and that is the problem Anything from mild pickiness to a complete refusal to eat. Difficulty chewing or swallowing. Drooling, or a permanently wet chin and chest, which is the classic one. Dropping food after picking it up. Preferring soft foods and abandoning hay. Weight loss. Reduced activity. Unusually small droppings, because less is going in.

Why it happens

Teeth stay the right length by grinding against each other, and the grinding comes almost entirely from chewing hay sideways. A pig on a pellet-and-vegetable diet with hay as an afterthought does not do enough of that motion, and the teeth drift out of alignment. Once they are out of alignment they wear unevenly, which makes the misalignment worse. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why page {{P:hay}} is the prevention for this page. Vitamin C deficiency also affects the ligaments that hold the teeth in their sockets, which is a second route to the same place, and injury or a genetic jaw fault are the less common ones.

Treatment, and what to expect

Diagnosis needs a proper examination, which means an otoscope or endoscope and usually sedation: a conscious guinea pig's mouth is a narrow tube and the important teeth are at the far end of it. Treatment is crown reduction and correction of the occlusal angle under anesthesia, and severely diseased teeth are extracted. Chronic cases often need the procedure repeated every 4 to 6 weeks, which is why prevention is worth so much: this is a lifelong, recurring cost once it starts. Ask directly whether the practice has the equipment and does this routinely, because a general small-animal clinic often does not.

What you can actually check at home Look at the incisors weekly: the top pair should be shorter than the bottom pair and meet squarely, not splay sideways or cross over. Feel along the jawline for lumps, which can be root overgrowth pushing down through the bone. Then weigh the pig, because the real early-warning system for a problem you cannot see is a number that has started falling. Page {{P:droppings}}.
Never trim a guinea pig's teeth yourself Clipping incisors with nail clippers shatters the tooth lengthwise and can split it into the root, causing an abscess. It is done with a dental burr, under anesthesia, by a vet. Anything sold for home use should be ignored.

Scurvy: stages, treatment & recovery

The most common preventable illness in pet guinea pigs, and the easiest to miss early.

Page {{P:vitc}} covers the requirement. This page is what happens when it is not met. Without dietary vitamin C, collagen production breaks down over days to weeks, and collagen is structural: it holds together joints, gum tissue, blood vessel walls and healing wounds. The clinical picture follows exactly from that. The early signs are vague enough to be blamed on age, weather or mood, which is why this remains common in households that believe they are feeding well.

StageWhat you see
EarlyA rough or puffed coat that has lost its lie. Mild lethargy. Slightly reduced appetite, which appears within 2 to 3 weeks of a deficient diet. Subtle stiffness on standing up
ProgressingSwollen, painful joints. Reluctance to move or to bear weight, sometimes read as laziness. Difficulty picking food up. Weight loss. Wounds that will not close
AdvancedBleeding gums. Spontaneous bruising. Bleeding into the joints. Diarrhea. A genuine emergency at this point

The veterinary description of the presentation is anorexia or difficulty picking up food, a rough coat, lameness or partial paralysis, swollen joints, generalized pain, and delayed wound healing. A pig that hurts everywhere often looks, to an owner, like a pig that has become unfriendly.

Treatment

This is a vet visit, not a wait-and-see, and particularly so once joint pain or gum bleeding is visible. Treatment is three things together: correcting the husbandry that caused it, vitamin C given by mouth or by injection at a therapeutic dose the vet calculates, and pain relief, because scurvy hurts and a pig in pain does not eat. Assisted feeding is often needed alongside, because the animal has usually been eating poorly for a while by the time it is diagnosed. Page {{P:stasis}}.

Recovery

Early and moderate cases often improve within one to two weeks of proper treatment. Advanced cases with real joint damage take longer and can leave lasting stiffness, which is the strongest argument for catching this early rather than after the signs are unmistakable. Recovery is also the point at which to fix the underlying routine for good, because a pig that has had scurvy once is in a household where the daily vegetable habit did not hold.

Do not treat a suspected case yourself with a pet-shop supplement Therapeutic dosing is different from maintenance dosing and it is calculated on body weight. More importantly, the early signs of scurvy overlap almost exactly with the early signs of dental disease, arthritis and pain from a bladder stone, and treating a stone with vitamin C wastes the time the animal has. Get the diagnosis, then fix the diet.
The prevention, restated A quarter of a red bell pepper per pig per day, fresh, alongside unlimited hay and a small measured portion of in-date pellets. That is the entire intervention. Nothing on this page happens in a household that does that.

Respiratory infection & pneumonia

A leading cause of death in this species, and it moves fast.

Pneumonia is a leading cause of death in guinea pigs, especially in young ones, and it is usually bacterial. Bordetella bronchiseptica and Streptococcus pneumoniae are the two agents named most often. Respiratory infections here progress quickly, and there is one anatomical fact that makes them more dangerous than they would be in another animal: guinea pigs are obligate nasal breathers. They cannot switch to breathing through the mouth when the nose blocks. An open-mouthed guinea pig is in serious trouble.

Same-day signs Sneezing, especially repeatedly. Discharge from the nose or eyes, or crusting around either. Any noise on breathing: clicking, wheezing, rattling. Labored breathing, or the flanks visibly working. Open-mouth breathing. Loss of appetite and lethargy alongside any of the above. A pig sitting hunched with its neck extended.

Where it comes from

  • Drafts and damp. The commonest environmental contributor and the easiest to fix. Page {{P:climate}}.
  • Ammonia from bedding that is not changed often enough, concentrated at floor level where the animal breathes. This is also why glass tanks are a poor choice. Page {{P:setup}}.
  • Dust, from wood shavings, very dusty hay, or a cage in a busy room being swept around.
  • Rabbits. A rabbit can carry Bordetella without any signs at all and pass it to a guinea pig, in whom it causes fatal pneumonia. This is the concrete reason not to house the two species together, over and above the dietary and size mismatch. Page {{P:pairs}}.
  • Stress and crowding, particularly in a newly bought young pig that has been through a shop.
  • Vitamin C deficiency, which suppresses the immune response. Page {{P:scurvy}}.

Treatment

Antibiotics, chosen carefully, plus supportive care: fluids, warmth, assisted feeding, and sometimes oxygen and nebulization. The choice of antibiotic matters enormously in this species and page {{P:antibiotics}} is the page to take with you. Treatment courses are typically longer than an owner expects, and stopping early because the pig looks better is a common route to a relapse with a harder infection.

A sneeze is not always an infection, but it is always worth watching Hay dust, a dusty bedding change and a stray strand up the nose all produce an isolated sneeze in a completely healthy pig. What separates that from the start of something is repetition, discharge, and any change in appetite or activity. One sneeze on a Tuesday is nothing. Sneezing several times a day for two days is a call.

Bladder stones & urinary disease

Common, painful, and the one area where the daily vegetable list has consequences.

Guinea pigs form urinary stones readily, and the stones are typically calcium carbonate and related crystals. This is a species that excretes surplus calcium through the kidneys rather than storing or discarding it another way, so a persistently high calcium load in the diet ends up in the bladder as sediment and then as stones. Blood in the urine, hematuria, is a characteristic finding.

Signs Straining in the litter corner, often in a hunched position, sometimes for a long time with little produced. A squeak or cry while urinating, which is the sign owners remember. Blood in the urine or pink staining on the bedding. Frequent small urinations. A wet or stained rear end. Loss of appetite and lethargy alongside. A pig straining and producing nothing at all is a blockage and is an emergency now, because a fully obstructed bladder is fatal within hours to a day.

What raises the risk

FactorWhat to do
Alfalfa hay or alfalfa pellets in an adultThe single biggest dietary lever. Grass hay and timothy-based pellets for any healthy adult. Page {{P:hay}}
The same high-calcium greens every dayKale, spinach, parsley and chard are all good food. Rotate them rather than feeding one daily, which is what page {{P:veg}}'s tiers are for
Low water intakeDilute urine is the best protection there is. Offer both a bottle and a bowl, keep water fresh, and never flavor it. Page {{P:vitc}} explains why vitamin C in the water works against this
Hard tap waterWorth considering if a pig has already had stones. Ask your vet before switching to filtered water rather than doing it on your own
Obesity and inactivityA pig that moves less empties its bladder less completely. Floor time is a urinary intervention as well as an enrichment one. Page {{P:mistakes}}

Diagnosis and treatment

Urinalysis and an x-ray, since these stones are radiopaque and show clearly. A stone in the bladder is removed surgically; there is no dietary way to dissolve a formed calcium carbonate stone, and delaying only lets it grow. Sludge without a formed stone is sometimes managed with fluids, pain relief and dietary change. Recurrence is common, so a pig that has had one stone gets a permanently reviewed diet and periodic rechecks. Cystitis without stones also occurs and is treated with antibiotics chosen with page {{P:antibiotics}} in mind.

White chalky urine is not automatically a problem Guinea pig urine dries to a chalky white or cream residue on bedding, because of the calcium carbonate it normally carries. That in itself is normal. What is not normal is gritty sediment, pink or red staining, or a pig that is uncomfortable while producing it. Page {{P:droppings}} covers the daily version of this check.

Ovarian cysts & reproductive disease

Very common in sows, easy to see once you know the sign, and often silent until then.

Ovarian cysts

Ovarian cysts are the most common reproductive tract disorder in guinea pig sows, and they are common enough that any middle-aged or older intact sow with vague signs deserves to be assessed for them. Fluid-filled cysts develop on one or both ovaries, and because some of them produce hormones, the effects reach well beyond the abdomen.

The sign that gives it away Symmetrical hair loss along both flanks, running down each side of the body, with the skin underneath looking normal rather than inflamed or scabby. That pattern in a sow is ovarian cysts until proven otherwise, and it is what separates this from mites, which itch and damage the skin (page {{P:skin}}). Other signs are a swollen or pear-shaped abdomen, crusty or enlarged nipples, reduced appetite, lethargy, and a sow becoming unusually grumpy or aggressive with a cage mate.

Diagnosis is by ultrasound. Treatment options are hormonal, which shrinks some cysts and can be a reasonable choice for an older sow, draining, which usually refills, or ovariohysterectomy, which is definitive and also removes the risk of uterine disease later. The surgery is a real abdominal procedure and needs a vet who does guinea pig anesthesia routinely. Page {{P:antibiotics}}.

Pregnancy, and pregnancy toxemia

Guinea pig pregnancy is risky, which is the reason page {{P:sexing}} is written the way it is. Gestation runs 59 to 72 days, averaging 68, and the young are born fully furred with their eyes open and start eating solid food within hours. The two serious complications are the fused pubic symphysis in a sow bred for the first time after about 6 to 9 months, covered on page {{P:sexing}}, and pregnancy toxemia.

Pregnancy toxemia A metabolic collapse in late pregnancy or just after birth, and it is potentially fatal. The risk is highest in overweight sows and in late-term pregnancy, and it can be triggered by any period of not eating, by stress, or by a sudden change in food. It presents as sudden appetite loss and lethargy in a pregnant or newly delivered sow, and it moves quickly. Any pregnant sow that goes off her food is an emergency, at once, not at 8 to 12 hours.

Mammary and other reproductive disease

Mammary tumors occur in both sexes, boars included, and are found as a firm lump near a nipple. They are frequently treatable when caught early and removed, so the weekly hands-on check on page {{P:droppings}} is the thing that catches them. Uterine and cervical disease presents most often as bleeding from the vulva outside of any normal cycle, which is always a vet visit.

Mites, lice, ringworm, lumps & bumblefoot

The outside of the animal, and two of these transfer to you.

Mange mites

Trixacarus caviae is a burrowing mange mite and it causes severe itching. The veterinary description is worth quoting because owners routinely misread it: the scratching can be so intense that the animal appears to be having a seizure. A guinea pig that suddenly runs in circles, throws itself over, or shrieks and convulses is very often a mite case rather than a neurological one, and it is one of the most treatable serious-looking things in this book. Hair loss, scabbing and thickened crusted skin along the back and flanks come with it. Mites are often latent in a pig for a long time and flare when the animal is stressed, pregnant, or ill.

Treatment Ivermectin at 0.4 mg/kg by injection under the skin, repeated every 10 to 14 days for four treatments, or selamectin at 15 mg/kg applied to the skin once, are both effective. Treat every pig in the household, not only the one showing signs, and clean and replace bedding and porous cage furniture during treatment. Pain relief matters too: a badly affected pig hurts.

Lice

Guinea pig lice are host-specific, do not live on people, and cause milder disease than mites. You may see them as tiny moving specks along the neck and behind the ears. Heavy infestations produce hair loss and crusted lesions. Treated similarly, and again treat everyone in the cage.

Ringworm

Not a worm but a fungal skin infection, usually Trichophyton mentagrophytes or T. benhamiae. It appears as circular patches of hair loss with flaky, crusty skin, classically starting around the face, nose and ears and spreading. It transmits from guinea pigs to humans, so it is worth diagnosing properly rather than guessing, and worth gloves while treating. Treatment is antifungal, topical or oral depending on extent, together with a thorough environmental clean, and it takes weeks.

Lumps in the neck

Cervical lymphadenitis produces large, one-sided swellings in the neck, sometimes called lumps, from infected lymph nodes. Treatment is surgical removal of the affected nodes with systemic antibiotics. Any firm lump anywhere on a guinea pig warrants an appointment: abscesses, cysts, lipomas and tumors all present as a lump and telling them apart is a needle and a microscope, not a look.

Bumblefoot, or pododermatitis Inflammation and ulceration of the soles, showing as redness, callus-like swellings, open sores, and eventually cellulitis and deep infection. It is caused by pressure and contamination: wire flooring, damp or dirty bedding, obesity, and hard surfaces with nothing soft to stand on. Early cases are treated with topical care, bandaging, pain relief, vitamin C, and correcting the husbandry that caused it. Advanced cases can reach the bone and are very hard to resolve, which makes the solid clean floor on page {{P:cage}} a genuinely medical requirement rather than a comfort one. Check the soles during the weekly handling check.

Antibiotics, anesthesia & the vet conversation

Print this page. It is the one to hand over at a clinic you have not used before.

A guinea pig's gut flora is predominantly Gram-positive, and the fermentation it performs is what digests the animal's food. Antibiotics that act preferentially against Gram-positive organisms wipe out that flora and allow toxin-producing bacteria, particularly Clostridium difficile, to overgrow. The result is antibiotic-associated enterotoxemia, and it kills. This is not a rare idiosyncratic reaction: it is a predictable consequence of the wrong drug in this species, and it is the single most important thing a guinea pig owner can know about veterinary care.

Antibiotics implicated in enterotoxemia in guinea pigs Penicillin, ampicillin, streptomycin, clindamycin, lincomycin, spiramycin, chlortetracycline (aureomycin), erythromycin and bacitracin have all been implicated, given by mouth or by injection. Narrow-spectrum drugs aimed at Gram-positive organisms are the highest risk. If a prescription for your guinea pig is on this list, ask about it before the first dose. Ask, do not refuse: there are situations where a vet chooses one of these deliberately and manages the risk. What should never happen is it being prescribed without anyone noticing the species.

Any antibiotic can disturb the flora, so all of them are used with caution here, and enterotoxemia is also triggered by stress, an abrupt diet change, and gastrointestinal stasis itself. Signs are diarrhea, a drop in appetite, lethargy and collapse, appearing during or shortly after a course, and it is an emergency.

Questions worth asking, at any clinic

  • How many guinea pigs does the practice see in a month?
  • Do you anesthetize guinea pigs routinely, and for dentals specifically?
  • Do you have the equipment to examine the cheek teeth properly?
  • Who is on out-of-hours, and do they see exotics?
  • What is your policy on fasting before anesthesia?
  • Can I bring a cage mate to the appointment?
Two things that are different from a dog or a cat Guinea pigs are not fasted before anesthesia. They cannot vomit, so the reason for fasting a dog does not apply, and a herbivore that stops eating before surgery arrives at the operating table with a slowing gut. A short period to clear food from the mouth is all that is normally involved. If a practice tells you to withhold food overnight, ask them to confirm that against exotic-species guidance.
Bring the cage mate. Guinea pigs are separated badly, both by the animal that leaves and the one left behind, and a pig recovering alone in a strange room eats less. Most exotics practices will house a companion alongside, and the food intake after surgery is usually what determines the outcome.
And the everyday version A guinea pig recovering from anything eats less than it should, and eating is the whole game in this species. Ask for pain relief as a matter of course rather than only when the animal looks sore, ask about assisted feeding before you leave, and take a syringe and recovery formula home with you.

Droppings, weight & body condition

Two minutes a day and five minutes a week, and they catch nearly everything on the last ten pages.

Droppings

Normal guinea pig droppings are firm, oval, dark brown, uniform in size, and plentiful: a healthy pig produces a great many every day. They are the fastest read you get on how the gut is doing, and reading them is free.

What you seeWhat it usually means
Fewer than usual, or noneThe gut is slowing. Page {{P:stasis}}. At 8 to 12 hours with none, call
Small, dry, hard, or pointed at one endNot enough is going in, or not enough water. Often the first sign of dental disease
Soft, clumped or stuck togetherToo much fresh food or a sudden diet change, most often. Pull back the vegetables for a day and watch
Truly liquid, or a soiled wet rearDiarrhea. Not the same as soft droppings, and a same-day call
Slimy, mucus-covered, or with bloodVet, today
Chalky white residue in the urine patchesNormal calcium carbonate. Gritty sediment or pink staining is not. Page {{P:urinary}}

A guinea pig also eats some of its droppings directly from the vent. This is normal and necessary: the caecum produces a softer, nutrient-rich dropping that the animal reingests to absorb what a single pass through the gut misses. An obese, arthritic or long-coated pig that physically cannot reach is missing part of its nutrition, and that is worth a vet conversation rather than a shrug.

Weight, weekly

A digital kitchen scale reading in grams is the most useful diagnostic tool an owner of this species can own. Weigh every pig on the same day each week, at the same time, and write it down.

ReadingWhat to do
Stable within about 30 g week to weekNormal fluctuation. Carry on
Down 30 to 50 g, or about 5%Watch closely, check hay intake and droppings, weigh again in 2 to 3 days
Down more than 50 g, or falling three weeks runningVet appointment. Dental disease and megabacteria-style quiet losses show here first
Climbing steadilyToo many pellets or too much fruit. Check the portion against page {{P:pellets}}
The weekly hands-on check, done while the pig is on the scale Feel along the spine and hips: in good condition they are palpable but not sharp, and the body reads as a rounded loaf rather than a pear or a ridge. Then check, in order: eyes and nose clear, teeth meeting squarely, ears clean, coat free of scabs and bald patches, feet with no redness or swelling on the soles, rear end clean and dry, and a run of both hands over the body and belly feeling for lumps. Boars over two get the rectal pouch check from page {{P:grooming}}. Five minutes, once a week, catches most of section three early.
