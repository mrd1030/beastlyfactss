Enclosure size, type & where it goes

Measured in square feet of floor, because guinea pigs run along the ground and do not climb or jump well.
GroupMinimum floor areaNotes
One guinea pig7.5 sq ftRoughly 30 by 36 in (76 by 91 cm). Possible, but not the recommended way to keep the species.
A pair10.5 sq ftThe number to buy against, since a pair is the recommended minimum.
A larger groupAdd roughly 2 to 4 sq ftFor each additional guinea pig beyond the first.

Bigger is always better here. Guinea pigs are active and use the room they are given, so the minimum is where to start, not a target to stop at. Why a pair is the starting point is on page {{P:pairs}}.

The floor and the sides

  • Solid floor, never wire. Wire flooring is a common cause of bumblefoot, a painful infection of the soles of the feet.
  • Sides at least 12 in (30 cm) high. Height is not floor space, and a species that does not climb or jump well gets nothing from it.
  • Good ventilation. Glass tanks and plastic bins both ventilate poorly, so they are out.

What to buy

A C&C enclosure, short for cubes and corrugated plastic, is built from wire storage cubes joined into a pen on a corrugated plastic base. It gives real floor area for the price and can be extended as a group grows. A ready-made single-level habitat of about 8 sq ft, at around $100 to $109, covers a single guinea pig. A pair needs a larger modular or C&C habitat, which runs closer to $140. The enclosure is the biggest line in the setup budget, and it is worth getting the size right the first time rather than upgrading later.

Where it goes

  • Out of direct sun and away from drafts and cold windows. The temperature page covers why.
  • Out of reach of household cats and dogs.
  • Somewhere it can see the room and hear quiet household life. An enclosure tucked away in a spare room gives a guinea pig less to take in, as long as a hide lets it get away from the noise when the family gets loud.
  • Never shared with a rabbit, even for company. A rabbit that looks healthy can carry bacteria that make a guinea pig ill.
Buy once The common mistake is a cage that is technically big enough for one animal and too small for two. Measure the floor, not the label, and plan for the pair.

Temperature, humidity & heat stress

This is a health page, not a comfort page: a guinea pig has no sweat glands and cannot cool itself.

Humidity30 to 60%

The room is the enclosure's whole climate system: there is no lamp, no heater and no dial. Put a digital thermometer and humidity gauge in the room, out of direct sun, and check it. A room that feels merely warm to you can already be dangerous, and the comfortable ceiling sits only a few degrees below the point where trouble starts. Keep the enclosure out of direct sun and away from drafts.

Signs of heat stress, in order

  1. Rapid or open-mouth breathing. Faster, shallower breaths than normal, sometimes visible as panting.
  2. Heavy drooling, sometimes leaving the fur around the mouth wet, with a racing heart.
  3. Weakness and reluctance to move, with pale gums.
  4. Severe signs: tremors, convulsions, collapse, coma and death if untreated.

Do not wait for all four. A guinea pig can deteriorate quickly once heat stress starts, so the first sign, not the fourth, is the point to act.

Do, at once

  • Move it to the coolest room, with good airflow and out of direct sun.
  • Offer cool, not ice-cold, water, and do not force it to drink.
  • Cool it gradually with a cool damp towel or a light wipe of cool water on the ears and feet.
  • Get to a vet immediately. A guinea pig that looks calmer can still relapse.

Do not

  • Do not plunge it into cold or ice water: that can send a stressed small animal into shock.
  • Do not assume it will recover on its own once it looks calmer.
  • Do not leave it in a parked car, even briefly, or in a carrier in the sun.

Keeping the room cool

  • Move the enclosure to the coolest room, with air conditioning or at least moving air.
  • Freeze water bottles and wrap them in a towel, so a guinea pig can choose to lie against one. Direct ice is too cold.
  • Keep unlimited fresh water in more than one container, and offer water-rich foods such as cucumber.

Cold and drafts

A guinea pig handles a cool room reasonably well as long as the drop is gradual. The cold risk is a draft or damp bedding, which lose heat far faster than still, dry air at the same reading and can leave a cold-stressed animal with a respiratory infection. Extra hay gives it something to burrow into.

Bedding, hides & cage layout

What goes on the floor, what goes on top of it, and where.

Bedding

OptionVerdictNotes
Paper-based beddingUseRecycled paper products are free of loose fibers. The usual first choice.
Fleece linersUseA washable option that also avoids loose fibers.
Kiln-dried pineAcceptableThe one wood product generally accepted. Drying removes most of the oils.
Cedar, raw pine, sawdustNeverThe oils and dust irritate the respiratory system over time.

Wood shavings and chips are best avoided. Whatever the bedding, it has to stay dry: damp bedding is a cold risk and a foot risk, and unsanitary bedding is linked to bumblefoot.

Hides are not decoration

Constant access to a secure hiding place is close to a baseline need. A guinea pig is a prey animal, and one with nowhere to retreat stays on alert even in a calm room. Secure hiding options are linked to lower stress indicators.

  • PVC (polyvinyl chloride) pipe, hide huts and fleece tunnels all work, and a seagrass hideout or tunnel adds something to chew in the same footprint.
  • In a cage with more than one guinea pig, provide multiple hides. A single hide in a shared space tends to become something one animal guards, and a lower-ranking animal needs somewhere to go.
  • Put out more than one of anything worth having, such as a tunnel, for the same reason.

Layout

Floor

Solid, with a thick layer of dry bedding. Spot-clean soiled areas every day.

Hay

Unlimited, in a rack or a pile, and also scattered across the enclosure so a shy guinea pig can always eat somewhere.

Hides

More than one, in different spots, so nobody is shut out of cover.

Water and food

A bottle with a working nozzle, or a heavy bowl, and a dish for pellets. A foraging mat or puzzle takes the place of a vegetable bowl.

Chewing is a need A guinea pig's teeth grow for life, so gnawing is physical as well as natural. Untreated wood chews, hay-based chew sticks and cardboard tunnels all hold up. Skip cotton rope and other fabric fibers, which guinea pigs chew and swallow and which can block the gut.

Cleaning, water & hygiene

Guinea pigs are messy, so cleaning is a daily habit, not a monthly event.
How oftenWhat
Every daySpot-clean soiled bedding. Change the water and check that the bottle nozzle runs. Pull uneaten fresh vegetables after 30 minutes to a couple of hours, so they do not spoil.
OftenChange all of the bedding, before it stays wet.
RegularlyClean and disinfect water bottles. Some guinea pigs push a slurry of pellets up into the sipper tube, which makes frequent cleaning more important for them.

Why it matters

  • Feet. Dirty or damp bedding is one of the causes of bumblefoot, along with wire flooring.
  • Lungs. A damp, drafty enclosure is a more realistic road to a respiratory infection than a cool room.
  • Gut. Fresh vegetables left out go off, which is why uneaten ones come out after a couple of hours at most.

Water

Fresh water has to be available all day, in a sipper bottle or a heavy bowl. A bottle is the better default, because guinea pigs drink more from one, and more water helps the urinary tract. Whichever you use, change the water daily and check the nozzle often.

Never add vitamin C to the water It breaks down fast once dissolved, often losing much of its potency within hours, and it changes the taste, which can make a guinea pig drink less. A guinea pig that drinks less is at greater risk of bladder stones. The right ways to supply vitamin C are on page {{P:vitc}}.

Hygiene

  • Wear gloves and wash your hands when treating a guinea pig for ringworm, because it can spread to people. Some mite infestations can spread to the household too.
  • Keep food and water dishes clean, and clean and disinfect bottles regularly.
