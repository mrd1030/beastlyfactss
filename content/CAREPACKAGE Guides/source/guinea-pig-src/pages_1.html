Why this package exists A guinea pig is sold as a starter pet and priced like one, and almost every part of that framing is wrong. It needs more floor space than a rabbit hutch provides, a companion of its own species, a daily fresh vitamin C source, a room that stays cool, and an exotics vet who knows which antibiotics are dangerous to it. Every figure here is cross-checked against veterinary sources, listed on page {{P:sources}}, and every temperature and dimension is given in both imperial and metric.
Not a substitute for A cavy-savvy exotics veterinarian. This is a husbandry reference for day-to-day care. Anything urgent is a professional call, not a page in this book, and the window for urgency in this species is short: a guinea pig that has stopped eating is on a clock measured in hours. Find a vet before you need one and write the number on page {{P:emergency}}.

Cost overview

Initial setup, for a pair

$265 to $515 for the enclosure and equipment, plus $20 to $120 for the pigs themselves and $100 to $200 for two first vet exams. Page {{P:budget}}.

Monthly ongoing, a pair

$80 to $190. Hay, fresh vegetables and bedding are the three big lines, and they are consumed constantly rather than bought once.

The number that surprises people Guinea pigs are cheap to acquire and expensive to run. The animal is the smallest line in the budget and the hay is one of the largest, because a guinea pig eats roughly its own body size in hay every day and it never stops. Budget for two animals from the start rather than adding a second later and being surprised by the jump.

Enclosure size, shape & flooring

Measured in square feet of unobstructed floor, not in the label on the box.

Guinea pigs are ground animals. They do not climb, they do not use height, and they run in short bursts along the floor, so the only measurement that means anything is unobstructed floor area. The Merck Veterinary Manual gives a minimum of 7.5 sq ft (0.7 m²) for one guinea pig, plus roughly 2 to 4 sq ft for each additional animal. The welfare figure in wide use for a bonded pair is 10.5 sq ft (1 m²), and since a pair is the normal way to keep this species, that is the number to buy against.

GroupFloor areaRoughly
One guinea pig7.5 sq ft (0.7 m²)30×36 in (76×91 cm)
A bonded pair10.5 sq ft (1 m²)30×50 in (76×127 cm)
Three13 sq ft (1.2 m²)30×62 in (76×158 cm)
Four16 sq ft (1.5 m²)30×76 in (76×193 cm)
What does not work, and why Wire or mesh flooring. A solid floor is essential, because wire causes pododermatitis and foot and limb injuries. This is not a comfort preference. Page {{P:skin}}.
Glass aquariums and plastic bins. Not recommended: ventilation is poor and ammonia from urine builds up at floor level, where the animal breathes.
Multi-level cages with ramps. An upper shelf is not floor space for a species that does not climb, and a ramp is a fall risk for an animal with a fragile spine.
Anything sold as a "guinea pig starter cage." Almost all of them are under 4 sq ft. They are hamster cages with a different picture on the box.

What to buy instead

A C&C enclosure, built from wire storage cubes and a corrugated plastic base, is what most rescues and welfare organizations recommend, because it gives real square footage for the price of a small commercial cage and it can be extended as the group grows. A large commercial habitat around 8 sq ft is the ready-made alternative. Either way the sides need to be at least 12 in (30 cm) high, which is enough: a guinea pig does not jump, and a lid is only needed where a cat or a dog lives in the house.

Where it goes

  • A room the household uses. Guinea pigs are social with people as well as with each other, and a garage or spare room is isolation.
  • Out of direct sun and off a draft. Both are on page {{P:climate}}, and the sun one is genuinely dangerous.
  • Off the floor if there is a dog or a cat, and out of the path of a vacuum cleaner or a slamming door. A prey animal reads all of it.
  • Somewhere you can carry hay to and bedding from twice a week without resentment. The setup you can actually maintain is better than the ideal one you cannot.
  • Not outdoors, in most climates. An outdoor hutch exposes the animals to temperature swings, predators and flies, all of which this species handles badly.

Bedding, hides & cage layout

What goes on the floor, what goes on top of it, and where.

Bedding

Bedding should be recycled paper product, fleece, or another material free of loose fibers and strings. Wood shavings and chips should be avoided, because the aromatic oils are associated with respiratory irritation and disease; cedar is the worst and raw pine is not much better. Kiln-dried pine is the one wood product that is generally considered acceptable, since the drying drives off most of the oils, but paper and fleece avoid the question entirely.

OptionTrade-off
Paper beddingBest odor control and the most forgiving if you cannot clean often. Ongoing cost, and it is the largest single consumable after hay
Fleece linersCheapest over a year and comfortable underfoot. Needs washing every 2 to 3 days, needs an absorbent layer underneath, and needs sweeping daily or it holds moisture against the feet
Kiln-dried pineAcceptable, cheap, and dusty. Avoid entirely if any pig has a respiratory history
Cedar, raw pine, sawdustNot acceptable at any price

Hides, and why there is always one spare

A hide is not decoration. A prey animal with nowhere to retreat stays on alert even in a completely calm room, and research on captive guinea pig welfare links secure hiding options directly to lower stress indicators. Provide one hide per guinea pig plus one extra, and give every hide two openings. A single hide in a shared cage becomes something one animal guards rather than something both can use, and a one-door tunnel becomes a place a lower-ranking pig gets cornered.

Water: offer both a bottle and a bowl Bottles stay clean and are easy to read for volume; bowls are how a guinea pig naturally drinks and let a sick animal drink without effort. Offering both removes the failure mode where a blocked or airlocked bottle goes unnoticed for a day. Check the bottle actually runs every morning by touching the ball, and wash both weekly.

Temperature, heat stress & household climate

This is a health page, not a comfort page. Heat kills guinea pigs.

Guinea pigs have no sweat glands, so they cannot lose heat by sweating, and a coated animal in a cage cannot move somewhere cooler. Merck gives the range as 65 to 79°F (18 to 26°C) at 30 to 60% humidity, and VCA puts the ceiling for a rodent enclosure at 80°F (27°C). Treat 80°F as the line. The RSPCA adds the shape either side of it: heat stress can begin as low as 75°F (24°C) and is most commonly seen above 82°F (28°C), and it puts the genuinely comfortable band narrower still, at 64 to 73°F (18 to 23°C). A cheap digital thermometer and hygrometer next to the cage removes all the guesswork, and it is the single most useful $15 item in this book.

Signs of heat stress, in order Short, rapid breathing or panting. Drooling or slobbering. Weakness and reluctance to move. Pale gums. Then convulsions, collapse and death. Before any of that, the Blue Cross point: a pig lying stretched flat with its back feet trailing behind it is telling you it is too warm and wants help cooling down. It is not relaxing.
If you find a pig overheating Move it somewhere cool immediately and dampen the fur with cool, not cold and not iced, water. Cold water can send a small, already stressed animal into shock, and direct contact with ice burns thin skin, which is why a frozen bottle always goes in a towel. Then go to the vet, without waiting to see whether it improves: the RSPCA describes treatment as supportive, oxygen and intravenous fluids while cooling the patient, and a pig that looks recovered can still be in trouble internally.

Keeping a room cool without air conditioning

  • Move the cage to the coolest room in the house, usually a north-facing ground-floor one.
  • Close curtains and blinds on the sunny side in the morning, before the room heats.
  • Ceramic or stone tiles in the cage. They pull heat out of an animal lying on them and cost nothing.
  • Frozen water bottles wrapped in a towel, placed so the pigs can choose to lie against them or not.
  • A fan moving air across the room, never blowing directly at the cage.
  • Extra water, in more than one container, and water-heavy foods: cucumber, romaine, melon and apple.

Cold and drafts

Cold is much less dangerous than heat for a healthy adult in a normal house, but drafts are a genuine risk factor for respiratory infection, which is a leading cause of death in this species. Keep the cage off cold exterior walls and away from doors, windows that get opened, and air-conditioning vents. Extra hay and a covered hide are what a pig actually uses to stay warm. Never use a heat pad inside the cage: a guinea pig cannot reliably move away from one and it will chew the cord.

Light and routine Guinea pigs need no special lighting, but they do need a predictable day. They are crepuscular by preference, most active in the morning and evening, and they sleep in short bursts rather than one long block, which means a cage in a room that is dark and silent for sixteen hours is as unnatural as one lit all night. A normal household rhythm suits them.
