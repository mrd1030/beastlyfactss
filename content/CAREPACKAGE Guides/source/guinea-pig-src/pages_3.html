Daily vegetables & greens

One to two cups per pig per day, rotated, and led by bell pepper.

Fresh produce is where the vitamin C actually comes from, and the veterinary guidance is 1 to 2 cups of fresh produce per guinea pig per day, offered as one or two servings and rotated across several vegetables rather than the same thing every day. Rotation matters for two reasons: it spreads the calcium and oxalate load, which is what the bladder pages care about, and it keeps a species prone to fixed food preferences willing to try things.

TierFoods
Daily, the vitamin C coreBell pepper, red, yellow or orange for preference (green is fine but lower). Ripe tomato in small amounts. Romaine and other dark leaf lettuces. Cucumber, mostly water but useful in hot weather

How to serve it

  • Wash everything. Guinea pigs are small and eat a lot relative to their weight, so pesticide residue matters more than it would for a person.
  • Chop into pieces a pig can hold, and scatter or hide it rather than putting it in one dish. Page {{P:mistakes}}.
  • Serve at room temperature. Straight from the fridge is a genuine cause of digestive upset in a small herbivore.
  • Take uneaten produce out after 30 minutes to a couple of hours, sooner in a warm room. Wilted greens are one thing; spoiled greens are a gut problem.
  • Introduce anything new gradually, one item at a time, in a small amount. Page {{P:hay}} explains why this species reacts badly to abrupt change.

Grass, and a note about lawns

Fresh grass is excellent food and pigs love it, but only from ground that has never been treated with pesticide, herbicide or fertilizer, and never from anywhere dogs, cats or wild animals use. Pick it by hand rather than mowing: cut grass from a mower heats and begins to ferment within minutes, which is a real cause of bloat. Introduce grass slowly, over a week or two, for a pig that has not had it before.

A weekly prep, so the daily version actually happens Wash and chop a week's worth of the sturdier vegetables on one evening and keep them in a sealed box in the fridge, then take out and warm one portion per pig each morning. Leafy greens go in fresh. The households where fresh food quietly stops after month one are the ones where it needed preparing from scratch every day.

Pellets, fruit, treats & the never-feed list

The smallest part of the diet, and the part with the real dangers in it.

Pellets

Pellets are a supplement to hay, not a replacement, and the portion is small: the veterinary figure is 1 to 2 teaspoons per guinea pig per day, and popular care guides commonly give about 1/8 cup, roughly 2 tablespoons. Those disagree by a factor of several, and the honest reading is that a guinea pig eating plenty of hay and 1 to 2 cups of fresh produce needs the smaller amount, while the larger figure suits a growing, pregnant or underweight animal. Start at 1 to 2 teaspoons and let hay intake and body condition decide. A pig eating all its pellets and little hay is being given too many.

  • Guinea-pig pellets specifically. Rabbit and other rodent pellets contain no added vitamin C, because those animals make their own.
  • Timothy-based, with stabilized vitamin C on the label, and used within 90 days of manufacture. Page {{P:vitc}}.
  • Plain uniform pellets only. Anything with seeds, nuts, corn, dried fruit or colored pieces is a muesli mix, and pigs selectively eat the sweet parts and leave the rest.
  • Alfalfa-based pellets belong with young, pregnant or nursing pigs, not healthy adults. Page {{P:hay}}.

Fruit and treats

Treats of all kinds, fruit included, stay under about 10% of the diet, which for an animal this size means a piece the size of a fingernail rather than a slice. Small amounts of orange, kiwi, apple, pear, strawberry, blueberry or melon are fine a couple of times a week, with pits and seeds removed. There is no commercial treat that is better than a piece of bell pepper, and the yogurt drops, honey sticks and seed bars on the pet shop shelf are actively worse than nothing.

Never feed Avocado, linked to heart muscle damage.
Onion, garlic, chives and leeks, which can cause destruction of red blood cells, with signs sometimes taking 3 to 5 days to appear.
Rhubarb, high in oxalic acid and linked to bladder stones. Chocolate.
Raw potato, unripe tomato, and the leaves or plants of either. Mushrooms. Fruit pits and seeds.
Meat, eggs and dairy. A guinea pig is a strict herbivore; none of it belongs in the cage.
Iceberg lettuce. No nutrition, and enough water to loosen droppings.
Not toxic, still wrong Oats, seeds, nuts, peanut butter, corn, bread, pasta and dry cereal are all too high in starch or fat. The problem is not poisoning, it is that a guinea pig will pick these over hay and pellets every time, which produces nutrient gaps and obesity in an animal whose whole digestive system depends on eating fiber all day. Bought treat sticks are usually made of exactly these things glued together with honey.

Water

Fresh water available at all times, changed daily, with the bottle and bowl washed weekly to stop a biofilm forming. Check a bottle by pressing the ball every morning rather than by looking at the level: an airlocked bottle looks full. Page {{P:setup}} explains why offering both a bottle and a bowl is worth the extra minute.

Grooming, nails, coats & boar cleaning

Three routine jobs, and one that only applies to older boars.

Nails

Guinea pig nails grow continuously and there is nothing in a cage that wears them down, so trimming is a permanent part of ownership: Lafeber's interval is every 6 to 8 weeks, sooner for a house pig on soft bedding. Take only the tip, clear of the pink quick visible through a pale nail. On a dark nail, hold a torch behind it and the quick shows against the light; failing that, take a very small amount at a time and stop as the cut surface changes texture. Keep styptic powder or cornstarch out and open before you start. Front nails grow faster than back ones and curl if left, and a curled nail grows into the pad.

Coats

Unlike a rabbit, a guinea pig has no seasonal molt: shedding runs low and continuous all year, so loose fur is the baseline.

CoatGrooming
Short, smooth (American)Lafeber's floor for a short coat is every few days, not weekly. A soft brush, and essentially maintenance-free beyond that
Rosetted (Abyssinian)Every few days. Debris collects in the whorls, so check them rather than just brushing over them
Short and dense (Teddy, Rex)Every few days. The coarse coat holds bedding; skin underneath is worth checking for dryness
Long (Peruvian, Silkie, Sheltie, Texel)Daily brushing, and the rear kept trimmed short permanently. A long coat over the rear mats with urine and droppings and is a common route to skin infection and flystrike. Many owners keep long-haired pigs in a permanent short clip, a kindness rather than a compromise
Baths, rarely and never routinely Merck's position: a bath is rarely needed unless the pig is visibly soiled, and when one is necessary the water stays shallow enough that she only stands in it, belly level, never submerged. Wash a soiled rear end, or the whole animal on a vet's prescription. Small-animal shampoo, no human product, dried thoroughly in a warm draft-free room. A damp guinea pig chills fast, and chilling is a respiratory risk. Page {{P:respiratory}}.

Boar cleaning

Male guinea pigs have a rectal pouch, a pocket just behind the anus, and in older intact boars it commonly becomes impacted with softened droppings he can no longer clear. Owners call it boar butt. It smells, causes local irritation and infection, and has been linked to urinary tract infections. Check any boar over about two weekly, tipping him gently back on your lap to look. If the pouch is full, clean it with a cotton swab and a little mineral oil or a vet-recommended cleanser, slowly, stopping if he objects strongly. Ask your vet to show you once; it is easier demonstrated than described.

The grease gland is separate, and both sexes have one, at the base of the spine just above the tail area. It is more active in boars, and it gets greasy, matted and smelly. Lafeber's lighter option for light buildup is a cotton swab and a mild cleaning agent. For a gland that has caked, coconut oil worked in to loosen the wax, then a small-animal shampoo and a thorough rinse. It is cosmetic rather than medical unless the skin underneath is inflamed, in which case page {{P:skin}} applies.

Sexing, neutering & the breeding deadline

Mis-sexing is common, and the consequences arrive fast.

How to sex a guinea pig

Sit down, rest the pig on your lap with its back against you so it is upright and supported, and look at the genital area with the animal's rear tipped gently toward you. Press very lightly just above the opening with a fingertip.

  • Boar: gentle pressure above the opening extrudes the penis, and there is a distinct rounded shape you can feel under the skin. The whole area reads as a shallow "i" shape, a small circle above a line.
  • Sow: the same pressure opens a "Y" shaped slit, and nothing extrudes. The genital opening sits closer to the anus than in a boar.
  • Both sexes have nipples, so nipples tell you nothing. Testicles in a young boar are not obvious, so their apparent absence tells you nothing either.

If you are not certain after two goes, the vet will do it in thirty seconds at the first health check, and pet shops get this wrong often enough that a rescue's word is worth more than a shop's label.

The timings that make this urgent A sow can conceive from 4 to 5 weeks old, and boars start mounting at about a month. Mixed-sex litters are separated by three weeks of age, no later, and that includes separating a young boar from his own mother. Gestation is 59 to 72 days, averaging 68, which is very long for a rodent, and a litter is usually 2 to 4 but can be as many as 13. A sow can also be mated again within hours of giving birth.
Why a first litter after about 6 to 9 months is dangerous A guinea pig's pubic symphysis, the cartilage joint at the front of the pelvis, stays flexible in a sow who has given birth, but in one who has not it ossifies and fuses over her first year. After that the pelvic canal cannot widen enough to pass a guinea pig's very large, fully-furred, open-eyed newborns, and a natural birth becomes impossible: the outcome is obstructed labor and a caesarean, or death. This is the single strongest reason not to keep an intact boar and a sow together and hope. It is also why "we will just let her have one litter" is a bad plan for an adult rescue sow of unknown history.

Neutering

Neutering in guinea pigs is done on boars, not sows: a sow spay is major abdominal surgery reserved for a medical reason such as ovarian cysts (page {{P:repro}}), while a boar castration is comparatively straightforward. It does not change temperament and it is not a solution to a boar-boar conflict; the reason to do it is to allow a boar to live with sows.

The four to six week rule A neutered boar stays fertile for weeks after surgery while sperm already present clears. Keep him away from any sow for at least four to six weeks after the operation, and ask your vet for their own figure. Litters conceived a fortnight after a castration are a well-known and entirely avoidable event. Anesthesia in this species also carries real risk and needs a vet who does it regularly. Health red flags & the 8 to 12 hour rule

For most pets, a skipped meal is nothing. For this one it is the alarm.

A guinea pig is a hindgut fermenter whose digestion depends on food moving through continuously. When eating stops, the gut slows, and a slowed gut is both a cause and a consequence of not eating, so the situation compounds rather than plateaus. The point to call a vet is 8 to 12 hours without eating or without producing droppings, and once gastrointestinal stasis is established it can become life-threatening within 24 to 48 hours. With prompt treatment many guinea pigs start improving within 24 to 72 hours, which is the argument for calling early rather than watching.

Same day, no waiting Not eating, or eating noticeably less, for 8 to 12 hours.
No droppings, or droppings suddenly small, dry or few.
Labored breathing, open-mouth breathing, or any clicking or wheezing. Guinea pigs are obligate nasal breathers and cannot fall back on mouth breathing.
A hunched, tense posture, or audible teeth grinding, which is pain.
A bloated or firm abdomen.
Lying stretched flat, panting or drooling in warm weather. Page {{P:climate}}.
Straining to urinate, blood in the urine, or crying while urinating. Page {{P:urinary}}.
Diarrhea, or a wet, soiled rear end.
Any sudden loss of coordination, head tilt, or seizure.

Within a few days

  • Weight down more than 5% off the pig's own baseline
  • Drooling, a wet chin, or dropping food while chewing
  • Hair loss, scabs, or intense scratching
  • A new lump anywhere, particularly on the neck
  • Limping, stiffness, or reluctance to move
  • Discharge from eyes or nose, or repeated sneezing
  • A pig that has stopped popcorning or stopped talking
  • Hair loss along the flanks in a sow. Page {{P:repro}}

GI stasis, bloat & a pig that stops eating

The emergency this species is most likely to have, and the one with the shortest fuse.

Gastrointestinal stasis is the gut slowing or stopping. It follows from inadequate fiber, dehydration, pain from anywhere in the body, stress, or a sudden diet change, and it then feeds itself: a pig that hurts stops eating, and a pig that stops eating hurts more. Bloat is the related emergency in which gas accumulates faster than it can be passed, distending the abdomen and pressing on the diaphragm. Both are urgent. Neither is treatable at home.

The seven reasons a guinea pig stops eating

  1. Dental disease. The most commonly cited cause by a distance, and usually invisible without a vet's examination because the problem is at the back of the mouth. Page {{P:dental}}.
  2. GI stasis itself, once it is under way.
  3. Pain or illness elsewhere. Respiratory infection, bladder stones, arthritis or an injury all suppress appetite as a secondary sign.
  4. Environmental stress. A new home, a moved cage, a new cage mate, building noise. Real, and usually short-lived, but never assume it.
  5. Dietary neophobia. A new hay or pellet brand refused because it is unfamiliar. Page {{P:hay}}.
  6. Pregnancy toxemia, in an overweight or late-term sow. Page {{P:repro}}.
  7. Vitamin C deficiency. Reduced appetite is one of the earliest signs and appears within 2 to 3 weeks of a deficient diet. Page {{P:scurvy}}.
Signs, and the line Reduced or absent droppings, or droppings gone small and dry. Lethargy or reluctance to move. A hunched or tense posture. Audible teeth grinding. A bloated or firm abdomen, sometimes with a hollow drum sound when tapped very gently. Reduced drinking. Labored breathing as a distended gut presses upward. Call at 8 to 12 hours without food or droppings. Not tomorrow morning.

What the vet will do

Assisted feeding with a syringe of recovery formula is the core of it, alongside pain relief, fluids, and a prokinetic drug to restart gut movement where appropriate. An x-ray distinguishes gas from an obstruction, which matters because a true obstruction is a surgical problem and gas is not. Underlying causes are chased at the same time, and the teeth are always checked. Recovery generally begins within 24 to 72 hours of treatment starting.

Syringe feeding at home, only under direction Your vet will likely send you home syringe-feeding a critical care formula every few hours, including overnight. Two things make it safer: give small amounts and let the pig chew and swallow at its own pace rather than pushing the plunger, and aim the syringe into the side of the mouth behind the incisors, never straight down the throat. A guinea pig that is fighting hard is a guinea pig at risk of inhaling the food. Stop, wait, and try again in ten minutes. Keep a syringe and a bag of recovery formula in the house before you need them; it is the one supply that is always needed at the worst possible hour.
