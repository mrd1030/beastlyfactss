Diet by age & unlimited hay

A guinea pig's gut depends on near-constant food, so skipping a meal is a much bigger deal here than for most pets.
Daily componentAmount
Grass hay (timothy, orchard or oat/meadow)Unlimited, always available, roughly 80% of the diet
Guinea pig pellets with vitamin CAbout 1/8 cup (about 2 tablespoons) per guinea pig
Fresh leafy greens and vegetablesRoughly 1 cup of leafy greens per guinea pig, within a daily range of 1 to 2 cups of fresh produce
Treats, fruit includedUnder about 10% of the daily diet
WaterFresh and available at all times

Hay is the diet, not the filler

Guinea pigs graze near-constantly, so continuous access matters more than counting meals. Hay is unlimited at every life stage and should never run out. It does two jobs nothing else does: its long fiber keeps digestion moving, and the constant chewing wears down teeth that never stop growing. Hay is also the main prevention for dental disease and for a slowed gut. Alfalfa is a different thing, a legume with more calcium and protein than a healthy adult needs, so it is fed only at the stages below.

By age

StageWhat changes
BabyStarts nibbling hay, pellets and vegetables within hours of birth, even while still nursing, and is fully weaned by 2 to 4 weeks.
Juvenile, under about 6 monthsDoes best with alfalfa hay added alongside or instead of grass hay: the extra protein and calcium support growth.
Healthy adultGrass hay is the staple. Alfalfa's richer profile can contribute to obesity if fed regularly, and its calcium adds to the bladder stone risk.
Pregnant, nursing, senior or illBenefits from alfalfa, and needs 30 mg/kg or more of vitamin C, against 10 to 25 mg/kg for a healthy adult (page {{P:vitc}}).

A day's feeding

  • All day: fresh hay and water, topped up before they run low.
  • Morning: a measured portion of pellets. Measuring daily makes a drop in appetite obvious at once.
  • Any time: fresh vegetables as a small handful, with anything uneaten pulled after 30 minutes to a couple of hours.
Change foods slowly Guinea pigs set their food preferences early and do not adapt well to sudden changes. A new hay type or pellet brand can be refused simply for being unfamiliar. Introduce anything new gradually, one item at a time and in a small amount, and treat a guinea pig that has genuinely stopped eating as a medical problem, not fussiness (page {{P:stasis}}).

Vitamin C: the daily rule

The one requirement generic small-pet advice always misses.

Guinea pigs cannot make their own vitamin C or store it. Rabbits, hamsters and chinchillas can, which is why general small-pet advice so often misses this. Without a daily source, collagen production breaks down over days to weeks, and collagen is what holds joints, gums, blood vessels and healing wounds together. That failure is scurvy (page {{P:scurvy}}).

WhoPer kilogram a dayIn daily terms
Healthy adult10 to 25 mg/kgAbout 20 to 25 mg for a typical adult
Growing, pregnant, nursing or ill30 mg/kg or moreAbout 30 to 40 mg for a growing or pregnant guinea pig

The requirement scales with weight, so a flat figure is the wrong shape for the answer: a 1.1 kg (2.4 lb) boar and a 700 g (1.5 lb) sow do not need the same dose. Hold the daily figures in your head at the vegetable drawer, and hand the per-kilogram figures to a vet.

Where it should come from

Fresh vegetables, led by bell pepper, every day. A quarter of a medium bell pepper covers most of a typical adult's daily need. Red, yellow and orange peppers carry substantially more than green ones, and pepper is high in vitamin C and low in sugar. Dark leafy greens are strong secondary sources to rotate in. For a sense of the range, one cup of each of these carries about this much vitamin C:

Food, per cupVitamin CFood, per cupVitamin C
Turnip greens260 mgCollard greens140 mg
Mustard greens252 mgParsley140 mg
Dandelion greens200 mgSpinach60 mg
Kale192 mgOrange50 mg

What does not work on its own

  • Pellets. Most are fortified at the mill, but vitamin C degrades with heat, light and humidity, and a meaningful share is gone within weeks, even in an unopened bag. Use pellets within 90 days of the manufacture date and buy a fresh bag more often than feels necessary. A guinea pig fed a complete-looking diet can still get scurvy if the label is the only vitamin C source.
  • The water bottle. Vitamin C degrades quickly in water and changes the taste, and a guinea pig that drinks less is worse off.

A supplement, as oral drops or tablets given by mouth, is a reliable way to close a gap, and a vet directs it when a guinea pig is ill. It backs up fresh food and does not replace it.

Can you give too much? There is a wide safety margin with normal fresh-food feeding, because the excess is water-soluble and mostly passed in the urine. The goal is steady daily intake from real food, not a big single dose. Consistently very high supplemental doses have been loosely linked to kidney stones in some cases.

Vegetables & pellets

Where the vitamin C comes from, and the small, measured portion that sits on top of the hay.

Vegetables and greens

Fresh vegetables go in daily as a small handful, rotated among varieties, and the leafy greens come to roughly a cup per guinea pig. Rotation matters because a species prone to fixed preferences stays willing to try things, and because several greens carry a lot of calcium.

FoodVerdictWhy
Bell pepperStapleThe standout: high in vitamin C and low in sugar. Red, yellow and orange carry more than green.
Romaine lettuce, cilantroSafeSafe greens, and decent secondary sources of vitamin C.
Ripe tomato, cucumber, carrot, celerySafeCucumber is mostly water, useful in hot weather. Ripe tomato only: unripe tomato is toxic.
Broccoli, cauliflower, dandelion greensSafeGood sources of vitamin C to rotate in.
Kale, spinach, parsleyRotateGood food, but they add to the calcium load that ends up in the urine, so do not feed one of them every day.
Iceberg lettuceNeverEssentially no nutritional value.
  • Offer the vegetables as one small handful, any time of day, and take out anything uneaten after 30 minutes to a couple of hours, so it does not spoil.
  • Add a new vegetable on its own and in a small amount, so any change in appetite or droppings can be traced to it.
  • Hide chopped vegetables in a foraging mat or puzzle, or under hay, instead of a bowl: it is enrichment as well as food.

Pellets

Pellets are a measured topping on the hay, about 1/8 cup (about 2 tablespoons) per guinea pig, typically in the morning.

  • Guinea pig pellets only. Rabbit and other rodent pellets contain no added vitamin C, because those animals can make their own.
  • Plain and timothy-based. Choose plain pellets, because ones with nuts, seeds or dried fruit are too high in fat. Look for stabilized vitamin C on the label.
  • Used within about 90 days of manufacture, since the vitamin degrades with light, air and time.
  • Alfalfa-based pellets belong with young, pregnant, nursing or ill animals, not healthy adults.
Why the rotation matters for the bladder Calcium that the body does not use ends up in the urine, and high-calcium foods add to the risk of bladder stones. Rotating greens, leaving alfalfa for the stages that need it, and keeping the water flowing are the dietary half of prevention (page {{P:urinary}}).

Fruit, treats & the never-feed list

The smallest part of the diet, and the part with the real dangers in it.

Fruit and treats

Fruit is an occasional treat, not a staple. Small amounts of orange, kiwi, apple or pear are fine. Fruit is high in sugar, and too much can upset the balance of the intestinal bacteria and cause diarrhea. All treats together, fruit included, stay under about 10% of the daily diet.

FoodVerdictWhy
Orange, kiwi, apple, pear (small bits)TreatSugar. A small piece now and then.
Nuts, seeds, peanut butter, cornSkipNot toxic, but too high in starch or fat. Guinea pigs pick them over hay and pellets, leading to nutrient gaps and obesity.
Bread, pasta, oats, dry cerealSkipSame problem: too high in starch.
Alfalfa hay, for a healthy adultSkipToo much calcium and protein. See page {{P:diet}}.
Iceberg lettuceSkipEssentially no nutritional value.
Meat, eggs, dairyNeverA strict herbivore. None of it belongs in the cage.

Genuinely toxic

Never feed Avocado (linked to heart muscle damage) · chocolate · onions, garlic, chives and leeks (can destroy red blood cells, with signs sometimes taking 3 to 5 days to appear) · rhubarb (high in oxalic acid, linked to bladder stones) · raw potato, unripe tomato, and the leaves or plants of either · mushrooms · fruit pits and seeds

Two rules that prevent most trouble

  • Check before offering. If a food is not on the vegetable page or this one, look it up before it goes in the cage.
  • Nothing goes in the water. Vitamin C in particular breaks down fast and can put a guinea pig off drinking.

The treats that suit this species best are the ones that are also food: a piece of bell pepper, a leaf of cilantro, a handful of fresh hay hidden somewhere to be found.

Weight & body condition

A gram scale is the most useful tool a guinea pig owner can have.

Weight changes show up before most illnesses do. Weigh each guinea pig once a week, at the same time of day, and write the number down (page {{P:ownerlog}}). The trend over several weeks says more than any single reading.

AdultNormal weight
Boar (male)900 to 1,200 g (2 to 2.6 lb)
Sow (female)700 to 900 g (1.5 to 2 lb)

Each animal has its own steady figure, and that figure, not the range, is the one that counts.

What the scale is telling you

ReadingWhat to do
SteadyCarry on.
A steady fallA reason to call a vet. Dental disease is one common cause, and anything that makes a guinea pig eat less will show here first (page {{P:dental}}).
A steady climbUsually too much food and too little exercise. Check the pellet and fruit portions and add supervised floor time. Make any change to the diet gradually.
A sudden gain or lossPossibly a medical problem, a tumor or a pregnancy included. Have a vet rule those out before changing the diet.

Body shape

Guinea pigs are shaped like a rounded brick, so the scale is not the whole test. Look down at the animal from above: sides that bulge outward point to excess weight. A guinea pig that has changed how it walks or become lethargic is worth a closer look too.

A weekly look-over, done while it is on the scale

Bright, clean eyes, with no crusting, cloudiness or discharge
A clean nose and ears
Clean, unstained teeth, with the top front teeth overlapping the bottom
A coat with no bald patches, scabs or lumps
Equal weight on all four feet, and no sores on the soles
A clean, dry back end and formed droppings
Foraging and weight When you make food harder to find, a good way to add enrichment, weigh each guinea pig daily while you raise the difficulty, and ease off if one starts losing weight.
