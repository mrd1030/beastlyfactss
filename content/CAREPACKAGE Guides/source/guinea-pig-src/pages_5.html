Solid floor, no wire or mesh anywhere underfoot
Single level. No ramps, no upper shelf counted as floor space
Sides at least 12 in (30 cm), open top, good ventilation

One hide per pig plus one spare, each with two openings
In a room the household uses, out of sun and off drafts

Pellet bag within 90 days of its manufacture date

Bell pepper daily, roughly a quarter of a medium one per pig
Water bottle and bowl both offered, both washed weekly
Nothing added to the water, ever

Two guinea pigs, sexed by you or by a vet
Two of everything: hides, water sources, hay piles, dishes

First year, roughly $1,160 to $2,730, and the fund that is not in it Setup plus the animals plus two first exams plus eleven months of running. The spread is almost entirely hay and bedding, and buying hay by the bale from a feed store rather than in pet shop boxes cuts the largest recurring line by more than half. Separately: a sick visit runs $100 to $300 or more and a bladder stone surgery or a dental under anesthesia considerably more, both common enough in this species to plan for. Insurance is limited, so put something aside monthly from the day they arrive.

Where not to save: floor space, hay quality, and the vet. Where you can: hides and chews, which are cardboard and untreated wood, and bedding, if you switch to washable fleece and actually do the laundry.

First 30 days checklist

In order. Week one is mostly leaving them alone and watching what goes in and out.

Before they arrive

Enclosure built, sited, bedded, and furnished with two hides plus a spare
Hay in, in two or three places, plus a rack
Exotics vet found and the number written down
Thermometer running for a few days to confirm the room's real range
Ask the seller what hay, pellets and vegetables they are on, and buy some
Quarantine space ready if you already keep guinea pigs

Days 1 to 7

Keep to the food they arrived on. Change nothing this week
Talk near the cage and hand-feed a piece of pepper without lifting anyone
Count droppings roughly every morning. This is the single most useful thing you can do in week one
Weigh on day one and again on day seven. Write both numbers down
Confirm the water bottle actually runs by pressing the ball, daily
Confirm the sex of each pig yourself. Page {{P:sexing}}

Days 8 to 14

Health check with the exotics vet, both pigs, whether or not anything looks wrong
Ask the vet to show you a nail trim and, for a boar, the rectal pouch check
Start short lap sessions on the floor. Page {{P:handling}}
Begin transitioning hay or pellets to your chosen brand, mixed in gradually. Page {{P:hay}}

Days 15 to 30

Daily vegetables established as a routine, bell pepper included every day
First supervised floor time in a proofed room. Page {{P:mistakes}}
Weekly weighing and the hands-on check established. Page {{P:droppings}}
First full cage strip and clean
First nail trim
Syringe and recovery formula bought and put in the cupboard
What normal looks like by day 30 Eating hay constantly, wheeking when the fridge opens, popcorning during floor time, chatting to each other, sleeping with eyes open or half open, and coming to the front of the cage rather than bolting for a hide when you walk in. Guinea pigs settle faster than most people expect once they have cover, company and a routine, and slower than expected if any one of the three is missing.

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Fifteen minutes a day, an hour a week, and one job that changes with the weather.

Every day

Fresh water, bottle and bowl. Press the ball to check it runs
Hay topped up, scattered, at least twice
Measured pellets, 1 to 2 tsp per pig
Fresh vegetables, bell pepper among them, taken out after a couple of hours
Spot-clean the litter corner and soiled bedding
Look at the droppings while you do it
Check the room thermometer
Floor time, or lap time, and watch each pig move and eat

Every week

Weigh every pig and log it. Page {{P:ownerlog}}
The five-minute hands-on check. Page {{P:droppings}}
Full bedding change, or fleece washed and swapped
Wash water bottle, bowl and dishes properly
Brush long or rosetted coats. Page {{P:grooming}}
Rotate a chew or move a hide to somewhere new

Every month

Full cage strip and wash, with the pigs safely in a pen
Nail trim, roughly every 3 to 4 weeks
Restock hay and pellets, checking the pellet manufacture date rather than the sell-by
Read the weight log as a trend rather than as this week's number
Rectal pouch check on any boar over about two years old. Page {{P:grooming}}

Heat waves, power outages & travel

For this species the emergency that matters is heat, and the rule in an outage is keep feeding.

A heat wave, with or without power

Losing air conditioning in hot weather is the genuine emergency for a guinea pig, far more than losing heating in cold weather. Work down this list in order.

  1. Move them to the coolest room in the house, usually a ground-floor room on the shaded side. A basement is often the best place in the building.
  2. Close curtains and blinds early, before the sun reaches that side, not after the room is already hot.
  3. Ceramic or stone tiles in the cage. Keep spares in the fridge and rotate them.
  4. Wrapped frozen bottles, positioned so the pigs can lie against them or move away.
  5. Damp towel over part of the cage with air moving past it, never sealing the cage in.
  6. Extra water in more than one container, and water-rich foods: cucumber, romaine, melon and apple.
  7. If a pig shows heat stress, damp the fur with cool, not iced, water and go to the vet. Page {{P:climate}}.
In a power cut, keep feeding A guinea pig's danger in an outage is not cold, it is a gut that stops moving. Hay, pellets and vegetables all continue exactly as normal, and water is offered more often rather than less. This is the opposite of the advice for a reptile, where temperature comes first, and it is the single most important sentence on this page. Extra hay to burrow into is how a guinea pig keeps warm; a heat pad in the cage is a chew hazard and a burn risk.

Travel

SituationWhat to do
Vet tripSolid carrier lined with a towel over bedding, hay inside, a piece of cucumber or pepper for moisture rather than a water bottle that will spill. Drape a towel over most of it: a prey animal wants somewhere to hide, not a view of the waiting room. Take the cage mate. Page {{P:anesthesia}}
A long driveCarrier belted in with its side facing the direction of travel, so a hard stop does not throw them face first into the end. Out of direct sun, air conditioning on but not blowing at the carrier. Stop every couple of hours to offer water and check them. Hay for the whole journey
Moving houseThey travel with you, not in the van. Set the cage up in a quiet room first, keep the same bedding smell if you can by carrying one unwashed fleece across, and expect a week of settling
HolidaysGuinea pigs are not left alone overnight. Water fouls, a bottle blocks, a pig stops eating and nobody sees. Use the sitter sheet on page {{P:sitter}}
Outdoors in summerA run on the grass is excellent, in shade, supervised, never on treated ground, never in the middle of the day, and never left unattended with a predator or a hot spell possible
Never leave a guinea pig in a parked car Not for five minutes, not with the windows cracked. A car interior reaches lethal temperatures for this species faster than for a dog, and heat is the thing this animal is least able to survive.

Glossary

Terms used earlier in this book, in the sense they were used.
BoarA male guinea pig
BumblefootPododermatitis. Inflammation and ulceration of the soles, from wire floors, damp bedding, obesity or hard surfaces
C&C cageCubes and Coroplast. An enclosure built from wire storage cubes and corrugated plastic, the standard way to get real floor area affordably
CavyThe correct name for the animal, and the reason a guinea-pig-experienced vet is called cavy-savvy
CecotropeA softer, nutrient-rich dropping produced by the caecum and eaten directly from the vent. Normal and necessary
EnterotoxemiaOvergrowth of toxin-producing gut bacteria, often after the wrong antibiotic. Page {{P:antibiotics}}
GI stasisThe gut slowing or stopping. The most common emergency in this species
Grease glandA gland at the base of the spine, more active in boars, that gets waxy and matted
Hindgut fermenterAn animal that digests fiber by bacterial fermentation in the caecum and large intestine. Why fiber and antibiotic choice both matter so much
MalocclusionTeeth that no longer meet correctly and therefore no longer wear correctly
NeophobiaRefusal of unfamiliar food. Pronounced in this species, and a reason to change diets gradually
Obligate nasal breatherAn animal that cannot switch to breathing through its mouth. An open-mouthed guinea pig is an emergency
Ovarian cystThe most common reproductive disorder in sows. Classic sign is symmetrical flank hair loss
PopcorningA sudden vertical jump with a twist. Play, and a sign of a comfortable animal
Pubic symphysisThe cartilage joint at the front of the pelvis. It fuses in a sow who has never given birth, which is why a first litter after about 6 to 9 months is dangerous
Rectal pouchA pocket behind the anus in boars, which becomes impacted in older intact males. Page {{P:grooming}}
RumblestruttingA low vibrating call with a swaying walk. Dominance or courtship, and normal during introductions
ScurvyVitamin C deficiency. Collagen production fails, so joints, gums, vessels and healing all break down
SowA female guinea pig
Trixacarus caviaeThe burrowing mange mite of guinea pigs. Causes itching severe enough to look like a seizure
UrolithiasisUrinary stones, typically calcium carbonate in this species
WheekThe loud rising food call. A learned behavior aimed at people, not an instinct
