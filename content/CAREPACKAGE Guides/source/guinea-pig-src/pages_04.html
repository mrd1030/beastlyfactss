Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Being lifted off the ground looks, to a guinea pig's instincts, exactly like a predator attack.

Picking one up

  1. Approach from the front, where it can see you, and talk to it and stroke its head before you lift.
  2. One hand under the chest, just behind the front legs.
  3. The other hand scoops and supports the hindquarters at the same time. Both ends, always.
  4. Bring it in against your chest so it feels supported and secure rather than dangling.
Stay low Sit on the floor or a bed whenever you handle a guinea pig. A fall from even a modest height can be fatal for an animal this size, and that risk alone is reason enough to keep handling close to the ground. Never lift one by the limbs, the scruff or loose fur. Never flip one onto its back: it can damage the spine and is highly stressful even when it does not.

How often, and for how long

Daily handling builds trust. Keep sessions short at first and let them grow as the guinea pig relaxes. Many eventually enjoy long lap time, but that comfort is earned gradually, not assumed from day one. A well set up enclosure gives a guinea pig the secure home base that makes the trust-building easier.

Signs it is stressed

  • Freezing in place
  • Trying to hide or burrow away from you
  • High-pitched squeaking
  • Struggling to get free

Signs it is content

  • A relaxed, loose body posture
  • Soft chirping or purring sounds

If you see stress signs, set the guinea pig back down gently instead of pushing through the session. Guinea pigs show clear stress signals well before they would ever nip or bite, and most need only minimal restraint for basic handling and a vet exam. Reading those signals and responding to them is the whole skill.

Children

Young children should never carry a guinea pig unsupervised. The combination of a squirming animal and unsteady hands is a common way falls happen. Supervise any handling by a young child closely.

Grooming, nails & coats

Three routine jobs, and one that only applies to older boars.

Nails

Guinea pig nails need a trim roughly every 6 to 8 weeks. Overgrown nails are uncomfortable to walk on and, past a certain length, curl back into the pad. They also feed bumblefoot.

  • Cut only the tip, clear of the quick: the blood vessel through the center of the nail, visible as a pink line on light nails.
  • On a dark nail, hold a flashlight behind it, and the quick shows before you cut.
  • Keep styptic powder out and open before you start, not after a nick. If you nick the quick, pressing the powder on for a few seconds normally stops the bleeding.
  • A guinea pig that fights every trim can be wrapped snugly in a towel with one foot worked free at a time. Do one or two nails per sitting, so it does not come to dread the whole process.

Coat and brushing

A guinea pig has no dramatic seasonal molt: shedding runs low and continuous, so a steady trickle of loose fur is normal. Coat length decides the workload.

CoatBrushing
Short-hairedAt least every few days.
Long-haired (Peruvian, Silkie, Texel)Daily, all year. These coats grow continuously rather than staying at a fixed length.

Mats are not just cosmetic. Mats that drag on the ground pick up bedding, urine and droppings, which means odor and skin irritation, and mats around the hindquarters can stop a guinea pig cleaning its own grease gland. Work a metal comb through in short sections. If matting has set in badly, trimming the fur short enough that it no longer drags is a reasonable fallback.

The grease gland

Guinea pigs have an active grease gland at the base of the spine, just above the anus, especially prominent in males. It can get greasy, matted and smelly. A mildly greasy patch means the gland is doing what it normally does, not that the animal is sick. Once buildup sets in, a butt bath or a gentle clean with a cotton swab and a mild cleaning agent is better than leaving it. It does not need daily attention: checking it when you trim nails is enough.

Baths

A guinea pig rarely needs one, and it is easy to over-stress the animal. Spot-clean a dirty patch with a damp cloth and dry it thoroughly. The one routine exception is the shallow butt bath for a greasy gland: shallow water, and the guinea pig dried thoroughly afterward.

Older boars Males, older intact ones especially, have a pouch behind the anus where droppings can pack in. Check older boars for it, and ask a vet to show you how to clean it the first time.

Sounds & body language

A prey animal that hides illness well and talks more than most small pets.

Guinea pigs are most active around dawn and dusk, though many adjust to their owner's day. They communicate with wheeking, chutting, bubbling, rumbling, chattering, whining, chirping and shrieking, which makes them unusually expressive for a small rodent.

SoundWhat it usually means
WheekA loud, rising squeal. Hunger or excitement. It is a learned response to expected food, which is why the fridge door brings one on.
Purring, soft gurglingContentment.
ChuttingA gentle, rhythmic call that goes with calm exploring and curiosity.
RumblingA deep vibrating sound, often with a swaying walk. Normal during an introduction.
Teeth chattering or clackingA warning. Keep your distance and do not push.
Squealing, whining or a sharp shriekFear or pain. Check on the animal.
Not communication Labored breathing, wheezing, clicking and coughing are signs of illness. They call for a vet, not a list of sounds to enjoy (page {{P:respiratory}}).

Body language

  • Popcorning, repeated bouncing hops and twists, is a sign of excitement and a comfortable guinea pig.
  • Freezing in place is what a frightened or stressed guinea pig does. It can make illness harder to spot, because a prey animal hides signs of pain.
  • Hiding or burrowing away from a hand means it is stressed. A guinea pig that stays hidden all the time, shows little interest in food or its surroundings, or chews the bars repeatedly is telling you something about the setup or its health.

Normal behavior that looks odd

  • Eating its own droppings. Guinea pigs eat some of their droppings directly from the body, and that is normal.
  • Neophobia. Fear of anything new is common, so a new food or toy can be refused or ignored at first.
  • Constant foraging. A wild guinea pig can spend up to 80% of the day foraging and feeding, most actively in the morning and evening.
A change in a habit is a symptom Because guinea pigs mask illness, a change in routine behavior, such as eating less, hiding more or losing interest in their surroundings, deserves attention promptly, not a week of watching.

Pairs, groups & who lives together

The decision that shapes everything else, and the one where the honest answer is not a preference.

Guinea pigs are herd animals. A guinea pig kept alone tends to become withdrawn or depressed, which is why pairs or small groups are the standard from the start. It is the one rule that sets the species apart among common beginner pets, and it doubles the budget: food and vet visits for two, not one.

Which pairings work

PairingWhat to expect
Two sowsWorks well, and needs no surgery.
Brothers raised togetherWorks well, and needs no surgery.
A neutered boar with one or more sowsWorks well. The boar must be neutered, meaning he has had surgery so he can no longer father litters, before he lives with a sow, because pregnancy and birth carry real risks for a sow.
Unrelated adult boarsCan be paired, but it can take several attempts to find a match.
An intact boar with a sowDo not. The risks to the sow are on page {{P:sexing}}.
A rabbitNever. A rabbit can carry bacteria that make a guinea pig ill without showing any signs itself.

Personality decides a pairing more than sex does. Neutering does not change a boar's behavior or stop fighting, so it buys nothing in a same-sex pair: its only job is to make a boar safe to house with sows.

Two of everything that matters

  • Hides. More than one, so a lower-ranking guinea pig always has somewhere to go.
  • Tunnels and other items worth having. More than one, for the same reason.
  • Water, bowls and hay. Enough bottles, bowls and piles of hay that nobody has to compete for them.
  • Floor space. 10.5 sq ft is the minimum for a pair, so more is better.

When a companion matters most

A prey animal separated from its bonded companion in an unfamiliar place carries two stresses instead of one. A hospitalized guinea pig can benefit greatly from a bonded cage mate staying alongside it, and the reason is appetite: guinea pigs tend to stop eating when stressed, and one recovering alone in a strange cage has one more reason to skip a meal. Ask the clinic whether it can house a cage mate, and take the companion along when the visit allows it.

Where to get a pair Shelters and rescue groups often have bonded pairs and are the first place to look. Whatever the source, check the sex of each animal yourself before two of them share a cage.
Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Introducing & bonding two guinea pigs

Most of what an introduction looks like is normal. The setup decides how it goes.

A new guinea pig is quarantined behind a closed door for 2 to 3 weeks before it meets anyone, so it cannot pass mites or illness to the others. Then the introduction happens on neutral ground, never in the resident's cage, where a newcomer is treated as an invader.

  1. Pick a room neither guinea pig lives in, bigger than the cage and with no furniture to hide behind. A bathroom or hallway works.
  2. Set it up bare. Towels on the floor, water bottles, and two large piles of hay, but no houses and no pellet bowls, both of which start fights.
  3. Put them in and sit still. Keep your hands off and stay quiet for at least an hour, counted from when they settle, not from when they go in.
  4. Prepare the cage meanwhile. Clean it out completely so it smells of no one, and give every guinea pig its own hide with two exits, plus enough bottles, bowls and hay for all.
  5. Move them in together once they are calm and tired, and watch for another hour or two.
Normal, leave it alone Mounting · loud chattering · chasing · pulling tufts of hair · rumble-strutting with the fur puffed up · raising noses at each other · wide yawns that show the teeth
Separate them Two guinea pigs rearing up on their hind legs at the same time · a locked, rolling fight · any deliberately drawn blood, which ends the introduction for the day

Never break up a fight with bare hands

A fighting guinea pig bites hard enough to reach bone. Drop a towel over both animals and separate them with the towel. Keep a towel within reach during every introduction.

What to expect

Chasing and rumbling carry on through the first week. By about two weeks, the new order has usually settled. If it does not settle, personality is usually the reason, since it decides a pairing more than sex does.

Order matters Quarantine first, sexing confirmed before they ever share a space, neutral ground, then the clean cage. Quarantine exists so a newcomer cannot carry mites or illness into the group.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Cover and work-for-it food lower stress and keep a prey animal active.

Foraging comes first

A guinea pig fed from one bowl loses most of its natural day. Scatter hay across the enclosure instead of piling it in a corner, and hide small amounts of chopped vegetables in a foraging mat, a wooden foraging puzzle or a tunnel rather than a dish. Make it harder as they learn: wrap a vegetable in a paper parcel or a small cardboard box that has to be chewed open, cover a dish with paper, or set out several dishes with food in only some. Start a box open until they know food lives inside, then close it bit by bit. In a 2025 survey of 1,181 guinea pig keepers, Elsbacher and colleagues found that those who offered food enrichment more often reported more play and friendlier behavior.

Chew, new things and views

  • Chew items. Untreated wood chews, hay-based chew sticks and cardboard tunnels hold up. Rotate a few options, since guinea pigs vary in what they will engage with.
  • New things, slowly. Many guinea pigs are neophobic, so add one item at a time. Choose sturdy pieces too large to swallow, and skip cotton rope and other fabric fibers.
  • Sound and view count too: a guinea pig that can see the room and hear quiet household life has more to take in, as long as a hide lets it get away when the family gets loud.

Supervised floor time

A large enclosure cannot fully replace open floor. Time in a guinea-pig-proofed room or playpen lets them run, popcorn and investigate new smells and textures. Keep the space free of cords, gaps they could squeeze into, and anything toxic, and supervise directly: never leave them unattended outside the enclosure.

Ten mistakes, and the fix

The mistakeWhat to do instead
One guinea pig, kept aloneTwo, from the start
Wire flooringA solid floor, always
A glass tank or plastic binAn enclosure with good ventilation, such as a C&C pen
Cedar, raw pine or sawdust beddingPaper, kiln-dried pine or fleece

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a healthy guinea pig & quarantine

Look at the animal, then at where it lives.

Where to get them

Shelters and rescue groups are the first place to look, and they often have pairs. Whoever the seller is, look at where the animals live first: it should be clean, have little odor, and not be overcrowded. Ask where the guinea pigs came from, whether a vet has checked them and what they are used to eating, and ask whether the seller will take one back if a vet finds it sick or pregnant. A young female kept in with males may already be pregnant, since sows can conceive from 2 months old or younger.

What to look at before you take one home

Well fed and alert
Bright, clean eyes with no crusting or cloudiness
A clean nose and ears
Formed droppings, with no diarrhea
A clean, dry back end
A coat with no bald patches
Clean, unstained teeth, the top front teeth overlapping the bottom
Equal weight on all four feet, with no sores on the soles

A nervous guinea pig is normal. It should calm down and respond to gentle stroking.

Quarantine is not optional

A new guinea pig that joins a household with others goes into quarantine behind a closed door for 2 to 3 weeks before it meets anyone, so it cannot carry mites or illness into the group. It guards against mites, ringworm and other illness. Ringworm also spreads to people.

The first vet visit

Find a vet who sees guinea pigs before you need one, and book a wellness exam early. A general small-animal clinic is not always equipped for guinea pig problems, so ask how many guinea pig procedures the clinic handles in a typical month. An annual wellness exam runs $50 to $100, and no vaccines are required for this species. Take the cage mate along when the visit allows it.

Bring to the visit A hard-sided, ventilated carrier with a hide spot and a layer of hay, your weight log, a note of appetite and droppings, and the questions on the vet-visit page (page {{P:vetvisit}}).

Sexing, neutering & the breeding deadline

Mistaking the sexes is common, and the consequences arrive fast.

How to sex a guinea pig

Hold the guinea pig upright against your chest with its hindquarters supported, and press gently just above the genital opening.

SexWhat you see
BoarThe penis can be eased out, and the testicles show as two scrotal sacs. The area looks like a dotted letter i.
SowNothing comes out. The opening is a Y-shaped slit.

A newborn female can look swollen, and a young male's testicles are small, so inexperienced sellers and owners often get young animals wrong. If you are not sure, have a vet check at the first visit.

The timings that make this urgent

EventWhen
A sow can conceiveFrom 4 to 5 weeks old
A boar becomes fertileAt about 8 to 10 weeks
A sow can conceive againWithin hours of giving birth, so the boar is removed before the birth or straight after it
Pups weanedAt 14 to 28 days
Gestation59 to 72 days; a litter is usually 2 to 4 pups, born furred and soon eating solid food

Why a first litter late in life is dangerous

A sow's pelvic bones separate to let the pups out. If she has not had young by roughly 6 to 9 months, those bones fuse, and a first litter after that can end in obstructed labor. That, along with the risks of pregnancy itself, is why a boar is neutered before he lives with a sow.

Neutering

A male must be neutered before he lives with a female. It does not change a boar's behavior or stop fighting, so it buys nothing in a same-sex pair. A neutered boar can still father young for some weeks after surgery, so he stays apart from sows until the vet says it is safe. Fees are set clinic by clinic, and a quote should include the anesthesia and pain relief: get one from a vet who sees guinea pigs before settling on a mixed pair. Spaying a female is the more complicated operation (page {{P:repro}}).

Section 06 · The Law

Is a guinea pig legal where you live?

The least restricted animal on the exotic pet law map.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet guinea pig is legal with no permit in 51 and legally allowed on a condition in 1, Hawaii. No state bans it, none requires a permit to keep one, and only Hawaii requires a permit to bring one in.

WhereStatusThe rule
HawaiiConditionalListed by binomial, Cavia porcellus (carried with the older name Cavia cobaya), on the List of Conditionally Approved Animals (Hawaii Administrative Rules § 4-71-6.5). Bringing one in needs an import permit from the Department of Agriculture and Biosecurity before it ships and a certificate of veterinary inspection signed within seven days of arrival, and it enters through Honolulu airport only.
CaliforniaLegalTitle 14 § 671 restricts the whole order of rodents, then names exceptions, domesticated guinea pigs among them. Cities and counties may still prohibit what the state allows.
ConnecticutLegalThe whole rodent order sits in Category Two, which bars private possession, and nine species are excepted. The guinea pig is one (§ 26-55-6).
GeorgiaLegalRodents need a license except the genus Cavia, named in Rule 391-4-8-.05.
New JerseyLegalOn the closed exempt list at New Jersey Administrative Code 7:25-4.4, the only route to keeping an animal there with no permit.
New York CityLegalNamed in Health Code § 161.01(c), which also bars the capybara and other large rodents.
District of ColumbiaLegalInside the permitted category "domesticated rodents and rabbits" (District of Columbia Code § 8-1808(j)(1)(C)).
MassachusettsLegalOn the domestic animal list (Massachusetts regulations, title 321, section 9.02(3)). An unlisted vertebrate there is presumed wild.

Legal with no permit: 51 jurisdictions

In twelve of them, California, Colorado, Illinois, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, Montana, Nevada, North Carolina, Ohio, Oregon and Washington, the state lets cities or counties be stricter, so check your local ordinance (a city or county law) too. In Nevada, Clark County, Las Vegas and Henderson are all stricter than the state.

What does bind a guinea pig owner

State law is effectively silent, so the limits that bite are lease terms, homeowners' association rules and the occasional municipal cap on the number of animals. No state or federal rule in the United States requires guinea pigs to be kept in pairs; Switzerland's Animal Protection Ordinance (Article 13, in force since 2008) does, and that rule circulates online without the country attached.
