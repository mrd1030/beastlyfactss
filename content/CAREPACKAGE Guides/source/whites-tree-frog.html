Cost overview

Initial setup

The frog is $20 to $60 for a standard animal, more for a blue or other designer color. Itemized, the setup with its first supplements comes to $295 to $565, and the terrarium is the biggest single line. With the frog and a first vet exam, plan on $365 to $775. Page 29 has every line.

The number that should drive the decision Not the price. The lifespan. A frog bought for a twelve-year-old is very likely still alive when that child is finishing college. The purchase is the cheap part of this animal, and the routine on page 33 is what you are actually signing up for.

The vertical enclosure & where it goes

An arboreal frog measures its home in height. The floor is where it goes to drink.

18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) is the standard minimum for one adult, with the 24 in as height, because this is a climbing species that spends its time off the ground. A 20-gallon tall tank is a smaller figure you will also see for one adult; this package uses 18×18×24 in. For a small group of two to four, all of a similar size, step up to 24×18×24 in (61×46×61 cm). A front-opening glass terrarium with a mesh top is the usual choice: the door lets you work at frog level, and the mesh is where the UVB and heat sit.

The lid has to fit and lock. Those toe pads climb glass without effort, and an escaped frog in a heated house dries out fast. Height only counts if it is furnished: sturdy branches, broad leaves and cork mounted high, so the frog can sit near the top, supported and out of sight.

The temperature gradient & heating

A gentle, reliable gradient. Intense heat is not the goal with this species.
Zone°F°C
Cool side, low in the enclosure70 to 7521 to 24
Basking area, high on one side80 to 8527 to 29
Nightmid-70sabout 24
Do not let the warm end pass9032
Act if anywhere falls below6518

The two bold lines are the limits for this species: a basking area that does not exceed 90°F (32°C) and a night that does not fall below 65°F (18°C). They are the edges, not targets.

Why gentle

A frog sets its own temperature by moving between warmer and cooler spots, so the gradient exists to give it a choice rather than to push heat into it. A frog that wants the warm branch will use it, and one that wants to cool down needs the low, cool side to go to. In most homes the room already supplies the cool side, and the only equipment needed is a small source that lifts one upper corner into the basking range.

The heat source

Side-mounted heat matOn the outside of one side wall, not under the floor, since this frog lives high up and the substrate holds water
ThermostatEither source runs through one, so a hot room or a failed timer cannot cook the enclosure. Its probe goes where the frog actually sits, held with a probe clip or suction cup, never tape
Two readingsDigital, one high at the warm end and one low at the cool end, from a two-probe unit or a second thermometer. Analog dials drift, and one reading tells you nothing about a gradient
Never inside the enclosure Every heat source sits outside the glass or on top of the mesh. A frog climbs everything, and a bulb, mat or heat rock it can reach is a burn. Its belly skin is thin and permeable, and a sluggish, cold frog does not move off a hot surface fast enough.

The humidity cycle that dips

The detail first-time keepers get backwards: too wet, all the time, causes more disease than a dip between mistings.

Hold a baseline of 50 to 60%, let misting push it to 70 to 80% once or twice a day, and then let it fall back. Mist in the evening, when this nocturnal frog wakes up, so the spike arrives when it is active and drinking off the leaves. Constant, unbroken high humidity is directly linked to bacterial and red-leg problems, so resist the instinct to keep everything perpetually damp. The waxy coating on this frog's skin is part of why it tolerates drier air than most tree frogs.

Holding the cycle in practice

  • Read it twice. A digital hygrometer mid-height, away from where you spray. Read it an hour after the evening mist and again the next afternoon. Those two numbers are the cycle; a single reading whenever you walk past tells you almost nothing.
  • Mist the surfaces, not the frog. Leaves, glass and cork, with dechlorinated water, until they bead.
  • Too dry between mistings? Cover part of the mesh top, add live plants, or keep the substrate lightly damp. Change one thing, then wait two days to judge it.
  • Never dries? Uncover mesh, mist less, and check the substrate is moist rather than soggy. Condensation all day, a sour smell or mold are the signs.
Ventilation is half of humidity Moist air that never moves is what bacteria want. Adequate ventilation for an amphibian is 1 to 2 fresh air changes an hour, which in a home terrarium means a mesh top or vents that are never fully sealed. Live plants help both ways: they hold humidity and they freshen the air.

The water that is safe to mist with

A frog drinks through its skin. Whatever is in the mister and the dish goes straight into the animal.

Use only dechlorinated tap water or spring water, for the dish and for misting. Never distilled or reverse-osmosis water. Distilled and RO water are usually not electrolyte-balanced, and without rebalancing they can be fatal to an amphibian. That sounds backwards, since purer water seems safer, but an animal that exchanges water and salts through its skin is not helped by water with nothing in it.

WaterVerdictWhy
Tap, with a conditionerUseThe practical default. Pick a conditioner that states it neutralizes both chlorine and chloramine
Bottled spring waterUseFine for the dish and the mister. Check the label says spring, not distilled
Tap, left standingOnly ifStanding open for 24 to 48 hours lets chlorine leave. It does nothing to chloramine, which needs a conditioner such as sodium thiosulfate to split it. Unless you know your supply uses no chloramine, treat the water anyway
Fresh, untreated tapNeverChlorine and chloramine are fine for people and harmful to permeable skin
Distilled or RONeverNot electrolyte-balanced. Can be fatal without remineralizing

There is no published hardness target for this species. The rules with real backing are the ones above, plus water free of ammonia, pesticides and heavy metals. Treat the water before it goes in, not after.

The water dish

Wide enough for the frog to sit in and soak, and shallower than the frog is tall when it sits with its legs folded. Tree frogs are weak swimmers and a deep dish can drown one, a froglet especially. A heavy, smooth dish that will not tip is easiest to keep clean.

Empty, rinse and refill it every day, or every other day at the very least. The frog soaks and defecates in it, and a dish left to sit is the infection source behind bacterial and skin problems. Refill with treated water at room temperature, never straight from the faucet.

Keep a jug of ready water Treat a gallon jug at a time, label it, and use it for both the mister and the dish. It makes the right water the convenient water, and it is the water you will want on hand during a power outage, page 34. A dedicated spray bottle that has never held cleaner or plant food is part of the same habit.
A frog living in its dish is telling you something Soaking is normal. A frog that sits in the water most of the day and night is usually too warm or too dry, so check the gradient on page 6 and the cycle on page 7. If both read right, it is a reason to look harder at the frog: page 20.

UVB, light & the day length

Not strictly required. The frog survives without it, and does better with it.

Low-level UVB is the recommendation for this species. It is nocturnal, so it never needed the strong lamps a basking lizard does, but a frog under working UVB makes its own vitamin D3, and vitamin D3 is how it uses the calcium in its food. Missing UVB is one of the two causes of metabolic bone disease, a condition frequently seen in captive amphibians. A gentle 5% tube is cheap insurance against it.

SettingNumber
Tube5% UVB T5 HO tube: T5 is the slim fluorescent tube size and HO (high output) the tube format; the 5% rating, its UVB output, is what keeps it gentle. In a reflector fixture across the top
DistanceThe basking branch at least 6 in (15 cm) below the fixture, and the tube within about 18 in (46 cm) of where the frog sits
Strength at the frogAn ultraviolet (UV) index (UVI), the standard scale for UVB strength, of roughly 0.7 to 1.6 where it basks, if you own an ultraviolet (UV) meter. Without one, the distance above is the control
Day length12 hours on, 12 hours off, on a timer. A regular cycle matters more than the exact hour
Replace the tubeEvery 6 to 8 months, on a schedule, whether or not it still lights
At nightDark. A nocturnal frog needs no night light, and the room's own dim light is enough to see by
Glass blocks it Ordinary glass and most plastics filter out UVB, so a tube sitting on a glass lid, or a terrarium near a sunny window, delivers far less than it appears to. Mount the fixture over mesh. And a window is never the UVB source: through glass it is only a heat trap, and page 5 explains why that matters more.

The bulb that still glows

A T5 UVB tube keeps producing visible light long after its UVB output has dropped. That is why it is replaced on a date rather than when it looks tired, and why the date it went in belongs on the equipment log on page 37. A frog's schedule is shorter than the 6 to 12 months common for reptile tubes, so do not borrow a lizard keeper's schedule. A UV index meter is the only way to know the tube is at the right height and when to replace it; UV cards show total UV, not the UVB that makes vitamin D3.

UVB and the supplement jar are one decision

Whether the calcium dust needs to carry vitamin D3 depends on the lighting. A frog under a working tube makes its own; a frog without one does not, and needs calcium with D3. More is not better in the other direction either: an excess of vitamin A has been suggested to interfere with vitamin D metabolism, which is one reason to dose a multivitamin on schedule rather than generously.

Keep the light gentle This is a frog that sleeps through the day against cover. Bright light with nowhere to get out of it is stress, not enrichment. The broad leaves and cork in the enclosure are what make a lit enclosure livable, and a frog that is always fully exposed under the tube needs more cover, not less light.

Substrate, plants & furnishings

The floor holds the humidity, and everything above it is where the frog actually lives.

Substrate

ChoiceVerdictNotes
Coarse orchid barkGoodLarge pieces hold moisture without packing down
Coco fiber or coco huskGoodHolds humidity well. Keep it lightly moist, never soggy
Bioactive soil mixGoodA planted, living floor. More work to set up, less to maintain
Sphagnum moss on topAddA layer over any of the above helps hold the humidity cycle
Paper towelSimpleEasiest to clean and to check droppings on, harder to hold humidity. Good for quarantine
Fine, loose substrate, small gravel, small barkNeverEasy to swallow along with an insect. Page 26
Cedar or pine mulchNeverToxic to amphibians

Soil is replaced when it starts to smell or stays waterlogged, because wet, dirty substrate is what builds ammonia. Bark and moss can be taken out and washed as needed, and the more often the better.

Plants

Dense, layered planting is the one enrichment item with real evidence behind it: in the closest study, tree frogs given plant cover grew larger and faster and chose the planted side. Use broad-leaved plants sturdy enough to hold a heavy frog. Live plants must be free of fertilizer and pesticide residue, on the leaves and in the soil, which means rinsing new plants and repotting them; keeping them in small pots makes cleaning easier. Artificial plants fill the dense parts cheaply and wipe clean, but they do not hold humidity or freshen the air the way live ones do. A mix is the sensible answer.

Furnishings

  • Sturdy branches and cork. This is a stocky frog, and anything it climbs has to take its weight without sagging or shifting.
  • Broad perches near the top. Wide leaves, flat branches and cork flats rather than thin stems, at several heights, with the best spots high up.
  • A daytime sleeping spot. A large piece of bark set diagonally a couple of inches from the back wall lets the frog tuck in behind it to sleep. Dark paper on the outside of the back glass adds a dim, secluded wall.
  • The water dish, low and stable, from page 8.
The visibility test If the frog is always immediately visible, there is not enough in there. A well-furnished enclosure is one where you sometimes have to look for it.
A frog rubbing its nose on the glass Some frogs push along the bottom of the glass trying to get out, and a nose rubbed raw is an open door for infection, covered on page 25. A strip of paper a couple of inches tall around the outside base of the glass often stops it.

Cleaning without soap, and household chemicals

A frog's skin absorbs what touches it, and the water dish collects whatever lands in it.

The cleaning routine

WhenWhat
DailySpot clean: wipe droppings off leaves, branches and glass. Pull uneaten insects within a few hours of feeding. Empty, rinse and refill the water dish, or every other day at the very least
As neededTake out bark and moss and wash them, the more often the better. Change paper towel lining often
When it smellsReplace soil when it starts to smell or stays waterlogged
Plain hot water, not soap Clean with plain hot water. Soaps and disinfectants can leave a residue the frog absorbs through its skin. When something genuinely needs disinfecting, rinse it very well and dry it completely before it goes back. Refill the dish with treated water, never fresh tap water.

When disinfecting is the right call

After an illness, before reusing a quarantine setup, or when an enclosure comes to you secondhand. Use plain, unscented household bleach (not splashless or scented) at 30 mL per liter of water (about 2 tablespoons per quart), left in contact for at least 30 minutes, then rinsed thoroughly and dried completely. Do it with the frog in another room, and let the item air out until no smell is left.

Where you wash it

Not the kitchen sink, and not a bathtub people use. Amphibians carry Salmonella without looking sick, and the bacteria travel on the dish, the decor and the water. A utility sink or a plastic tub kept only for the frog is the right place. If a bathtub has to be used, clean it thoroughly afterward. Wash your own hands with soap and running water when you finish, away from the frog.

Keep a frog-only kit A spray bottle, a sponge, a dish brush, paper towels and a jug of treated water, kept apart from household cleaning supplies. Nothing in it is ever used with a cleaner, so nothing in it can carry residue back.

Section 03 · Feeding

Feeding by size & age

A sedentary frog with an appetite that does not switch off. Overfeeding is the default unless someone watches its shape.

Two facts about this species meet at the food dish. It spends most of the day sitting still, and it eats whatever is put in front of it. Put those together and a well-meaning keeper produces an overweight frog without ever feeling they overdid it. So the schedule below is a starting point, and the frog's body condition on page 14 is what corrects it.

StageHow oftenWhat and how muchDusting
Froglet, up to about 1.6 in (4 cm)DailyOne to four pinhead cricketsTwice a week
Growing juvenileDailyCrickets sized to the frog, a few at a sittingTwice a week
Smaller adult, under about 3 in (7.5 cm)2 to 3 times a week2 to 4 insects, crickets about three weeks oldOnce a week
Adult, over 3 in (7.5 cm)2 to 3 times a week3 to 4 insects, a few larger cricketsOnce a week

Larger items, an earthworm or a fuzzy mouse, come no more than once a month for an adult. Waxworms and superworms are once a week at most.

How a feeding works

  • Evening. This frog wakes and hunts after dark, so feed in the evening, around the time of the main misting.
  • Loose or from forceps. Insects can go loose into the enclosure or be offered from blunt, round-tipped forceps, whichever the frog responds to. Forceps let you count exactly what each frog ate, which matters in a group.
  • Prey it can find at its level. Insects that climb the planting suit it better than ones that vanish into the substrate. Releasing them into cover rather than presenting them is also enrichment.
  • Clear up. Pull any insects still loose a few hours later, before they drown in the water dish or scatter into the planting.
  • Never free-feed. A bowl of insects left in permanently is how this species gets fat.
The amount matters more than the interval Whether an adult eats on Monday and Thursday or Tuesday and Saturday makes little difference. Whether it gets four crickets or ten makes a great deal. Write the counts on the owner log on page 36 for the first couple of months, and compare them against the ridge test.
A frog that stops eating Check the temperature and humidity first, since a cold or badly set up frog eats less. If they read right and the frog still refuses food, treat it as a health sign, not a preference: loss of appetite is among the first signs of both chytrid and red-leg. Page 20.

An unfed cricket delivers very little, and an unsupplemented cricket carries about 1 part calcium to 9 parts phosphorus. Fixing that takes two separate steps, and neither replaces the other.

Vitamin A deficiency in particular shows up as a frog that can no longer catch prey with its tongue.

Body condition: reading the tympanum ridges

This species is round by design. Obesity is read off the head, not the waistline.

White's tree frogs are nicknamed dumpy tree frogs for their round, waxy-skinned build, and a moderate fat roll over the shoulders is normal for the species. So general roundness tells you very little. What tells you something is the ridge of fat that sits just above the tympanum, the round external eardrum behind each eye, and fat building in the armpits.

The ridge test, three readings

What you seeReadingWhat to do
No visible ridges above the eardrumLikely underweightFeed more, or feed more often. Check the frog is actually eating what goes in
Ridges present and definedRightKeep the current schedule and keep checking
Ridges prominent, sagging or folding over, fat bulging over the tympanum and in the armpitsObeseFeed less: fewer insects per sitting, fatty items cut out, and more room and reason to climb.

When it is past a diet fix

In an overweight frog the excess fat starts to show clinically: a cloudy look to the eyes from lipid deposits in the cornea, a body cavity that looks enlarged from fat laid down inside it, and fat in the crests above the eyes heavy enough to interfere with vision. Any of those is a vet visit, not just a smaller dinner, because sustained obesity raises the risk of fatty liver disease.

Make it a monthly habit Once a month, look at the head from the side in good light, take a photo from the same angle, and note the reading on the owner log on page 36. If you own a kitchen scale that reads in grams, weigh the frog in a small lidded tub at the same time. A series of photos makes a slow change visible in a way no single look at the frog does.

Handling with plain water & no soap

The risk runs the direction people do not expect: not the frog hurting you, but you hurting the frog.

This is one of the few frogs that tolerates being picked up, and most White's tree frogs handle well once they are used to it, more so than the great majority of pet frogs and toads. What makes technique matter is the skin. Amphibian skin is permeable and absorbs what it touches, including oils, lotions, soap residue and chemicals from your hands. Even the natural oils and salts on clean human skin can damage it.

The technique

StepHow
HandsWash thoroughly with plain water only, no soap, immediately before handling, and rinse well so no lotion, sanitizer or soap remains
Or glovesPowder-free vinyl gloves (nitrile is a second choice), never latex, wetted with dechlorinated water. After lotion, cleaning chemicals or a manicure, gloves are the safer choice however well you have washed. Deaths have been reported in tadpoles exposed to latex, nitrile and, to a lesser extent, vinyl, so vinyl is the cautious choice for a froglet
Pick-upLet the frog walk onto a flat, wet hand rather than grabbing it, and support its body. Sit low, over something soft, in case it jumps
SessionRoughly 5 to 15 minutes, and no more than about twice a week
AfterwardBack into the enclosure, then wash your hands with soap and running water, away from the frog

First-timer mistakes

  • Handling with lotioned or chemical-exposed hands. By far the most common and most damaging mistake.
  • Handling too often, which adds stress even to a tolerant species.
  • Wetting hands or gloves with tap water straight from the faucet instead of treated water.
  • Treating handling as enrichment. For an amphibian it mostly is not; it is something the frog tolerates.
Salmonella, and children under 5 Healthy-looking amphibians carry Salmonella in the gut with no sign of illness, and it spreads hands and surfaces to mouth rather than by bites. Wash with soap and running water after touching the frog, its water or anything in the enclosure. Keep the frog and its equipment out of the kitchen. Children younger than 5 should not handle the frog or touch its enclosure at all, since they are the most likely to become seriously ill. Older children are supervised and wash every time.
What to expect A sedentary animal, so do not expect a lot of movement during a session. This is a calm, low-drama frog by amphibian standards. A frog that struggles or leaps away every time is asking for shorter sessions or a break, and a new frog gets none at all until it is through quarantine on page 18.

Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Shedding, color, calling & normal behavior

Most of what worries a new owner is normal. This page sorts the normal from the worth-a-look.

Skin shedding you will rarely see

A White's tree frog sheds the whole outer layer of its skin on a regular cycle, and at room temperature that cycle is short: about every 2 days at 73 to 91°F (23 to 33°C) and every 3 to 4 days in cooler conditions. The frog pulls the skin off and eats it as it comes away, so a keeper rarely sees a shed happen and almost never finds shed skin lying in the enclosure. Shedding also does a job: it is one of the ways a frog clears microbes from its skin.

Color

A normal White's tree frog ranges from light blue-green through emerald to almost gray, often with scattered white or gold spots along the sides, and it can change shade somewhat. Its green is blue pigment under a yellow layer, and where that yellow layer is thin the frog looks blue. That is also why the species was named caerulea, blue: the first specimens shipped to England in 1790 had turned blue in preservative.

Calling, sleeping and sitting still

Males call, and call through the year, so a frog heard calling is a male. The species is nocturnal: it sleeps through the day pressed against cover, often tucked behind bark or leaves, and wakes in the evening to hunt. Long stretches of sitting in one place are normal for an animal this sedentary. In the wild, in the dry season, these frogs coat themselves in a secreted cocoon that holds moisture in.

What the research says

No enrichment study exists for this species. The closest and most useful one is on red-eyed tree frogs, another arboreal tree frog: Michaels, Antwis and Preziosi (2014) found that providing plants affected body size, growth rate and the bacterial communities on the skin, and the frogs showed a strong preference for planted areas over bare ones. That is a fitness result rather than an observer scoring activity, which is rare for amphibians: a 2014 review of the whole topic found only fourteen relevant primary research papers. Skin bacteria matter because they are part of how a frog defends itself against pathogens, chytrid included.

Priority order

1. Dense planting at several heights
2. Broad perches near the top of the enclosure
3. Humidity with real drying cycles, and treated water
4. Prey released into cover, not presented
5. Varying where and when food appears
6. Same-size company, if you want a group
7. Novelty, last, and gently
Move the food An ambush feeder will happily sit in one spot and be fed the same way forever. Release insects into the planting at night and vary where they go in. In dart frogs, simply changing the position of the food dish increased activity, which for an animal this sedentary is a real result from a free change, and more movement is part of the obesity fix on page 21.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a frog, quarantine & the law

The healthiest frog is chosen well, moved gently and kept apart until it has proved itself.

Choosing one

Buy captive-bred. It is widely available, and wild-caught frogs often carry parasites and disease and adjust less well to captivity. A standard animal runs $20 to $60. Blue and other designer colors cost more, but a bluish frog in a store is not proof of a blue morph: the green comes from blue pigment under a yellow layer, the shade shifts, and a frog at a morph price should come from a breeder who can say what its parents were.

Pass on any frog showing listlessness, weight loss, cloudy eyes, skin discoloration or redness on the belly and legs, swelling, labored breathing, or loose stools. Ask what it has been eating and when it last ate, and watch it take an insect if you can.

Bringing it home

StepHow
Move it in a containerA small clear container, never a net, so the frog is never forced through mesh. A ventilated tub with a moist paper towel works for the trip home
Temperature firstLet the container sit beside the new setup until both read the same before the frog moves across. A fast temperature swing is one of the most common ways a new arrival gets stressed
Then leave it aloneLights, gradient and humidity cycle running for a few days before it arrives; no handling until quarantine ends

Quarantine: six to eight weeks

A new amphibian is kept apart for 6 to 8 weeks, the usual period. The shortest in use is 30 days, and 60 is preferred. Use its own tub or enclosure with paper towel substrate, its own spray bottle, dish and tools, in a different room if you can, and look after the established frogs first and the new one last. If it shows any illness partway through, the clock resets rather than pausing.

Use the window for a first vet exam: a fecal test for parasites and, ideally, a skin swab test for chytrid, the fungus that causes chytridiomycosis. Chytrid is the reason quarantine is close to mandatory for amphibians: the pet trade is a documented route by which an amphibian chytrid fungus has already spread between continents.

Never release a frog outdoors Not a healthy one, not an unwanted one. A captive frog carrying a pathogen becomes a wild exposure event the moment it is loose. A frog you cannot keep goes back to a breeder, a rescue or a reptile and amphibian society.

Where it is legal

No state-by-state review of this species has been done for this package, so it carries no state table. Check before you buy. Rules for nonnative amphibians run from wide open to closed: Nevada's list clears all nonindigenous amphibians except bullfrogs, and Oklahoma exempts nonindigenous reptiles and amphibians from its import permits. At the other end, Hawaii allows an animal only if it appears on its approved lists and bars anything never written down, Massachusetts runs a closed list of exempt species, and the District of Columbia's list of allowed animals names no amphibian at all. Your state wildlife agency is the answer that counts, and a city can be stricter than its state.

Group housing by size, sexing & breeding

They tolerate company well. The one rule is not optional.
Every frog in a group is a similar size A White's tree frog will attempt to swallow anything it can fit in its mouth, including a smaller frog. A mixed-size group is a feeding accident waiting to happen. Juveniles grow at different rates, so a group that started matched needs checking as it grows.

Setting up a group

  • Space: 24×18×24 in (61×46×61 cm) for two to four frogs of a similar size, against 18×18×24 in for one.
  • Cover: enough planting and cork that each frog can be out of the others' line of sight, and enough high perches that nobody is pushed to the floor.
  • Feeding: forceps feeding lets you see that each frog eats its share, so a dominant eater does not get fat while a shy one thins out. Check every frog against the ridge test on page 14, not just the group.
  • A new frog goes through quarantine before it meets the group, page 18. If one frog falls ill, it comes out to its own tub until the vet has seen it.

Sexing an adult

Most frogs for sale are juveniles, and a juvenile usually cannot be sexed: the adult features may not have appeared yet, and the species takes about two years to mature.

What happens if a pair breeds

A male and female kept together can breed. In captivity breeding follows the wet season the frogs get in the wild, and keepers who want eggs trigger it by imitating heavy summer rain. The male grips the female from behind in amplexus, which can last for days, and she lays 150 to 300 eggs in still water. Hatching starts 28 to 36 hours after laying.

The tadpoles are the long part. In good conditions some finish metamorphosis in 2 to 3 weeks, most are out of the water within 2 months, and a few stay tadpoles for up to a year. The froglets then take about two years to reach breeding age. That is hundreds of animals needing homes, food and space.

Company without the project Keep two of the same sex. Because juveniles are hard to sex, that usually means buying adults whose throats show which is which. Two males will call; two females are the quiet option.

Health red flags & finding a vet

Hardy for an amphibian, but amphibian illness moves fast, and treatment options narrow the longer it waits.
Call a vet today if The frog is lethargic and losing weight, especially if it is new or has been near a new animal. The belly or legs show redness or pinpoint bleeding. Skin is coming away in patches, or there is excess mucus. It cannot turn itself upright, or the body or limbs are swollen. Breathing is labored. It has been exposed to a cleaner, spray or pesticide. The jaw looks misshapen, a leg is bent, or it twitches or cramps after moving.
Book this week if The eyes look cloudy. The eardrum ridges are folding over. It has refused food with the temperature and humidity confirmed in range. Shed skin is lying uneaten. Stools have been loose, or absent, for longer than its normal pattern. The nose is scraped from rubbing on the glass.

Finding the right vet

Not every exotic vet sees amphibians, and amphibian medicine has real limits, which makes the vet you choose matter. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians runs a find-a-vet directory. Call ahead and ask whether the practice treats frogs, and whether it can run a skin swab for chytrid. An exam typically costs $50 to $150, and an exotic emergency visit fee alone often starts around $250 before treatment. Annual exams are recommended for exotic pets, and one during quarantine catches what arrived with the frog.

Getting the frog there

A well-ventilated plastic container with a moistened paper towel on the bottom, the lid covered or closed, kept at a steady temperature and out of the sun. The full transport routine is on page 34.

Obesity, fatty eyes & fatty liver

The defining health risk for the species, and the one entirely in the keeper's hands.

Obesity shows up in this species more than in almost any other pet frog, driven by the same enthusiastic appetite that makes it so satisfying to feed. It is the most common problem in captive adults, and unlike most of this section it is fixed at home.

Obesity
CauseOverfeeding, and fatty feeders in particular: waxworms and superworms as anything more than a weekly treat, free-feeding, and a bare enclosure that gives a sedentary frog no reason to move. Many amphibians keep eating as long as prey is available, so the frog will not stop on its own
SignsFat bulging visibly over the tympanum, the ridges above it sagging or folding over, and fat building in the armpits. General roundness is not the sign: a moderate shoulder roll is normal for this frog
ResponseThe ridge test on page 14, then a diet change at home: fewer insects per sitting, fatty items cut out, and more planting and height to climb through
RecoverySlow and steady, judged monthly on the ridges and photos rather than by the week. Once ridges read defined, go back to the normal adult schedule on page 12, not the old one
Fatty eyesCorneal lipidosis
CauseCholesterol and lipid deposited in the cornea, from a diet too high in fat
SignsA cloudy look to the eyes, or whitish plaques on them. In an obese frog, fat in the crests above the eyes can also start to block its vision
DiagnosisA vet exam. Cloudy eyes have other causes too, including the corneal swelling of a bacterial infection on page 23, which is a far more urgent problem
RecoveryTreatment is largely ineffective, but correcting the diet may stop it getting worse. That is the strongest argument for catching weight gain at the ridge stage
Fatty liverHepatic lipidosis
CauseSustained obesity raises the risk of this serious fatty liver condition
SignsAn obese frog that also looks enlarged through the body from fat laid down inside it, loses its appetite, or becomes lethargic
ResponseA vet visit. Advanced cases with liver involvement are past a home diet fix
RecoveryDepends on how far it has gone, which is why the monthly ridge check exists
The whole prevention plan, in one line Adults 2 to 3 times a week, 3 to 4 insects, waxworms once a week at most, a dense tall enclosure, and a look at the eardrum ridges every month.

Chytridiomycosis

The fungal disease behind amphibian declines worldwide. In a pet, an emergency that quarantine prevents.

Chytridiomycosis is caused by the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, Bd for short. It is known to affect hundreds of amphibian species, frogs, toads and salamanders alike, and has been linked to wild population declines going back to the 1970s. It infects the skin, the organ a frog drinks and partly breathes through.

Chytrid
CauseBd, carried in by an infected or contaminated animal, its water, or equipment that touched either. A new frog that skipped quarantine is the usual route
SignsLethargy and weight loss. Brown to red discoloration of the skin, and reddening of the belly that can look like red-leg. Excessive skin shedding and excess mucus. Swelling, abnormal posture, incoordination, and losing the ability to turn upright. Sudden death can be the only sign
DiagnosisA real-time PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test on a swab of the skin, a lab test that detects the fungus's DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid, the genetic code). It is rapid and it is the test to ask for. A skin scraping under the microscope can show the organism, but it is not always seen
TreatmentVet-prescribed antifungal baths, typically itraconazole daily for about 10 to 11 days. Keeping the frog at the warm end of its range, as the vet directs, may help halt the infection
RecoveryIf it is diagnosed early, treatment can be effective. The vet decides on retesting. Everything the frog touched is disinfected or replaced, and the frog stays apart from any others until cleared
If you suspect it Isolate the frog at once in a clean tub with paper towel and treated water, wash your hands, use separate tools, and call the vet the same day. Do not wait to see if it eats. Do not move any water, plants or decor from that enclosure into another. Keep the frog in its normal range, within the 70 to 85°F (21 to 29°C) gradient, until the vet sets a treatment temperature.

Prevention is the real defense

  • Quarantine every new amphibian for 6 to 8 weeks, page 18, and ask for a chytrid swab during it. Treatment options are limited once the disease takes hold.
  • Never share water, plants or tools between enclosures without disinfecting them.
  • Buy captive-bred, from a seller who quarantines.
  • Never release a frog, and never dump enclosure water outdoors where wild amphibians live.
The shed connection Frogs shed their skin every few days, and shedding helps clear skin microbes. In chytrid-infected frogs, heavier infections go with faster sloughing. So a frog suddenly shedding far more often than usual, or with shed skin lying uneaten, is a reason to book a swab.

Red-leg syndrome & bacterial infection

Usually traced back to a dirty or poorly ventilated enclosure, exactly the kind of thing an owner controls.
Red-legBacterial dermatosepticemia
CauseBacteria, typically Aeromonas, with Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, Citrobacter and others also found. These live in the environment and take hold in a frog stressed by dirty water, a stale dish, constant high humidity and poor ventilation
SignsA red flush on the pale skin of the legs and belly, progressing to pinpoint bleeding under the skin and red-purple patches. Lethargy, loss of appetite, emaciation, skin ulcers. Cloudy eyes can come with it, from corneal swelling
DiagnosisA vet takes blood, or fluid from the body cavity if there is any, for culture, so the antibiotic is matched to the bacteria. Chytrid can look similar and may be tested for at the same visit
TreatmentAggressive and immediate: antibiotics chosen by the culture, often with antifungals and fluids. A full review of the enclosure goes with it
RecoveryGuarded. Even with early, aggressive treatment the likelihood of death is high, which is why prevention and a same-day call matter. A frog that recovers goes back into a setup that has been fixed, not the one that made it sick
Red on the belly or legs is a same-day call Isolate the frog in a clean tub with treated water and paper towel, keep it at its normal temperature, and phone the vet. Do not try antibiotics or salt at home.

Bacterial and skin infections

CauseEnclosures kept too humid without adequate ventilation, or a water dish that is not cleaned often enough. A scraped nose or a wound gives bacteria a way in
SignsDiscolored or reddened patches, sores, excess mucus, swelling, a frog that is dull and off its food. Shed skin left uneaten can be an early hint
DiagnosisAlways a vet when signs appear: skin infections in frogs can be bacterial or fungal, and the treatment differs
RecoveryThe real fix is usually in the setup itself: humidity that dips on page 7, a dish emptied daily on page 8, and ventilation that is never sealed off
The husbandry pattern behind all of it Overfeeding drives obesity. A dirty or under-ventilated enclosure drives red-leg and the other bacterial infections. Skipping quarantine is how chytrid gets in. All three are preventable, and a reliable thermometer and hygrometer catch the humidity drift before it becomes an infection.

Metabolic bone disease & vitamin A

Two nutritional diseases, both prevented by the supplement schedule and the UVB tube.

Metabolic bone disease, also called nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, is frequently observed in captive amphibians. The frog's body pulls calcium out of its own skeleton to keep its blood level up, and the bones soften and deform.

Bone diseaseMetabolic bone disease
CauseToo little calcium and vitamin D3 in the diet, missing or expired UVB, and insects with an inverted calcium to phosphorus ratio: undusted, un-gut-loaded crickets are the classic route
SignsEarly on, twitching or spastic cramping after exertion, and weakness. Later, a misshapen or droopy jaw, broken long bones, a curved spine, bent limbs, bloating, and sometimes a cloacal prolapse (tissue pushed out of the vent, the frog's single rear opening). Advanced cases end in tetany, uncontrolled muscle spasm
DiagnosisA vet exam and a review of the diet, the supplement routine and the UVB tube's age. Bring the jar and the date the tube went in
TreatmentCorrecting the husbandry, plus calcium given by mouth daily for around 30 days at a dose the vet sets, with UVB provided
RecoveryWeeks to months, and permanent deformities may remain. The aim is to stop it progressing
Prevention is cheap and simple Gut-load the insects, dust them on schedule (adults about once a week, juveniles about twice), use calcium with D3 if there is no UVB, and replace the UVB tube every 6 to 8 months. Page 13 and page 9.

Vitamin A deficiency: short tongue syndrome

CauseToo little vitamin A reaching the frog, usually a diet of unsupplemented insects
SignsLethargy and wasting, and a frog that strikes at prey and misses because its tongue no longer works properly: the tongue's lining changes and it stops catching
DiagnosisA vet exam and diet history. The classic story is a frog that is plainly hungry and keeps failing to catch
RecoveryVet-directed supplementation and a corrected diet. Do not dose vitamin A at home: excess has been suggested to interfere with vitamin D metabolism, so more of everything is not the answer
Why it is easy to miss A frog with early bone disease still looks round and still eats. The first real clue is often behavior, weakness or a cramp after exertion, rather than appearance. Weak limbs, a droopy jaw or twitching are a vet visit, not a supplement change and a wait.

Skin injuries, chemical exposure & dehydration

Three problems that all start at the skin, and all have a cause in the room.
Nose rubRostral abrasion
CauseRepeatedly striking or rubbing the snout on glass, screen or any barrier, usually a frog pushing along the bottom of the glass trying to get out, or one with too little cover
SignsA scraped, pink or raw snout, sometimes with a sore that does not heal
ResponseA raw snout can become a source of infection, so a sore that is red, swelling or not closing is a vet visit. Remove the cause the same day: a paper strip around the outside base of the glass, more cover, and a check that the gradient and humidity are in range
RecoveryA minor scrape heals once the rubbing stops. If the frog keeps at it, look for what it is trying to escape: heat, crowding, or a cagemate
ChemicalsToxic exposure
CauseAnything absorbed through the skin: disinfectants such as iodine, chlorhexidine and quaternary ammonium, chlorine or ammonia in the water, phenol cleaners, pesticides, cigarette smoke, or residue on a keeper's hands
SignsReddening of the skin, blood spots on the skin, increased mucus, and behavior swinging from hyperactive to lethargic
ResponseMove the frog into fresh, dechlorinated water at its normal temperature, shallow enough to sit in, then call the vet and tell them what it was exposed to. Remove the source, and air the room out
RecoveryDepends on the chemical and the dose. Everything the frog touched gets rinsed in plain hot water and dried, or replaced
Dehydration
CauseA dry enclosure, an empty or fouled water dish, too much heat, or a frog that escaped into a dry house. Amphibians need moisture to prevent drying out, and a frog loses water through its skin
SignsSunken eyes, color changes, dry to tacky skin and a thick slime coat. Activity and feeding both drop
ResponseA shallow soak in clean, dechlorinated water at the frog's normal temperature, no deeper than it sits. A frog that does not perk up, or one found dry and limp, is an emergency: a vet can give replacement fluids
RecoveryMild cases correct with the soak; severe ones need the vet. Either way, find out why: hygrometer, dish, warm end, lid
Burns and falls A heat source the frog can reach will burn it, and the belly skin is thin. Every heat source sits outside the enclosure, page 6. Falls from a sagging branch or a dropped hand cause injuries too: anchor every perch, and handle low over something soft. Any open wound on an amphibian goes to the vet, since the skin is the frog's barrier against infection.
Found an escaped frog? Pick it up with wet hands, put it in a container with a damp paper towel and a lid, and give it a shallow soak in treated water at room temperature. If it is dry, tacky or limp, call the vet. Then find how it got out, because it will try the same gap again.

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

The two edges are 65°F (18°C) and 90°F (32°C). Most of this page is keeping the frog between them, and damp.

When the power goes out

  • Put a battery thermometer where the frog sits. Act if it is heading below 65°F (18°C).
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold in the heat that is left.
  • Or move the frog in a ventilated tub with damp paper towel to the warmest room, usually one without exterior walls.
  • A wrapped hand warmer or hot water bottle beside the tub, never touching the frog.
  • Keep misting by hand with your stored treated water: the dish and the humidity still matter.
  • No candles, gas stove or open flame near the enclosure. Hold off feeding until warmth is back.
  • More than a day below 65°F (18°C): board the frog with a vet or a friend who has power.

When it gets too hot

  • Turn the heat source off first. In a heat wave the room is the heater.
  • Get the enclosure out of any sun and away from exterior walls, to the coolest room in the house.
  • More air: uncover the mesh, and run a fan across the room, not into the enclosure.
  • Make sure the dish is full of fresh treated water at room temperature, and mist the surfaces.
  • A frozen bottle wrapped in a towel can sit beside the enclosure, never inside it.
  • Warm end past 90°F (32°C) with a frog that is hyperactive, uncoordinated or lethargic, the signs of overheating: cool the room, give it fresh treated water at room temperature, and call the vet.

Transport and vet trips

  • A clean, well-ventilated plastic container with a moistened paper towel on the bottom and the lid on. No loose substrate and no water dish to slosh.
  • Set it inside a dark bag, box or small cooler to hold the temperature steady and keep the view calm.
  • In cold weather warm the car first, and on a trip of more than about 30 minutes below 65°F (18°C) add a wrapped hand warmer beside the container, not touching it. In hot weather cool the car first and keep the container out of the sun.
  • Never leave the frog in a parked car, hot or cold. Check it at stops rather than only on arrival.
