Cost overview

Initial setup

The bird runs $75 to $250 depending on the color mutation, with normal grey usually cheapest because it is the wild type and needs no selective breeding. A complete first setup lands most buyers at $320 to $860, and the cage is routinely the biggest single line, often more than the bird. Page 30 itemizes it.

Ongoing

Food, toys and cage upkeep run roughly $300 to $565 a year including the annual exam, which is $25 to $47 a month. An avian exam alone is $85 to $200 before any testing, and a reproductive emergency runs $300 to $800 or more. Bird insurance barely exists, so the emergency fund is you. Page 30 itemizes both.

The number that should drive the decision Not the price. The lifespan. A cockatiel bought for a ten-year-old is very likely still alive when that child finishes college, and the commonest reason one does not reach fifteen is a seed bowl nobody ever changed. If you are not prepared to still be feeding this bird in 2046, buy something else.
The one thing this species is famous for Night frights. A cockatiel startled in total darkness thrashes violently inside the cage and can injure a wing, a foot or its face on the bars before you reach the light switch. It is the single most preventable injury in this book, and the fix is a dim night light. Page 7.

Cage size, bar spacing & placement

The purchase that is hardest to undo, and the one number that is a safety limit.

Credible sources give different cage figures and both are printed here rather than averaged. LafeberVet gives a minimum of 20 to 24 in (50 to 60 cm) long and wide for a cockatiel. Care references commonly cite 20×20×30 in (51×51×76 cm) as a minimum, and many keepers aim closer to 24×24×30 in (61×61×76 cm) for comfortable movement. Treat 20×20 in of footprint as the floor below which a cage is not adequate, and 24×24×30 in or wider as what to actually buy. Nobody has ever regretted the bigger cage.

Shape matters as much as volume, and a cockatiel has a long tail to fit inside it. This is a horizontal flyer that crosses open ground in short fast bursts, not a climber, so a tall narrow cage sells you height the bird will not use and a tail it will wear against the bars. Given 30 in of width or 30 in of height at the same price, take the width every time.

Bar spacing: 1/2 in (13 mm) LafeberVet gives 0.5 to 0.75 in (1.3 to 1.9 cm) as the range. Care guidance for pet cockatiels is stricter and gives half an inch or smaller, and that is the figure to build to: a cockatiel's head is small enough that the top of the published range is a genuine entrapment risk, and a trapped bird panics and injures its neck long before you find it. Measure the cage yourself rather than trusting the box. Avoid round cages, which give a bird no corner to settle into, and check the finish: galvanized bars and hardware are a zinc source, covered on page 8.

Perches, dishes & what to leave out

The inside of the cage does more for the bird than the outside dimensions do.

Perches

A cage furnished with identical dowels loads the same points of the foot all day, every day, which is how pressure sores and foot fatigue start. Give the bird a choice: natural branch, rope, and a variety of diameters, roughly 1/2 to 3/4 in (13 to 19 mm), placed at different heights and far enough apart that crossing the cage takes a real hop or a wingbeat rather than a step.

TypeWhat it does
Natural branchIrregular diameter along its length, so the foot changes grip constantly. Apple, willow, manzanita and eucalyptus are all used. Unsprayed, scrubbed and dried, and free
Rope perchSoft and grippy, good for an older bird. Inspect weekly and retire it once it frays: loose fibers wrap around toes, and cockatiels are noted for swallowing them. Page 23
SwingWorth adding. Cockatiels frequently choose to sleep on one
Cement or mineral perchOne, low down, for nail wear. Never the highest perch, where the bird stands longest
Sandpaper perch coversLeave them out entirely. They abrade the sole of the foot and do not shorten a nail

Dishes and water

Two food dishes and one water dish, none of them under a perch. Stainless steel is easiest to actually get clean. Cockatiels bathe in the drinking water and foul it fast, so change it daily as a rule and again whenever it looks used. A separate shallow bathing dish two or three times a week is appreciated and keeps the drinking water usable.

The floor

Plain paper, changed daily. Newspaper, butcher paper or plain paper towel all work. The point of a paper liner is that you can read the droppings off it, which is the cheapest daily health check you have and is covered on page 26. Anything that hides a dropping, corncob, walnut shell, sand or wood chips, defeats that and grows mold underneath.

Leave these out of the cage Grit or gravel. Cockatiels hull seed before swallowing the kernel, so they do not need it, and a sick bird will gorge on it and obstruct. Mirrors, for a single hen especially, since a mirror is a mate and a mate is a hormonal trigger. Nest boxes, happy huts and any enclosed dark cavity, for the same reason: page 17. Anything galvanized, which is a zinc source. And frayed rope, loose threads and unravelling fabric, which this species eats.
Cuttlebone and a mineral block, permanently Both stay in the cage for the life of the bird. They are a calcium and trace mineral source and a beak surface, and they matter most for a hen, who will pull calcium out of her own skeleton to build an eggshell if the diet does not supply it. Light, sleep & night frights

Day length is the lever the whole hormonal section turns on, and darkness is where this species gets hurt.

A cockatiel needs 10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness every night. Not a dim room: dark, quiet, and undisturbed. A bird living in a lit living room until midnight is being told the days are long, and long days are a breeding signal. Almost everything on pages 17 and 18 traces back to a photoperiod nobody was managing.

If the family room stays lit and loud, the answer is a second smaller cage in a quiet room used only for sleeping, moved to at the same time each night. A cage cover works if the room genuinely goes quiet. What does not work is deciding the bird will adjust.

Night frights, and the two-dollar fix Cockatiels are far more prone than most parrots to thrashing panics in the dark. A car headlight sweeping the wall, a moth on the window, a smoke alarm chirp, and the bird explodes off the perch into the bars. Broken blood feathers, a bleeding cere, a damaged wing and a foot caught in a toy are all normal outcomes. Birds do not see in the dark, which is why a startled cockatiel cannot find a perch.

Leave a dim night light on in the room. Just enough to dispel the dark, not enough to shorten the night. Do not black the cage out completely: a cover on three sides and part of the top gives security while leaving the bird able to see where it is. If a night fright happens anyway, turn a light on and talk to the bird quietly until it settles back onto a perch. Then check it over for blood before you go back to bed.

Why sleep is a health page, not a lighting note

A sleep-deprived cockatiel is a louder, bitier, more anxious cockatiel, and sleep debt is one of the more common invisible drivers behind feather plucking on page 24. It is also the cheapest thing in this book to fix. Before you change the diet, buy a toy, or call a behaviorist about a screaming bird, count the hours of real darkness it is actually getting. It is very often nine.

A treat thirty minutes before lights out A small predictable snack shortly before the room goes dark gives the bird a reason to settle rather than to be startled by the change, and it puts food in a small bird before its longest fast of the day. Make it part of the routine on page 33.

Household hazards & bird-proofing

The most important page in this package. A bird's lungs make ordinary household air lethal.

A bird's respiratory system is built to extract oxygen on a continuous one-way flow, which is what makes flight possible and what makes airborne poisons far more dangerous to a cockatiel than to you. Fumes that make a person cough kill a 90 g bird. Nothing else on this list matters if this page is wrong.

PTFE: the one that kills with no warning Nonstick cookware releases polytetrafluoroethylene gas when overheated to about 536°F (280°C), which an empty or forgotten pan reaches in minutes. The gas causes direct caustic damage to the lung, and the Merck Veterinary Manual notes that acute death is often the only clinical sign. Documented cases describe multiple birds dead within half an hour of a single overheated pan, and the fumes travel: a bird in another room is not safe.

Cookware is not the only source. Merck also lists irons and ironing board covers, some self-cleaning ovens, heating elements in some reverse-cycle heat pumps, and some heat lamps. If a bird lives in the house, the practical answer is to replace nonstick pans with stainless steel or cast iron, never run an oven's self-clean cycle, and keep the cage out of the kitchen entirely.

Houseplants and food left out

A flighted cockatiel lands on things and tastes them. Avocado is the food most consistently flagged as genuinely toxic to birds, along with chocolate, caffeine and alcohol; page 12 has the full list. Assume any houseplant is on the table until you have checked it, and clear the counter before the bird comes out, because a cockatiel will find the wine glass.

The five-minute pre-flight check Windows and doors shut. Blinds partly drawn. Ceiling fan off. Hob off and pans away. Toilet lids down and sinks empty. Other pets out of the room. Candles out. Do it in the same order every time and it becomes automatic, which is the point.

One cockatiel or two, and the whistling trade-off

The decision that shapes everything else, and it is not the one most buyers think it is.

Cockatiels are flock birds. In the wild they are essentially never alone, and a bird kept singly has to get that flock from you. That is entirely possible, and it produces the tame, whistling, shoulder-riding cockatiel most people picture. It is also a real daily commitment: a solo bird needs a couple of hours of genuine interaction, most days, for the next fifteen years. A cockatiel that does not get it becomes chronically stressed, and that shows up as screaming, feather damage and illness rather than as sulking.

Answer this honestly first Will this bird get two hours of your attention a day, on the bad weeks as well as the good ones? If yes, one bird gives you the better pet and the calmer hormonal picture. If the honest answer is "some days," get two. A pair of cockatiels that entertain each other is a far kinder outcome than a single bird waiting alone for a person who is busy, and the difference in how tame they are is smaller than people expect.

If you go with two

Two hens or two cocks avoids the egg problem entirely and they will still bond. A mixed pair will breed given any cavity at all, so if you want a mixed pair without chicks, the rule is no nest box, no happy hut, no dark enclosed anything, and a photoperiod you actually control. Never simply put a new bird straight into the cage of an established one: page 27 covers the quarantine period and the introduction.

The talking question, answered honestly Cockatiels are whistlers far more than talkers. Males learn and endlessly repeat melodies, and some pick up short phrases; hens rarely do either to any degree. Buying a cockatiel specifically to get a talking bird is a bad bet, and a bird bought for a trick it may never perform is a bird at risk of being rehomed. Buy it for the whistling and the company, and treat words as a bonus.

Diet: pellets, seed & converting a seed eater

The single biggest lever on how long this bird lives. Everything in section 03 traces back here.

VCA gives the target as 75 to 80% formulated pellets, 20 to 25% fresh vegetables and fruit, and seed as a very small part of the diet rather than the base of it. That is the whole prescription. A seed mix is high in fat and carbohydrate and deficient in protein, vitamin A, and calcium, and a bird left to choose picks the sunflower and millet and leaves the rest, so the diet the bird actually eats is worse than the one on the label.

This is not a preference, it is the disease list Hypovitaminosis A, calcium deficiency, obesity and fatty liver disease all come from a seed bowl, and Merck notes that even a half-seed, half-pellet diet leaves a bird vitamin A deficient. Pages 20 and 18 are the consequences. If you change one thing after reading this book, change this.

How the day actually works

Cockatiels are free-choice eaters, not meal eaters. They have a fast metabolism and graze all day, so refresh the pellet bowl in the morning when the bird is hungriest and top it up rather than letting it sit empty. Fresh produce goes in daily and comes out after about two hours before it spoils. Treats and table food stay under roughly 10% of the weekly diet, and portions are smaller than they look: a teaspoon to a cockatiel is about what a dinner plate is to you.

Converting a seed eater, five methods

VCA is blunt that this can take days, weeks or months. There is no fast version, and none of these is a hunger strike. Pick one, or run several together.

MethodHow
Morning firstOffer pellets alone first thing, when the bird is hungriest, and put the seed back later in the day
Mix and shiftMix pellets into the seed and slowly decrease the seed share over weeks
Spread it flatScatter pellets on a flat surface or a shallow tray, which reads as ground feeding to a grass-seed eater
Grind it inGrind pellets to powder over a moist food, then increase the chunk size week by week

Withdraw the seed only once the bird is reliably eating pellets and produce, and weigh it through the change, on the gram scale from page 16. A bird that is losing weight is not converting, it is starving, and that is the one failure mode that matters.

Millet, honey sticks and grit Millet spray is the highest-value treat you own and the easiest thing to overfeed; keep it for training and hand-taming rather than the cage. VCA describes honey sticks plainly as seeds stuck together with sugar and honey, and similarly nutrient-deficient. And do not offer grit: cockatiels hull the seed before swallowing the kernel, so they have no use for it, and a sick bird will overeat it and obstruct.
Supplements A bird genuinely eating 75 to 80% pellets does not need added vitamins or minerals; the pellet is already fortified and stacking supplements on top can do harm. A bird still on seed, or a laying hen, is the exception, and that is a conversation with your vet rather than a jar off a shelf. The cuttlebone stays in either way.

Safe vegetables, greens & herbs

The 20 to 25% of the diet that is not pellets, and the part that fixes vitamin A.

Vegetables are where a cockatiel gets the vitamin A its ancestors got from fresh growing seed heads, and vitamin A deficiency is the commonest nutritional disease in pet birds. The rule that does the work is simple: dark green and dark orange. Pale, watery vegetables are not harmful, they are just close to empty, and they take up room in a small bird's day.

Getting a refuser to eat them

Many cockatiels reject vegetables on sight for weeks. Nothing about that is unusual and none of it means the bird dislikes the food; a cockatiel decides what is food by watching its flock eat it. What works, in rough order:

  • Eat it in front of the bird, obviously and with some enthusiasm, from your own plate. This is the single most effective trick with this species
  • Change the format, not the food. A refused chunk of carrot gets eaten grated, or woven through the cage bars, or clipped to a skewer
  • Offer it first thing, before the pellet bowl goes in
  • Chop it into the size of the pellets the bird already accepts, and mix
  • Keep offering. Twenty refusals then acceptance is a normal pattern, not a failure
Herbs count, and they are cheap Parsley, cilantro, basil, dill and mint are all safe and all get shredded with enthusiasm. A supermarket herb pot on the windowsill gives fresh greens for weeks and doubles as destructible enrichment. Rinse anything you did not grow, and never use plants sprayed for pests.
Two hours, then out Fresh food is a bacterial growth medium at room temperature. Put it in, let the bird work on it, and take the remains out after about two hours. A dish of yesterday's vegetables is a health risk, not a leftover.

Fruit, treats & the never-feed list

The smaller half of the fresh share, and the short list that is genuinely toxic.

Fruit sits inside the same 20 to 25% as the vegetables, not on top of it, and it should be the smaller part. It is mostly sugar and water, cockatiels love it, and a bird filling up on banana is a bird not eating kale. Wash everything, cut it small, and remove pits and apple seeds.

Treats, and the portion problem

Treats should stay under about 10% of the weekly diet, and the practical failure is not what people give but how much. A cockatiel weighs about 90 g. A level teaspoon of anything is a substantial meal, a whole millet spray is a day's calories, and half a grape is a portion. Break treats down before they go in, and give them by hand where possible, so the treat buys you training as well as calories. Page 14.

The seed dish is a treat dish now Once the bird is converted, the way to keep seed in the picture without undoing the work is to stop putting it in a bowl. Use it as the reward in a foraging toy, or as the payment in a training session on page 13. Same seed, a tenth of the quantity, and the bird has to work for it, which is the point.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Cockatiels finally have research of their own, and it points at the food bowl.

Two studies now exist on cockatiel enrichment specifically, a 2020 paper on cockatiel behavior in aviaries and a 2025 paper concluding that environmental enrichment improves behavior and welfare in captive cockatiels, with reduced feather-damaging behavior singled out as the outcome. Most parrot enrichment research has gone to larger or endangered species, so a bird kept in millions of homes was studied late.

The structural finding across parrots is the one to act on. Food offered in a bowl produces foraging times of well under an hour a day, and abnormal repetitive behavior fills the rest. That is not a small change to the routine, it is most of the bird's waking behavior missing.

Priority order, highest value first

  1. Move the food out of the bowl. Deliver part of the daily ration through things that have to be worked at. Start easy: a bird that has only ever been bowl-fed does not know food is inside a device, so stage one is food sitting visibly on top of a toy, not hidden inside it
  2. Company, whether a compatible bird or a real daily routine with you. Page 9
  3. Varied perch diameters and materials. A foot health intervention and a choice intervention at once. Page 6
  4. Shreddable, destructible material, rotated. Paper, softwood, palm, untreated natural fiber. Material that survives the week has not done its job

Handling, taming & reading the crest

Trust first, technique second. The crest tells you which one you are on.

The first two weeks: do nothing

A new cockatiel needs 7 to 14 days to settle before any handling attempt. Feed it, change the water, talk to it, sit near the cage and read out loud, and otherwise leave it alone. Rushing this is the commonest reason a bird takes months to tame instead of weeks, because everything you do in week one teaches the bird whether hands are safe.

The progression

  1. Sit beside the cage at the bird's level and talk softly. No hands
  2. Offer millet through the bars until the bird takes it without backing off
  3. Rest a still hand inside the cage, holding the millet, and wait. Do not follow the bird
  4. Move the millet closer to your hand each session until the bird has to stand on you to reach it
  5. Introduce the step-up cue, then practise it away from the cage where the bird is less territorial

Sessions of 10 to 15 minutes are plenty, and the bird's comfort sets the pace, not a schedule. Going back a step after a bad session costs one day. Pushing through a bad session costs a week.

Offer a flat palm, not a finger Fingers pointing down, thumb tucked in, so the bird steps onto a flat surface, or use a handheld perch. A single upright finger is an awkward thing to grip and most birds bite it rather than stand on it. And never chase a loose cockatiel to catch it: a panicked bird can fly into a window hard enough to injure itself or exhaust itself into collapse, and forcing the moment costs you weeks of trust for one evening's convenience.

Reading the crest

The crest is close to a mood dial, and reading it before a bite is one of the more useful skills with an unfamiliar bird.

CrestWhat it means

Stress signals, stop and wait

Hissing. A flattened or agitated crest. Lunging. Tail flicking. Fluffed feathers with rapid breathing. Scrambling to the far side of the cage. Neck stretching to keep you in view.

Comfort signals, carry on

Soft chirping. Preening while you are there. Beak grinding. A relaxed upright crest. Stretching one wing and leg together. Sleeping on one foot in your presence.

Where not to touch a cockatiel Head and neck only. Stroking the back, under the wings, or around the vent mimics a mate and is a direct hormonal trigger, which is how an affectionate handling routine turns into the egg problem on page 17. This applies to cocks as well as hens. Scratch the head, leave the body alone.

Wing clipping & flight

You will be asked about this in week one, usually by whoever is selling the trim. Here is both sides.

Wing clipping means shortening some of the primary flight feathers so the bird cannot gain height. It is not permanent: the cut feathers are dead structures and they are replaced at the next molt, so a clip lasts one molt cycle, roughly six to twelve months. It is also not a behavior tool. A clipped cockatiel is not tamer, calmer, quieter or less hormonal than a flighted one. It is a bird that cannot fly.

The case for clippingThe case against
Removes the escape risk from an open door or windowThe same risk is removed by closing the door. A clipped bird startled outdoors can still catch a gust and go, and it cannot fly back
Removes the collision risk from windows and mirrors in a new homeFlight is the bird's main exercise. Clipped birds gain weight and lose muscle, and both feed the pages in section 03
Makes handling an untamed bird easier in the first weeksHandling built on a bird that cannot leave is compliance, not trust. Page 14 builds the real thing
Reduces the risk in a home with a hob, a fish tank or a dogA clipped bird lands hard. Keel and leg injuries from bad landings are the common consequence, especially on hard floors
Blood feathers, and why a trim is not a haircut A growing feather has a live blood supply in its shaft and looks dark and full near the base. Cut one and it bleeds, sometimes badly enough to matter in a 90 g bird. Only fully mature feathers are ever trimmed, and knowing the difference is the whole skill. If you clip, an avian vet or an experienced groomer does it, and you watch the first time. Do not attempt a first clip yourself off a video.

If it is done, what a correct clip leaves

A correct trim leaves the bird able to glide down to the floor under control from a standing perch, symmetrically on both wings so it does not spiral. It does not leave a bird that drops like a stone, and it never removes every primary. Ask for a light, symmetrical trim and check the landing before you leave.

The honest recommendation

For most households, a flighted cockatiel in a bird-proofed room is the better outcome, and the effort goes into the pre-flight check on page 8 rather than into the bird. Clipping is defensible in a specific and temporary situation, a first month with an untamed bird, a house with young children, an owner who genuinely cannot secure the doors, and in those cases it is a decision to revisit at the next molt rather than a permanent policy.

Train a recall either way A cockatiel that flies to your hand on cue is safer than a clipped one, because you can retrieve it from anywhere in the room without a chase. Millet, a short distance, a consistent cue, twice a day. It is the single most useful behavior in this book and it takes a fortnight.

Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

VCA notes reproductive problems are even more common in cockatiels than in budgies. This is the page that prevents them.

A cockatiel hen will lay eggs with no male present and no intention on your part. She does it because the environment told her to, and every clutch costs her calcium she pulls out of her own skeleton. Chronic egg laying is laying clutch after clutch, out of season, and it ends in metabolic bone disease, weakness, and the emergency on page 18. It is close to entirely preventable, and the levers are environmental rather than medical.

The trigger list, and what to do about each

TriggerRemove it
Long daysThe main lever. 10 to 12 hours of darkness normally; to break a cycle, cut to 8 hours of light a day under vet advice. Page 7
Any dark enclosed cavityNest box, happy hut, tent, a cardboard box, the space behind the sofa, a kitchen drawer she found. All of it goes
Petting the back, wings or ventHead and neck only, by everyone in the house. Page 14

Do not remove the eggs This is the counterintuitive one and it is the one owners get wrong. A cockatiel clutch is 3 to 8 eggs, average 5, and incubation runs 19 to 21 days, so a hen allowed to sit typically gives up after roughly three weeks and stops.   • Calcium, permanently available. Cuttlebone and a mineral block in the cage, not offered occasionally. A hen on a seed diet is a hen in calcium debt

  • Weigh her daily through a laying period. Page 16
  • Extra protein while she is laying: a little cooked egg is traditional and effective
When the environment is not enough A hen who keeps laying after everything above has been done needs an avian vet, not another month of adjustments. Hormone therapy exists, in the form of injections or implants, and it is used exactly for this. Ask for it before she is depleted rather than after the first egg-binding episode.

Egg binding

A decision you make in ten minutes, not a condition you monitor. Read this before it happens.

Egg binding is a hen unable to pass an egg. VCA names cockatiels among the small birds most often affected, and states plainly that small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg-bound, from compromised circulation and pressure on the airways. There is no wait-and-see version of this page.

Signs, roughly in the order they appear Weakness, and sitting low or on the cage floor rather than perching. Straining, as if trying to pass a dropping, often repeatedly and with nothing produced. A visibly swollen or firm lower abdomen. Fluffed feathers, wide stance, laboured breathing. Sometimes an egg visible at the vent, or tissue prolapsing out of it. In late cases, leg weakness or paralysis on one or both sides, from the egg pressing on the nerves.

Any of these is a same-day emergency call. Not tomorrow morning.

Why it happens

CauseDetail
Low calciumThe commonest reason. Calcium is what makes the oviduct contract, so a depleted hen cannot push. Seed diets are deficient in calcium, selenium, and vitamins D and E
Obesity and lack of exerciseBoth from the same high-fat seed diet. Page 20
Chronic layingEach clutch depletes her further, so the risk rises with every one. Page 17
A first clutch, or an old henBoth carry higher risk
An oversized or misshapen eggOften itself a nutritional problem: soft-shelled and shell-less eggs point straight back at calcium
Cold, or no suitable place to layAn environmental contribution rather than a cause on its own

What the vet will do

Supplemental heat, injectable fluids, calcium and vitamin D3 are the first-line supportive treatment, and many hens pass the egg once those are on board. Oxytocin may be given to stimulate contraction. If that fails, the egg may be lubricated and eased out, or collapsed by aspirating its contents through a needle so the shell can be removed in pieces. Surgery, a salpingohysterectomy, is the last resort and is also the permanent solution for a hen who binds repeatedly.

What you can do in the car Keep her warm and quiet in a small carrier, ideally around 85 to 90°F (29 to 32°C) with a covered heat source she cannot touch directly, and get moving. Warmth and humidity are genuinely supportive. Do not try to pull, squeeze or manipulate the egg: the shell breaks inside her, and that turns a treatable emergency into a surgical one. Do not give calcium orally to a collapsed bird.
Prevention is four things A pellet diet, a permanent cuttlebone, 10 to 12 hours of darkness, and no cavities. That is it. Every hen this happens to is a hen who was missing at least one of them, and the four together cost roughly nothing.

Vitamin A, calcium & fatty liver

One cause, three diseases. This page is what a seed bowl actually buys.

Hypovitaminosis A

The commonest nutritional disease in pet birds, and Merck is specific that it is not only all-seed diets that cause it: even a diet of half seed and half pellets leaves a bird deficient. Vitamin A maintains the lining of the airways, mouth and eyes, so a deficient bird loses its first line of defence against infection, which is why so many respiratory cases on page 21 are nutritional underneath.

SignWhat you see
Airway and sinusNasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eye, conjunctivitis, laboured breathing
MouthWhite plaques inside the mouth and around the eyes, and loss of the normal papillae on the roof of the mouth. Your vet looks for this specifically
KidneysExcessive drinking and excessive urination, which shows on the paper as a wet ring
FeathersPoor feather quality, and feather picking. Page 24
AppetiteAnorexia, often the last sign rather than the first

Treatment is injectable vitamin A given by a vet in acute cases, alongside treating any secondary infection, but the actual fix is the diet conversion on page 10 and the dark orange and dark green vegetables on page 11.

Calcium deficiency

Calcium runs through this species by a different route than in most parrots: not through a hypocalcemia syndrome, which Merck highlights in African greys rather than cockatiels, but through eggs. A laying hen mobilizes calcium out of her own skeleton to build shells, and on a seed diet she never replaces it. Signs of depletion are weakness, incoordination, tremors, depression, seizures, and fractures that happen from nothing. In a breeding hen it shows first as thin-shelled, soft or shell-less eggs, which is the warning that egg binding on page 18 is next.

Prevention is a permanent cuttlebone and mineral block, a pellet diet that carries vitamin D3 so the calcium can be used, and controlling laying at all. Page 17.

Obesity and hepatic lipidosis

A high-fat seed diet plus a cage-bound bird produces obesity, and obesity in a bird means fat deposited in and around the liver. Merck's working figure for obesity is roughly 20% over ideal weight, which in a 90 g cockatiel is about 18 g and is invisible without the scale on page 16. Fat accumulation in the abdomen presses on the air sacs, so the first thing many owners notice is that the bird gets breathless crossing the room, or goes lame.

Fatty liver disease is quiet until it is not A bird with hepatic lipidosis may show nothing for a long time, then present with a distended abdomen, laboured breathing, poor feather quality, or overgrown, soft or oddly coloured beak and nail keratin, which is a genuinely useful early sign because you can see it during a head scratch. It is managed by a vet with a controlled diet change. Do not crash-diet a fat bird yourself. Rapid weight loss in a bird mobilizes fat into a liver that is already struggling and can kill it.
The whole prevention plan, in one line Pellets at 75 to 80%, dark vegetables daily, seed as a training reward rather than a meal, a cuttlebone in the cage, and enough flight to keep the chest muscle on. Everything on this page is downstream of that.

Respiratory disease & psittacosis

Breathing trouble in a bird is always an emergency, and this species is named first on the psittacosis list.

A bird's respiratory system has very little reserve. There is no mild version of avian breathing difficulty, and a cockatiel that is visibly working to breathe is a bird in trouble now. Learn what normal looks like while it is healthy: 40 to 50 breaths a minute at rest, with the tail perfectly still.

Same-day signs Tail bobbing with each breath, which is the bird recruiting its whole body to move air. Open-mouth breathing. A clicking, wheezing or rasping sound. A change or loss of voice, which means the problem is at the syrinx. Discharge or bubbles at the nostrils, or a stained, matted cere. Sitting fluffed with the neck stretched up. Any of these is a phone call, not a wait.

Psittacosis, and the part that concerns you

Psittacosis, or avian chlamydiosis, is caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. The public health compendium on this disease names cockatiels first among the pet birds most often behind human cases, alongside parakeets and conures, so this is not a footnote for this species.

In the birdDetail
Often nothing at allA cockatiel can carry it with no signs whatsoever, shedding it in droppings and respiratory secretions to other birds. This is why a new bird is quarantined and tested rather than trusted. Page 27

PBFD, polyomavirus & other viral disease

The reason a new bird is quarantined and tested, rather than introduced.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

PBFD is a circovirus affecting over 50 species of bird, concentrated in parrots from Africa, Australia, New Zealand and Indonesia, which puts the cockatiel squarely in the susceptible group. It is rare in New World parrots such as macaws, amazons and conures. Infection can happen at any age but is most common in birds under three years old.

FormWhoWhat happens
PeracuteNewly hatched chicksDeath, often before any feather sign
AcuteYoung birds growing their first feathersRapid, severe feather abnormality during that development
ChronicOlder birdsProgressive worsening at each successive molt, which is how most owners meet it

Signs. Feathers come in stunted, club-shaped, or pinched off at the shaft, with premature molting and eventually bald areas. In cockatoos, and the cockatiel is one, the beak becomes elongated, excessively flaky or unusually shiny, and breaks easily, because the powder down that normally dusts and dulls the beak has failed. A glossy beak on a cockatiel is worth a mention to your vet.

Spread and diagnosis. The virus enters through the mouth, nose or cloaca and is shed heavily in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust, which is what makes shared air a route. Incubation is anything from weeks to years. Diagnosis is a blood test or an oral swab for the virus DNA, or a skin and feather biopsy.

There is no treatment and no cure Most affected birds die of secondary infections within months to years. That is the entire reason page 27 exists as a page: a 30-day quarantine and a PCR panel before a new bird joins an existing one is the only control you have, and it is cheap compared with what it prevents.

Avian polyomavirus

A second virus quarantine is designed to catch, and one that behaves very differently. It is most associated with budgerigars, where it is known as budgerigar fledgling disease, but it infects other psittacines including cockatiels. The dangerous form is in unweaned chicks, where it causes crop stasis, haemorrhage under the skin, and death within a day or two, and it is largely a breeder and pet-shop problem rather than a risk to a settled adult bird. A vaccine exists and is used in aviaries. Its practical relevance to a pet owner is the same as PBFD's: it is on the PCR panel, and it is a reason not to buy a bird from a shop with sick birds in the next cage.

What to ask for before you buy

  • Whether the bird has been tested for PBFD, polyomavirus and chlamydia, and to see the results
  • Whether any bird in the building has been ill in the last month
  • Whether birds from different sources share air, dishes, or a cleaning cloth
  • Whether you can return the bird if a vet check within a week finds something. A good breeder says yes without hesitating
Book the well-bird exam for week one Not week four. A baseline exam on a bird that looks fine gives your vet a healthy reference to compare against later, catches the things you cannot see, and starts the relationship you will want at 10pm in year six. Budget it as part of the purchase, on page 30.

Foreign bodies, heavy metal, mites & injuries

The shorter conditions, including two the cockatiel is specifically known for.

Swallowed rope and fabric

Cockatiels are noted as frequent offenders for ingesting rope fibers, and the combination of curiosity and a beak built for exploring makes it a real risk in an under-proofed home. A swallowed length of fiber can obstruct the gut or, worse, act as a linear foreign body that saws through it. The same behavior wraps loose threads around a toe and cuts off the blood supply.

Prevention: inspect rope perches and fabric toys weekly, retire anything frayed rather than trimming it, and keep the bird off blankets, towels and knitted things during out-of-cage time. Signs of an obstruction are regurgitation, straining, a sudden stop in droppings, and a bird that goes quiet. Same-day vet.

Heavy metal poisoning

The other thing this species is known for swallowing. Sources and signs are on page 8; what matters here is that both lead and zinc are treatable with chelation if caught, and that the signs, weight loss, regurgitation, diarrhea, weakness, excessive drinking, incoordination and in bad cases seizures, look like a dozen other things. A bird with unexplained neurological signs gets a metal level checked. Zinc chelates faster than lead, because zinc is not stored in bone.

Giardia, and the itch nobody expects

The cockatiel differential worth knowing Giardia is an intestinal protozoal parasite that cockatiels are specifically noted as susceptible to, and its effect is not what you would guess. VCA describes infected birds as having loose stools or being intensely itchy, attacking themselves violently, especially under the wings, and that itch can drive a feather-destructive habit that then looks entirely behavioral. A cockatiel that plucks under the wings deserves a fecal test before anyone starts rearranging the cage. Page 24.

Feather plucking & behavioral health

Easier to prevent than to reverse, and it is a medical question before it is a behavioral one.

Feather-destructive behavior is the bird damaging or removing its own feathers, most often on the chest, legs, and under the wings, where it can reach. The head is the one place a bird cannot pluck itself, which is a useful diagnostic: feather loss including the head is a disease, not a habit. Everything else needs working through.

A vet visit comes first, every time Assuming plucking is behavioral before a vet has ruled out a medical cause is the commonest and costliest mistake with this species. The medical list is long and several of its entries are specifically cockatiel problems: Giardia and its violent under-wing itch (page 23), vitamin A deficiency (page 20), PBFD (page 22), bacterial skin infection, and pain from an internal problem. Fix a real cause and the behavior often stops. Rearrange the cage instead and you lose months.

Catching it early

The window matters. Established plucking becomes self-soothing and habitual, and can continue after the original cause is gone, which is why prevention is worth so much more than treatment here. Watch for time spent preening one specific area beyond normal grooming, small bald patches on the chest or under the wings appearing gradually, and any increase in agitation that coincides with a change at home. Act at the first two, not at the bald patch.

Screaming is the same conversation Persistent screaming in a cockatiel is almost always a contact call from a bird that has lost its flock, and it responds to the same list: more company, more to do, more sleep, a predictable routine. What makes it worse is reacting to it, because any reliable response, including an annoyed one, teaches the bird that the call worked. Answer the quiet chirps and the whistles instead. That is the whole technique.
When to bring in a professional If plucking escalates after several weeks of consistent change, or if the bird has broken skin or drawn blood, do two things in parallel: an avian vet visit to rule out a medical cause, and a referral to a certified avian behaviorist if the bird is cleared. Waiting makes the behavior more entrenched, and this is one of the few cases where the specialist is genuinely better than more effort.

Molt & seasonal behavior

The normal process people mistake for illness, and the abnormal one people mistake for molt.

What a normal molt looks like

Molting is the orderly replacement of the whole plumage, usually once or twice a year, most often after the breeding season. Feathers are shed and regrown in a symmetrical, staggered pattern, matching feathers going on each wing so the bird can still fly, and new feathers push up before the old ones drop. You should never see a bald bird. What you will see is loose feathers on the cage floor and pin feathers: new feathers still encased in a waxy keratin sheath, which look like short spines, particularly around the head and neck.

During a moltWhat is happening
Quieter, touchier, sleeps moreGrowing a full set of feathers is metabolically expensive. A molting bird is genuinely tired and its head is full of sore pin feathers. Handle less, scratch gently
Eats moreThe demand for protein and calcium rises sharply. This is the time for a little cooked egg, and for making sure the pellets are actually being eaten
Wants more bathingMisting or a shallow dish softens the sheaths. Offer more often than usual
Off food, brieflyHeavy molt is a recognized reason a cockatiel goes off its food. Watch the scale. A drop is still a drop
Head scratches become a favorOnly the flock can open the sheaths on the head. Gentle rolling of a sheath between finger and thumb is welcomed by a tame bird, but never touch a sheath that is still blue at the base
The three-way differential owners get wrong Molt: symmetrical, new feathers visibly coming through, feathers found intact on the cage floor, head included, bird otherwise well.
Plucking: asymmetrical, confined to where the bird can reach, head feathers untouched, chewed or broken shafts left behind. Page 24.
PBFD: feathers coming in wrong rather than falling out, club-shaped, pinched or stunted, worsening at each molt, often with a shiny or flaking beak. Page 22.
If you cannot tell, photograph the bird and the floor and take both to the vet.

The hormonal season

Cockatiels are triggered into breeding condition by lengthening days and abundant food, and in the northern hemisphere that usually means spring, though housed birds under artificial light lose the seasonality and can go hormonal at any time, including autumn. What you will see: more regurgitating onto a toy, mirror or hand, more territorial defence of the cage, a hen shredding paper and hunting for cavities, a cock whistling incessantly and displaying with wings held out and heart-shaped, and a general increase in biting.

None of it is bad behavior and none of it responds to discipline. It responds to the levers on page 17: shorten the day, remove the cavities, stop the body petting, and move the food into foraging.

Support the molt, do not medicate it A molting bird needs food, sleep, water to bathe in, and to be left alone a bit. It does not need a molt supplement, and a bird eating 75 to 80% pellets is already getting what those products sell. If a molt drags on for months, goes bald in patches, or the bird stays unwell after it, that is a vet visit rather than a longer molt.

Reading droppings & hydration

The cheapest daily health check you own, and the reason the tray is lined with plain paper.

A bird dropping has three components, and telling them apart is the whole skill. The feces is the solid, formed, coiled part. The urates are the chalky white part, uric acid crystals that let a bird excrete nitrogen with almost no water. The urine is the clear liquid halo around the outside. Any of the three can change independently, and which one changed is what tells you where the problem is.

What you seeWhat it usually means

The 24-hour rule Get familiar with your own bird's normal droppings, because once you know them, anything else is abnormal by definition. A single odd dropping is not an emergency; you changed the food, or it ate a blueberry. Droppings that stay abnormal for more than about 24 hours are a vet visit. Photograph the paper before you throw it away, and take the photo with you.

Hydration

A dehydrated bird has dry, tacky mouth tissue, dull sunken eyes, skin over the keel that stays tented when gently lifted, and urates that go thick and yellow. Cockatiels drink relatively little and get a real share of their water from fresh food, which is one more reason page 11 matters. Change water daily, and again after a bath: a cockatiel will bathe in its drinking bowl and then be reluctant to drink from it.

Line the tray so you can read it Plain paper, changed daily, no grate in the way if you can manage it. Corncob, sand and wood chip all hide droppings, absorb them, and grow mold underneath, which turns the most informative surface in the cage into the least. Page 6.

Quarantine & adding a second bird

The one page that protects a bird you already own. Skipping it is how a healthy flock ends.

A new bird can carry PBFD, polyomavirus or chlamydia with no visible sign at all, shedding all three in droppings, crop secretions and feather dust. Quarantine is not about whether the new bird looks well. It looks well precisely because that is how these diseases work.

30 days minimum, and test The public health guidance for birds exposed to other birds at shows, sales or events is to quarantine for at least 30 days and test before adding them to a group. Take that as the floor. In a household with an existing bird, 45 to 60 days is the safer figure, because it covers a slow shedder and gives time to repeat a test. There is no version of this that takes a weekend.

How to actually run it

RuleWhy
A separate room, ideally not on the same air handlingFeather dust carries PBFD, and it travels further than droppings do. A different floor is better than a different corner
Separate dishes, tools, and cleaning clothsCross-contamination is usually a bucket or a sponge, not the birds

Then the introduction

  1. Cages side by side, a few feet apart, for several days. Let them see and call to each other with no contact
  2. Move the cages closer over a week, watching for interest rather than lunging at the bars
  3. Neutral ground first. The first physical meeting happens on a play stand or a table, never inside either cage, and never inside the resident bird's cage. That is the resident's territory and it will be defended
  4. Short supervised sessions, extended as they get boring. Normal negotiation is beak fencing, some chasing and a lot of noise; a real fight is one bird pinning another or grabbing a foot and not letting go. Separate for that, not for the noise
  5. A shared cage last, and only if it is genuinely big enough for two, with two of every resource so nothing is worth guarding
They may simply not like each other Cockatiels are social but they are not indiscriminate, and a pairing can fail. Have a plan for two cages permanently before you buy the second bird, not after. Two birds who live beside each other and call back and forth are still much better company than one bird alone.
Quarantine even if it is your only bird A 30-day settling period in a quiet room, with a vet exam at the start of it, is good for a single new bird too. It catches problems while your expectations are still forming, it gives the bird the low-stimulus start page 14 asks for, and it means you find out about a health problem before you have fallen in love with the bird.

Power outages, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in an outage is not the cold. It is what people light to fix the cold.
The rule that inverts from every other pet A reptile in a power cut is a temperature problem. A cockatiel in a power cut is a fumes problem. Candles, kerosene and propane heaters, generators running near a window, a gas hob left on for warmth, and a wood stove that smokes are all far more dangerous to a 90 g bird than a cold night is. If the power goes out, the first thing you decide is what you are not going to light in the bird's air.

If the power goes out

  • Keep feeding. A cockatiel has almost no energy reserve and eats through the day. Food and water stay available whatever else is happening
  • Move the cage to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors, and cover three sides with blankets to hold warmth while leaving airflow at the front
  • Body heat beats fire. A carrier under a blanket in an occupied room, or a covered hot water bottle the bird cannot touch directly, is safer than any flame
  • Ventilate anything you do burn. If a heater has to run, it runs in a different room with the door closed and a window cracked, and the bird is not in that room
  • Keep the night light plan. A battery night light in the emergency box means an outage does not also become a night fright. Page 7
