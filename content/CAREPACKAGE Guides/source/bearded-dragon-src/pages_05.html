Red flags & finding a vet

What to watch for, what to tell the vet, and how to find one before you need one.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see A juvenile refusing food for 2 to 3 days, or an adult for 1 to 2 weeks outside brumation, or any refusal with lethargy, abnormal stool or a swollen belly · tremors or muscle twitching · swelling in the jaw or limbs, limping, or trouble moving, climbing or lifting the body · labored or open-mouth breathing, or discharge from eyes, nose or mouth · a beard that stays dark for days · diarrhea, or runny or foul-smelling stool for more than 2 days · straining without producing waste, or no stool in 5+ days (3 in a juvenile that eats) · stargazing · any skin lesion or discoloration · swollen or bleeding gums, or cheesy material in the mouth · a toe or tail tip turning dark, dry or hard · eyes swollen or closed more than 2 days · blistered, blackened or peeling skin · sunken eyes · any tissue protruding from the vent · a gravid female digging or straining for more than 24 to 48 hours with no eggs.

What to tell the vet

Current basking and cool-side temps, and how you measured them
UVB tube type, age, distance, and whether there's mesh in the way
Diet breakdown (insects versus greens) and supplement schedule over the last 2 weeks
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening
Last normal bowel movement, and what it looked like
Weight trend from the Owner Log, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag if you can

Why UVB and temperature come up first

MBD, respiratory infection, impaction and appetite loss trace back to a UVB or temperature problem more often than anything else, which is why a vet asks about your setup first.

Finding a reptile vet

Many dog and cat practices do not see reptiles, so find one before the first emergency. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory of its members. Ask a clinic how many reptiles it sees, whether it treats bearded dragons routinely, whether it runs fecal tests in house, and who covers emergencies after hours, then write the number on page {{P:emergency}}. See the vet at least once a year, twice if your vet prefers; the yearly visit adds blood work (a blood count and a liver and kidney panel) to the exam and fecal test.

Metabolic bone disease

The most common serious illness in pet bearded dragons, and almost always preventable.

What it is: without enough usable calcium, the body pulls calcium out of the skeleton to keep the blood level steady, and the bones soften and bend. The veterinary name is nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism. Cause: insufficient UVB, insufficient calcium, too much phosphorus relative to calcium, or a dragon kept too cool to digest, over weeks to months. Juveniles under two are hit hardest because their bones are still forming, and a growing dragon builds every gram of new bone from calcium it can only absorb with vitamin D3.

StageSigns
EarlyLower appetite and energy; less moving around; twitching toes, a slight tremor when held; reluctance to climb; a jaw that looks a little soft or receded
LaterA swollen or rubbery lower jaw; trouble walking, or no longer lifting the body clear of the floor; soft, bowed or swollen limbs; a bent or zigzag tail or spine; twitching that progresses to full muscle spasms; fractures from minor falls; cloacal prolapse (tissue from the vent)
SevereSeizures

Diagnosis: physical exam plus an X-ray to check bone density, sometimes a blood calcium panel. Response: vet diagnosis and treatment immediately. This does not self-correct and worsens quickly. Treatment: correcting the lighting and the diet comes first, plus fluids, feeding support and injected calcium for a dragon already in crisis. Typical treatment cost runs $200 to $500 or more. Recovery: caught while the dragon is still eating, the outlook is good, and mild cases improve over weeks to months. Treatment does not straighten a jaw or limb that has already healed crooked, so a dragon that recovers from severe MBD usually keeps its shape for life.

While you wait for the appointment Remove climbing furniture and lower the basking platform. A dragon with weakened bones can fracture a limb in a fall that wouldn't hurt a healthy one. Handle as little as possible and support the whole body when you must.
Prevention beats treatment A T5 HO tube at the right distance, replaced on time (page {{P:uvb}}), and the calcium schedule (page {{P:supplements}}) prevent nearly all MBD. This is the single highest-leverage thing in this whole package. A coil bulb, a tube behind glass, or a tube past its replacement date are the usual ways it fails.

Impaction, dehydration & prolapse

Three problems that often travel together: a dry, cold or blocked gut, and the straining that follows.

Impaction

What it is: a blockage in the digestive tract. Cause: loose substrate, oversized or hard-shelled feeder insects, dehydration, or temperatures too low to digest properly. Signs: no droppings for several days despite still eating, straining without producing waste, a firm lump along the belly, lethargy, loss of appetite, and weakness or dragging in the hind legs. Diagnosis: the vet feels the belly and takes an X-ray to locate and size the blockage. Recovery: mild, early cases sometimes resolve with vet-guided warm soaks, fluids and gentle belly massage; a real blockage may need imaging, fluids or surgery.

Concrete threshold If your dragon hasn't defecated in five or more days, or a juvenile that normally goes daily hasn't in three, it's time to see a vet. Don't wait longer to "see if it resolves." Surgery, when needed, commonly runs $800 to $2,500.

Dehydration

In a reptile, dehydration usually comes from a long stretch off food or no water it can reach, not from vomiting or diarrhea as in mammals. Chronically low calcium makes a dragon less able to cope with it.

Signs of dehydration Wrinkled or loose skin outside a shed, sunken eyes, thick or stringy saliva, yellow or hard urates, lethargy, and skin that stays tented for a moment when gently pinched on the side. Mild cases respond to soaks and hydrating foods (page {{P:stool}}). A dragon that is sunken and lethargic needs a vet for fluids, not more soaking at home.

Prolapse

Pink or red tissue protruding from the vent that doesn't go back in on its own within a few minutes. It is a symptom of an underlying problem, not a condition on its own: dehydration, constipation, straining while laying eggs, and the muscle weakness of MBD are the usual triggers. Keep the tissue moist with a clean wet cloth or saline, never try to push it back in, and get to a vet the same day. It is always treated as an emergency.

Respiratory infection, atadenovirus & yellow fungus

Three conditions worth knowing by name before you ever see them.

Respiratory infection

Cause: most often an enclosure that is too cool, or humidity that sits high for days. Signs: open-mouth or labored breathing at rest, audible clicking or wheezing, mucus or bubbles at the nose or mouth, nasal discharge, cloudy eyes, puffing the throat repeatedly, lethargy, loss of appetite. Diagnosis: physical exam and listening to the lungs; the vet will ask about your temperature setup directly. Response: it can be fatal without treatment and needs prescription antibiotics, not a wait-and-see approach. Correct the temperature and humidity in parallel, or it recurs. Occasional gaping while basking is normal temperature control, not infection.

Atadenovirus (ADV)

What it is: a highly contagious virus, most often seen in young dragons, spread by direct contact and by droppings on shared tools, dishes, hands and enclosures. Signs: failure to thrive, stunted growth, chronic poor condition despite correct husbandry, weakness, paralysis, and neurological signs, most distinctively "stargazing," the head held tilted up and back. It causes fatal liver and gut disease. Reality check: there is no cure, only supportive care, and some infected adults show nothing while shedding the virus. A vet can test a new dragon with a PCR swab. Quarantine any new reptile for 3 to 6 months in a separate room with separate tools (page {{P:arrival}}), and never share feeder tubs between animals.

Yellow fungus disease

What it is: a contagious, aggressive fungal skin infection caused by Nannizziopsis guarroi, long grouped under the older name CANV (short for Chrysosporium anamorph of Nannizziopsis vriesii). Signs: yellow, tan or brown discolored, crusty or thickened scales, often starting on the head, limbs or belly, sometimes with a foul odor, that spread and ulcerate over weeks. Diagnosis: skin biopsy or culture. Response: prompt, aggressive antifungal treatment for months, and strict isolation from other reptiles. The outlook ranges from guarded to grave (uncertain at best, often poor), so see a vet the day you notice the first patch. It spreads by contact and shared substrate or tools.

The common thread All three are far less common in a correctly heated, correctly lit, single-dragon enclosure with good quarantine hygiene for anything new. Prevention here is mostly husbandry, not luck.

Parasites, mouth rot & eye problems

Less dramatic than the last page, and far more common.

Internal parasites

Pinworms and coccidia (single-celled gut parasites) turn up even in dragons from good breeders. A low pinworm count on its own often causes no real trouble in an adult; coccidia in a juvenile can trigger real illness fast. Signs: runny or foul-smelling stool, undigested food in the droppings, weight loss, poor appetite, lethargy. Loose stool is often the first sign, before foul-smelling stool and weight loss follow. Many infections show nothing at all, which is why testing beats waiting.

  • A fecal exam at the first vet visit and every year after catches most of these before they get serious.
  • Bring a fresh sample, less than 24 hours old, in a sealed bag. Older samples make some parasites harder to identify under the microscope.
  • Never deworm blindly with an over-the-counter product. The dose depends on which parasite is present and the dragon's exact weight, and treating blind can underdose a real infection or dose a dragon that never needed it.

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

Signs: swollen or bleeding gums, yellow-white cheesy material in the mouth, drooling, refusing food, a jaw that looks puffy on one side. Dark or stained teeth on their own are normal in adults. Cause: stress, low temperatures, a mouth injury from live prey or hard food, sometimes MBD. Response: a vet visit for cleaning and antibiotics, and not one to put off: left alone, the infection spreads into the jawbone.

Eye problems

Eyes held shut or a little puffy for a day or two during a head shed is normal. Swollen, closed or weeping eyes that last longer usually point to a UVB bulb too close or too strong (coil bulbs at short range are the classic cause), a vitamin A imbalance, a scratch, or an infection. Check the distance between the UVB tube and the basking spot first, since it is the most common cause and the easiest to rule out. See a vet if it persists past two days or comes with discharge.

Shedding, tail and toe rot & burns

Skin problems a weekly look catches early.

A normal shed

Juveniles shed far more often than adults. The skin looks dull and gray for a few days first, and appetite often dips. Help it along by raising humidity toward the top of the 30 to 40% range during the shed itself, not permanently, and by giving rough surfaces to rub on: cork bark, slate and branches.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Most common on toes, the tail tip and around the eyes. Rings of old shed can tighten around a toe or tail tip and cut off its blood supply, and the damage builds across shed cycles. To loosen it, soak the dragon in water at enclosure temperature, chin-deep, for 30 minutes, supervised throughout, then ease the loose shed off a toe with a damp cotton swab. Never peel unshed skin by force. Low humidity is the usual cause but only one of six: skin parasites, poor nutrition, infection, nothing rough to rub on, and low thyroid function all do it too. Repeated bad sheds in a correct setup are a vet conversation, not a humidity problem.

Tail rot and toe loss

Signs: a toe or the tail tip turns dark, dry and hard, and the dark area creeps upward over the following days. Cause: stuck shed cutting off circulation is the most common, but a bite from a cage mate or an oversized feeder insect, or a fall, does the same damage. Response: vet promptly. Caught early it is often treatable; caught late, the tissue has died and the only option is amputating the affected section to stop it spreading. Bearded dragons don't regrow lost tail tissue, so whatever is amputated is permanent. Check toes and the tail tip at every shed.

Burns

Burns most often come from hot rocks, an unguarded bulb, or a basking surface running hotter than intended, and show as blistered, blackened or peeling skin, usually on the back or belly. Cool the area with room-temperature water and fix the heat source immediately. Anything more than a small red patch needs a vet, because reptile burns often look worse over the following days rather than better. Check the basking surface with the infrared thermometer on any new setup.

The shed check At every shed, look at each toe and the tail tip in good light. A thin ring of old skin is easy to soak off today, and a dark, dry toe tip next week is a vet visit.

Reading poop, urates & hydration

Stool is the earliest free health check you get. Look at it before you clean it up.

Urates are the white part of a reptile's droppings. Instead of liquid urine, the body turns nitrogen waste into uric acid, made in the liver from protein digestion, and passes it as a white to cream paste alongside the stool. Its color and texture are the fastest daily read on hydration you have.

Hydration

Most of a dragon's water comes from food. Many barely touch a dish, some drink from one regularly, so keep a shallow dish available and refresh it daily. Beyond that:

  • Mist the salad, not the enclosure walls. Water droplets on greens get eaten; a damp enclosure just raises humidity into the range that risks respiratory infection.
  • A shallow lukewarm soak, 15 to 20 minutes, once or twice a week, chest-deep at most and never unsupervised. Most dragons drink during it.
  • Hydrating foods: hornworms, silkworms, cucumber or melon pieces, cactus pad, as extras rather than staples.
  • A dragon that won't drink: drip water onto the snout with a syringe or dropper and let it lick. Never squirt water into the mouth.

The number that matters is your own dragon's baseline. A real change from it, in how often, how it looks, or how it smells, is the signal, not any fixed count.
