Enclosure size, type & where it goes

4×2×2 ft (120×60×60 cm, roughly 120 gallons) is the adult minimum, not an upgrade to get around to eventually. As a rule of thumb, enclosure length should be at least three times your dragon's total body length. A juvenile-sized enclosure is outgrown within a year, so buying the adult size from day one costs the same or less than buying twice. A front-opening enclosure makes daily handling and cleaning far easier than a top-opening one.

PVC (polyvinyl chloride, a rigid plastic) or sealed wood enclosures with glass fronts hold heat far better than all-glass tanks, weigh less, and are what most experienced keepers use. A glass tank works, it just needs stronger heating to hold the gradient, and a screen top changes the UVB mounting distance (page {{P:uvb}}).

Never House two bearded dragons together. They are solitary and will stress, fight, or resource-guard even in large enclosures. Cohabitation is not "social enrichment" for this species.

Where it goes

  • Out of the kitchen and away from anywhere food is made or served.
  • Out of direct sun. A glass enclosure in sunlight heats far faster than the open air around it, even briefly.
  • Away from heavy foot traffic, and out of sight of other pets.
  • In a room that stays above 65°F (18°C) at night, if you can, so no night heat is needed.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Cover three sides to cut down on reflections. A dragon that keeps mistaking its reflection for a rival can develop chronic stress.
  • Two hides minimum, one on each end, so your dragon never has to choose between security and temperature. Anchor everything an adult can climb so nothing shifts under its weight.

Humidity30 to 40% baseline. Brief spikes after a soak are normal; days above 50 to 60% cause respiratory and skin trouble.

Measure the basking surface with an infrared thermometer aimed at the platform, not the air. Surface temperature is what the dragon actually feels, and it is the number stick-on dial thermometers get wrong. A basic hygrometer (humidity gauge) is worth the few dollars it costs.

Two fixtures, not one

UVB and heat are separate jobs, and mixing them up is one of the most common setup mistakes. A T5 HO UVB tube gives ultraviolet light but almost no usable heat. Pair it with a separate halogen or incandescent basking bulb over the same platform, run through a dimming thermostat, and confirm the surface temperature with the infrared thermometer rather than trusting the wattage.

Wattage is a starting guess, not a target. A 75 watt halogen is the usual starting point in a 4×2×2 ft enclosure; let the thermostat and the infrared thermometer settle it.

Heat that can't burn

  • Keep every lamp at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) above the highest point the dragon can reach, behind a guard if it sits inside the enclosure. A lamp the dragon can touch will burn it at any setting.
  • No basking surface needs to read above 122°F (50°C). Past that, the enclosure is overheating.
  • Skip hot rocks and unguarded heat sources entirely. They are a common cause of thermal burns and add nothing a proper basking setup doesn't do better.
  • At night, add heat only if the room drops below 65°F (18°C), and only with a source that gives no light.

Thermostats, probes & timers

The thermostat is the safety device

A thermostat sits between the heat source and your dragon. It measures temperature at one point and cuts or lowers the power once that point reaches the setting, which stops a bulb that reads 105°F this morning from overshooting badly on a warm afternoon. Without one, a bulb draws full power with no ceiling at all.

Heat sourceController
Halogen or incandescent basking bulbDimming (proportional) thermostat. It turns the power down instead of switching the bulb off and on, which halogens hate and which makes the light flicker.
Ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projectorPulse proportional or dimming thermostat. Both heaters react fast enough that a plain on/off unit can overshoot before it cuts.
UVB tubeNever on a thermostat. It goes on a timer only.

Where the probe goes

Put the probe at the dragon's level on the basking platform; if your controller's manual gives another spot, follow the manual and still confirm the surface with the infrared thermometer. Hold it with a probe clip or suction cup, never tape, and never on the cool side. Tape over a probe throws off its reading, and a dragon can stick to exposed adhesive and tear scales. Set the thermostat so the infrared reading on the platform lands in the basking range. Never run any heat source unregulated.

How a thermostat fails

  • The probe moves. This is the most common failure, and it is silent: the display keeps showing an accurate reading for the wrong spot. Check the probe's position at every spot clean.
  • The thermostat sticks on. Rarer and more dangerous, because nothing limits the heat. Read the warm-end thermometer daily. The cool end always reads lower, and that gap is the gradient. A warm end that keeps climbing is the only warning of a stuck thermostat or a shifted probe.
  • Test it before the dragon arrives. Run the setup for 24 to 48 hours and watch the thermostat dim the bulb as the probe warms.

Timers and the light schedule

Put every light on a plug-in timer so the day length never depends on you remembering. 12 to 14 hours of light in summer and 10 to 12 in winter matches the natural cycle, and the seasonal shift is often what cues brumation, the winter slowdown on page {{P:brumation}}. All lights go off at night.

Night heat has no light

A light bulb cannot be used for night heat. If the room falls below 65°F (18°C) overnight, use a ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector on its own thermostat: both give heat with no visible light. Skip red, blue, and "moonlight" night bulbs entirely. A home that stays above 65°F at night needs no night heat at all.

UVB: tube, strength & mounting distance

This is the one piece of equipment worth spending real money on. UVB hitting the skin is how a dragon makes vitamin D3, and without vitamin D3 it cannot absorb calcium from its food. Use a linear T5 HO tube rated 10 to 14% UVB, each mounted to its own maker's distance chart; the distances below are for a 12% tube. Fit it in a reflector, spanning about two-thirds of the enclosure length on the basking side, never behind glass or plastic, which block UVB almost entirely. A mesh screen passes some UVB and filters the rest, so the mounting distance depends on whether a screen is in the way. Coil and compact bulbs are not adequate: they put UVB in one small spot instead of across the floor the dragon uses.

UVI targets

Bearded dragons are Ferguson Zone 3, open or partial-sun baskers. Aim for a gradient from 0 on the cool side up to 4.0 to 4.5 at the basking spot, or 3.0 or lower for hypomelanistic, silkback and translucent morphs, which burn more easily. The dragon sets its own dose by moving between light and shade, so keep the cool end dim.

Bulb schedule

Run 10 to 14 hours a day on a timer. Replace every 6 to 12 months for T5 HO, every 6 months for a T8 (an older, weaker tube 1 inch across). Output fades long before the bulb dims, so replace by the calendar and write the date on page {{P:equiplog}}.

Mounting distance

Distances are for a 12% T5 HO tube in its reflector, with nothing in between on the left. A 10% or 14% tube varies by length and output, so follow its own distance chart.

Measure from the tube to the highest point the dragon can reach, not just the basking slab. Every perch, branch and hide top must sit at or below the chart's distance from the tube, or the dragon can climb into too much UVB.

UVB: meter & daylight lamp

Measure, don't assume A handheld UVI meter removes the guesswork. A UV index meter is the only way to know the tube is at the right height and when to replace it; UV cards show total UV, not the UVB that makes vitamin D3. It is the most expensive item in a reptile kit and highly recommended: read the UVI at the basking spot, adjust the height to 4.0 to 4.5, and re-check every three months.

The optional daylight lamp

A bright daylight LED (light-emitting diode) lamp, around 6500 K (kelvin, the scale for the color of light), alongside the UVB tube helps with activity and appetite. It gives white light only: no UVB and no basking heat.

Substrate, furnishings & bioactive setups

Substrate

Paper towel, newspaper, or slate or ceramic tile are the safest options, especially while you're new. Tile holds basking heat like rock, wipes clean and cannot be swallowed. A separate dig box (page {{P:enrichment}}) gives a dragon on tile somewhere to dig.

Never Loose sand (including calcium sand), crushed walnut shell, wood chips, or gravel. All carry a real impaction risk if ingested, whatever the label says (page {{P:impaction}}).

Furnishings checklist

Basking platform (rock, slate, or sturdy wood) directly under the lamp
Two hides: one warm side, one cool side
Sturdy, well-anchored climbing branches at more than one height
Shallow water dish the dragon can climb out of easily
Food dish or slate for greens, wiped daily
Dig box, and a deeper lay box for adult females (page {{P:eggs}})

Bioactive setups

A bioactive enclosure is planted, with a living substrate and a cleanup crew of isopods and springtails, tiny invertebrates that break down waste. It gives a dragon somewhere to dig and explore and cuts full substrate changes to almost none once the crew is established. It is a bigger commitment than paper towel and not where most people should start, and it changes the floor and the cleaning, nothing else: the heat, UVB and diet stay exactly the same.

PartWhat it takes
SubstrateA pre-formulated arid bioactive substrate, 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) deep across the floor, topped with dry leaf litter. Deep enough for roots, digging, and a slightly damp bottom layer under a dry surface. A homemade sand, topsoil and clay mix makes a good loose substrate but is not bioactive-ready on its own. Use organic topsoil with no fertilizer if you mix your own.
Cleanup crewArid species: powder orange or powder blue isopods, dwarf white isopods, and arid springtails. Seed them in the damp lower layer and give them a few weeks to a month to multiply before the dragon goes in.
PlantsA dragon eats its plants, so every one must be safe to eat, free of pesticide, and able to take a desert basking lamp: succulents, spineless cacti, arid grasses. Rinse nursery plants first. Skip pothos, a poor pick for a grazing lizard. Keep plants out of the basking zone so they don't shade the dragon from the tube.
UpkeepWater plant bases or mist lightly and let the surface dry; over-misting turns an arid build into a swamp. Pick up visible waste daily and let the crew handle the rest. No monthly strip-down. Top up leaf litter about once a year. Run well, the substrate can go 10 years or more.

Cleaning & hygiene

Cleaning removes the waste. Disinfecting kills what is left. Hygiene keeps it off your hands.

Salmonella Healthy reptiles shed Salmonella bacteria in their droppings without being ill, on and off, and one negative test clears nothing. It travels from the dragon, its enclosure and anything it walks on to hands, then to mouths or food, so wash with soap and running water after every contact. Do not kiss the dragon, hold it to your face, or eat or drink while holding it. Children under 5, adults 65 and older and anyone with a weakened immune system should not handle the dragon or touch anything in its enclosure. Pregnant people are also at higher risk. An outbreak traced to pet bearded dragons, closed in December 2025, made 20 people sick across 14 states, put 9 in the hospital and killed 1.

Three clocks

WhenWhat to do
DailyRemove droppings, urates (the white part of the waste), uneaten food and shed skin as soon as you see them. Empty, wash and refill the water dish. Wipe the food dish.
WeeklyWipe down glass, walls, dishes, decor and the basking platform with a reptile-safe cleaner.
MonthlyMove the dragon to a secure temporary tub. Take everything out, scrub it with detergent and hot water, disinfect the enclosure, decor and dishes, rinse, dry, and put in fresh substrate.

Paper towel or newspaper is replaced whenever it is soiled. A bioactive enclosure skips the monthly strip-down: pick up what you can see and let the cleanup crew handle the rest.

Disinfecting, step by step

  • Scrub first. A film of waste shields bacteria from disinfectant, so it only works on a surface already cleaned.
  • Dilute household bleach 1:32, which is half a cup of bleach per gallon of water. It is inexpensive and works against bacteria and many viruses.
  • Keep it wet for 10 minutes. Bleach needs that long in contact to work, so don't wipe it straight off.
  • Rinse until no bleach smell remains, then let the enclosure and every item dry completely and air out before the dragon goes back in. Plan on a full day of airing.
Never the kitchen sink Never wash the dragon, its dishes, decor or enclosure in a kitchen sink, a bathroom sink, or a bath people use, or anywhere food is prepared. Keep a plastic tub for the job, or work in a utility sink or outdoors. If a bathtub does get used, clean it thoroughly afterward. Wash bedding and decor separately from anything used for food, and keep the dragon out of the kitchen entirely.

If someone in the house gets sick

The signs are ordinary ones: diarrhea, fever, stomach cramps. That is why the link gets missed. Tell the doctor there is a reptile in the house, even if nobody remembers touching it, since enclosure surfaces are enough on their own. It matters most for a child under 5, an adult 65 or older, someone pregnant, or someone with a weakened immune system, in whom the infection can spread beyond the gut.
