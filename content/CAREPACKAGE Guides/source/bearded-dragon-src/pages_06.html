Section 09 · Owner Tools

First 30 days

Day 1

Weigh the dragon in grams and photograph it and the setup for a baseline
Confirm basking surface, cool side and night temps with the infrared gun and a digital thermometer, not "feels warm"
Book the first exam with a reptile vet for within 48 hours, with a fresh stool sample under 24 hours old (page {{P:arrival}})
If another reptile lives here, set up the separate quarantine room and its own tools
Fill in the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}})
Start the equipment dates (page {{P:equiplog}})

Week 1

Let your dragon settle: hands off except feeding and spot-cleaning (page {{P:handling}})
Watch appetite and stool every day
Measure UVI with a meter if you have one, or verify the mounting distance (page {{P:uvb}})

Weeks 2 to 3

Begin short, gentle handling sessions if appetite and behavior look normal
Settle into a consistent feeding and supplement schedule (page {{P:supplements}}), and start the salad habit even if it's ignored
Start the Owner Log with a weekly weigh-in (page {{P:ownerlog}})

Week 4

Review the weight trend: steady gain for a juvenile, stable weight for an adult (page {{P:growth}})
Set a phone reminder for the UVB replacement date and a monthly thermostat check
Do the first monthly deep clean (page {{P:cleaning}})
Introduce the first enrichment items (page {{P:enrichlog}})

Days 31 to 90

Book any follow-up the vet recommended, and act on the fecal result if parasites showed up
Re-measure UVI directly if you have a meter; don't rely on the bulb's age alone
Look back at the Owner Log for any slow drift in weight, appetite, or temps
Fill in the pet-sitter sheet before you need it (page {{P:sitter}})
Keep any quarantine running to the full 3 to 6 months before the dragon shares a room, tools or air with another reptile
Recheck the sex at 6 months, and add a lay box if it's a female (page {{P:eggs}})

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

Read the warm-end and cool-end thermometers; a warm end that keeps climbing is the warning sign
Salad and insects per the age schedule (page {{P:diet}})
Dust supplements on the right days (page {{P:supplements}})
Wash and refill the water dish; remove uneaten insects before lights-out
Spot-clean droppings, and look at them before you do (page {{P:stool}})
Quick visual check: eyes, beard, posture, belly, appetite
Wash your hands after every contact

Weekly

Weigh in grams and log it (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Wipe down glass, walls, dishes, decor and the basking platform
Optional 15 to 20 minute soak, more often during a shed
Check the basking surface with the infrared gun
Rotate the greens so no single one is the whole salad

Monthly

Deep clean: everything out, scrubbed, disinfected, rinsed, dried, fresh substrate (page {{P:cleaning}})
Confirm the thermostat still cuts in, the probe hasn't moved, and the timers are on schedule
Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings
Reposition one furnishing; check climbing items still fit the dragon's size

Every 3 months

Measure UVI with a meter if you have one; adjust height or replace the bulb
Check nails and tail tip for overgrowth or old stuck shed

Seasonal

Shift the timer: 12 to 14 hours of light in summer, 10 to 12 in winter
Reassess room temperature swings; add night heat only if the room drops below 65°F (18°C)
Watch for early brumation signs in fall, and book the pre-brumation vet check (page {{P:brumation}})
Adult females: lay box ready from late winter through summer (page {{P:eggs}})

Yearly

Wellness exam, fecal test and blood work; many reptile vets prefer twice a year (page {{P:redflags}})
Replace the T5 HO tube by 12 months at the latest, sooner if the UVI has dropped (a T8 at 6 months)
Replace opened powders by the date on the jar
Section 09 · Owner Tools

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

A healthy adult tolerates one cool night without harm. The risk is days without heat, and feeding a dragon that can't digest.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Aim to keep the dragon above 65°F (18°C); 60°F (16°C) is the floor, and a day or two below it is when problems start.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold what heat is left.
  • Move the enclosure, or the dragon in a tub, to the warmest room in the house.
  • Use hand warmers or a hot-water bottle wrapped in a towel, placed beside the dragon, never touching it.
  • Stop feeding insects. Offer greens and water only until the basking spot is back.
  • Past a day or two below 60°F (16°C), board it with a vet, a friend with power, or a reptile shop.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, gas stoves, or open flame anywhere near the enclosure.
  • Don't put a heat pack in direct contact with the dragon; it can't move away fast enough and will burn.
  • Don't feed insects "to keep its strength up." Undigested food in a cold gut rots.
  • Don't warm a cold dragon in hot water; lukewarm only, and only once it's alert.
Outage kit Hand warmers, a hot-water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated tub, and your vet numbers written on paper.

In a heat wave

The cool end must stay at 75 to 85°F (24 to 29°C), so check it through the day: the thermostat can dim the basking bulb but cannot cool the room. Digging at the coolest corner, gaping, or sitting in the water means it is too hot. Keep the enclosure out of direct sun, which heats glass far faster than the room.

Leaving town

Transport and vet trips

  • A ventilated plastic tub or small carrier with a towel to grip, in a dark room-temperature bag or box. No loose substrate, no water dish.
  • Warm the car before loading in cold weather; a wrapped hand warmer beside the tub for trips over 30 minutes below 65°F (18°C). Check the tub every 20 to 30 minutes at a stop.
  • Don't feed insects the morning of a trip. Never leave the dragon in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly.
  • Moving house: set the enclosure up and running at the new place before the dragon arrives, same as day one.
