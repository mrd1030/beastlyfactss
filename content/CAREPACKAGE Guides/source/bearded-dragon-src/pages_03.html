Section 03 · Feeding

Diet by age

Bearded dragons are omnivores, and the ratio flips with age: juveniles eat mostly insects, adults mostly plants. This mirrors a real dietary shift documented in wild dragons, not an arbitrary husbandry rule. Only feed when the basking surface is at temperature. A dragon needs that heat to make stomach acid and digestive enzymes, so a dragon that can't bask can't digest.

Don't feed an adult like a juvenile Growth has stopped and metabolism has slowed by 18 months. An adult still eating a juvenile's insect ration gets fat, and fatty liver disease is the long-term risk. Fatty feeders such as waxworms and superworms as a daily food are the usual culprits.

Why a dragon stops eating

Most refusals are normal. Work through the list before assuming illness:

BrumationThe winter slowdown in dragons over a year old (page {{P:brumation}})
SheddingA short appetite drop around the shed
A gravid femaleEggs, with or without a male: off food 1 to 2 weeks before laying (page {{P:eggs}})
StressA new home, a small enclosure, another dragon, too much handling
Too coolA cold basking spot slows or stalls digestion
Illness or impactionWith other signs: lethargy, abnormal or no stool, straining, a swollen belly
A picky phaseBored with one food. Rule it out before assuming the worst
When it is a vet call A juvenile off food for 2 to 3 days, since it has far less room to wait. An adult off food for 1 to 2 weeks outside brumation. Any refusal at any age that comes with lethargy, abnormal stool or a swollen belly.

Captive-bred only, gut-loaded, and no wider than the space between the eyes.

Sizing and storage

Prey wider than the gap between the eyes is a choking and impaction risk, and in juveniles it can cause hind-leg weakness. When in doubt, go smaller and feed more. Keep feeders in a ventilated tub with room to move, since crowded crickets die fast, and never feed an insect that smells off or came from a tub with dead ones in it.

Gut-loading: feed the feeders

An insect is a container: what it ate last is what your dragon gets. Feed feeders a calcium-rich diet for 24 to 72 hours before they go in, 48 hours being the usual figure: collard, mustard, turnip or dandelion greens, butternut squash, or a dry commercial gut-load sold on its calcium content. Carrot, potato and apple keep insects alive but add almost no calcium, and spinach, beet greens and Swiss chard work against it. Insects empty their gut once off the food, so feed them out within a few hours. Gut-loading does not replace dusting (page {{P:supplements}}).

Wash, chop to bite size, and replace the salad daily rather than leaving it to wilt. If greens are refused, offer them before insects in the morning with a little grated squash or a berry mixed in. Juveniles often ignore salad until about 6 months; keep offering it.

Why the tiers exist. Oxalates, in spinach, chard, beet greens and parsley, bind calcium in the gut so the dragon can't absorb it, even from a food that is calcium-rich on paper. Goitrogens, in kale, cabbage and broccoli, can interfere with the thyroid in large, regular amounts. Staples dodge both.

Building the daily salad Two or three staple greens chopped fine (collards, mustard greens and dandelion greens is a reliable rotation), one staple vegetable grated on top, and a pinch from the occasional tier. Rotate the greens week to week so no single one is the whole salad, and on a calcium day with no insect feeding, dust the salad instead.

Supplements: calcium, vitamin D3 & multivitamin

The supplement schedule and the UVB distance are the two things a dragon's bones depend on.

SupplementHatchling & juvenileAdultNotes
Plain calcium (no vitamin D3, phosphorus-free)Near-daily, at most feedings2 to 3× per weekThe everyday dust. Light coating, not a snowball.
Calcium with D3Replaces plain calcium 2× per week2× per weekA backup to UVB, not a substitute.
Multivitamin (reptile-specific)1 to 2× per week1 to 2× per weekSkip the D3 calcium that day if the multivitamin already contains D3.
Why not D3 at every feeding Under a correct T5 HO tube the dragon makes its own vitamin D3. Adding supplemental D3 at every feeding on top of that risks vitamin D toxicity, which calcifies soft tissue and is far harder to treat than a calcium shortfall. Plain calcium is the daily dust; the D3 version is insurance.

Why the tube beats the powder

This has been tested directly. Oonincx and colleagues (2010) raised 84 hatchling bearded dragons either on rising doses of oral vitamin D3, up to four times the standard dose, or under UVB for 2 to 12 hours a day. At six months, the dragons under UVB had blood levels of the main circulating form of vitamin D3 18 times higher than the supplemented dragons, and as little as 2 hours of UVB a day kept those levels adequate. No dose of powder came close. That is why D3 in the dust is a backup and never the plan.

Dusting and gut-loading are two jobs

Feeder insects are a poor calcium source on their own. A dragon needs more calcium than phosphorus in its diet, roughly 2 parts to 1, and analyses by Finke put unsupplemented crickets near 1 part calcium to 9 of phosphorus and mealworms near 1 to 14. Black soldier fly larvae are the one common feeder close to or above 1 to 1. Gut-loading improves what is inside the insect; the powder adds calcium on the outside. Neither replaces the other, and neither counts for much without working UVB.

  • Dust right before feeding. Powder falls off, and an insect left dusted for an hour has groomed much of it away.
  • Read the label. The daily dust is a plain calcium carbonate powder with no added vitamins. Keep a D3 formula for the twice-weekly slot only.
  • Date the jars. Replace opened powders by the date on the jar, and log the date on page {{P:equiplog}}.
Females need more during a laying cycle Making eggs, even infertile ones, pulls calcium from her bones. Correct UVB and the schedule above cover most of it. Ask your vet about a temporary increase during an active cycle rather than guessing at a dose, and after she lays, dust her food with plain calcium daily for about two weeks (page {{P:eggs}}).

Growth, weight & body condition

The scale and the tail base say whether the feeding schedule is working.

Growth reference

Ranges are deliberately wide and healthy dragons fall outside them. What matters is the trend: steady gain for a juvenile, a stable weight for an adult.

AgeTotal lengthWeight
Hatchling (0 to 1 month)3 to 4 in (8 to 10 cm)under 10 g
2 months5 to 9 in (13 to 23 cm)10 to 40 g
3 to 4 months8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm)30 to 90 g
5 to 6 months11 to 16 in (28 to 41 cm)80 to 200 g
8 to 10 months13 to 20 in (33 to 51 cm)150 to 350 g
12 months16 to 22 in (41 to 56 cm)250 to 450 g
18 months and up (adult)16 to 24 in (41 to 61 cm)300 to 500 g typical; large males heavier

The weekly weigh-in

  • Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before the first feeding, so a full stomach doesn't skew the reading.
  • Use a kitchen or postal scale that reads to single grams, and write every reading down on page {{P:ownerlog}}.
  • Watch the line, not the number. A juvenile's climbs, with the odd flat or slightly lower week around a shed. An adult's stays roughly flat, apart from a dip around a shed or a laying cycle.

Body condition at a glance

Underweight

Hip bones and spine visible, tail base thin and triangular instead of round, the fat pads on top of the head sunken into hollows, eyes sunken. Skin wrinkles when the dragon isn't shedding. Rule out parasites and a basking spot that is too cool first, then feed up under vet guidance.

Ideal

Tail base full and rounded, head fat pads level with the skull, belly flat and off the ground when standing, a defined waist from above. Weight stable week to week in an adult, climbing steadily in a juvenile.

Overweight

Bulging fat pads on the head, jowls under the jaw, rolls at the armpits, a tail base thicker than the thighs, belly dragging. Common in adults fed like juveniles. Cut fatty feeders, shift toward salad, add climbing and free-roam. Fatty liver disease is the long-term risk.

The tail base is the most reliable place to look: under- and overweight dragons both show it first. A dragon from smaller stock that has always tracked the low end of its age bracket is not a problem to fix; that is its normal.

When a weight change is a vet visit A juvenile that stops gaining for 3 to 4 weeks. An adult that loses about 10 percent of its body weight outside brumation, or any weight loss that comes with appetite loss, lethargy, or abnormal stool. Bring the weight log; a trend line tells a vet more than a single reading.
