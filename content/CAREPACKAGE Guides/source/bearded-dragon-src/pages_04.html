Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

One of the most handleable pet lizards there is, once it has had time to settle.

Wait before the first session

Give a new dragon 7 to 14 days to settle before handling it beyond feeding and cleaning. It needs time to learn where the food and the basking spot are, and that its new home isn't a threat. That settling clock is separate from quarantine, which runs far longer: if another reptile already lives in the house, handling can start after two weeks but always comes last in the day, after the resident animals, with hands washed in between.

DaysWhat to do
1 to 3Hands off, except feeding and spot-cleaning.
4 to 7Calm presence near the enclosure, a hand resting inside at feeding time.
7 to 14First short handling sessions once appetite and stool look normal. Shorter and more frequent for juveniles, which are more fragile.

How to pick one up

Let the dragon see your hand coming, from the side or below, never from above, which reads as a predator strike. Never surprise or grab it. Scoop from underneath with both hands, supporting the chest, belly and all four legs so nothing dangles, and don't squeeze to hold it still: squeezing sets off an escape attempt rather than calming one. Never pick up or lift a bearded dragon by the tail.

How often and how long

Once it is settled and eating normally, a short session every day builds trust faster than occasional long ones. Start short and build up to about 10 to 15 minutes. Heat sets the ceiling: out of the enclosure too long, a dragon's body temperature drops, so a cooler room means a shorter session.

  • Don't handle within about an hour of a meal. Dragons need to bask to digest, and handling too soon can cause regurgitation.
  • Handle low, over a bed or couch, not standing. A dragon will launch off a hand without warning, and a fall that looks minor can cause a serious injury.
  • Mid-shed, let the skin come off on its own.
Put it down when you see A beard turning dark or black · skin darkening along the back and sides · backing away from your hand, or threatening to bite · struggling to get free · flattening, gaping or hissing. None is dangerous on its own, but each one means the session is over. End it calmly.

Who should hold it

A bearded dragon suits older children, with an adult supervising. Children under 5 should not handle one at all, and neither should adults 65 and older or anyone with a weakened immune system, because a healthy-looking dragon can still carry Salmonella (page {{P:cleaning}}). Wash hands with soap after every session, even a short one. Handled correctly and consistently, most bearded dragons become easygoing, and some seek out time on a shoulder or in a lap.

Glass surfing

Repetitive pacing or scratching along the glass. Work through the usual causes in order: a reflection or another pet in view; an enclosure that's too small, or too hot with no real cool side; boredom or hunger; and, in an adult female, a gravid dragon looking for somewhere to lay. Cover reflective surfaces on three sides and reduce disruptive activity near the enclosure before assuming it's just "personality." Enrichment helps only once the enclosure itself is right; fixing the underlying trigger resolves it faster than covering glass alone.

Quick differentiator Brumation is slow and low-energy. Glass surfing is repetitive and high-energy. If you're not sure which you're seeing, the energy level is usually the tell.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Undersizing the enclosure "because it's just a baby," then never upgrading once it's an adult.
  • Buying a UVB bulb once and never tracking its replacement date, or running a coil bulb and assuming it counts.
  • Running a basking bulb with no thermostat, or putting the probe on the cool side where it never triggers.
  • Skipping the cool side entirely, leaving no way for the dragon to cool down.
  • Dusting calcium with D3 at every feeding on top of strong UVB, or skipping calcium entirely because "the UVB handles it."
  • Feeding an adult like a juvenile, leading to obesity and fatty liver over time.
  • Trusting packaging that says "reptile safe" without checking it for this species. Loose sand is the classic example.
  • Skipping quarantine when introducing a new reptile, or sharing tools and feeder tubs between animals.
  • Estimating UVI by eye. Output can look bright while being too weak, or too strong for a sensitive morph.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after correct heat, UVB, diet, hydration and enclosure size, never instead of them. In this order:

1. Climbing structure

Sturdy branches, cork and ledges at varied heights let a dragon survey from above and choose its own distance from the heat. Rough textures help grip and wear the nails. Anchor everything; unstable stacked rocks are a crush-injury risk.

2. Dig box

A tub the dragon can turn around in, filled 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) deep with a 50/50 mix of organic topsoil and washed play sand, kept slightly damp. Never calcium sand. Adult females need a deeper version (page {{P:eggs}}).

3. Visual security

Partial barriers, plants, or a half-covered side let a dragon retreat from view without leaving the warm zone, which lowers baseline stress in busy rooms.

4. Foraging and hunting

Tong-feeding, short supervised insect hunts, scattered or hung greens, and a rolling treat ball the dragon nudges until insects fall out. Remove uneaten insects before lights-out.

5. Supervised free-roam

10 to 15 minutes to start, building toward 15 to 30 for some adults, in a warm, draft-free room with gaps blocked, other pets out, and cords, toxic plants and anything small enough to swallow removed. End it if the dragon cools, panics, blackens its beard or turns lethargic. Never unsupervised. Outdoor time is optional and more advanced: supervised, in a secure pen, with shade and water.

6. Novelty rotation

Reposition one furnishing every few weeks. Constant rearranging is stressful; a recognizable layout with one new thing is the sweet spot.

Choosing a dragon, quarantine & the first vet visit

Young, captive-bred, checked over in good light, and in front of a vet within 48 hours.

A young, captive-raised dragon makes the better pet. A breeder who hatched it can tell you its hatch date, its food, and when it last ate and shed. Buy only where the enclosures are clean and large enough. A rescue or an expo animal often comes with no reliable history: not a reason to refuse it, but a reason to run the full quarantine below.

Ask the seller: the hatch date; whether it was captive-bred; what it eats and when it last ate; whether it has been treated for anything; and whether it comes with a health guarantee. A good breeder answers without looking anything up.

Quarantine: 3 to 6 months

If another reptile already lives in the house, keep the new dragon completely apart for 3 to 6 months. The hobby often quotes 60 to 90 days, but that is a floor; some infections show nothing for months. With no other reptile, skip the separation but not the vet visit.

  • A separate room. Two enclosures on one shelf share the same air.
  • Paper towel, a hide and a water dish. Paper shows stool and regurgitated food at a glance.
  • Separate tools, and the new dragon last. Its own tongs, dishes and feeder tub, cared for after every other animal, with hands washed in between.
  • Restart the clock if anything flags: a positive fecal test, a regurgitated meal, unexplained weight loss. At the end, disinfect everything that touched it.
The first vet visit, within 48 hours Book a reptile vet before the dragon comes home (finding one is on page {{P:redflags}}). The first exam should include a weight, a look inside the mouth, eyes and nostrils, a skin and skeleton check, and a fecal test, so bring a fresh stool sample under 24 hours old in a sealed bag. Ask about a PCR (polymerase chain reaction) swab, the test for ADV (atadenovirus), before this dragon ever meets another. That visit is the baseline every later one is measured against.

Sexing & females that lay

Every female owner needs this page, whether or not a male has ever been in the house.

Telling male from female

Reliable from about 4 to 6 months; under 3 months it is very difficult, so treat pet-store sexing of a hatchling as a guess and recheck at 6 months. In a dim room, gently lift the tail to about 90 degrees and shine a phone flashlight down through the base of the tail from above. Males show two hemipenal bulges, one either side of a central dip, just past the vent. Females show a single central bulge or nothing at all. Adult males also have larger femoral pores (a row of waxy pores along the underside of the thighs), a bigger head and a darker beard display, but none of these is reliable on its own in a young dragon.

Females lay without a male

From roughly one to two years old, many females develop egg follicles and lay infertile clutches, typically 15 to 30 eggs, sometimes two or three clutches in a season. Some never do. Neither is a problem by itself. What matters is recognizing it and giving her somewhere to dig.

Signs she is gravid

  • A big appetite for a few weeks, then going off food in the one to two weeks before laying.
  • Restlessness: digging at corners, glass surfing, pacing the enclosure, especially in the afternoon.
  • A rounder belly that feels like a bag of marbles when she walks. Never squeeze to check; eggs bruise easily.

The lay box

A container she can turn around in, bigger rather than smaller, filled 6 to 12 in (15 to 30 cm) deep with a 50/50 mix of organic topsoil and washed play sand, damp enough that a tunnel holds its shape without dripping. Too dry and her tunnels collapse; too wet risks mold. Put it on the warm side as soon as you see the signs and leave it until she's done. She may dig several test holes over a few days, then lay in one sitting and fill it in.

After she lays Remove the infertile eggs. She'll be thin and hungry: feed her well, dust her food with plain calcium daily for about two weeks, and offer a soak. A female that cycles constantly, several times a year, every year, is worth a vet conversation about long-term options, including spaying.

Egg binding

Dystocia: a female who cannot pass the eggs she is carrying. A same-day emergency.

Egg binding is common in reptiles and can be life-threatening. A normal gravid female and one in trouble look alike at first, since both stop eating. The line is clear once you know it: a normal gravid female stays bright, active and alert; a female with egg binding quickly becomes sick, weak, or unresponsive.

Call a reptile vet the same day if She has been digging and straining for more than 24 to 48 hours with no eggs, or laid part of a clutch and then stopped and looks exhausted · she shows lethargy, sunken eyes, hind-leg weakness, refuses water, or has a firm lump in the belly that doesn't shift over several days · she is past the normal "off food" stage but losing condition instead of moving toward laying.

What causes it

  • Husbandry: wrong UVB, wrong temperatures, low humidity, or no suitable place to lay.
  • Her body: obesity, poor muscle tone from too little activity, and age.
  • The eggs: oversized or malformed eggs, or something blocking the passage.
  • Nutrition and illness: too little calcium or protein, dehydration, infection, constipation, or an abscess or mass.

What the vet does

A vet confirms it with an exam, gentle feeling of the belly, blood work and X-rays, sometimes ultrasound. Unlike in mammals, a reptile can hold eggs for weeks or months past the normal window, which makes the urgency easy to misjudge; it is a reason to get a diagnosis, never a reason to wait at home. With no blockage, treatment can mean fluids, calcium, and a hormone injection to start laying. With a blockage, inducing her is unsafe and surgery is needed, usually removing the egg-carrying organs so the problem cannot recur. A related condition, follicles that never ovulate, shows up as masses on imaging and goes down the same route. The longer it waits, the fewer options remain. A lay box that is always available to an adult female is the cheapest prevention there is.

Brumation

A seasonal slowdown, not a medical crisis, once a vet has ruled illness out.

Brumation is a reptile's winter slowdown, cued by shorter days and cooler nights. It is not true hibernation: a brumating dragon stays somewhat responsive and still gets up now and then to drink. It is most common in dragons over a year old; a hatchling or young juvenile that goes quiet and stops eating is far more likely to be sick than seasonal.

Signs, over several weeks: eating less, then not at all · more time in a hide, away from the basking spot · sleeping earlier and getting up later · less activity and interest in handling · a darker beard or body in some dragons · little or no stool. Adults often pass only urate, with no solid stool, for days at a time.

Brumation or illness? The same signs describe a sick dragon: low temperatures, parasites, dehydration and poor nutrition all look like this. Brumation develops slowly, over weeks, in a dragon over a year old, as the days shorten. A dragon going downhill quickly, at a normal room temperature, out of season, or under a year old is a vet visit, not a waiting game.

The pre-brumation vet check

Before letting a slowdown run, have a reptile vet do an exam and a fecal test, so the dragon goes in healthy rather than already sick or carrying parasites; one that goes in thin or with a parasite load can come out far worse. Once it is cleared, don't force-feed it, and don't try to stop brumation in a healthy adult.

The weekly routine

Weigh itEvery week, in grams, to catch weight loss long before it shows.
Offer a soakShallow and lukewarm, about 15 minutes, chest-deep at most, never unsupervised. Most ignore it; some drink.
Greens, no insectsFresh greens every few days. No insects: a dragon that isn't basking can't digest them.
Shorten the light8 to 10 hours on the timer, or lights off if it is fully asleep in its hide and not coming out to bask.
Hold the floorKeep the room above 65°F (18°C), checked on a thermometer, with a ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector on its own thermostat if it drops below overnight.

Brumation typically runs 1 to 3 months. Resume the full schedule the day the dragon reappears and basks on its own. Appetite usually returns within 2 to 3 weeks: offer small amounts at first, gut-loaded and dusted from the first meal back.

Call the vet if It stretches past about three and a half months · weight drops more than about 10 percent · appetite hasn't returned within 2 to 3 weeks of waking · sunken eyes, thick or stringy saliva, diarrhea, foul or bloody stool · limb swelling, tremors, or trouble lifting the body · any of this under a year old, or a slowdown at a normal 60 to 70°F (16 to 21°C) room temperature with no seasonal change in the light.

Legal without a permit almost everywhere in the United States, with a short list of exceptions.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet bearded dragon is legal with no permit in 46, barred in 2, permit-only in 2, and legal on a condition in 2.
