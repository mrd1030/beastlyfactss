Section 03 · Feeding

Diet & feeding by age

Ball pythons are carnivores fed whole prey: frozen-thawed mice and rats, the nutritionally complete staple from hatchling to adult. Unlike most pets, the schedule gets slower as the animal gets bigger, not faster. A healthy adult needs a meal only every one to two weeks, and that slow metabolism is a feature of the species, not a problem to solve.

AgeHow oftenWhat
Hatchling, 0 to 6 monthsEvery 5 to 7 daysFuzzy mice (a mouse with its first fur), moving up to hoppers (a just-weaned mouse)
Juvenile, 6 to 18 monthsEvery 7 to 10 daysHoppers to adult mice, then small rats as the snake grows
Adult, 18 months and upEvery 10 to 14 daysFor most adults, a medium rat

Two checks set the size

The prey is no wider than the snake at mid-body, its thickest point, and weighs about 10 to 15% of the snake's body weight: a 400 g snake takes prey of 40 to 60 g. Too large risks regurgitation; too small gives too little nutrition over time. Obesity is real and common in captive ball pythons, and a snake that eats every ten days is doing exactly what it should.

Where and how to feed

Feed inside the enclosure, with long tongs. Moving a ball python to a separate feeding tub was standard advice for years, but the added stress is now understood to raise both regurgitation and refusal in an already food-shy species. As long as loose substrate isn't a choking hazard, feeding in place is the better practice. Tongs every time also prevent the bites that come from a snake smelling rodent on your hand.

  • Wait 48 to 72 hours after a meal before handling. Handling on a full stomach is one of the most common causes of regurgitation, which is hard on the digestive system and slow to recover from.
  • Water: a heavy bowl big enough to soak in, changed daily, since the snake soaks in it as well as drinking from it.
  • No supplements. Whole prey is the complete diet. As a rule a snake fed healthy, well-fed rodents needs no vitamin or calcium powder, so the quality of the rodent matters more than anything dusted on it.
  • Weigh it. The scale catches overfeeding earlier than your eye does (page {{P:growth}}).

A new snake, and a young one

Leave a new ball python alone for one to two weeks before the first meal, and offer the prey type and size the seller was using. Refusals in the first weeks in a new home are common, and some snakes go weeks or longer before they settle. A young snake that has been eating for you is different: a hatchling or juvenile that refuses two meals in a row, about two weeks at its schedule, gets the refusal checks (page {{P:refusals}}) and a weekly weigh-in, and any weight loss goes to a vet, since a youngster has no reserves to spare.

Never feed live prey A live rodent can bite and seriously injure a snake, sometimes fatally, especially one that isn't hungry enough to strike immediately. Frozen-thawed removes that risk entirely. The full never-feed list comes next.

Mice and rats are the staple, and for most snakes they stay that way for life. Everything else is a situational tool.

Thawing, warming & freezer storage

The step most often done carelessly, and it matters more than the schedule.

Thawing, step by step

  1. Thaw it fully. In the refrigerator, the standard method, or sealed in a bag and submerged in cool to lukewarm water, changing the water as needed until there is no frozen core left.
  2. Never on the counter for hours, and never in a microwave, which heats unevenly: the inside can stay frozen while pockets on the outside get hot enough to burn the mouth, and the prey can rupture.
  3. Warm it gently. Don't feed it straight from the refrigerator. Prey goes in warm to the touch, never cold. A few minutes under a lamp, or warm water run over the outside of the sealed bag, does it.
  4. Judge by touch. It should feel warm to the touch. Warm gradually and check often rather than leaving it under heat and walking away.
Why warmth matters Ball pythons hunt partly by heat. The pits along the upper lip pick up infrared radiation from anything warmer than its surroundings, and a cold, thawed rodent doesn't register as prey the same way. Overheating is the opposite mistake: hot water or a lamp left too long can cook the prey inside, which risks the body cavity rupturing when the snake constricts or swallows, and can scald the inside of the snake's mouth.

Presenting it

Offer the warmed prey with long tongs, inside the enclosure (page {{P:diet}}). A snake that ignores a well-warmed meal is telling you something about its setup, its shed or the season, and the order to check those in comes next.

Freezer storage

  • Storage life. Food held at a steady 0°F (−18°C) stays safe indefinitely, but quality declines the longer it sits. Don't let stock sit a year or more just because it is technically fine.
  • Freezer burn, the gray, dried-out patches on long-frozen prey, is a quality problem rather than a safety one, and it happens when packaging isn't airtight. Keep prey in its original sealed packaging or rebag it well.
  • Thaw once. Never refreeze a rodent that has been thawed. If you thaw more than the snake will eat, the extra is a loss.
Handling it safely yourself Frozen feeder rodents have been linked to several Salmonella outbreaks in people. Wash your hands with soap and water right after handling frozen rodents or anything in the area where they were stored, thawed or prepared. Keep them away from where human food and drink are stored, prepared, served or eaten, and use containers and utensils that are only for feeder rodents. Don't thaw prey in a sink you wash dishes in, and never use a microwave or kitchen utensil meant for human food.

Why a ball python stops eating

This species is famous for refusing food. A refusal in a snake in good condition is usually normal, not an emergency.

A healthy-looking ball python in good body condition that turns down a meal or two is not automatically in trouble. Check the causes in this order, the enclosure first:

 CauseHow you can tellDo
1Temperature or humidity offThe most common husbandry cause. Read the thermometers and hygrometer, and check the thermostat probeFix the gradient and humidity before anything else (page {{P:temperature}})
2A shed underwayDull skin, cloudy or blue eyesWait. The refusal lifts on its own once the shed is complete (page {{P:shedding}})
3StressA new enclosure, too much handling, or too little hide coverLeave it alone, add cover, cut handling back
4Breeding season or the seasonal slowdownRoughly October to March, males especially, with no change to the enclosureNormal while its weight holds. Weigh it and wait (page {{P:winter}})

If everything checks out

With the temperature right, the humidity right and no other symptoms, it is often reasonable to offer food again in a couple of weeks before assuming anything is wrong. When you do, make the offer as good as it can be: the prey warmed properly, since a cold rodent doesn't register to the heat pits (page {{P:thawing}}), and, for a stubborn or newly arrived snake, an African soft-furred rat, whose different scent can trigger a strike a mouse or rat does not (page {{P:prey}}).

How long is too long

A healthy adult ball python can go 3 to 6 months without eating and lose surprisingly little condition, and some fast through an entire breeding season without lasting harm. A female brooding eggs can go 4 to 6 months, living off fat built up beforehand. What turns a fast into a problem is what comes with it.

When a refusal becomes a vet visit Rapid weight loss, or weight down more than 10% from the starting weight, or visibly thinner along the spine and tail base. Lethargy alongside the refusal. Mucus around the mouth, or wheezing. Still refusing by spring, or refusing out of season while losing weight. Any of these ends the wait.
Refused or regurgitated? A refused meal is a snake that never struck. A regurgitated meal was eaten and came back up. The first is usually normal and needs patience. The second means something in the setup, the handling or the snake's health is wrong, and it has its own plan (page {{P:parasites}}).

Growth, weight & body condition

The scale says whether the feeding schedule is working. The cross-section says what that weight looks like.

Weight, not length

Length is easy to picture and the wrong number to build a growth record on, because length and body mass don't grow in step. Falk, Snow and Reed (2017), measuring 248 wild Burmese pythons, found mass climbs disproportionately faster than length as a snake gets bigger, so two snakes of the same length can carry very different muscle and fat. A gram or kitchen scale captures muscle, fat and gut content in one number.

  • Monthly is a reasonable default for a growing juvenile, less often once an adult has leveled off.
  • Every 1 to 2 weeks through a long refusal or the winter slowdown, and weekly during quarantine.
  • Same scale every time, and write every reading on page {{P:ownerlog}}. One weigh-in tells you almost nothing; three or four that climb, hold flat or drop tell you a great deal.

What growth looks like

Hatchlings start at about a foot (30 cm) and reach roughly 3 ft (90 cm) by age three; adults settle at 3 to 5 ft (90 to 150 cm), females significantly larger. Weight has a few fixed points; beyond them, a steady climb in a juvenile and a stable line in an adult are what matter.

  • A young female around 800 to 1,000 g often stops eating for a stretch as follicles start to develop (page {{P:eggs}}). Smaller meals, offered less often, until she eats normally again.
  • Breeding females mature at around 1,500 g, from about 27 to 31 months. Males stay smaller.
  • A loss of more than 10% of body weight in any fast is the line where waiting stops being reasonable.

Body condition: the cross-section test

Pick the snake up, support it well, and look at it end-on, imagining a slice straight across the body. Run a gentle hand along the back.

Underweight

Triangular in cross-section, with the muscle sloping away from a visible ridge down the center of the back, where the spine sits closer to the surface.

Ideal

A rounded loaf or slightly rounded rectangle, full and even with no sharp angles. Along the back it feels like muscle over bone, not a sharp ridge or a padded gap.

Overweight

Round in a different way: skin folds along the sides, or a soft, doughy feel over the ribs where fat has built up under the skin.

The 9-point body condition scale used for dogs and cats does not apply to snakes. Use this check at every weigh-in.

When a body change is a vet visit A snake that reads triangular over several checks, especially with a falling weight line, refused meals or lethargy, needs a vet rather than more food: thinness in a reptile is as often parasites, illness or a husbandry problem as it is underfeeding. A snake that is consistently round needs its prey size or feeding frequency adjusted, never meals skipped outright, since sudden fasting brings its own problems in an animal already carrying extra weight.
