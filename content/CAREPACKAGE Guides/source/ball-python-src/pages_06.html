Plan for, don't count on avoiding A sick or emergency visit runs $100 to $500 or more, with imaging or surgery higher. A few hundred dollars set aside matters as much as any item on this list.
Where not to cut corners The thermostat and the enclosure. The thermostat is the difference between a heat source and a burn, and the enclosure's size and material drive the humidity that most of the health section depends on. Save on decor before you save on either.

First 30 days

Day 1

Weigh the snake in grams and photograph it and the setup for a baseline
Confirm warm side, cool side and humidity on real instruments, then leave the snake alone
Book the first exam with a reptile vet for within 72 hours, with a fresh stool sample (page {{P:arrival}})
If another reptile lives here, start the quarantine log (page {{P:equiplog}})
Fill in the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}})

Weeks 1 to 2

Hands off: fresh water and spot cleaning only, no handling and no rearranging (page {{P:handling}})
Let it settle one to two weeks, then offer the first meal at the prey type and size the seller used. A refusal while it settles is ordinary (page {{P:refusals}})
Check the thermostat probe hasn't moved at every spot clean

Weeks 2 to 3

Begin short handling sessions only once it is eating on a normal schedule
Never within 48 to 72 hours of a meal, and not at all once the eyes go blue

Week 4

Start the Owner Log with the next weigh-in (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Check the humid hide is still genuinely damp and top up the moss
Do the first monthly check of the thermostat and probe
Introduce the first enrichment item (page {{P:enrichlog}})

Days 31 to 90

Book any follow-up the vet recommended, and act on the fecal result
Watch the first shed closely and log it, including whether both eye caps came off (page {{P:shedding}})
Look back at the Owner Log for any slow drift in weight, appetite or humidity
Do the first full substrate change: monthly if messy, at least every 3 months (page {{P:cleaning}})
Fill in the pet-sitter sheet before you need it (page {{P:sitter}})
Keep any quarantine running the full 3 to 6 months before the snake shares a room, tools or air with another reptile

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

Read the warm-end and cool-end thermometers and the hygrometer; a warm end that keeps climbing is the warning sign
Change the water
Spot clean as soon as waste appears, and look at it first (page {{P:stool}})
Quick visual check: breathing, mouth, eyes, belly scales
Wash your hands after every contact

Every feeding

Feed on the interval for its age, and log the prey and whether it was eaten (page {{P:diet}})
No handling for the next 48 to 72 hours

Weekly

Scrub the water bowl and any soiled decor; 3% bleach left on 10 minutes, rinsed and dried (page {{P:cleaning}})
Check the humid hide is damp, and the probe is still on the floor of the warm hide

Monthly

Weigh a growing snake and log it (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Confirm the thermostat still cuts the heat at its set point
Full substrate change if the snake is messy
Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings

Every 3 months

Full substrate change at the latest, enclosure disinfected and dried
Check the hides still fit snugly as the snake grows

Seasonal

Re-check the room in winter: the cold-weather drop is when respiratory infection spikes
Expect less appetite from roughly October to March; weigh every 1 to 2 weeks while it lasts (page {{P:winter}})

Yearly

Wellness exam with a reptile vet (page {{P:redflags}})
Measure the snake against the enclosure: it should be at least as long as the snake
Replace an optional UVB tube on the maker's schedule, 6 to 12 months

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

A healthy adult tolerates one cool night without harm. The risk is days without heat, and feeding a snake that can't digest.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Keep the snake at or above its night range, 72 to 75°F (22 to 24°C). Below it for more than a day or two is the classic winter trigger for respiratory infection. A battery thermometer in the enclosure tells you the real number.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap.
  • Move the enclosure, or the snake in a ventilated tub, to the warmest room, usually one without outside walls.
  • Use a wrapped hot-water bottle or hand warmers beside the snake, never touching it.
  • Stop feeding. Water only until heat is reliably back.
  • Past a day or two below the floor, board it with a vet, a friend with power, or a reptile shop.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, gas stoves or open flame anywhere near the enclosure: a fire and carbon monoxide risk.
  • Don't put a heat pack in direct contact; a cold, sluggish snake can't move away fast enough and will burn.
  • Don't feed "to keep its strength up." Undigested food in a cold gut is a bacterial risk, not a benefit.
  • Don't warm a cold snake in hot water: lukewarm only, and only once it is alert.
Outage kit Hand warmers or a hot-water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated locking tub, and the vet numbers on paper.

In a heat wave

The air anywhere in the enclosure must stay at or below 95°F (35°C). A thermostat can cut the heat source but cannot cool the room, so read both ends through the day while the heat lasts.

Transport and vet trips

  • A ventilated, locking plastic tub with a towel to grip, inside a dark, room-temperature bag or box. No loose substrate, no water dish.
  • Warm the car first in cold weather, and add a wrapped hand warmer beside the tub for trips over 30 minutes below 65°F (18°C). Check the tub every 20 to 30 minutes at a stop.
  • Don't feed right before a trip. Never leave the snake in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly.
  • Moving house: set the enclosure up and running at the new place before the snake goes in, the same as day one.

Enrichment checklist & log

Setup

Cover across the whole floor, not only inside the two hides
Stable climbing structure braced at both ends, low enough that a fall isn't one
A water basin big enough for the snake to fully enter
Living plants and leaf litter breaking up sightlines

Monthly review

Add or shift one piece of cork or a branch, without blocking the warm spot
Offer a scent: a shed skin from another enclosure, or prey dragged across the substrate before feeding
Check the snake uses more of the floor than last month, not less
