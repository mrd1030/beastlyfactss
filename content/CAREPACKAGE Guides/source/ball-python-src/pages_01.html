How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

Units used throughout Every temperature and size is given twice. Temperatures appear in degrees Fahrenheit (°F) with degrees Celsius (°C) beside them. Sizes appear in inches (in) and feet (ft), with centimeters (cm) beside them. A snake and its weight log are measured in grams (g), the unit a kitchen scale shows. Humidity is a percentage (%) of the moisture the air could hold. Prices are in United States dollars.

If you haven't set up yet

Check the law first, on page {{P:legal}}: a ball python is legal without a permit in most of the United States, but not everywhere. Then read pages {{P:profile}} to {{P:cleaning}} in order before buying anything. Enclosure material, heat control and humidity are the three decisions that are expensive to redo, and the three most often chosen wrong. Get them right the first time, and use the Budget & Shopping List on page {{P:budget}} to buy once.

If your ball python is already home

Audit what you have against the Setup Checklist in the print list below, and go to the Red Flags page (page {{P:redflags}}) if anything seems off. If the problem is a snake that won't eat, start on page {{P:refusals}} instead: refusal is common in this species and usually normal. If any heat source is running without a thermostat, fix that today, before anything else on this list.

Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific namePython regius, called the royal python in Europe
Adult size3 to 5 ft (90 to 150 cm); females significantly larger than males
Captive lifespan20 to 30 years with good care, and some live far longer. About 10 years on average in the wild
Native rangeGrassland, open forest and farmland across West and Central Africa, from Senegal to Uganda
Diet typeCarnivore: whole frozen-thawed mice and rats, one meal every 5 to 14 days depending on age (page {{P:diet}})
Enclosure minimum (adult)4×2×2 ft (120×60×60 cm, roughly 120 gallons), at least as long as the snake. PVC (polyvinyl chloride, a rigid plastic) preferred over glass
Humidity55 to 65%, raised to 70 to 80% through a shed (page {{P:humidity}})
The one hard ruleEvery heat source runs through a thermostat. No exceptions (page {{P:thermostats}})
Sexual maturityMales from about 16 to 18 months; females from about 27 to 31 months, at around 1,500 g (page {{P:eggs}})
Handling difficultyBeginner to intermediate. Defensive as a juvenile, generally mellow as a settled adult
ConservationNear Threatened on the International Union for Conservation of Nature Red List, pressured by collection and export for the pet trade and by habitat loss

Cost overview

Initial setup

$40 to $100 for a standard snake, $510 to $1,245 for the equipment, plus $50 to $150 for the first vet exam: $560 to $1,395 before the snake. Every line is on page {{P:budget}}.

Yearly ongoing

$230 to $575 a year, about $15 to $50 a month, for frozen rodents, substrate, electricity, a UVB tube if you run one, and the yearly vet exam.

Budget for the worst case too A sick or emergency visit runs $100 to $500 or more, and imaging or surgery pushes that higher. Neither is likely if you follow this package, but an animal that lives 20 to 30 years will need a vet at some point. Set the money aside now rather than deciding in an emergency.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • The name comes from the defensive pose, not the diet. A stressed ball python curls into a tight ball with its head tucked in the center, so a new snake that won't uncurl isn't being difficult. It just hasn't decided yet that hiding is unnecessary.
  • It hunts with heat. Pits along the upper lip detect the body warmth of nearby prey, letting a ball python strike accurately in total darkness.
  • A brooding female coils around her eggs without warming them. Brashears and DeNardo (2015) measured six python species at brooding temperatures and found no heat production in five, the ball python among them.
Section 01 · Profile

The species, morphs, size & lifespan

What you are buying, where it came from, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

One species

The ball python is Python regius, native to the grasslands, open forests and farmland of West and Central Africa. In the wild it eats small mammals, birds and amphibians, and it is an ambush predator that spends most of its life concealed, which is why cover matters so much in its enclosure. It is the most popular pet snake in the world.

Where pet ball pythons come from

Almost the entire wild supply traces to three countries at the center of the range: Benin, Ghana and Togo, which have exported close to four million ball pythons since 1978, most of them to the United States. Snakes were taken straight from the wild through the 1980s and 1990s; since the early 2000s most exports have been ranched, meaning eggs and gravid (egg-carrying) females are collected from the wild and the hatchlings raised briefly and shipped. The species makes up roughly 80 percent of all snake exports under Appendix II of CITES, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, the treaty that tracks trade in species that could become threatened by it. The captive-bred hobby grew out of the 1990s, when the first albino ball pythons were bred.

Captive-bred snakes make the better pets: less likely to bite, more likely to accept the food offered, and less likely to carry parasites. Wild-caught and imported animals routinely carry much heavier parasite loads, and because the two get mixed at shows and in the trade, no seller's word replaces the fecal test on page {{P:arrival}}.

Morphs, and what a price does not tell you

A morph is a selectively bred line with a different color or pattern. There are hundreds of them, combined into more than 7,000 recorded combinations. A standard snake runs $40 to $100, a common single-gene morph $50 to $150, and designer morphs $250 to $5,000 or more. One widely bred gene carries a known health cost: the spider morph is linked to wobble syndrome, a heritable neurological disorder that shows as side-to-side or twisting head movements, poor coordination, and trouble striking or constricting prey. Rose and Williams (2014) first described it from a survey of breeders and keepers, and Starck and colleagues (2022) found malformed balance organs in the inner ear of every spider morph they imaged. A spider, or any combination carrying the gene, may struggle to feed, and its offspring can inherit the condition. A wild-type, the natural pattern with no morph gene, or a single-gene morph with no known defect is the lower-risk first snake.

Size and lifespan

GrowthHatchlings start at about a foot (30 cm) and reach roughly 3 ft (90 cm) by age three.
Adult length3 to 5 ft (90 to 150 cm). Females are significantly larger than males.
Lifespan20 to 30 years in captivity is common, against about 10 in the wild. Some live far longer: a zoo female laid eggs at 62 years old.

A ball python bought for a child can still be in the house when that child is grown. Plan for the whole span: the enclosure, the frozen rodents and a reptile vet for every one of those years.
