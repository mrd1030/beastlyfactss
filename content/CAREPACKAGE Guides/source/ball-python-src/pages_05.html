Red flags & finding a vet

What to watch for, what to tell the vet, and how to find one before you need one.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see Wheezing, clicking, open-mouth breathing, or mucus or bubbles at the mouth or nostrils (urgent) · belly scales discolored, spreading or blistering · pinpoint bleeding on the gums, pus, cheesy material or a sour smell in the mouth · visible mites · any tissue protruding from the vent · the head held up and back, tremors, or a snake that can't right itself · a burn · a meal brought back up · a refusal that comes with weight loss, lethargy, mucus or wheezing · watery stool more than once in a row, blood in the stool, or straining with nothing passed · an eye cap left on after a shed.

What to tell the vet

Warm side and cool side temperatures, and whether every heat source runs on a thermostat
Current humidity, read on a digital hygrometer
Date of the last meal, its size, and whether it stayed down
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening
Last bowel movement, and what it looked like
Weight trend from the Owner Log, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag if you can

Why temperature and humidity come up first

Ball pythons are generally hardy, but sensitive to husbandry mistakes in specific, predictable ways. Most of what shows up in this section traces back to temperature, humidity or hygiene, which is why a vet asks about your setup before anything else, and why most of it is preventable.

Finding a reptile vet

Not every practice treats reptiles, and the time to learn that is not during an emergency. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians runs a find-a-vet directory of its members, and at minimum your vet should be a member in good standing. When you call a clinic, ask how many reptiles it sees, whether it treats ball pythons routinely, whether it runs fecal parasite tests in house, and who covers reptile emergencies after hours. Write the number on page {{P:emergency}}. After the first exam, a ball python should see the vet once a year.

Respiratory infection & scale rot

One from too cold or the wrong humidity, the other from too wet and too dirty.

Respiratory infection

The signature ball python illness, and the one every keeper should be able to recognize. Cause: temperatures running too low, humidity wrong in either direction, a dirty enclosure, or ongoing stress. Vets see a seasonal spike each winter as enclosures cool along with the weather outside, and a power outage that holds the snake below 72°F (22°C) for more than a day or two is the classic trigger. Signs: wheezing, an audible clicking, open-mouth breathing, mucus or bubbles around the mouth or nostrils, nasal discharge, lethargy, and sometimes gaping.

Response: it needs antibiotics prescribed by a vet, not a wait-and-see approach. Left untreated it can progress to pneumonia and become fatal. While you arrange the visit, raise the enclosure toward the top of its normal range, check the humidity, and isolate the snake if you keep others. A sick reptile is kept at the upper end of its preferred range because warmth there helps the immune system and the drugs work, which is also why an enclosure shouldn't sit at the bottom of its range by default.

Learn this one by sound Clicking or wheezing is the clearest audible signal in this package. If you hear it: warm side to target, humidity checked, the snake isolated from others, and a call to a reptile vet today.

Scale rot (infectious dermatitis)

Cause: damp, dirty substrate, excess moisture, or poor hygiene in the enclosure. It is the exact failure of chasing a humidity number without ventilation. Signs: discolored belly scales, brown, reddish or yellow; blisters or sores; scales that feel soft or damaged; and a foul smell.

Response: move the snake to a clean, dry enclosure immediately and correct the humidity. Mild cases respond to antiseptic or antibiotic treatment, but anything spreading or blistering needs a vet.

Concrete threshold Any belly scale that is discolored and spreading, or blistered, is a vet visit, not a wait-and-watch. The fix that prevents both conditions is the same: humidity held in its band with real ventilation (page {{P:humidity}}), and the cleaning schedule on page {{P:cleaning}}.

Mouth rot, mites & thermal burns

One rides on another problem, one arrives on a new animal, and one is prevented by a single device.

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

Often secondary to stress, injury or ongoing poor husbandry rather than arriving on its own. Signs: pinpoint bleeding on the gums, thick excess mucus or pus, cheesy-looking buildup in the mouth, a sour smell, visible swelling, open-mouth breathing, and refusing food. Response: it needs veterinary treatment and does not resolve on its own. Because it usually rides on another problem, bring an honest account of your setup to the visit.

Mites

Snake mites (Ophionyssus natricis) are tiny blood-feeding parasites that spread fast through direct contact and shared equipment, which is how a new animal brings them in. Signs: tiny black, brown, reddish or orange specks on the scales, clustered around the eyes, the chin or under the scales; long soaking sessions, as if trying to drown them; rubbing against surfaces; a drop in appetite. A heavy burden kills through anemia or infection, and mites carry bacteria and viruses between snakes, inclusion body disease among them.

  • Check: gently rub the snake while it rests over a sheet of white paper, and look for anything that falls off.
  • Treat: see a vet once mites are confirmed. Treating the snake alone is not enough: the whole enclosure has to be deep-cleaned at the same time, because mites persist in the environment after the snake looks clear.

Thermal burns

A common and entirely preventable injury, caused by a heat source running without a thermostat, a probe that has slipped off the surface it should read, or a heater the snake can touch. Hot rocks are the textbook case. Every heat element runs through a thermostat, with its probe where the snake lies (page {{P:thermostats}}) and any overhead heater at least 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) out of reach. A burn is a wound: fix the heat source first, then see a reptile vet.

Quarantine is the real fix for mites A new snake kept apart for 3 to 6 months, on paper towel, with its own tools and cared for last, is how mites are kept out of a collection in the first place (page {{P:arrival}}). On bare paper, checked daily, mites usually show within the first few weeks if they are there at all.

Internal parasites, regurgitation & obesity

Less dramatic than the last two pages, and far more common.

Internal parasites

Every newly arrived ball python should get a fecal exam before you assume it is healthy, even if it looks fine and eats well. Parasites often cause no visible signs for months, and by the time they do, the burden can already be significant. Wild-caught and imported animals carry far heavier loads than captive-bred snakes, and the two get mixed at shows and in the trade.

A fecal test findsWhat it means
Protozoa (single-celled parasites)Most found in reptile stool are harmless. Some, such as Entamoeba invadens, cause serious illness in snakes.
Nematodes (roundworms)Strongyloides and Rhabdias turn up often, ranging from minor to a cause of gut blockage or pneumonia, depending on the species and the load.
Cryptosporidium serpentisThe one that worries vets most. It thickens the stomach lining and causes repeated regurgitation after meals, has no reliably effective treatment, and can be fatal.

Watch for: weight loss in a snake still eating normally, regurgitated meals, and abnormal stool: watery, heavy with mucus, or with visible worms. Treatment depends entirely on what the test finds. The vet picks the drug and the dose for the parasite identified; this is never a guess or an over-the-counter product.

Regurgitation

A meal eaten and then brought back up. It is hard on the digestive system and slow to recover from. The usual causes are handling within 48 to 72 hours of a meal, prey wider than the snake, the stress of a separate feeding tub, or an enclosure at the wrong temperature. It can also be a parasite sign, and in pythons an early sign of inclusion body disease.

After a regurgitation Stop feeding for a cycle and call a reptile vet, especially in a python. Fix any of the causes above before the next meal.

Obesity

Real and common in captive ball pythons, and the result of overfeeding. Signs: a body round in cross-section, with skin folds along the sides or a soft, doughy feel over the ribs (page {{P:growth}}). Fix: adjust the prey size or the feeding interval to the schedule for its age, never by skipping meals outright, since sudden fasting brings its own problems. The scale catches it earlier than your eye does.

Inclusion body disease & prolapse

One has no cure and is kept out by quarantine. The other is always an emergency.

Inclusion body disease (IBD)

A fatal viral disease of boas and pythons, caused by reptarenaviruses. It shows as nerve signs, most recognizably stargazing, the head held up and back at an odd angle as if staring at the sky. Other signs: tremors, facial tics, abnormal tongue flicking, disorientation, loss of the righting reflex (the snake can't turn itself back over when flipped), regurgitation, and in advanced cases seizures.

How it spreadsThrough body fluids: bites during breeding, fight wounds, contaminated droppings, and snake mites carrying infected blood between animals.
Boas and pythons differA boa can carry the virus for months to years with few or no signs. A python goes the other way: once signs start, most die within days or weeks.
DiagnosisPCR (polymerase chain reaction, a lab test that finds a germ's genetic material) and a biopsy.
TreatmentNone. Once nerve signs appear, most vets discuss euthanasia rather than treatment.
Other reptilesNot established in corn snakes and other colubrids, or in lizards, so treat it as a boa and python risk.
Quarantine is the only defense A carrier can look completely healthy for the whole of a short quarantine, which is why a real one runs 3 to 6 months, in a separate room, with the new snake cared for last (page {{P:arrival}}). Any nerve sign in a new or established snake is an emergency: keep it well away from every other reptile and call the vet.

Prolapse

Internal tissue pushed out through the vent and visible from outside. Most often it is the cloaca itself, but it can be the hemipenes in a male or a section of the colon. It is always an emergency.

  • Keep the tissue clean and moist with a damp paper towel or a clean cloth, and get to a vet immediately. Exposed tissue dries and swells quickly, and the longer it stays out, the harder it is to save.
  • Never try to push it back in yourself.
  • It is a symptom, not a disease. Straining from constipation or a blockage, dehydration, internal parasites, egg binding or bladder stones can all push tissue out. The visit is about finding and fixing that cause too, or it tends to recur.

Shedding, stuck shed & eye caps

A healthy shed comes off in one piece. Look at every one.

The shed cycle

StageWhat you see
Pre-shedColors dull, eyes turn cloudy blue, the snake hides more and may refuse food. The eyes usually stay cloudy about 4 to 7 days.
ClearingThe eyes go clear again. The snake is not finished: the shed usually follows about 4 to 7 days later.
Active shedThe snake rubs against rough surfaces to start the peel, then crawls out of its skin.

Humidity doesn't separate the old skin from the new: a fluid the snake releases under the old skin, carrying enzymes, does that, and the cloudy blue eye is that fluid seen through the clear scale over the eye. Humidity matters at the end, when the loosened skin has to come away in one piece. So through a shed, raise humidity to 70 to 80%, make sure the humid hide is genuinely damp, give the snake cork, slate or a rough branch to rub on, and leave it alone: no handling.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Skin that comes off in flakes and patches instead of one piece. Low humidity is the most common single cause, but one of six: skin parasites, poor nutrition, infection, nothing rough to rub against, and low thyroid function all do it too. Rings of old skin left on the tail tip can tighten across shed cycles and cut off its blood supply.

  • To loosen it, soak the snake in chin-deep water at enclosure temperature for 30 minutes, supervised throughout, then gently work the loose skin free. Never peel unshed skin by force.
  • Repeated bad sheds in a correct setup point to parasites, nutrition or an underlying condition. That is a vet conversation, not a humidity problem.

Retained eye caps

The clear scale over each eye, the spectacle or eye cap, sheds with the rest of the skin. When it stays on, the snake's vision and behavior often look completely normal, which makes it one of the easiest problems to miss. After every shed, find the skin and check that both eye caps came off with it.

Never pick at an eye cap Removing a retained eye cap is a veterinary job: days of eye ointment until the cap falls off or can be lifted with fine forceps. The eye underneath is not replaceable. See a vet rather than reaching for tweezers.

Reading droppings, urates & hydration

Stool is the earliest free health check you get. Look at it before you clean it up.

Urates are the white part of a reptile's droppings. Instead of liquid urine, the body turns nitrogen waste into uric acid, made in the liver from protein digestion, and passes it as a white, chalky paste. A snake has a single opening for both, so the urate comes out with the stool. Its color and texture are the fastest daily read on hydration you have.

How often is normal

A snake generally doesn't pass stool until a meal is mostly digested, so its droppings run on the feeding cycle, not a daily one, and a big meal means a longer gap. The number that matters is your own snake's baseline. A clear gap beyond it, or a sudden change in either direction, is the signal, not any fixed count. Log the date of each one on the Owner Log.

Hydration

  • Urates first. Smooth, white urate generally means a well-hydrated snake. Dry, hard or gritty urate is the signal to act on.
  • The water bowl is not decoration. Heavy, big enough to soak in, changed daily and scrubbed weekly. A terrestrial snake that shows dry urates gets easier access to more water.
  • Humidity is part of hydration. Hold the 55 to 65% baseline. Air that runs too dry tends to show up weeks later as a bad shed.

Reptiles hide illness well. A change in the droppings often shows weeks before the snake looks sick.
