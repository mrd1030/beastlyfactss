Quick profile

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.
Scientific namePogona vitticeps (inland or central bearded dragon)
Adult size16 to 24 in (41 to 61 cm) nose to tail; 300 to 500 g typical, big males heavier
Captive lifespan10 to 15 years typical with consistent care, occasionally longer
Native rangeArid and semi-arid woodland, scrubland and rocky desert of inland eastern Australia
Diet typeOmnivore, ratio shifts from insect-heavy to plant-heavy with age
Enclosure minimum (adult)4×2×2 ft (120×60×60 cm, about 120 gal), front-opening preferred
UVB requirementFerguson Zone 3 open basker: linear T5 HO tube, basking-spot UVI 4.0 to 4.5
Sexual maturityAround 12 to 18 months; females can lay infertile eggs without a male (page 15)
Handling difficultyBeginner to intermediate; generally tolerant once settled
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

Cost overview

Initial setup

$420 to $860 for equipment, plus $75 to $200 for the first vet exam and fecal test.

Monthly ongoing

$60 to $130 for feeders, greens, supplements, electricity, bulbs and the yearly vet visit, averaged.

Budget for the worst case too Emergency care isn't rare enough to ignore. Impaction surgery commonly runs $800 to $2,500; MBD treatment $200 to $500+. Neither is likely if you follow this package, but both are real enough to plan for.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • The "beard" is a flap of spiny skin under the chin that darkens and puffs out during stress, territorial display, or breeding, not a sign of aggression by itself.
  • They darken their backs to absorb heat when cool and lighten when warm. University of Melbourne researchers measured the effect in wild dragons.
  • They can see ultraviolet light through a fourth type of cone cell, which is part of why lighting affects appetite and mood, not just bones.

Housing & enclosure

4×2×2 ft (120×60×60 cm, roughly 120 gallons) is the adult minimum, not an upgrade to get around to eventually. As a rule of thumb, enclosure length should be at least three times your dragon's total body length. A front-opening enclosure makes daily handling and cleaning far easier than a top-opening one.

PVC or sealed wood enclosures with glass fronts hold heat far better than all-glass tanks and are what most experienced keepers use. A glass tank works, it just needs stronger heating to hold the gradient, and a screen top changes the UVB mounting distance (page 7).

Never House two bearded dragons together. They are solitary and will stress, fight, or resource-guard even in large enclosures. Cohabitation is not "social enrichment" for this species.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Cover three sides of the enclosure to cut down on reflections. A dragon that repeatedly mistakes its reflection for a rival can develop chronic stress.
  • Skip hot rocks and unguarded heat sources entirely. They're the most common cause of thermal burns and add nothing a proper basking setup doesn't already do better.
  • Two hides minimum, one on each end, so your dragon never has to choose between security and temperature. Anchor everything an adult can climb so nothing shifts under its weight.

Temperature, UVB & humidity

ZoneTarget (°F)Target (°C)
Basking surface, adult / juvenile95 to 110 / 105 to 11535 to 43 / 41 to 46
Warm side (ambient air, not the basking surface)about 90about 32
Cool side75 to 8524 to 29
Nighttime low65 to 7518 to 24
Humidity30 to 40% baseline. Brief spikes after a soak are normal; days above 50 to 60% cause respiratory and skin trouble.

Measure the basking surface with an infrared thermometer aimed at the platform, not the air. Add overnight heat only if the room drops below 65°F (18°C), using a ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector on a thermostat, never a colored bulb.

Two fixtures, not one

UVB and heat are separate jobs. A T5 HO UVB tube gives UV but almost no heat. Pair it with a separate halogen or incandescent flood ($25 to $50) over the same platform, run through a dimming thermostat (page 7), and confirm the surface temperature with the infrared thermometer rather than trusting the wattage.

UVB is not optional

This is the one piece of equipment worth spending real money on. Use a linear T5 HO tube (Arcadia 12% Desert or Zoo Med ReptiSun 10.0), spanning about two-thirds of the enclosure length on the basking side, never behind glass or plastic, which block UVB almost entirely. Mesh blocks a third to a half, so the mounting distance on page 7 depends on whether a screen is in the way. Coil bulbs are not adequate.

UVI targets

Bearded dragons are Ferguson Zone 3 open baskers. Aim for a gradient from 0 on the cool side up to 4.0 to 4.5 at the basking spot, or 3.0 or lower for hypomelanistic, silkback, and translucent morphs, which burn more easily.

Bulb schedule

Run 10 to 14 hours a day on a timer. Replace every 6 to 12 months for T5 HO, every 6 months for T8. Output fades long before the bulb dims, so replace by the calendar and write the date on page 31.

Thermostats, timers & UVB distance

The thermostat is the safety device

A thermostat sits between the heat source and your dragon and stops a bulb that reads 105°F this morning from overshooting badly on a warm afternoon. Use a dimming (proportional) thermostat for halogen or incandescent basking bulbs: it turns the power down instead of switching the bulb off and on, which halogens hate and which makes the light flicker. Use an on/off or pulse thermostat for a ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector. Put the probe beside the basking platform, not on it and never on the cool side. Never put the UVB tube on a thermostat, and never run any heat source unregulated.

Wattage is a starting guess, not a target. A 50 to 75 W halogen flood usually reaches adult basking temperature in a 4×2×2 ft enclosure at 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm); a cool room or a big platform may need 100 W or two smaller bulbs side by side for a wider hot spot. Let the thermostat and the infrared thermometer settle it.

Timers and the light schedule

Put every light on a plug-in timer so the day length never depends on you remembering. 12 to 14 hours of light in summer and 10 to 12 in winter matches the natural cycle, and the seasonal shift is often what cues brumation (page 20). All lights and heat go off at night unless the room falls below 65°F (18°C); then use a ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector on its own thermostat. Skip red, blue, and "moonlight" night bulbs entirely.

UVB mounting distance

Distances are for a 12% Desert or ReptiSun 10.0 T5 HO. Higher-output tubes such as the Arcadia 14% Dragon need more, typically 15 to 18 in (38 to 45 cm) with no mesh. Follow the chart for the exact bulb, and treat mesh as a 30 to 50% loss.

Measure, don't assume A Solarmeter 6.5 costs about one vet visit and removes the guesswork: read the UVI at the basking spot, adjust the height to 4.0 to 4.5, and re-check every three months to catch a fading bulb.

Substrate, furnishings & handling

Substrate

Paper towel, newspaper, or tile are the safest options, especially while you're new. Experienced keepers sometimes run a bioactive substrate (a deep naturalistic mix with live plants and a cleanup crew of isopods and springtails), but it's a bigger commitment and not where most people should start. A separate dig box (page 13) gives a dragon on tile somewhere to dig.

Never Loose sand (including calcium sand), crushed walnut shell, wood chips, or gravel. All carry a real impaction risk if ingested, whatever the label says (page 17).

Furnishings checklist

Basking platform (rock, slate, or sturdy wood) directly under the lamp
Two hides: one warm side, one cool side
Sturdy, well-anchored climbing branches at more than one height
Shallow water dish the dragon can climb out of easily
Food dish or slate for greens, wiped daily
Dig box, and a deeper lay box for adult females (page 15)

Handling

Give a new dragon 7 to 14 days to settle before handling beyond feeding and cleaning. Approach from the side or below, never from above, which reads as a predator strike. Scoop from underneath, supporting chest, belly, and all four feet, never by the tail, and keep early sessions to 5 to 10 minutes. A dragon that flattens, darkens its beard, gapes, or hisses is telling you to stop; end the session calmly. Once it's eating normally, short daily sessions build trust faster than occasional long ones.

  • Don't handle within about an hour of a meal. Dragons need to bask to digest, and handling too soon can cause regurgitation.
  • Handle low, over a bed or couch. Bearded dragons have no fear of heights and will launch off a hand without warning.
Salmonella Healthy reptiles carry Salmonella with no sign of it. Wash your hands with soap before and after handling, keep the dragon and its gear away from food-prep areas, and never clean dishes in the kitchen sink. Children under five, pregnant people, and anyone immunocompromised should avoid handling.

First days in a new home

Days 1 to 3: hands-off except feeding and spot-cleaning. Days 4 to 7: calm presence near the enclosure, hand resting inside at feeding time. Days 7 to 14: first short handling sessions once appetite and stool look normal; shorter and more frequent for juveniles.

Diet & feeding by age

Bearded dragons are omnivores, and the ratio flips dramatically with age. This mirrors a real dietary shift documented in wild dragons, not an arbitrary husbandry rule. Only feed when the basking surface is at temperature; a dragon that can't bask can't digest.

Why not D3 at every feeding Under a correct T5 HO tube the dragon makes its own D3. Adding supplemental D3 at every feeding on top of that risks vitamin D toxicity, which calcifies soft tissue and is far harder to treat than a calcium shortfall. Plain calcium is the daily dust; the D3 version is insurance.

Gut-loading

Feed the feeder insects for 24 to 48 hours before they go in: leafy greens, carrot, squash, or a commercial gut-load. What the insect ate last is what your dragon gets, and dusting does not replace it. Feeders are on page 10, plants on pages 11 and 12, hydration on page 21.

Never feed Avocado, rhubarb, fireflies, wild-caught insects, or any insect wider than the space between your dragon's eyes. The full never-feed list is on page 12.

Sizing, storage and gut-loading

Prey wider than the gap between the eyes is a choking and impaction risk, and for juveniles it can cause hind-leg weakness. When in doubt, go smaller and feed more. Keep feeders in a ventilated tub with a dry food (oats or a commercial gut-load) plus fresh greens, carrot, or squash for moisture, and never feed an insect that smells off or came from a tub with dead ones in it.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Undersizing the enclosure "because it's just a baby," then never upgrading once it's an adult.
  • Buying a UVB bulb once and never tracking its replacement date, or running a coil bulb and assuming it counts.
  • Running a basking bulb with no thermostat, or putting the probe on the cool side where it never triggers.
  • Skipping the cool side entirely, leaving no way for the dragon to thermoregulate down.
  • Dusting calcium with D3 at every feeding on top of strong UVB, or skipping calcium entirely because "the UVB handles it."
  • Feeding an adult like a juvenile, leading to obesity and fatty liver over time. Waxworms and superworms as a daily food are the usual culprits.
  • Trusting packaging that says "reptile safe" without checking whether it's actually safe for this species. Loose sand is the classic example.
  • Skipping quarantine when introducing a new reptile, or sharing tools and feeder tubs between animals of unknown health status.
  • Estimating UVI by eye instead of measuring it. Output can look bright while still being too weak, or too strong for a sensitive morph.

Enrichment, by priority

Enrichment comes after correct heat, UVB, diet, and enclosure size, never instead of them. In this order:

1. Climbing structure

Sturdy branches, cork, and ledges at varied heights let a dragon survey from above and choose its own distance from the heat. Anchor everything; unstable stacked rocks are a crush-injury risk.

2. Dig box

A tub the dragon can turn around in, filled 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) deep with a 50/50 mix of organic topsoil and washed play sand, kept slightly damp. Never calcium sand. Adult females need a deeper version (page 15).

3. Visual security

Partial barriers, plants, or a half-covered side let a dragon retreat from view without leaving the warm zone, which lowers baseline stress in busy rooms.

4. Foraging and hunting

Tong-feeding, short supervised insect hunts inside the enclosure, and scatter-fed or hung greens all engage natural behavior. Remove uneaten insects before lights-out.

5. Supervised free-roam

10 to 15 minutes to start, building toward 30 for some adults, in a warm draft-free room with gaps blocked, other pets out, cords and toxic plants removed. End it if the dragon cools, panics, or blackens its beard. Never unsupervised, never in a cold room, never a glass enclosure left in direct sun.

6. Novelty rotation

Reposition one furnishing every few weeks. Constant rearranging is stressful; a recognizable layout with one new thing is the sweet spot.

Sexing, growth & body condition

Telling male from female

Reliable from about 4 to 6 months. In a dim room, gently lift the tail to about 90 degrees and shine a phone flashlight down through the base of the tail from above. Males show two hemipenal bulges, one either side of a central dip, just past the vent. Females show a single central bulge or nothing at all. Adult males also have larger, waxy femoral pores along the inside of the thighs, a broader head, and a bigger beard display. Pet-store sexing of hatchlings is a guess; recheck at 6 months.

Growth reference

Ranges are deliberately wide and healthy dragons fall outside them. What matters is the trend: steady gain for a juvenile, a stable weight for an adult. Weigh weekly in grams on a kitchen scale and log it on page 30.

Body condition at a glance

Underweight

Hip bones and spine visible, tail base thin and triangular instead of round, the fat pads on top of the head sunken into hollows, eyes sunken. Skin wrinkles when the dragon isn't shedding. Rule out parasites and low temperatures first (page 19), then feed up under vet guidance.

Ideal

Tail base full and rounded, head fat pads level with the skull, belly flat and off the ground when standing, a defined waist from above. Weight stable week to week in an adult, climbing steadily in a juvenile.

Overweight

Bulging fat pads on the head, jowls under the jaw, rolls at the armpits, a tail base thicker than the thighs, belly dragging. Common in adults fed like juveniles. Cut fatty feeders, shift toward salad, add climbing and free-roam. Fatty liver disease is the long-term risk.

When a weight change is a vet visit A juvenile that stops gaining for 3 to 4 weeks. An adult that loses about 10 percent of its body weight outside brumation, or any weight loss that comes with appetite loss, lethargy, or abnormal stool. Bring the Owner Log; a trend line tells a vet more than a single reading.

Females, eggs & egg binding

Every female owner needs this page, whether or not a male has ever been in the house.

Females lay without a male

From roughly one to two years old, many females develop egg follicles and lay infertile clutches, typically 15 to 30 eggs, sometimes two or three clutches in a season. Some never do. Neither is a problem by itself. What matters is recognizing it and giving her somewhere to dig.

Signs she is gravid

  • A big appetite for a few weeks, then going off food in the one to two weeks before laying.
  • Restlessness, digging at corners, glass surfing, pacing the enclosure, especially in the afternoon.
  • A rounder belly that feels like a bag of marbles when she walks. Never squeeze to check; eggs bruise easily.

The lay box

A container she can turn around in, filled 6 to 12 in (15 to 30 cm) deep with a 50/50 mix of organic topsoil and washed play sand, damp enough that a tunnel holds its shape without dripping. Put it on the warm side as soon as you see the signs and leave it until she's done. She may dig several test holes over a few days, then lay in one sitting and fill it in. Remove infertile eggs afterward. She'll be thin and hungry: feed well, dust plain calcium daily for two weeks, and offer a soak.

Calcium demand goes up Making eggs pulls calcium from her bones. Correct UVB and the supplement schedule on page 9 cover most of it; ask your vet about a temporary increase during a cycle, and never guess at doses. A female that cycles constantly is worth a vet conversation about long-term options, including spaying.

Egg binding (dystocia) is an emergency

Eggs that she cannot pass. It develops from low calcium, no lay site, obesity or poor muscle tone, or oversized or malformed eggs. Call a reptile vet the same day if you see any of these:

  • Digging and straining for more than 24 to 48 hours with no eggs, or a partial clutch and then she stops and looks exhausted.
  • Lethargy, sunken eyes, hind-leg weakness, refusing water, or a lump in the belly that doesn't shift over days.
  • Any female past the "off food" stage who is losing condition rather than laying.

Treatment ranges from fluids and calcium to induced laying or surgery. The longer it waits, the fewer options remain. A lay box that is always available to an adult female is the cheapest prevention there is.

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a reptile vet promptly if you see A juvenile refusing food for 2 to 3 days, or an adult for 1 to 2 weeks outside genuine brumation · sunken eyes · black beard combined with lethargy · limb swelling, limping, tremors, or trouble lifting the body · discharge from eyes, nose, or mouth · diarrhea lasting more than 2 days · straining without producing waste, or no stool in 5+ days (sooner in a juvenile that normally goes daily) · any tissue protruding from the vent · a gravid female digging for 48 hours with no eggs.

What to tell the vet

Current basking and cool-side temps, and how you measured them
UVB bulb brand, type, age, distance, and whether there's mesh in the way
Diet breakdown (insects vs. greens) and supplement schedule over the last 2 weeks

Metabolic bone disease & impaction

Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Cause: insufficient UVB, insufficient calcium, or too much phosphorus relative to calcium, over weeks to months. Juveniles under two are hit hardest because their bones are still forming. Early signs: twitching toes, a slight tremor when held, reluctance to climb, a jaw that looks a little soft or receded. Later signs: swollen or rubbery jaw, bowed limbs, a kinked tail or spine, difficulty walking or lifting the body, bones that fracture easily, and in severe cases seizures. Diagnosis: physical exam plus an X-ray to check bone density, sometimes a blood calcium panel. Response: vet diagnosis and treatment immediately. This does not self-correct and worsens quickly. Typical treatment cost runs $200 to $500+. Recovery: early, mild cases improve over weeks to months with corrected UVB and vet-guided calcium therapy; advanced bone deformity usually remains even after the disease itself is controlled.

While you wait for the appointment Remove climbing furniture and lower the basking platform. A dragon with weakened bones can fracture a limb in a fall that wouldn't hurt a healthy one. Handle as little as possible and support the whole body when you must.
Prevention beats treatment Correct UVB (pages 6 and 7) and the calcium schedule on page 9 prevent nearly all MBD cases. This is the single highest-leverage thing in this whole package.

Impaction

Cause: loose substrate, oversized or hard-shelled feeder insects, dehydration, or temperatures too low to digest properly. Signs: straining without producing waste, a firm lump along the belly, lethargy, loss of appetite, and in juveniles hind-leg weakness or dragging. Diagnosis: physical palpation and an X-ray to locate and size the blockage. Recovery: mild, early cases sometimes resolve with vet-guided warm soaks, fluids, and gentle belly massage; surgery is reserved for blockages that don't pass on their own.

Concrete threshold If your dragon hasn't defecated in five or more days, or a juvenile that normally goes daily hasn't in three, it's time to see a vet. Don't wait longer to "see if it resolves." Surgery, when needed, commonly runs $800 to $2,500.

Respiratory infection, ADV & yellow fungus

Three conditions worth knowing by name before you ever see them.

Respiratory Infection (RI)

Cause: most often prolonged cool temperatures or humidity that sits high for days. Signs: open-mouth breathing at rest, audible clicking or wheezing, mucus or bubbles at the nose or mouth, puffing the throat repeatedly, lethargy, loss of appetite. Diagnosis: physical exam and listening to the lungs; the vet will ask about your temperature setup directly. Response: vet visit for antibiotics; correct the temperature and humidity setup in parallel or it recurs. Occasional gaping while basking is normal thermoregulation, not RI.

Atadenovirus (ADV)

What it is: a contagious virus, most damaging in young dragons, spread by direct contact and by feces on shared tools, dishes, hands, and enclosures. Signs: failure to thrive, stunted growth, chronic poor condition despite correct husbandry, weakness, and neurological signs, most distinctively "stargazing," where the dragon holds its head tilted up and back. Reality check: there is no cure, only supportive care, and some infected adults show nothing while shedding the virus. A vet can test a new dragon with a PCR swab. Quarantine any new reptile for 60 to 90 days in a separate room with separate tools, and never share feeder tubs between animals of unknown status.

Yellow Fungus Disease

What it is: a contagious, aggressive fungal skin infection caused by Nannizziopsis guarroi (long grouped under the older name CANV). Signs: yellow, tan, or brown discolored, crusty, or thickened patches of skin, often starting on the head, limbs, or belly, sometimes with a foul odor, that spread and ulcerate over weeks. Diagnosis: skin biopsy or culture. Response: months of antifungal treatment and strict isolation from other reptiles. Prognosis ranges from guarded to poor, so early treatment matters. It spreads by contact and shared substrate or tools.

The common thread All three are far less common in a correctly heated, correctly lit, single-dragon enclosure with good quarantine hygiene for anything new. Prevention here is mostly husbandry, not luck.

Parasites, mouth rot, tail rot & eye problems

Less dramatic than the last page, and far more common.

Internal parasites

Pinworms and coccidia are common even in dragons from good breeders. Low pinworm counts often cause no trouble; coccidia in a juvenile can. Signs: runny or foul-smelling stool, undigested food, weight loss, poor appetite, lethargy. Many infections are silent. Response: a fecal exam ($30 to $60) on the first vet visit and every year after, with a fresh sample less than 24 hours old in a sealed bag. Never deworm blindly with an over-the-counter product; the dose depends on the parasite and the weight.

Mouth rot (infectious stomatitis)

Signs: swollen or bleeding gums, yellow-white cheesy material in the mouth, drooling, refusing food, a jaw that looks puffy on one side. Dark or stained teeth on their own are normal in adults. Cause: stress, low temperatures, a mouth injury from live prey or hard food, sometimes secondary to MBD. Response: vet visit for cleaning and antibiotics. It spreads to the jawbone if ignored.

Tail rot and toe loss

Signs: the tail tip or a toe turns dark, dry, and hard, and the dark area creeps upward over days. Cause: stuck shed cutting off circulation, an injury, a bite from a cage mate or a feeder insect, or a fall. Response: vet promptly. Caught early it may be treatable; advanced cases need the dead section amputated to stop it spreading. Check toes and tail tip at every shed (page 20).

Eye problems

Eyes held shut or puffy for a day or two during a head shed is normal. Swollen, closed, or weeping eyes that last longer usually point to the UVB bulb being too close or too strong (coil bulbs at short range are the classic cause), a vitamin A imbalance, a scratch, or an infection. Check the bulb distance on page 7 first; vet if it persists beyond two days or comes with discharge.

Burns and prolapse

Burns from hot rocks, an unguarded bulb, or a basking surface over temperature show as blistered, blackened, or peeling skin on the back or belly. Cool the area with room-temperature water, fix the heat source, and see a vet for anything more than a small red patch. Prolapse is pink or red tissue protruding from the vent that doesn't retract within minutes. Keep it moist with a clean wet cloth or saline, never push it back, and get to a vet the same day.

Brumation, stuck shed & glass surfing

Brumation

A natural winter slowdown in appetite and activity, most common in dragons over a year old and triggered by shorter days and cooler nights. Signs include reduced eating, more time hiding, sleeping earlier, and lower activity over several weeks. Confirm with a vet check and fecal test first to rule out illness, then let it happen. Don't force-feed. Typical brumation runs 1 to 3 months. Check in with a vet if it stretches past about three and a half months, comes with more than 10 percent weight loss, or appetite hasn't returned within 2 to 3 weeks of waking.

The brumation routine Weigh weekly. Offer a shallow lukewarm soak once a week and fresh greens every few days, but no insects while the dragon can't bask to digest them. Shorten the light cycle to 8 to 10 hours on a timer, or leave lights off if it's fully asleep in a hide, and keep the room from dropping below 65°F (18°C). Resume the normal schedule the day it reappears and starts basking.

Stuck shed (dysecdysis)

Most common on toes, the tail tip, and around the eyes, where low humidity or poor circulation interferes with normal shedding. Increase local humidity briefly with a damp towel or a shallow lukewarm soak, and never peel unshed skin by force. Toe and tail-tip stuck shed can cut off circulation if left unaddressed (page 19). A shallow soak two or three times a week during a shed cycle prevents most cases before they start. Juveniles shed every few weeks; adults a few times a year.

Glass surfing

Repetitive pacing or scratching along the glass. Work through the usual causes in order: a reflection or another pet in view; an enclosure that's too small or too hot with no real cool side; boredom or hunger; and, in an adult female, a gravid dragon looking for somewhere to lay (page 15). Cover reflective surfaces on three sides and reduce disruptive activity near the enclosure before assuming it's just "personality." Fixing the underlying trigger resolves it faster than covering glass alone.

Quick differentiator Brumation is slow and low-energy. Glass surfing is repetitive and high-energy. If you're not sure which you're seeing, the energy level is usually the tell.

Hydration

Most of a dragon's water comes from food. Many barely touch a dish, some drink from one regularly, so keep a shallow dish available and refresh it daily. Beyond that:

  • Mist the salad, not the enclosure walls. Water droplets on greens get eaten; a damp enclosure just raises humidity.
  • A shallow lukewarm soak, 15 to 20 minutes, once or twice a week, chest-deep at most and never unsupervised. Most dragons drink during it.
  • Hydrating foods: hornworms, silkworms, cucumber or melon pieces, cactus pad, as extras rather than staples.
  • A dragon that won't drink: drip water onto the snout with a syringe or dropper and let it lick. Never squirt water into the mouth.
Signs of dehydration Wrinkled or loose skin outside a shed, sunken eyes, thick or stringy saliva, yellow or hard urates, lethargy, and skin that stays tented for a moment when gently pinched on the side. Mild cases respond to soaks and food; a dragon that is sunken and lethargic needs a vet for fluids.

Section 05 · Owner Tools

Power outages, travel & transport

A healthy adult tolerates one cool night without harm. The risk is days without heat, and feeding a dragon that can't digest.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Keep the dragon above 60°F (16°C). Below that for more than a day or two is when problems start.
  • Drape thick towels or a blanket over the enclosure, leaving a vent gap, to hold what heat is left.
  • Move the enclosure, or the dragon in a tub, to the warmest room in the house.
  • Use hand warmers or a hot-water bottle wrapped in a towel, placed beside the dragon, never touching it.
  • Stop feeding insects. Offer greens and water only until the basking spot is back.
  • Past two days below 65°F (18°C), board it with a vet, a friend with power, or a reptile shop.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, gas stoves, or open flame anywhere near the enclosure.
  • Don't put a heat pack in direct contact with the dragon; it can't move away fast enough and will burn.
  • Don't feed insects "to keep its strength up." Undigested food in a cold gut rots.
  • Don't warm a cold dragon in hot water; lukewarm only, and only once it's alert.
Outage kit Hand warmers, a hot-water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a ventilated tub, and the vet numbers from page 23.

  • Moving house: set the enclosure up and running at the new place before the dragon arrives, same as day one.

ImpactionA gastrointestinal blockage, often from substrate, oversized prey, dehydration, or low digestion temperatures.
MBDMetabolic Bone Disease: weakened, deformed bones from inadequate UVB or calcium.
OxalatesCompounds in spinach, chard, beet greens, and parsley that bind calcium so it can't be absorbed. Why those foods are rare, not staple.
PhotoperiodThe number of hours of light per day. 12 to 14 in summer, 10 to 12 in winter, set by a timer.
QuarantineKeeping a new or unwell reptile fully separate from others, with separate tools and hand-washing between animals, for 60 to 90 days.
StargazingHolding the head tilted up and back for long periods. A neurological sign, most often linked to ADV. Urgent, not wait-and-see.
T5 HOA high-output linear fluorescent tube format. The standard UVB source for this species; coil and compact bulbs are not equivalent.
UrateThe white, semi-solid part of reptile waste, the equivalent of urine. Its color and texture are a hydration gauge.
UVIUV Index: the measurement scale used to describe basking-spot UVB intensity, read with a Solarmeter 6.5.
Yellow fungusNannizziopsis guarroi (historically grouped as CANV), a contagious, aggressive fungal skin infection.
