The species, morphs, size & lifespan

What you are buying, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

A salamander that keeps its larval body

The axolotl is a salamander from the lake and canal system of Xochimilco, in Mexico City, that keeps its larval body for life: feathery external gills, a finned tail and a fully aquatic habit. The word for this is paedomorphosis, and it is not the same as staying a baby. An axolotl reaches full adult size and breeds normally. Nor is it strictly permanent: the tissues still respond to thyroid hormone, and a high enough dose in a laboratory can push one through metamorphosis into a land-dwelling adult. What is underactive is the hormone system that would trigger it. In a home tank that never happens, which is why a correct setup has no land area and no basking spot.

Wild and captive

In the wild the species is close to gone. Surveys of the Xochimilco canals counted about 6,000 axolotls per square kilometer in 1998 and 36 in 2014, a fall of 99 percent, driven by pollution and by introduced carp and tilapia that eat eggs and young. In laboratories and home tanks it is common: most laboratory axolotls descend from six of the 34 animals shipped from Mexico City to Paris in 1864.

Morphs, in plain terms

A morph is a color variety. Every morph needs exactly the same cold water, food and care.

MorphWhat it is
Wild typeDark olive and speckled, the color of every wild axolotl.
LeucisticMostly white with dark eyes: fewer pigment cells develop, and most fail to reach the skin. The pale pet-shop axolotl.
AlbinoNo black pigment at all, because the enzyme that makes it is broken. The gene came from a tiger salamander crossed into the laboratory colony.
MelanoidSolid dark, with many black pigment cells and none of the reflective ones. Sometimes sold as melanistic.
GFP (green fluorescent protein)Carries a jellyfish gene for a glowing protein, first put into axolotls in 2006 for research. Glows green under ultraviolet light, and can be combined with any color.
Mosaic and chimeraBuilt from two genetically different cell lines, so one body carries patches of two morphs. A mosaic does not breed more mosaics, which is the whole price premium.

The common colors come from single genes, mostly recessive, so two wild-type parents can both carry a hidden color. A dark morph hides early redness and fungus, so look closer under good light.

Size and lifespan

Most adults finish at 9 to 12 in (23 to 30 cm). An axolotl commonly lives 10 to 15 years in captivity and 5 to 6 in the wild. Plan for house moves, summers away and a decade or more of hot Augusts.

Tank size, type & where it goes

A bottom-dweller that walks more than it swims, in a tank that has to stay cold.

Size

A 20 gal long tank (about 76 L) is the minimum for one axolotl, and a 40 gal breeder (about 151 L) is the better answer if you keep two. Floor space matters more than height for this bottom-dwelling species, so a long, low tank beats a tall one of the same volume. Use a secure lid: an axolotl can climb out given the chance, and it dries out fast on the floor.

Volume also buys time. A large volume of water with a cycled filter holds its temperature and its chemistry between changes. Research colonies do the opposite, keeping each adult in about half a gallon of water that is replaced at least every other day, and that works because the water is changed before waste builds up. What fails is the middle ground: a small tank with no cycled filter and only weekly changes.

Where it goes

  • The coolest room you have, away from windows, sunlight and heat-producing appliances. A cool room does part of a chiller's work for free.
  • Somewhere you can reach the bottom with a siphon, since water changes are a weekly job.
  • Near an outlet with room for a chiller or a fan, if the room runs warm in summer (page 8).
Never a heater For almost every other aquarium animal, warmth is the fix. Here it is the hazard. If the tank came as a kit with a heater, take it out. An axolotl tank also needs no land area and no basking spot, because the animal never leaves the water.

One axolotl or two

SetupVerdictWhy
One adult aloneSafestAxolotls are not social and do not need company. Housing separately is the safer default.
Two adults of similar sizePossibleOnly with plenty of space, in a 40 gal breeder. Cohoused axolotls bite at each other's limbs and gills, most at feeding time.
Juveniles togetherNoJuveniles are known for cannibalism. Raise young axolotls apart.
Mismatched sizesNoBiting is worst when sizes differ, and anything that fits in the mouth gets swallowed.

A male and a female together will breed, which is a much bigger commitment than it sounds (page 21).

Temperature: the numbers that matter

The single most important number in this package, and the one most often missed.
Water readingWhat it means
60 to 64°F (15.6 to 17.8°C)The target.
Above 72°F (22°C)The ceiling. Extended time above it causes chronic stress and suppresses the immune system.
Above about 75°F (24°C)Danger. Axolotls go off their food, turn sluggish, develop ascites (fluid swelling in the abdomen), float and cannot stay down, and pick up bacterial and fungal infections. The heat itself can be fatal.
41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C)Not a target for a healthy tank. It is the range vets hold a sick axolotl in for weeks during treatment, which shows that cool water is not the danger warm water is.

Why warm water is so dangerous here

Two pieces of water chemistry stack. First, warm water holds less oxygen, at the moment a warmer animal is burning through more of it, and this one takes most of its oxygen straight from the water through its gills and skin. Second, warm water makes ammonia more toxic. Ammonia exists in two forms, and the un-ionized form is roughly 100 times more toxic than the other. Its share rises with temperature and with pH, so a small reading that a cold tank at pH 7.4 shrugs off becomes a real problem in a warm tank at pH 8. Heat does not just stress the axolotl directly. It turns every other small water problem into a bigger one.

Measure where the animal lives Use a reliable thermometer in the water, not a strip on the outside glass, and read it every day. It is the reading most likely to change without warning, and catching drift early is the whole point.

Which plan your room needs

Your roomWhat to plan for
Holds the low to mid 60s°F (around 16 to 18°C) all yearA thermometer, a fan on standby, and the tank away from windows and heat sources.
Cool most of the year, a few hot daysAir conditioning in that room and a fan across the water, ready before summer. Frozen bottles only as the emergency backup.
Above the mid 60s°F for weeks at a timeA chiller. Reaching for frozen bottles more than a couple of times a season is the sign it is time to buy one.

Whatever the method, change the water temperature slowly: no more than about 1°F an hour as a rule, never a drastic jump in either direction.

Chillers, fans & cooling without one

Ways to take heat out of a tank, the strongest first, and how to size a chiller.
MethodHow it works and where it fits
Room air conditioningLowers the temperature the tank keeps trying to match. Nothing tank-side beats controlling the room.
Fan across the water surfaceEvaporative cooling: moving air over open water pulls heat out of it. The best cheap active method. Needs an open top or a mesh lid, not glass.
Lights off, lid cracked openRemoves a heat source and lets warm, humid air escape. Free, and worth doing the moment a heat wave starts.
Less canister filter flowA canister motor makes its own heat, and higher flow pushes it into the tank. A modest assist; never drop flow below what filtration needs.
Insulation and shadeBlocks heat from a sunny window or a warm room. Best for preventing a slow daytime climb.
Frozen water bottlesSealed and floated, never loose ice. An emergency stopgap only: each bottle chills the water hard, then does nothing once it melts, and that swing is a stressor in its own right.
Aquarium chillerThe only method that holds a set temperature whatever the weather. $150 to $650, plus its electricity.

Run extra aeration alongside any of these. It does not cool the water, but warm water holds less oxygen, and more surface movement puts more in.

Sizing a chiller

A chiller is sold by the volume of water it can cool, and that rating comes with conditions. One maker rates its 150 L (about 40 gal) and 300 L (about 80 gal) units in an 86°F (30°C) room with the water starting at 82°F (28°C). The rated volume is the most a unit can handle under those test conditions, not a comfortable working load.

  • Add the water in the filter and any sump to the tank's volume, and pick a unit rated well above that total.
  • A 20 gal long sits comfortably inside a 40 gal rating. A 40 gal breeder is already at that unit's limit and belongs on the next size up.
  • Go up another size if the room regularly runs hotter than the 86°F the rating assumes.
  • Water is pumped through the chiller, so bring the return back through a spray bar or into a baffled corner. Axolotls are very sensitive to current.
The running cost The chiller's electricity is the cost most first-time owners do not budget for, and it varies most by climate and season. In a warm place, running one all summer adds up.

Water quality & the nitrogen cycle

Five numbers, a test kit, and the weeks of work that happen before the animal.

Axolotls excrete ammonia. In a cycled tank, one group of bacteria in the filter turns it into nitrite, and a second turns the nitrite into nitrate, which is far less toxic and leaves with water changes. A new filter needs weeks to grow those bacteria, so the tank goes through a full cycle before the axolotl goes in.

ReadingTargetAct whenTest
Temperature60 to 64°F (15.6 to 17.8°C)Above 72°F (22°C)Daily
Ammonia0 ppmAny reading2 to 3 times a week in a new tank, weekly once stable
Nitrite0 ppmAny reading2 to 3 times a week in a new tank, weekly once stable
NitrateUnder 10 ppmClimbing past 10 ppmWeekly
pH7.4 to 7.6Outside 6.5 to 8.0, or swingingWeekly

Use a liquid test kit that reads ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH. Stability matters more than the exact pH, and the high end is where ammonia bites hardest.

Cycling the tank before the axolotl

  1. Set the tank up complete, filter running and the water dechlorinated.
  2. Dose pure ammonia, with no added soaps or dyes, to 3 ppm, inside the usual 2 to 4 ppm range.
  3. Test daily or every other day, and dose back up to 3 ppm whenever it drops. Filter media from an established, healthy tank seeds the colony faster than anything sold in a bottle.
  4. It is done when a full dose of ammonia reads 0 within 24 hours, nitrite reads 0, and nitrate is building. All three, not just one.

Most cycles take four to six weeks in warm water, and real tanks have taken anywhere from about three weeks to two months. A cold axolotl tank runs at the slow end: a drop of just 1°C (about 2°F) can cut the bacteria's work on ammonia by around 30 percent. Overdosing does not speed it up; it stalls the second half.

If ammonia or nitrite shows up

  1. Change at least a third of the water with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, and 50 percent or more if the reading is clearly climbing.
  2. Skip or cut the next feeding, and remove any uneaten food.
  3. Check the filter is running and that nobody rinsed the media in tap water.
  4. Test again the next day, and repeat until both read 0.

The water itself

Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine, both deadly to skin and gills, so every drop gets a dechlorinator that handles both. Chloramine does not leave water that is left standing. Axolotls need mineralized water: never use straight RO (reverse osmosis) or distilled water without remineralizing it. No GH (general hardness, the dissolved minerals) or KH (carbonate hardness, which steadies pH) target for axolotls traces back to a published study, so this package prints none. Aquarium salt does not raise hardness.

Filtration, flow & lighting

Enough filter for a carnivore's waste, delivered gently, under a dim light.

Axolotls dislike current, and a filter built for an active fish will stress one. They are very sensitive to flow and can stop eating under a strong one. The answer is not a weaker filter. It is a properly sized filter whose output is broken up before it reaches the animal.

FilterHow it fits an axolotl tank
Sponge filterThe standard choice for axolotls: an air pump pushes water through a foam block, so the flow is gentle by design. Needs a separate air pump.
Canister filterHolds the most media. Send the return through a spray bar, a bar of small holes that spreads the flow out, or into a baffled corner.

Signs the flow is too strong

  • Gills curled forward toward the face. This can mean heat or water quality too, so check the thermometer and the test kit as well as the filter.
  • Going off food. An axolotl can stop eating under a strong current.

A gentle current on one side of the tank, with a genuinely still corner on the other, lets the axolotl choose its own water movement.

Looking after the filter

The bacteria colony lives in the filter media, not the water. Rinse media in a bucket of water taken out of the tank during a change, never under the tap, since chlorine and chloramine kill the colony. Never replace all the media at once. A filter converts ammonia and nitrite into nitrate; it does not remove the nitrate, which is the job of water changes.

Lighting

Keep it dim. Axolotls have no eyelids and are sensitive to bright light, so floating plants or a hide give shade. Put the light on a timer for a 12-hour day, so the tank goes fully dark at night. In a heat wave, switch the light off altogether, since it adds heat.

A filter does not replace water changes Nitrate builds up between changes whatever the filter does. Keep the weekly change even when every reading looks perfect.

Substrate, hides & furnishings

An axolotl gulps whatever sits by its food, so the tank floor is a safety decision.

Axolotls feed by suction: they open the mouth and take in everything in front of it, food and floor alike. Anything small enough to swallow and too big to pass is an impaction waiting to happen, and a blockage in the gut can need surgery.

FloorVerdictDetail
Bare bottomSafestNo impaction risk and the easiest to clean. The right answer for any juvenile under 5 in (13 cm).
Fine sand, grains under 1 mm (millimeter)GoodFine enough to pass if swallowed. For axolotls past 5 in, siphoned at every water change.
Gravel, small rocks, pebblesNeverSwallowed with the food and too big to pass. The classic fatal mistake.

Wherever the floor is sand, feed on a bare patch, in a dish or from tongs, so the mouthful is food and nothing else.

Hides

Give at least 2 hides per adult, ideally 3 to 4 of different shapes and heights, and make one of them a tunnel, which tested as the item most worth adding (page 19). Hides are how an axolotl controls its light level, so they matter more for an animal that cannot close its eyes.

  • Terracotta pots on their side with the drainage hole sealed.
  • Wide PVC (polyvinyl chloride, a rigid plastic) pipe sections with every edge fully sanded.
  • Smooth resin or ceramic caves.

Plants and decor

  • Live or silk plants for cover. Java fern and anubias tied to decor rather than buried in the floor.
  • Moss balls kept larger than the axolotl's head.
  • Floating plants for dappled shade.
  • Large stones and decor placed so they cannot topple or shift.
Two tests for anything new Run your hand over it: if it scratches you, it can tear an axolotl's skin. Then hold it up to the animal's head: anything small enough to swallow stays out of the tank. Every edge smooth, every piece stable, nothing loose and small.

Water changes, cleaning & hygiene

The one job that fixes more problems than every product on the shelf.

The weekly change

Change 20 to 30 percent of the water every week with dechlorinated water, which keeps nitrate down between tests. Test nitrate before the change. If it keeps climbing past 10 ppm, change more water or change more often, and check you are not overfeeding.

  1. Prepare the new water first. Dechlorinate it, then bring it to within a couple of degrees of the tank, checked with the same thermometer you read the tank with. A bucket that feels fine to a hand can be ten degrees off.
  2. Siphon from the floor, not the surface. Most of the waste settles on the bottom, so lift it off as the water comes out.
  3. Rinse filter media in the old water while you have a bucket of it, never under the tap.
  4. Log it: the date, the amount changed and the readings, on the owner log.

Every day

Remove uneaten food within 15 to 30 minutes of a meal. Leftover worm and pellet foul the water faster than anything else in the tank.

Cleaning tubs, tools and decor

JobHow
Scrubbing a tub or bowlBaking soda and salt mixed 2 to 1. Rinse well.
Disinfecting tools and housingHousehold bleach at 30 mL per liter of water, with at least 30 minutes of contact. Rinse thoroughly in fresh water, then let it dry before use.
QuarantineSeparate tools, net and siphon for a quarantine tub, never shared with the main tank.
Filter mediaMechanical sponge or floss can be rinsed freely. Biological media barely ever, gently, in old tank water, and never all at once.
Skin that absorbs what is in the water An amphibian's skin and gills take in whatever is dissolved in the water, which is why chlorine that is harmless to people is lethal here. So every drop of new water is dechlorinated, top-ups included, and anything cleaned with bleach is rinsed thoroughly and dried before it goes back near the tank.

Diet & feeding by age

A carnivore that gulps its food whole and slows right down as it grows.

Axolotls eat invertebrate prey and need no plant matter at all. Earthworms or nightcrawlers are the staple, paired with a sinking pellet made for axolotls or carnivorous amphibians. Everything else is variety alongside those two.

StageHow oftenWhat
Hatchling, 0 to 3 months2 to 3 times dailyLive food, such as daphnia and brine shrimp.
Juvenile, 3 to 6 monthsAbout once a dayCut worm pieces, small sinking pellets, frozen brine shrimp or daphnia.
Sub-adult, 6 to 12 monthsEvery 1 to 3 daysCut worm and pellets, sized to the animal.
Adult, 12 months and over, past about 7.5 in (19 cm)Every 2 to 3 daysWorm in head-sized pieces and pellets. Adults digest slowly in cool water and genuinely need the gap.

The 7.5 in mark is the point for the adult schedule, not full size: most adults go on to finish at 9 to 12 in (23 to 30 cm).

How much at each meal

  • Size each meal to roughly the axolotl's head: what it takes in 5 to 10 minutes.
  • Pull anything still uneaten after 15 to 30 minutes. Leftover worm and pellet foul the water.
  • Feed from long, blunt tongs, on a bare patch or in a dish, so the mouthful is food and not floor, and so you can see exactly how much each animal ate.
  • If you keep two, feed each one separately, since cohoused axolotls bite most at feeding time.
The most common feeding mistake is the schedule Feeding an adult on a juvenile's schedule. A grown axolotl fed daily becomes obese and loads the tank with waste. Obesity is a recognized problem in pet axolotls, and a measured portion at the adult interval is what prevents it.
Appetite is your daily health signal Poor appetite is one of the first signs of illness, and of warm or dirty water. Note every feeding response on the owner log. A refusal lasting 5 days or more is a vet call, after you have checked the thermometer and the test kit.

Worms, pellets & frozen foods

A short shopping list: one staple, one supplement, and some variety.
FoodTierNotes
Earthworms and nightcrawlersStapleThe gold standard: high in protein, well balanced, easy to digest and nutritionally complete. Cut into pieces, never fed whole.
Sinking pellets made for axolotls or carnivorous amphibiansStapleThe supplement to worms. Salmon-based or invertebrate-formulated pellets both work. Research colonies raise adults on sinking pellets above 30 percent crude protein alongside worms.
Frozen brine shrimp and daphniaYoung, varietyGood for hatchlings and juveniles, and as variety for adults. Not the whole diet on their own.
Frozen bloodwormsTreatA good occasional treat. Too thin to be the whole diet.

Worms: cut them up

Feed earthworms in portions rather than whole, even to a large adult: a piece sized to the head. That is also easier to count across the week than a whole nightcrawler of unknown length. For a juvenile, cut pieces are the only option.

Tongs and target feeding

Axolotls learn to take food from long, blunt feeding tongs, which is how research colonies feed them, and the tongs show exactly how much each animal ate. An axolotl notices food moving in the water through its lateral line (page 18), so a piece offered just in front of the face is found fast.

Making a meal take some finding

Dropping food in the same spot every time skips the hunting behavior. Scattering pieces so the axolotl has to find them is a free upgrade, as is a sand saucer: a shallow dish of sand under 1 mm with small food items hidden in it, which adds searching without loose sand across the floor. Rotate where you feed every few days. These change how the meal arrives, never how much the animal eats.

Feed on a clean patch Because an axolotl gulps its food, it takes in whatever sits next to it. Feed on a bare patch, in a dish or from tongs, so the mouthful is worm and nothing else.

The never-feed list & supplements

A few foods that cause real harm, and the supplement step this species does not need.
FoodTierWhy
Feeder fish, live or frozenNeverA real parasite risk, and many common feeder species carry an enzyme that causes a vitamin B deficiency over time.
Beef heart, chicken and other mammal meatNeverAxolotls cannot properly digest mammalian fat and protein.
Adult mealworms, crickets, crayfishNeverHard-shelled feeders. The exoskeleton is difficult to digest and can cause internal injury.
Any food dropped onto gravel or small stonesNeverThe axolotl gulps the stone with the food, and a stone that cannot pass blocks the gut.

The wrong food causes digestive and nutritional problems even without an impaction: feeder fish or mammal meat in place of worms and proper pellets is a slow harm, not just an emergency risk.

Supplements

None are generally needed. A varied diet of worms and formulated pellets covers this species' needs without added vitamins or minerals. There is no calcium dusting step and no vitamin schedule, which is one less thing to get wrong than with most exotic pets.

Handling & moving an axolotl

Why the answer is almost always no, and how to move one when you must.

Axolotls are not a hands-on pet, and that is about their bodies, not a preference. The skin is scaleless and sensitive, with no robust slime layer to fall back on the way a fish has. The feathery external gills do the actual work of breathing, so a snagged filament affects how the animal takes in oxygen, not just how it looks. And the skeleton is largely cartilage rather than solid bone. Handling risks skin and gill damage, real stress, and infection at any point of contact.

When a move is genuinely necessary

Tank maintenance, a rehouse, a health checkGuide the axolotl into a submerged container while it is still underwater, then lift the container. Keep it in water the whole time: air exposure and pressure are both real risks.

At the vet

A vet visit is the most common reason an axolotl leaves its tank, and the container does most of the work. Enrichment & common mistakes

Not toys. Choice: somewhere to rest, cover that feels secure, and food that takes some finding.

What has been tested

Billingham's 2017 study gave laboratory axolotls rocks, plants, plastic shelters, old compact discs and tunnels, and recorded what they did. A pair will breed

A mature male and female kept together will breed. The female lays her eggs 12 to 20 hours after mating, spread over one or two days. In managed care a female can lay 200 to 1,500 eggs every 3 to 6 months. At 70°F (21°C) the eggs hatch in about 2 weeks, sometimes 3 to 4, into tiny larvae that live on live food, 2 to 3 times a day.

Raising them is the real commitment. Juveniles are cannibalistic and are raised apart, so a single clutch means hundreds of animals to house, feed live food and rehome, and they keep coming every few months.

If you do not want hundreds of larvae Keep one axolotl, or two of the same sex, confirmed after maturity.

What sets off breeding

In one research vivarium, natural breeding followed a cool spell of 12 to 14°C (54 to 57°F) with about 11 hours of light a day. It is a reminder that the light cycle is part of the environment too.

No metamorphosis

An axolotl reaches maturity and breeds while still in its larval body, and it never leaves the water. The seasonal job in this species belongs to the keeper: getting the tank through summer heat, which is planned on the routine page.

The federal rule, Virginia & why the bans exist

The 2025 federal rule

A federal rule effective January 10, 2025 added 16 more salamander genera to the list of injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act, the federal law that lets the government restrict harmful wildlife, bringing the total to 36 genera. All of them are tied to the risk of Bsal, the salamander-killing fungus, and genus Ambystoma, which includes the axolotl and the tiger salamanders, is among the new ones.

What the listing restrictsWhat it does not restrict
Importing axolotls into the United States, and shipping them to or from Hawaii, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico or the US territories.Owning, breeding or selling an axolotl within the 49 continental states. The US Fish and Wildlife Service states that distinction directly in its own answers on the rule.

The twist is in two states. Wyoming and Alabama do not write their own lists for this; they bar whatever is on the federal injurious list. So for owners there, the January 2025 rule was the moment the animal became illegal to keep. And Hawaii's conditional yes sits beside a federal limit on shipping one there.

Virginia is legal, and has been since 2021

Virginia still shows up on ban lists across a surprising number of hobby sites. Since August 1, 2021 it has been legal to import, export, sell and possess axolotls there with no permit, after an amendment to the state's administrative code carved axolotls out of its general ban on selling salamanders. Why the bans exist

None of the restrictions protect axolotls. They protect native salamanders. The axolotl sits in genus Ambystoma with North America's mole and tiger salamanders, including California's own tiger salamander, which is federally threatened. Regulators weigh three risks: hybridization with native salamanders, an escaped population establishing itself, and disease, the chytrid fungi above all. California's label for the genus, a detrimental species, says so outright. New Jersey adds an enforcement reason: a gilled adult axolotl looks like the larva of a protected native salamander.

Heat stress & reading the gills

The condition most axolotl health problems trace back to, and the early warning on the animal's own head.

Cause: water above 72°F (22°C) for any length of time suppresses the immune system; above about 75°F (24°C) the heat itself can be fatal. Signs: gill tips curled forward, appetite loss, floating, and pale patches on the skin. Above about 75°F, axolotls turn sluggish, float uncontrollably, develop ascites and pick up bacterial and fungal infections. Response: bring the temperature down, steadily, and test the water while you do.

The water is too warm, right now

  1. Lights off, lid cracked open, and a fan blowing across the water surface.
  2. Close the blinds, and run the room as cool as you can.
  3. Float sealed bottles of frozen dechlorinated water, swapping them as they melt. Never loose ice: it shocks the water around each cube.
  4. Lower the temperature no faster than about 1°F an hour, never in a drastic jump.
  5. Check the filter output too: gills curled forward can mean too much current rather than heat.
  6. Test ammonia and nitrite, since warm water makes the same ammonia reading more toxic.
An axolotl already in crisis For an animal in outright heat crisis, from long exposure above 75°F (24°C), placing it in a dish in the refrigerator is a recognized stopgap while the main tank is cooled. It is a short emergency measure, not routine care.

Fungal & bacterial infection

The two infections that follow warm water and dirty water, and how to tell them apart.

Fungal infection

Signs: a white or gray, cotton-like growth on the gills or body, the water mold that is the most common fungal infection in the species. Cause: warm water, poor water quality, stress, or an existing injury that gave the fungus an opening. Response: correct the water quality and cool the tank. Salt baths can help, but the one published figure is a ceiling, not a dose, and the working dose belongs to an exotics vet (page 29). Severe cases need a vet-prescribed antifungal, and so does any case not improving within 48 hours of the water being right.

Bacterial infection

Signs: red streaks, reddened skin, open sores, and a swollen, fluid-filled body (ascites). Cause: most often Aeromonas or Pseudomonas bacteria, which take hold when the water runs warm or dirty. Response: a vet and prescription treatment. Water changes alone will not clear it. Caught early, it is treated with systemic antibiotics such as enrofloxacin or a trimethoprim-sulfonamide, and once advanced the outlook is poor, which is the reason to act on the first signs.

Tubbing, cooling & salt baths

Three interventions worth knowing before you need them, and where each one stops.

Tubbing

Tubbing means moving the axolotl out of its tank into a bare, unfiltered container of dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, changed completely rather than filtered. Research colonies house many adults this way as routine, in bowls with no circulating water, because water replaced regularly cannot build up waste. A tub is easy to watch against a plain background, isolates a sick or injured animal from a tankmate, and gives somewhere to go while a tank is put right.

  • A clean plastic tote, bin or bucket the axolotl can turn around in without bumping the walls. No gravel and no sharp decor; one simple hide such as a length of PVC pipe.
  • A full water change once daily as the baseline, twice for a genuinely sick animal, with fresh dechlorinated water checked by thermometer before the axolotl goes back in. A partial change defeats the point.
  • Move the axolotl in a submerged container, never by hand.
  • Keep testing the main tank in parallel: a tub fixes one animal's exposure, not whatever made the tank a problem.

Cooling as treatment

A sick axolotl on antibiotics for a bacterial infection may be held at 41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C) for several weeks, because cooler water appears to help. That belongs to a treatment a vet is running. Cooling a tub within the normal range as general support for a stressed animal is reasonable on your own, done gradually.

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

For an axolotl, a summer power outage is an oxygen problem and a heat problem at once.

When the power goes out

Oxygen is the fast risk. Once the filter stops, the surface stops moving, gas exchange slows, and dissolved oxygen starts falling within a couple of hours. So the first move is aeration, not the thermometer.

Do

  • Run a battery air pump with an airstone. Own one before you need it.
  • No pump: scoop water out and pour it back from a few inches up for a minute or two, every 10 to 15 minutes in a small tank, 20 to 30 in a larger one.
  • Unplug the filter, so you decide when it restarts.
  • In summer, keep heat out: blinds closed, the tank away from windows, the room as cool as you can.

Don't

  • Don't feed until the filter is back and stable.
  • Don't restart a filter that sat dead for hours without checking it: rinse the media in old tank water first, or decomposing waste flushes into the tank.
  • Don't use loose ice. Sealed frozen bottles, swapped as they melt.
  • Don't bring an open flame or a heat source near the tank.

Heat waves

The emergency runs in reverse for an axolotl: heat is the danger, not cold. Above about 75°F (24°C) it goes off food, develops ascites and floats. Use the cooling steps on page 25; for an animal already in crisis, a dish in the refrigerator is the recognized stopgap while the tank is cooled.

Travel

A sitter needs the pet-sitter sheet, walked through in person, and a thermometer they will actually read. In summer, the cooling has to work without you: test it before you leave.

Transport and moving house

  • Move the axolotl in a small, clear, watertight container filled with its own tank water, kept cool.
  • In warm weather: an insulated container in an air-conditioned car, never a hot trunk or a parked car.
  • For a move, pack the filter media in a sealed bag or tub of tank water, never tap water, with a battery airstone on a long drive. Set the filter up again as soon as the tank is filled, using that reserved water.
  • On arrival, match temperature before anything else, then move the animal in a submerged container.
