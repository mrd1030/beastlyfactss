Cost overview

The axolotl itself typically runs $30 to $100. Budget setup and ongoing costs on top of that.

Initial setup

$200 to $500 without a chiller (cool climates using fan-based cooling only), or $450 to $900 with a dedicated aquarium chiller.

Monthly ongoing

$15 to $45 for food, water conditioner, and filter media.

Budget for the worst case too Vet treatment for impaction or a severe fungal infection can cost well beyond routine care. Neither is likely if you follow this package, but both are real enough to plan for. Ask your vet for a specific estimate rather than guessing.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Axolotls are neotenic: unlike most salamanders, they keep their external gills and stay fully aquatic for life instead of metamorphosing onto land.
  • They have no eyelids, which is part of why bright light and glare are genuinely stressful for them, not just a lighting preference.
  • A tank lid isn't just for looks. Axolotls can climb well enough to get out of an open tank.

Tank size & cold-water setup

A 20-gallon long tank is the minimum for one axolotl; a 40-gallon breeder works better for two. Floor space matters more than height since axolotls are bottom-dwellers that rarely swim to the surface by choice. A secure, weighted lid is required. Axolotls can climb well enough to get out of an open tank.

Never Use a heater. In an axolotl's tank, warmth is the danger, not the fix. If your room runs warm, that's what a chiller or fan-based cooling is for, not a heater at any point.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Cycle the tank fully, ammonia at 0 ppm and nitrite at 0 ppm, before your axolotl goes in. See page 6.
  • Species-only tank: no fish. Fish can nip at gills or outcompete axolotls for food.
  • Juveniles under 5 inches: keep them on bare-bottom, not sand, until they're larger.
  • If you use a canister filter instead of a sponge filter, diffuse the output through a spray bar. Axolotls dislike current.

Cycling comes first

A tank needs to be fully cycled, ammonia holding at 0 ppm and nitrite holding at 0 ppm, before an axolotl goes in. An uncycled tank can't process waste fast enough, and ammonia or nitrite exposure is a water quality problem your axolotl will feel immediately. Test before you buy the animal, not after.

Cooling, not heating

Never use a heater. Use an aquarium chiller, or in a cool climate, fan-based cooling across the water's surface. Above 72°F, stress starts becoming chronic; by the mid-70s, conditions can turn fatal. A reliable cooling method is core equipment, not an accessory.

Test regularly, not just at setup Log water temperature, ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate in the Owner Log on page 18. A single good reading doesn't mean the tank is stable; a trend does.

Diet & feeding

Axolotls are carnivores. Earthworms or nightcrawlers are the nutritionally complete staple, cut to size for juveniles. Sinking pellets, salmon-based or invertebrate-formulated, work well as a supplement. Bloodworms make a fine occasional treat, not a staple.

AgeStapleSupplement/treatFrequency
JuvenileEarthworms/nightcrawlers, cut to sizeSinking pellets as supplementDaily
AdultEarthworms/nightcrawlersSinking pellets; bloodworms as occasional treatEvery 2 to 3 days

Why the staple matters

Earthworms and nightcrawlers deliver complete, balanced nutrition on their own, which is why they're the staple rather than a rotation of equal options. Pellets are a convenient supplement, not a replacement for the staple. Bloodworms are worth offering occasionally for variety, but leaning on them as a regular meal skips most of what a worm-based diet provides.

Never feed Anything too large for your axolotl to swallow easily. Oversized food is one of the two main causes of impaction, along with loose or coarse substrate. See page 10.

Watch appetite as a health signal

A steady feeding response, every 2 to 3 days for adults or daily for juveniles, is one of the simplest health indicators you have. Log appetite in the Owner Log on page 18 so a slow change doesn't slip by unnoticed.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Using a heater, or letting room temperature drift into the 70s. Overheating is the single biggest killer of pet axolotls.
  • Choosing gravel or small rocks as substrate, a leading cause of impaction.
  • Keeping fish in the same tank. They nip at gills or outcompete axolotls for food.
  • Running a filter with too much current. Axolotls dislike flow; keep it low with a sponge filter or a diffused spray bar.
  • Adding an axolotl before the tank is fully cycled.
  • Using bright lighting. Axolotls have no eyelids and are genuinely sensitive to bright light.
  • Handling more than necessary, covered in detail below.

Handling

Handling should be rare and reserved for genuine necessity, such as a tank move or a vet-directed exam. Axolotls are more handling-averse than most commonly kept reptiles and amphibians: their skin is delicate, their external gills can be damaged by contact, and they don't tolerate time out of water well.

Never Handle an axolotl more than necessary "just to hold it." If you must, use clean, wet hands or a soft wet net, and keep time out of water as short as possible.

Enrichment, by priority

Hides

A secure hide gives an axolotl somewhere to retreat, which matters for an animal with no eyelids and real light sensitivity.

Dim lighting

Keeping light low and indirect isn't just comfort, it's a genuine stress reducer for this species specifically.

Feeding variety

Rotating earthworms, pellets, and the occasional bloodworm treat gives some natural variation without changing the core diet.

Appropriate substrate

Fine sand or a clean bare bottom lets an axolotl move and forage naturally without an impaction risk.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact a vet promptly if you see Fuzzy white or gray patches on the skin or gills, curled gills, red or irritated skin, sudden lethargy, refusal to eat for an extended period, or a water temperature that has spiked above the safe range. Also call promptly if you've seen your axolotl ingest substrate or an oversized piece of food.

What to tell the vet

Current water temperature, and how long it's been elevated if relevant
Ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate readings
What you feed and how often
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening

Why water quality comes up first

Most of the conditions in this section, fungal infection, impaction, heat stress, and water quality burns, trace back to temperature or water quality more often than anything else. A vet will ask about your readings and your cooling setup before anything else for exactly that reason.

Fungal infection & impaction

Fungal Infection

Signs: fuzzy white or gray patches on the skin or gills. Response: correct temperature and water quality first, this is usually the underlying trigger, then cooler water changes and salt baths. For cases that persist despite correcting husbandry, a vet-prescribed antifungal is the next step.

Prevention beats treatment Correct water temperature (page 6) and clean, well-cycled water prevent most fungal cases before they start. This is the single highest-leverage thing on this page.

Impaction

Cause: ingesting substrate or an oversized piece of food. Response: for mild, early cases, fasting and cooler water under vet guidance sometimes resolves it. Surgery is reserved for blockages that don't pass on their own.

Don't wait to "see if it resolves" If you suspect impaction, especially if your axolotl stops eating or you know it ingested substrate or an oversized item, call a vet promptly. This is exactly the scenario the substrate and feeding guidance on pages 5 and 7 is meant to prevent.

Heat stress & water quality burns

Cause: temperature spikes or poor water quality. Signs: curled gills, lethargy, red or irritated skin. Response: an immediate water change and temperature correction; see a vet if the animal doesn't improve quickly or if signs are severe.

The single biggest killer Overheating is the single biggest killer of pet axolotls. Above 72°F, stress becomes chronic; by the mid-70s, it can be fatal. A reliable cooling method isn't optional gear, it's the one piece of equipment that keeps your axolotl alive through warm weather.

What raises the risk

  • Room temperature swings during warm months, especially without air conditioning
  • Skipped water changes, letting ammonia or nitrite build up
  • A tank that isn't fully cycled yet (see page 6)
  • Overstocking a tank relative to its filtration capacity

Why the two causes get grouped together

A temperature spike and a water quality problem produce overlapping signs, curled gills, lethargy, irritated skin, because both are stress on the same system. The response is the same either way: fix the water first, then reassess. Don't wait to see which cause it was before acting.

Check both, every time When you see these signs, test water temperature and ammonia/nitrite/nitrate together. Treating one while ignoring the other is a common way this recurs.

First 30 days & days 31 to 90

Week 1

Confirm water temperature and ammonia/nitrite/nitrate with an actual test kit, not by eye
Let your axolotl settle: minimal handling, focus on feeding and observing appetite
If you're planning a wellness check, do it while your axolotl is still settling in, not after something looks wrong

Week 2 to 3

Establish a consistent feeding schedule: daily for juveniles, every 2 to 3 days for adults
Start testing water weekly and logging results
Start the Owner Log on page 18

Week 4

Review the Owner Log for a steady temperature and water quality trend, not just single readings
Confirm the chiller or fan-cooling setup is holding temperature as expected
Introduce first enrichment items from page 19

Days 31 to 90

Keep testing water parameters on a regular schedule, don't stop once things look stable
Look back at the Owner Log for any slow drift in temperature, water quality, appetite, or gill condition
Start rotating enrichment on the monthly schedule from page 19
Reassess your cooling method before the first warm season it has to handle

Daily, weekly, monthly & seasonal routine

Daily

Check water temperature
Feed per the age-based schedule on page 7 (juveniles daily, adults every 2 to 3 days)
Quick visual check: appetite, gill condition, behavior

Weekly

Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and pH
Partial water change as your readings call for
Wipe down glass and check filter flow is still low
Log readings in the Owner Log on page 18

Monthly

Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings
Check chiller/fan performance and filter media condition
Inspect substrate cleanliness and depth

Seasonal

Reassess room temperature swings between summer and winter
Confirm your cooling method still holds 60 to 68°F during the hottest part of the year

Reference

Glossary

NeotenyThe retention of larval features, like external gills, into adulthood. Axolotls stay aquatic and never undergo full metamorphosis.
AmmoniaA toxic waste byproduct in aquarium water. Should read 0 ppm in a healthy, cycled tank.
NitriteA toxic intermediate product of the nitrogen cycle. Should read 0 ppm.
NitrateThe less-toxic end product of the nitrogen cycle. Kept ideally under 10 ppm with regular water changes.
CyclingEstablishing beneficial bacteria in a tank so it can process ammonia and nitrite before an animal is added.
ImpactionA gastrointestinal blockage, often from substrate or oversized food.
Sponge filterA low-flow filter using a porous foam block and gentle air-driven suction, well suited to axolotls' dislike of current.
Spray barA perforated tube on a filter's return line that diffuses outflow into a wide, gentle stream, used to keep canister filter current low.
ChillerA device that actively cools aquarium water, the primary way to keep temperatures safely in range in a warm climate.
