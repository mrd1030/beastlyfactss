How to use this package

Four ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

If you haven't set up yet

Read pages 4 through 14 in order before buying anything, and check page 6 first if you are in California, New Jersey, Maine or Washington DC. Cooling is the decision that shapes the whole budget, and it is far cheaper to plan for than to retrofit in July. Expect to spend several weeks cycling the tank before an axolotl goes in it. Then use the Budget and Shopping List on page 31 to buy once.

If your axolotl is already set up

Jump to the Setup Checklist and Targets on page 29 to audit what you have, and the Health Red Flags section starting on page 23 if anything seems off. Check the temperature and test the water before you change anything else. Most axolotl problems that look like disease are a thermometer reading or a water reading, and you cannot rule either out by looking at the animal.

If the water is already too warm

Go straight to page 8. Warmth is the single most common way a pet axolotl is harmed, and the fixes that work in the next hour are not the same as the ones that work all summer. Do not drop the temperature all at once, and do not add a heater at any point for any reason.

The one-line version An axolotl is a cold-water aquarium animal that gulps whatever fits in its mouth. Keep the water cold, keep the floor safe to swallow, and most of the rest of this package is maintenance.

What "never grows up" actually means

An axolotl is paedomorphic: it reaches full adult size and breeds normally while keeping the feathery external gills, tail fin and fully aquatic life of a larva. That is not the same as staying a baby, and it is not quite permanent either. The tissues still answer to thyroid hormone, and researchers can push an axolotl through the land-going metamorphosis a tiger salamander does on its own. What is switched off is the hormonal trigger, not the ability. In a home tank that switch essentially never flips, which is why nothing in a correct axolotl setup needs a land area or a basking spot.

Cost, commitment & fun facts

The animal is the cheap part. Cooling is what sets the budget.

The axolotl

$30 to $100 for a standard morph. Rarer colors such as mosaic or GFP run higher.

Setup, no chiller

$200 to $500, and only honest if your room genuinely holds under 68°F (20°C) year round.

Setup with a chiller

$450 to $900. The chiller alone is $150 to $400 and is the line that separates the two ends.

Monthly

$15 to $45 for food, conditioner, filter media and the chiller's electricity.

What you are actually committing to

CommitmentWhat it means in practice
10 to 15 yearsLonger than most people expect from a small aquatic animal. Plan for house moves, college, and summers away.
A cold room, or a chillerThe cost that varies most by climate and by season. A warm apartment in August is the scenario to budget for, not the mild week you buy the tank in.
Weekly testing and water changesAround 20 minutes a week once the routine is set, plus five minutes daily to check temperature and appetite.
A vet you have already foundAmphibian-experienced vets are uncommon. Finding one after something goes wrong wastes the days that matter most.
Budget for the bad month too Surgery for a swallowed gravel impaction, or a course of treatment for a severe bacterial or fungal infection, costs well beyond routine care. Neither is likely if you follow this package, and both are real enough to keep a small fund for. Ask your vet for a specific estimate rather than guessing.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • Axolotls regenerate limbs, and also spinal cord, heart and brain tissue, without scarring. A gill filament nipped by a tank mate usually regrows.
  • They have no eyelids. Dim, indirect light is not a preference, it is the condition they are built for.
  • They hunt by scent and water movement rather than sight, which is why a worm offered by tongs works better than food dropped anywhere in the tank.
  • The species is Critically Endangered in its only native home, the Xochimilco canal system in Mexico City, while millions live in tanks and research colonies worldwide.
  • A glass or mesh lid is not decoration. An axolotl can get itself out of an open tank.

Where axolotls are legal

Six outright bans, several permit states, and one myth that outlived its own repeal.

JurisdictionStatusBasis
CaliforniaBannedCal. Code Regs. tit. 14, section 671 lists genus Ambystoma as a detrimental restricted species. No pet permits are issued.
New JerseyEffectively bannedState environmental protection guidance lists axolotls among species that may not be kept as pets or sold. Permits are not issued for hobby purposes.
MainePermit, not issued for pets12 M.R.S. section 12152. The unrestricted amphibian list is frogs and toads only, and the list's conservation-status footnote would catch this species in any case: the axolotl is IUCN Critically Endangered.
WyomingBannedChapter 69 section 8(a) says a permit "shall not be issued" for injurious wildlife listed in 50 CFR 16, and the 2025 rule put genus Ambystoma there. It forecloses a permit rather than offering one.
AlabamaBannedBars possessing any amphibian listed as injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act, so the January 2025 federal rule moved Alabama automatically.
VermontPermit, not issued for petsSalamanders and newts are unrestricted as a group and then the whole genus Ambystoma is clawed back onto the Restricted list.
MassachusettsPermit always requiredThe class-wide amphibian clearance is subject to a categorical bar on IUCN-listed species, and this one is Critically Endangered.
HawaiiAllowed, conditionallyNamed on the List of Conditionally Approved Animals, so import and possession are allowed subject to Department of Agriculture conditions. A rare yes from Hawaii.
Washington, DCBannedD.C. Code section 8-1808(j)(1) permits seven categories of household pet. The reptile category is snakes, fish and turtles, and no amphibian appears anywhere on the list.
New MexicoBannedFifteen Ambystoma are on the Director's Species Importation List and the axolotl is not among them, so the default clause designates it group IV. Group IV importation is barred to the general public.
Virginia is legal, and has been since 2021 Virginia is still listed as a ban state across a surprising number of hobbyist sites and forum posts. Since August 1, 2021 it has been legal to import, export, sell and possess axolotls there with no permit, following an amendment that carved axolotls out of the state's general salamander-sale prohibition. If you have seen Virginia on a ban list, that list predates the change.

Why the bans exist

None of the restrictions above are about protecting axolotls. They protect native salamanders. Axolotls sit in genus Ambystoma alongside North America's mole and tiger salamanders, including California's federally threatened California tiger salamander, so regulators weigh hybridization, an escaped population establishing itself, and disease. California's "detrimental species" label makes that explicit.

The 2025 federal rule, and what it does not do

A federal rule effective January 10, 2025 added 16 more salamander genera to the injurious wildlife list under the Lacey Act, bringing the total to 36, all tied to the salamander chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal). Genus Ambystoma is among the newly added genera.

What that means for an owner: the listing restricts importing axolotls into the US and shipping them to or from Hawaii, DC, Puerto Rico and US territories. It does not restrict owning, breeding or selling one within the 49 continental states, a distinction the US Fish and Wildlife Service states directly in its own FAQ on the rule. With one twist worth knowing: Wyoming and Alabama do not write their own lists, they bar whatever USFWS lists as injurious. So for owners in those two states the January 2025 rule was not a federal technicality, it was the moment the animal became illegal to keep.

Check before you buy, not after Laws move, and a shipper's willingness to send one is not a legal opinion. Confirm your state and city rules before you buy a tank, and never release an axolotl or tank water into any waterway. Bsal is why that rule exists.

Tank size & the cold-water setup

A 20 gallon long (78 L) tank is the minimum for one adult, and a 40 gallon breeder is the better answer for two. Floor space matters far more than height: an axolotl is a bottom-dweller that walks more than it swims and only surfaces for a gulp of air. Water volume is not just space, it is stability. The larger the volume, the slower temperature and water chemistry drift, and drift is what actually harms this animal.

Never Put a heater in an axolotl tank. For almost every other aquarium animal warmth is the fix. Here it is the hazard, and the equivalent piece of equipment is a chiller or a fan, covered on page 8.

What the diagram does not show

  • Put the tank somewhere quiet and out of direct sun. Constant movement past the glass is a stressor, and a sunlit tank is a heat problem you cannot fan your way out of.
  • Cycle the tank fully before an axolotl goes in it. Ammonia and nitrite both have to hold at 0 ppm. See page 11.
  • Stand-height matters for you, not the animal. Water changes are a weekly job, so put the tank where you can reach the bottom with a siphon.
  • Leave a gap of an inch or two (a few cm) of air below the rim. Axolotls take occasional gulps of air at the surface.

Temperature: the numbers that matter

The single most important number in this package, and the one most often missed.

ReadingWhat it means
60 to 68°F (16 to 20°C)The keeping range. Veterinary guidance prefers the cooler half, 60 to 64°F (16 to 18°C), and a research colony targets 60 to 65°F (15 to 18°C).
Above 72°F (22°C)The ceiling. Sustained warmth past this point suppresses the immune system and stress becomes chronic rather than occasional.
Above 75°F (24°C)High probability of illness. Expect appetite loss, ascites (fluid swelling in the abdomen), uncontrolled floating, and bacterial or fungal infection. Prolonged exposure here kills.
Below about 50°F (10°C)Cold slows an axolotl down and cuts its appetite. Not an emergency the way heat is, and vets use cooler water as treatment, but a permanently cold tank is not the goal either.

Why warm water is so dangerous here

Three things happen at once. The immune system falters, so bacteria and fungi a healthy axolotl shrugs off get a foothold. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, and this animal breathes through exposed external gills. And those same bacteria grow faster in the warmth. Heat stress rarely arrives as a single symptom, which is why cooling the water is the first treatment for problems that look like something else.

Measure where the animal is Put the thermometer in the water at the bottom of the tank, not on the outside glass, and read it at the hottest part of the day. A tank that holds 65°F (18°C) at 8am and 74°F (23°C) at 5pm is not a 65°F tank.

Which method your situation needs

Your roomWhat to plan for
Under 68°F (20°C) all year, such as a basementA thermometer and a fan on standby is enough. Check it through a full summer first.
Cool most of the year, a few hot weeksFan, frozen bottles and air conditioning in that room. Have all three ready in spring, not on the day.
In the 70s°F (low 20s°C) for weeks at a timeA chiller. Nothing else holds a number that long, and a tank that spends every August above 72°F (22°C) is not one an axolotl survives for a decade.
Hot climate, no air conditioningA chiller as core equipment, in the budget from the start, with a plan for the outlet, the noise and the exhaust heat.

Cooling methods & the summer plan

Ways to take heat out of a tank, cheapest first, and one that buys margin instead.

Cooling methods, cheapest first

MethodBuys youNotes
Move the tankVariesOut of sun, off an exterior wall, onto a lower floor or into a basement. Free, and often the largest single drop available.
Cut heat sourcesSmallLights off, lid cracked open, shorter photoperiod, and turn a canister down: its motor makes heat that flow pushes into the tank.
Fan across the surfaceBest cheap fixEvaporative cooling. Aim a small fan over an open or partly open lid. Costs water, so top up with dechlorinated water and watch the level.
Insulate the tank sidesPreventionHelps most in a room that swings between day and night. Slows the gain rather than removing it.
More aerationMarginDoes not cool. Warm water holds less oxygen while the animal needs more, so run an air stone alongside.
Room air conditioningWhatever you setReliable, and cools you too. Expensive to run all summer in a hot climate.
Aquarium chillerSets a targetThe only method that holds a number whatever the weather. $150 to $400 plus running cost, and core equipment in a warm climate.
Frozen bottles are a stopgap, not a plan A sealed bottle of frozen dechlorinated water floated in the tank does drop the temperature, and it is worth knowing for a heat wave or a blackout. It is a poor routine method: the swing as it melts and gets replaced is itself a stressor, and it demands someone at home to keep swapping bottles. Never tip loose ice cubes into the tank. Aim for 1°F per hour, and never more than 1°C even for a tolerant species, in either direction.

Buying and running a chiller

  • Size up rather than down. A unit working at its limit runs constantly and leaves you no margin on the hottest day of the year.
  • Water has to be pumped through it, so check your pump's flow rate falls inside the chiller's rated range.
  • It exhausts the heat it removes into the room. Leave clearance, and keep it out of a closed cabinet.
  • They are not silent, which matters if the tank is in a bedroom.
  • Set the target mid-range, not at the edge, so normal cycling never takes the water out of range.
Test the plan before you need it Run your method against the worst conditions you expect while the tank is still empty, or at least before summer. A setup only ever tested in April is untested, and the failure shows up as a sick animal rather than an error message.

Filtration & flow

Enough filtration to process a messy carnivore's waste, delivered gently enough that it does not push the animal around.

Axolotls want slow, near-still water. They come from a canal system, not a stream, and a filter sized by flow alone will stress one badly. At the same time this is a large carnivore eating whole worms, so the waste load is real and the filter has to keep up. The way out is not a weaker filter. It is an adequately sized filter whose output has been broken up before it reaches the water.

The three jobs a filter does

TypeWhat it does, and why an axolotl tank needs it
MechanicalTraps uneaten worm, shed slime and waste before it breaks down. Strong mechanical filtration is worth having here, because leftover food is the main way water quality collapses in this tank.
BiologicalThe bacteria colony that turns ammonia into nitrite and nitrite into nitrate. This is the part you protect at all costs. It lives in the media, not the water.
ChemicalCarbon and similar media. Useful for clearing medication after treatment, not needed day to day. Remove carbon while dosing anything, since it strips the medication out.

Choosing and taming the filter

Good choices

  • A large sponge filter run off an air pump. Gentle by design, cheap, and it doubles as an air source in a power cut.
  • A canister rated for the tank or a size up, with the return sent through a spray bar aimed along the back glass or at the surface.
  • A hang-on-back unit with the outflow baffled, for example against a piece of filter foam or a bottle-cut deflector.

Signs the flow is too strong

  • Gills swept back flat and held there rather than fanning out.
  • The axolotl parked in one corner permanently, or repeatedly pushed off the glass.
  • Sand blown into drifts, or the animal bracing against the current to stay put.
  • Food carried away from the animal before it can find it.
Rinse media in tank water, never tap water Chlorine and chloramine kill the bacteria colony you spent weeks growing. When mechanical media clogs, swish it in a bucket of water siphoned out of the tank and put it straight back. Never replace all the media at once, and never run a brand new filter alongside nothing else and call the tank cycled.
Filtration does not replace water changes A filter converts ammonia and nitrite into nitrate. It does not remove the nitrate, and does nothing about what else builds up between changes. That is what page 13 is for.

Cycling, with or without an axolotl

The weeks of work that happen before the animal, and the recovery plan if it did not.

Cycling means growing the bacteria that convert ammonia to nitrite, and nitrite to nitrate, inside your filter media. Until that colony exists the tank cannot process waste, and ammonia and nitrite accumulate to levels that burn skin and gills. There is no shortcut and no way to tell by looking: the only evidence is a test kit reading zero on both while the tank is being fed ammonia. Expect four to six weeks.

Fishless cycle, the right way to do it

StepWhat to do
1Set the tank up complete: filter running, substrate in, hides placed, water dechlorinated, cooling running and holding temperature.
2Add an ammonia source and bring the tank to a measurable reading. Pure ammonia dosed to 2 to 4 ppm is the controllable way. Seed the filter with media, a sponge or gravel from an established, healthy tank if you can get it, which can cut weeks off the process.
3Test every day or two. Ammonia rises, then falls as nitrite appears. Nitrite then rises and falls as nitrate appears. Top the ammonia back up whenever it reads zero, so the colony keeps being fed.
4The completion test: dose ammonia to about 2 ppm, wait 24 hours, and test again. Ammonia 0 and nitrite 0 with nitrate present means the tank is cycled.
5Do a large water change to bring nitrate under 10 ppm, confirm the temperature is holding in range, and only then bring the animal home.
If the cycle stalls Nitrite that will not clear is the usual sticking point. Cold is usually why: a drop of just 1°C (about 2°F) cuts ammonia oxidation by around 30 percent, so a tank at axolotl temperatures cycles at the slow end by design. Check the temperature has not fallen further, that you have not rinsed media in tap water, that you are not overdosing ammonia past about 5 ppm, and that pH has not crashed. Patience beats additives here. A cycle that looks stuck is often three days from finishing.

If your axolotl is already in an uncycled tank

This is a common situation and it is fixable. Do not tear the tank down, do not replace the filter, and do not start dosing treatments. The animal itself is now the ammonia source, so the cycle will complete on its own. Your job is to keep the readings survivable while it does.

  • Test ammonia and nitrite every day, at the same time of day.
  • Watch from 0.1 ppm and change water at 0.25 ppm. Scale the change to the reading: 25 to 30 percent just over the line, 50 percent or more once either number is clearly climbing.
  • Feed less and remove every scrap of uneaten food. Less input, less ammonia.
  • Use a conditioner that temporarily detoxifies ammonia and nitrite between changes. It buys time, it does not cycle the tank.
  • Consider tubbing the animal in daily-changed water while the tank finishes, as on page 28. For a badly overloaded tank this is often kinder than the water changes.

Water changes & early warnings

The one job that fixes more problems than every product on the shelf combined.

The routine

SituationChange
Established, cycled tank20 to 30 percent once a week. Veterinary guidance puts the figure at about 30 percent weekly, and splitting it into two smaller changes is gentler if nitrate is climbing.
Nitrate still above 10 ppm after a changeIncrease the volume or the frequency, not the number of additives. Check that you are not overfeeding first.
Cycling with an animal in the tank25 to 50 percent whenever ammonia or nitrite reads above 0.25 ppm, daily if that is what it takes.
After medicatingAs directed by the vet, plus running carbon afterward to clear the residue.

How to do one without stressing the animal

  1. Prepare the new water first: dechlorinate it and match it to the tank's temperature. A bucket of cold tap water poured straight in is a shock.
  2. Siphon from the bottom, lifting settled waste and leftover food off the substrate rather than taking clean water off the top.
  3. Leave the axolotl in the tank. Netting it out stresses it more than the change does, and nets snag gills and toes.
  4. Refill slowly, pouring onto a plate, a hide or your hand so the stream does not blast the substrate or the animal.
  5. Log the date, the percentage and the readings on page 37.

Early warnings, in the order you will notice them

What you seeWhat it usually means
Gills curling forward toward the faceStress, most often heat or water quality. The earliest visible sign there is, and worth checking daily.
Gill filaments shortening or looking sparseLonger-running poor water quality, or too much flow. Gills regrow once conditions are corrected.
Appetite dropping offTemperature or water chemistry, before it is anything else.
Sitting at the surface, or floating and unable to sinkGas, impaction or heat. See pages 26 and 24.
Red or inflamed patches on skin or gillsAmmonia or nitrite burn. Test immediately and change water. See page 27.
Never do a full teardown Emptying a tank, scrubbing everything and refilling it destroys the bacteria colony and puts you back at week one of cycling, usually just as the animal is already unwell. Siphon the substrate and rinse mechanical media in tank water instead.

Substrate, hides & decor

An axolotl swallows whatever fits in its mouth, so the tank floor is a safety decision, not a styling one.

Axolotls feed by gulping: they open their mouth and inhale everything in front of it, food and floor alike. That single fact decides the substrate. Anything small enough to fit in the mouth and too large to pass through the gut is an impaction waiting to happen, and gravel impaction is one of the most common reasons a pet axolotl ends up in surgery.

SubstrateVerdictDetail
Bare bottomSafestZero impaction risk and the easiest to keep clean. The surface is slick, so some axolotls slip on it, and it shows every scrap of waste, which is arguably a feature. The right answer for any juvenile under about 5 in (13 cm).
Fine sand, under 1 mmGoodNatural to walk and forage on, and fine enough to pass through the gut if swallowed. Not risk-free, so keep it shallow, siphon it every water change, and use it only for adults.
Large smooth stonesSituationalSafe only if every stone is genuinely larger than the animal's head. Waste and uneaten food collect between them, so water quality is the trade.
Gravel, pebbles, coarse sandNeverThe classic fatal mistake. Swallowed, too big to pass, and the resulting blockage often needs surgery.

Hides and furnishings

Use

  • At least two hides per adult, ideally three or four of different shapes, so the animal can choose light level and company.
  • Terracotta pots on their side with the drainage hole sealed, wide PVC sections with every edge sanded, and smooth resin or ceramic caves.
  • Live or silk plants: java fern and anubias tied to decor rather than buried, and moss balls larger than the animal's head.
  • Driftwood and large stones, positioned so they cannot topple or trap a limb.

Avoid

  • Anything with a sharp edge, a rough cast seam or a gap a limb can wedge into.
  • Plastic plants with stiff, cut edges. Skin this thin tears on things a fish would ignore.
  • Small ornaments, marbles, glass beads and anything an axolotl could inhale.
  • Bright lighting of any kind. If plants need strong light, the animal needs somewhere shaded to sit out of it.
The head test Before anything goes in the tank, hold it up and ask one question: could this fit in the animal's mouth, and would it pass through if it did? Larger than the head, or fine enough to pass, are the only two safe answers. Everything in between is the category that causes impactions.

Choosing an axolotl, morphs & sexing

What to look at before you pay, what the colors actually are, and how to tell males from females.

What to check before you buy

Good signs

  • Full, feathery gills fanning outward and backward, with long filaments.
  • Clear skin with no fuzzy patches, red streaks, sores or missing toes.
  • A rounded body roughly as wide as the head, and a straight, unkinked tail.
  • An alert response to a passing shadow, and a seller willing to feed one in front of you.
  • A cool tank at the shop, and a known hatch date and current diet.

Walk away from

  • Warm water, gravel substrate, or fish sharing the tank.
  • Curled-forward gills, or gills worn down to stubs.
  • White cottony growth anywhere on the body or gills.
  • A floating animal, or one lying on its side.
  • Mixed larvae with visibly different head shapes. Tiger salamander larvae look similar young, then metamorphose onto land.

Morphs, in plain terms

Morphs are color varieties, not different species or different care. Every one of them needs exactly the same cold water. The common ones are wild type (mottled brown-green with gold flecking), leucistic (pale pink body with dark eyes, the classic pet-shop axolotl), white albino (pale with clear or pink eyes), golden albino (yellow-gold), melanoid (uniformly dark, no gold flecking) and copper (soft tan). Rarer combinations such as mosaic, chimera and the fluorescent GFP line cost more and are cosmetic only. One practical note: a dark morph hides early redness, fungus and gill color change, so a melanoid needs a closer look under decent light.

Bringing one home, quarantine & handling

The first week, and the reason this animal stays in the water.

Acclimation, step by step

  1. Get it home fast and dark. Keep the container out of the sun, and never leave it in a parked car.
  2. Float the sealed container in the tank for 15 to 20 minutes so the temperatures equalize. Temperature is the shock that matters most here.
  3. Add tank water a little at a time over 30 to 60 minutes, until the volume has roughly doubled, so chemistry shifts gradually.
  4. Guide it across into a submerged cup or container. Do not tip shop water into your tank, and do not use a net.
  5. Leave it alone. Dim the lights, skip the first meal, let it find a hide. Most eat within a day or two.

Quarantine

If you already keep an axolotl, a new one never goes straight into the established tank. Give it its own tank or tub, cycled or with daily water changes, for six to eight weeks, the period veterinary guidance calls adequate for a new amphibian. Zoos run a 30 day minimum and prefer 60. Watch it eat, move, breathe and pass waste normally, and use the window for a first vet check including a fecal test.

Why stricter than for a fish: Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans, Bsal, kills salamanders specifically and reached Europe through the pet trade. So a new arrival is quarantined even if it never leaves the house, and a captive amphibian is never released outdoors.

Handling: why the answer is almost always no

An axolotl has thin, scaleless, permeable skin, external gills doing the work of breathing, and a skeleton that is largely cartilage. There is no robust slime layer to fall back on the way a fish has. Handling risks skin and gill damage, stress and infection at any point of contact, and offers the animal nothing. This is a pet you watch, not one you hold.

Diet & feeding by age

A carnivore that hunts by smell, swallows whole, and slows right down as it grows.

Axolotls eat invertebrate prey and need no plant matter at all. Earthworms and nightcrawlers are the staple: nutritionally complete on their own, strongly scented, and accepted by almost every axolotl. Everything else in the diet is a supplement to that or a treat alongside it.

AgeFrequencyWhat and how much

Juvenile, 3 to 6 monthsAbout dailyChopped nightcrawler and small soft sinking pellets. Bare-bottom tank at this size.

Portion size, and how to tell

  • Offer only what the animal finishes in two to three minutes, and no piece wider than its own head.
  • Remove anything uneaten within 15 to 30 minutes. Leftover worm is the fastest route to an ammonia spike.
  • Target-feed with soft tongs, held just in front of the animal. It hunts by scent and water movement, not sight, so food dropped elsewhere in the tank is often food wasted and fouling the water.
  • Feed each animal separately if you keep two, so neither goes short and neither strikes at the other in the confusion.
The most common feeding mistake is not what you feed It is feeding an adult on a juvenile's schedule. Daily food for a grown axolotl produces an obese animal and a tank fighting its own waste. Obesity is a recognized problem in this species, and unlike most axolotl problems it builds so slowly that nobody notices until the body is visibly wider than the head.
Appetite is your best daily health signal A steady feeding response, every 2 to 3 days for an adult, is the simplest vital sign you have and it takes no equipment. Log it on page 37. Refusal is rarely the first thing to go wrong, but it is very often the first thing you notice.

Enrichment: hides, foraging & novelty

Not toys. Choice: somewhere to rest, cover that feels secure, and food that takes some finding.

The bar for "safe" is higher here No eyelids, delicate external gills and a real impaction risk from anything loose mean every item in this tank has to be smooth, stable and either larger than the head or fine enough to pass. That rules out a lot of what an aquarium shop sells as decor.

Structure and cover

Two hides per adult is the floor, three or four of different shapes and heights is better, because a hide is how an axolotl controls its own light level and its distance from anything alarming. Sideways terracotta pots with the drainage hole sealed, wide sanded PVC sections and smooth resin caves all work. Live or silk plants add cover: java fern and anubias tied to decor rather than buried, moss balls kept larger than the animal's head. A gentle current on one side of the tank paired with genuinely still water on the other lets the animal choose its own water movement instead of living in whatever you picked.

Foraging

Dropping food in the same spot and walking away skips the hunting behavior entirely. Tong-feeding, or scattering pieces of nightcrawler so the animal has to locate them by scent, is a real upgrade and costs nothing. A sand saucer, a shallow dish of sub-1 mm sand with small food items hidden in it, adds searching behavior in a contained way without loose sand across the whole floor. Rotate where you feed every few days.

Sensory novelty

A mild monthly rearrange of the non-critical decor, the plants, the extra tunnel, the spare stone, adds spatial novelty without taking away the hides the animal actually relies on. Introducing one new safe item for five to seven days and then rotating it out works the same way. Floating plants or a dimmed light create the dappled shade this species is drawn to. Strong current and bright light are not enrichment for an axolotl, they are just stress with a different name.

Heat stress & reading the gills

The condition behind most of this section, and the early warning system on the animal's own head.

Cause: water above 72°F (22°C) for any sustained period, and acutely above 75°F (24°C). Signs: gill tips curling forward toward the face, appetite loss, restlessness or the opposite, uncontrolled floating, ascites (fluid swelling in the abdomen), pale patches, and a general fragility that shows up as a fungal or bacterial infection a week later. Response: bring the temperature down steadily, and test the water while you do it. For an animal already in crisis, the veterinary literature names a dish in the refrigerator as a stopgap while the tank is cooled.

Cool it down, but not all at once Aim to lose no more than 1°F per hour, and never more than 1°C even for a tolerant species. Move the tank out of sun, fan the surface, float sealed bottles of frozen dechlorinated water and swap them as they melt, cut the lights, and stop feeding until the temperature is back in range. Never tip loose ice in, and never chase a fast drop with a large cold water change.

Fungal & bacterial infection

The two conditions that follow warm water and dirty water around, and how to tell them apart.

Fungal infection

Signs: white or grey cotton-like tufts growing from the skin or gill filaments, most often starting at a site that was already damaged. Cause: almost always secondary. Warm water, poor water quality, stress or an existing injury opens the door, and the fungus, usually a Saprolegnia species, walks through it. Response: correct the water first, cool the tank into the lower half of the range, and remove whatever caused the original injury. A salt bath is what most keepers reach for next, but the figures on page 28 are a published ceiling, not a prescription, so get the dose from a vet. A case that has not improved within 48 hours of the water being right, or one spreading over the body, needs a vet and a prescribed antifungal.

Bacterial infection

Signs: red inflamed patches, red streaking through the skin or into the limbs, open sores or ulcers, swelling, and an animal that is lethargic and off its food. Advanced septicemia may show as bleeding under the skin or a body-wide redness. Cause: commonly Aeromonas and Pseudomonas species, which are present in any tank and become a problem when water quality slips or the animal is already weakened. Response: this is a vet case, not a home one. Caught early, systemic antibiotics are usually curative. Left to advance, the prognosis is poor.

The preventable emergency, and how to tell it from the harmless version of the same symptom.

Cause: a swallowed object that cannot pass. Gravel is the classic, oversized food is the other, and both come straight from the way this animal feeds: it inhales whatever is in front of its mouth. Ammonia and nitrite burns

Tubbing, cooling & salt baths

Three interventions worth knowing before you need them, and where each one stops.

Tubbing

Tubbing means housing the axolotl in a plain container of dechlorinated, temperature-matched water changed completely every day, instead of in the tank. No filter and no cycle to maintain, because the daily change does that job. It is the standard answer while a tank cycles with the animal already in it, while an injured animal heals away from a tank mate, during quarantine, and whenever water quality has gone wrong faster than water changes can fix it.

  • A clean, food-safe plastic tub with a lid, big enough to turn around in and deep enough to cover the animal.
  • Change 100 percent of the water daily, matching temperature. Keep the tub cool and dark.
  • Never use soap or detergent on it, and keep the tub for this purpose only.
  • Feed as normal, removing uneaten food within 15 to 30 minutes.

Cooling as treatment

Cooler water slows bacterial growth and lowers the animal's metabolic demand, which is why published veterinary guidance for treating bacterial infection in axolotls holds them at 41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C) for several weeks during the course. Hobbyists call the home version fridging. Be precise about what it is: a therapeutic measure, under veterinary direction, in daily-changed tubbed water, brought down gradually rather than by moving an animal from a warm tank into a cold fridge. It is not routine husbandry and not a substitute for fixing the tank.

Power outages, heat waves & transport

For an axolotl, a power cut is a temperature problem and an oxygen problem at the same time.

When the power stops, three things stop with it: the chiller, the filter's flow, and the gas exchange that flow provides. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen at exactly the moment the water is warming up, and the bacteria colony starts dying within hours without flow. Unlike a tropical fish keeper, you are not holding heat in. You are keeping it out.

Do

  • Insulate: close the blinds, cover the tank sides with a blanket or foam, keep the room dark and shut.
  • Float sealed bottles of frozen dechlorinated water, swapping them as they melt, aiming for a slow hold rather than a fast drop.
  • Run a battery air pump into a sponge filter or air stone. That covers oxygen and keeps the media alive.
  • Stop feeding. No filter means no processing, and uneaten food consumes oxygen while producing ammonia.
  • If it runs long, move the biological media into an aerated container or the tank so the colony survives.

Don't

  • Don't tip loose ice into the tank, and don't chase a fast temperature drop. Aim for no more than 1°F per hour, and never more than 1°C even for a tolerant species.
  • Don't restart a filter that has been off for hours without rinsing the media. Stagnant media dumps decomposed waste into the tank.
  • Don't do a large water change in the dark with untested, unconditioned water.
  • Don't use candles, a gas stove or any open flame near the tank.

Leaving town

Trip lengthWhat is actually needed
1 to 3 nightsNothing much. A healthy adult fasts comfortably for a few days. Water change and a feed before you go, and no automatic feeder.
4 to 7 nightsA visit every two or three days to check temperature and filter, with food pre-portioned so it cannot be overfed.
Over a weekA sitter doing real visits, plus a water change if the trip runs long. Walk them through page 36 in person first. In summer, never leave a chiller-dependent tank unattended for a week.

  • Move the animal in a lidded container of its own tank water, inside an insulated cooler so it stays dark and cool.
  • Do not feed for 24 hours before a move. Less waste, and less chance of a regurgitated meal.
  • Guide the animal into the container underwater. No nets, no lifting it by hand.
  • Never leave the container in a parked car at any time of year.
  • On arrival, acclimate to temperature before anything else, as on page 17.

Glossary

AmmoniaThe first waste product of an animal's waste and decaying food, and highly toxic. The target reading is 0 ppm in a cycled tank.
BaffleAnything that breaks up a filter's outflow, from a piece of foam to a spray bar, so the current reaching the animal is gentle.
BsalBatrachochytrium salamandrivorans, a chytrid fungus lethal to salamanders. The reason for the 2025 US import restrictions on genus Ambystoma.
ChillerA device that actively cools aquarium water. The one cooling method that holds a target temperature regardless of the weather.
ChloramineA chlorine and ammonia compound used in many municipal supplies. Unlike chlorine it does not gas off if water is left standing, so a conditioner is required.
CloacaThe single vent behind the back legs used for waste and reproduction. Its shape is the main way to sex an adult axolotl.
CyclingEstablishing the bacteria colony that converts ammonia to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate. Roughly four to six weeks in a new tank.
DechlorinatorA water conditioner that neutralizes chlorine and chloramine in tap water before it reaches the animal.
ExophthalmiaBulging of one or both eyes. In axolotls it is associated with prolonged exposure to high nitrate.
GH and KHGeneral hardness (dissolved minerals) and carbonate hardness (resistance to pH swings). Axolotls need mineral content and do badly in very soft water.
ImpactionA gut blockage from a swallowed object, most often gravel or oversized food. The preventable emergency of this species.
LeucisticThe pale pink morph with dark eyes, the one most people picture. Pale skin, normal pigment in the eyes, and not the same as albino.
MorphA color variety. Morphs differ in appearance only, never in care requirements.
NitrateThe end product of the nitrogen cycle. Far less toxic than ammonia or nitrite, but keep it under 10 ppm with water changes.
NitriteThe intermediate product of the nitrogen cycle, also highly toxic. The target reading is 0 ppm.
PaedomorphosisReaching adulthood while keeping larval features. An axolotl breeds at full size while still gilled and fully aquatic. Often called neoteny.
QuarantineHousing a new animal separately, for several weeks, before it goes anywhere near an established one.
RegenerationRegrowth of lost tissue. Axolotls regrow limbs, gill filaments, and parts of the heart, spinal cord and brain without scarring.
Salt bathA short therapeutic bath. The published figures, 10 to 15 g per liter for 10 minutes, are the ceiling before salt does harm, so a vet sets the actual dose.
SaprolegniaThe fungal genus behind most of the white cottony growth seen on stressed or injured axolotls.
Sponge filterAn air-driven filter using a foam block. Gentle by design, which suits an animal that dislikes current, and it doubles as aeration in a power cut.
TubbingKeeping the animal in a plain container with a full daily water change instead of a filtered tank, used during cycling, quarantine, healing or treatment.

Where sources disagree, version history & about

Where sources disagree

Two figures are printed as ranges because credible sources differ. On temperature, veterinary guidance prefers 60 to 64°F (15.6 to 17.8°C) and a research colony targets 60 to 65°F (15 to 18°C), while the common hobby range runs to 68°F (20°C). This package uses 60 to 68°F with the cooler half preferred, since every source agrees on the ceiling. On adult size, vets give 9 to 10 in (23 to 25 cm) while larger individuals to about 12 in (30 cm) are documented, so both appear. A third figure is missing rather than disputed: the GH and KH numbers repeated for this species do not trace to a primary source, so this package prints no hardness target at all.
