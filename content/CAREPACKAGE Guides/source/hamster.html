Fun facts worth knowing

  • Nearly every pet Syrian is related. The species was brought into captivity from a single wild-caught female found near Aleppo in 1930, and almost every pet Syrian alive today descends from that one litter.
  • Their cheek pouches hold about a fifth of their body weight. That is why you can go a day or two without seeing a hamster eat while it works quietly through a cached hoard, and why "hasn't eaten today" needs reading carefully (page 14).
  • Bar-chewing is a measurement, not a habit. Wire-gnawing is one of the standard behavioral measures used to assess housing quality in small rodents, which is exactly why the bedding study on page 17 counted it.

Cost overview

The cheapest animal in this series to buy, and the one whose setup costs most relative to the sticker price.

The hamster

$15 to $25 for a Syrian, $8 to $30 for a dwarf with Roborovski and Chinese at the higher end, and $5 to $15 from a rescue. There is no morph-driven pricing here the way there is with reptiles.

The setup

$150 to $350, with the enclosure the biggest line at $30 to $90 and bedding at $20 to $35 because you are buying a lot of it. Full breakdown on page 27.

Every month

$15 to $50. Food is $5 to $12 of that; bedding and sand refills are the bigger recurring cost at $15 to $30, because deep bedding needs replacing to stay clean and dust free.

The vet

A routine exam is $40 to $80. Emergency care, most urgently for wet tail, runs $100 to $800 depending on severity.

The cheapest animal, not the cheapest pet This is the pattern worth remembering. A hamster costs less than a takeaway, and then demands a 700-plus square inch enclosure and six-plus inches of bedding that cost real money no matter how little the animal did. If the budget only stretches to the starter kit, the honest answer is to wait until it stretches further, because the enclosure is not the part you can economise on.
Lifespan changes the whole calculation A large veterinary study tracking hamster deaths found a median age at death of 1.75 years, with a range extending to about 3.65. Syrians tend toward the higher end at 2 to 3 years, dwarfs often shorter at 1.5 to 2.5. That is genuinely brief compared with almost any other pet, and it cuts both ways: less time to spread the setup cost across, but less lifetime expense overall, and a much shorter commitment to take on (page 23).
Where the money should go Enclosure first, bedding second, wheel third. Those three are the ones that decide whether the animal thrives, and they are also the three the starter kit gets wrong. Decor, tubes, and novelty items are the cheap part and the last part, in that order, and page 17 explains why the research puts them there.

Enclosure size & the starter-kit problem

Current welfare guidance calls for a genuinely large unbroken floor: roughly 700 to 775 sq in (4,500 to 5,000 cm²) for a Syrian and around 600 sq in (3,900 cm²) for a dwarf, with more always better. A 40-gallon breeder tank works for dwarfs; Syrians do better in something larger still, a 75-gallon tank or equivalent. Unbroken is the operative word: a floor split by tubes and levels does not add up to the same thing.

If you use a wire cage, bar spacing must be no more than 1/2 in (13 mm) for Syrians and 1/4 in (6 mm) for smaller species, Roborovski especially, because they squeeze through gaps that look impossible. And the cage has to be tall enough at the base to hold the bedding on page 7, which is where most commercial cages fail: they physically cannot hold the depth, which makes cage choice and bedding depth the same decision.

Never use cotton-style fluffy nesting material The stringy white fibre sold in pet shops as bedding wraps around limbs and can cut off circulation, and hamsters pouch it. Plain paper bedding, shredded plain paper, or hay are all safe nest materials. Give the hamster the substrate and let it build the nest itself.
Never Buy the starter kit and plan to upgrade later. It is too small for the floor space and too shallow for the bedding, and "we will get a bigger one soon" is how a hamster spends most of a two-year life in a box that measurably makes it chew the bars.

Bedding depth, and the study behind it

The single highest-value thing you can change, and the one no starter kit can accommodate.

What to use

Dust-free paper-based bedding or aspen shavings. Mixing a burrow-holding material with a looser one helps tunnels stay up instead of collapsing, which is the difference between a hamster that digs and a hamster that just redecorates. Avoid cedar and pine entirely: the aromatic oils in both irritate the respiratory system and are a direct cause of the infections on page 21.

How deep, honestly

DepthWhat it gets you
6 in (15 cm)The commonly cited minimum, and a genuine welfare requirement rather than decoration. This is the floor, not the target
16 in (40 cm)The depth at which every hamster in the study built a burrow and used it. This is the number to aim for
15 to 30 in (38 to 76 cm)Veterinary guidance specifically notes depths in this range enhance welfare for Syrians, by supporting natural burrowing
31 in (80 cm)The study's deepest group, in which wire-gnawing was not observed at all

Far deeper than most owners would ever guess a cage liner needs to be, which is exactly why it is worth stating in four rows rather than one.

The study, in three numbers Hauzenberger, Gebhardt-Henrich and Steiger assigned 45 male golden hamsters to groups with 80, 40 or 10 cm of wood shavings. The 10 cm group showed significantly more wire-gnawing and higher running wheel activity. Every hamster given at least 40 cm built a burrow and used it, with significantly less wire-gnawing. In the 80 cm group, wire-gnawing was not observed at all. Depth is the enrichment, and everything else on page 17 is secondary to it.

Where to put the depth

Deep on one side

If the enclosure cannot hold 16 in throughout, bank it: deep at one end for burrowing, shallower at the other for the wheel and dishes. Partial depth beats uniform shallowness.

Pack it, don't fluff it

Lightly compress the lower layers so tunnels hold their shape. A hamster cannot burrow through material that collapses behind it, and it will give up and chew instead.

Don't excavate it

Spot-clean the surface and the latrine corner. Do not dig out the burrow to clean weekly: you are demolishing the thing this whole page is about.

The wheel, the sand bath & the rest

The wheel: solid, and properly sized

A solid-surface wheel with no rungs is essential. Wire and mesh wheels cause foot injuries, and there is no version of that trade-off worth making. Size matters just as much: Syrians need at least an 8 to 11 in (20 to 28 cm) wheel and dwarfs 6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm). A wheel that is too small forces an arched running posture that is not good for the spine over time. The test is simple: a hamster should run with a flat back, not a curved one.

Don't read the odometer as a welfare score This comes straight from the bedding study: the hamsters on shallow bedding ran more. Heavy wheel use is not automatically the sign of a happy animal, and it can accompany exactly the same setup that produces bar-chewing. Provide the wheel because a hamster needs one. Do not use how much it runs as evidence the rest of the setup is right.

The sand bath, which is not an extra

A dust-free, non-clumping sand bath is a genuine species essential. Hamsters use it to manage coat and skin oils the way a bird uses a dust bath, and skipping it is not a minor omission. Use reptile-grade or children's play sand that has been sifted and baked, in a heavy dish deep enough to roll in, and change it when it clumps or smells. Do not use chinchilla dust, which is far finer and irritates hamster airways, and never use anything labelled clumping.

Cover, tunnels and foraging

Hamsters are prey animals and cross open ground reluctantly. Connected cover, buried tunnel sections, and scattered hides change how much of the enclosure gets used at all, which is measurable and is the point: a large enclosure the animal will not cross is a small enclosure. Scatter-feeding rather than bowl-feeding turns a two-minute event into a foraging session, and it costs nothing.

The rest of the list

  • A hideout, which is not optional either. A hamster with nowhere to hide is a chronically stressed one.
  • A valveless sipper bottle, checked daily for leaks, or a heavy shallow dish.
  • A food dish, though most of the food should be scattered rather than served.
  • Chew items: untreated wood blocks, sticks, and plain cardboard, which are what keeps the incisors down (page 20).
  • A digital thermometer, so the room's temperature is a reading rather than a guess (page 9).
  • A small kitchen scale in grams, for the weekly weigh-in on page 33.
Skip the exercise ball A hamster in a plastic ball cannot see where it is going, cannot smell the ground it is crossing, cannot stop, and cannot get away from anything. Ventilation slots also catch feet and toes. It is sold as enrichment and it is the opposite. If you want the hamster out of the enclosure, use a sealed, escape-proof playpen on the floor with cover in it, supervised.

Temperature, torpor & lighting

No heat lamp, no UVB, and one temperature threshold that genuinely matters.

RangeWhat it means
65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C)The range this package holds, kept stable, in a normal indoor room with no supplemental heat. VCA's rodent range runs to 80°F; 75 is the tighter line
Below 65°F (18°C)Out of the target range. Appetite and activity drop first, and this is where to start paying attention
Below 41°F (5°C)The figure Merck and LafeberVet both give for hibernation or torpor, which is dangerous and looks alarmingly like death (page 22)
Above 80°F (27°C)Heat stress. VCA puts the ceiling for a rodent enclosure here. Hamsters cannot sweat and a burrowing animal in a warm room has nowhere cooler to go

Cold is not torpor's only trigger: LafeberVet adds short days and both sources add a thin food supply, so 65°F is the point to act rather than where harm starts.

Keeping it stable

  • Put a digital thermometer in the room, at the height of the enclosure rather than up a wall, and read it rather than judging by how the room feels to you.
  • Keep the enclosure out of direct sun, off the floor where cold air pools, and away from a radiator, a draught, or an exterior door.
  • In a cold snap, move the enclosure to the warmest room rather than adding a heat source you cannot regulate. Deep bedding is its own insulation.
  • In a heat wave, move to the coolest room, add airflow, and give a ceramic tile in the shade to lie against. Never a frozen block a hamster can lie on directly.
Torpor is not hibernation Pet hamsters do not undergo true hibernation. What cold produces is torpor: a hibernation-like state with minimal activity and little or no eating, and a hamster in it can feel cool and stiff and appear dead. Gradual warming is the first response, never sudden heat. Page 22 covers how to tell the two apart, which is worth reading before you ever need it.

Lighting

No special lighting and no UVB required. A normal day and night cycle is sufficient. The thing worth remembering is that this is a nocturnal species, so bright light during its active hours is disruptive rather than helpful: a lamp left on over the enclosure in the evening is working against the animal. Keep the room dark at night, and if you want to watch a hamster being active, a dim red light disturbs it least.

Where to put the enclosure Somewhere with a stable temperature, out of direct sun, off the floor, away from a television or a speaker, and not in a bedroom if you are a light sleeper. A hamster is loudest at 2am, which is a genuine consideration and not a joke: a badly placed enclosure ends up moved to a colder, quieter room, and that is how the temperature problem starts.

Where to get one

  • A rescue is usually cheapest, and the animal comes with a known history and often an honest temperament assessment.
  • A small breeder who keeps their animals on deep bedding is the best source for a well-started hamster.
  • Pet shop stock is often young, sometimes mis-sexed, and occasionally already pregnant. Ask the age and check the sex yourself before you get home.

Syrians are easy to sex once grown, from the very different rear-end profile. If you are buying two dwarfs on the understanding that they are the same sex, get that confirmed by someone who can actually demonstrate it, and have a second enclosure ready regardless.

Diet & the schedule disagreement

The staple

A nutritionally complete, species-appropriate hamster pellet or lab block, not a loose seed mix on its own. Given a mix, hamsters pick out the sugary, fatty pieces and leave the fibre-rich ones, which contributes to dental problems and obesity. A seed-only diet is specifically flagged as too high in fat and nutritionally poor. If you like the variety a mix offers, use a pellet as the base and scatter a small amount of mix as foraging material rather than as the meal.

The schedule disagreement, stated honestly Sources genuinely differ. Some veterinary sources describe pelleted food as something that should stay continuously available, since hamsters are natural hoarders and rarely overeat pellets. Other feeding charts describe twice-daily measured feedings, roughly 2 tbsp a day for a standard-size hamster and about 1 tbsp for a dwarf, with a broader 1/8 to 1/3 cup range appearing elsewhere. Both approaches are legitimate. Pick one and stay consistent, and use the weekly weigh-in on page 33 to tell you whether it is working.

Reading a food label

A good hamster food lists a small number of recognisable ingredients and gives a protein figure. What you are avoiding is a bag that is mostly sunflower seeds, corn, and coloured extruded pieces, because that is a bag the hamster will eat selectively. If a mix is what you can get, buy it and use a plain pellet alongside as the actual staple, then scatter the mix as foraging material and expect to find the sweet pieces gone and the fibre left.

What good looks like

A named formulated pellet or lab block. Around 16 to 20% protein for an adult. Short ingredient list. No added sugar, molasses, or honey.

What to skip

Anything with yogurt drops or honey sticks included. Mixes that are visibly mostly sunflower seeds. Bags with no protein figure at all.

Changing brands

Over a week or two, mixing old with new. A sudden diet change is on the wet tail trigger list, which makes this worth doing slowly (page 19).

Buy small bags, not bulk Unlike hay for a rabbit, hamster food goes stale and loses vitamin content well before a big bag is finished, and a hamster eats very little. A bag you get through in two or three months is the right size, stored sealed and dark. This is one of the few places in this book where buying more is not better.

Scatter it, don't serve it

Hamsters are foraging hoarders. Scattering or hiding food around the enclosure rather than relying on a bowl encourages the natural behavior that is core to the species, and it turns feeding from a thirty-second event into something that occupies the animal. It also means you will find caches, which is normal and not to be tidied away wholesale, though a stash of fresh food needs finding before it spoils.

Weaning, if you have a litter

Dwarf hamsters typically wean around 3 weeks and Syrians nurse a bit longer, roughly 26 to 28 days, with all species fully weaned and self-sufficient by 4 to 5 weeks. Syrian litters must be separated before they mature, because the solitary rule on page 10 applies to siblings as much as to strangers.

Insect protein is not a treat, it is the wild diet Hamsters are genuinely omnivorous and eat insects in the wild. Mealworms or a little boiled unseasoned egg reflect that rather than indulging it, and they are worth offering regularly in small amounts rather than saving for special occasions. Two or three mealworms a couple of times a week suits a Syrian.

Why a hamster stops eating

Cheek pouches make this harder to read than it looks. A day of silence at the food bowl is not necessarily a day without food.

  1. Dental problems. Cited as the single most common cause of appetite refusal. Continuously growing incisors that overgrow or get injured make chewing painful, so the hamster avoids food while still hungry (page 20).
  2. Wet tail. A serious bacterial intestinal infection, especially dangerous in young or recently weaned hamsters, and fatal within about 48 hours untreated. Appetite loss is an early sign alongside soiled, wet hindquarters (page 19).
  3. Cold-induced torpor. Merck and LafeberVet put this below 41°F (5°C), with short days and a thin food supply as further triggers. Hamsters enter a hibernation-like state with minimal activity and little to no eating. Gradual warming is the first response (page 22).
  4. Stress. A new environment, handling, or cage changes cause a temporary, lower-urgency dip in appetite.
  5. Respiratory infection or other illness. Usually comes with labored breathing, discharge, or lethargy alongside reduced eating (page 21).
  6. Diabetes in predisposed dwarf species. Campbell's and Chinese dwarfs specifically, where it shows as appetite and energy changes alongside heavy drinking (page 21).
  7. Cheek pouch impaction. Uncommon, but soft or moist food can clump and lodge in a pouch. Watch for one-sided swelling, face-pawing, drooling, and a sudden disinterest in collecting food. This needs a vet.
The hoarding problem Because hamsters pouch and cache food, you may not witness eating for a day or more while the animal is fine and quietly working through a stash. Some can go 3 to 4 days on a pre-provisioned hoard. That is a completely different situation from a hamster visibly refusing available fresh food while showing other symptoms, which is the real emergency. Check the cache before you panic: if the stash is going down, the hamster is eating.

Handling: getting it right

Most hamster bites are not a mean pet. They are a startled, disoriented nocturnal animal defending itself.

The single most common way owners get bitten Reaching into the enclosure during the day while the hamster is deeply asleep. From the hamster's point of view that is not an attack, it is an ambush. Let it wake up and orient itself before you reach in, every single time, and handle in the evening when it is already up.

Getting it right

  1. Approach in the evening, or wake the hamster gently and wait: rustle the bedding, speak, let it come up and notice you.
  2. Offer a hand flat on the substrate with a treat on it and let the hamster climb on rather than reaching for it.
  3. Scoop from below with both hands cupped. Never grab from above, which reads as a predator taking it.
  4. Stay low, over a soft surface or sitting on the floor. Hamsters are quick and will jump or walk off a hand without checking what is below.
  5. Keep early sessions to a couple of minutes and build from there, and end while it is still going well.

What to expect, by week

WhenWhat handling looks like
Days 1 to 4None at all. Voice and smell only, from outside the enclosure. Let it dig and build a nest (page 28)
Days 5 to 10A flat hand resting on the substrate in the evening, with a treat on it. No lifting. End the session when the hamster loses interest, not when you do
Week 2Let it climb onto the hand and off again. Still no lifting. Repeat this until it is boring for both of you
Week 3Short lifts, a few inches above the substrate, over the open enclosure so a jump lands safely. A minute at most
Week 4 onwardLonger sessions on the floor or in a lap, still low down. Some hamsters get to twenty minutes; a Robo may never get past the hand-climb, and that is a fine outcome

Reading the signals

Relaxed

  • Grooming while on your hand, which is the clearest sign
  • Pouching a treat rather than eating it in place
  • Exploring outward rather than trying to get off

Stop now

  • Freezing flat, or pressing into a corner of your palm
  • Chattering teeth, or a raised paw
  • Rearing up on the hind legs with the front paws out

A hamster that has had enough should be put down rather than reassured. Ending a session before it goes wrong is what makes the next one easier, and in an animal with two years to give you, the pace of the first month sets the tone for most of that.

The first week & common mistakes

Almost all of these are decisions made at the pet shop, in the ten minutes before the hamster came home.

The first week

Do not handle a new hamster at all for the first few days. Let it settle, dig, and build a nest, and get it used to your voice and your smell nearby. Then start with a hand in the enclosure, then a treat from the hand, then a climb-on, then a lift. Rushing this stage is what produces a hamster that stays defensive for the rest of a short life, and there is no way to make up the time later. If you get bitten, do not drop the hamster, which is the reflex and the way the animal gets hurt: lower your hands to the substrate and let it off, then work out what startled it, because a bite is nearly always information about the approach rather than about the animal.

Enrichment: what the research says

Most small-pet enrichment advice is a list of things to buy. For hamsters there is a study with three numbers in it that reorders that list entirely.

The study

Hauzenberger, Gebhardt-Henrich and Steiger assigned 45 male golden hamsters to three groups with 80, 40 or 10 cm of wood shavings and measured behavior, including responses to acute stressors. The results split cleanly. Hamsters on 10 cm showed significantly more wire-gnawing and higher running wheel activity than the other groups. All hamsters given at least 40 cm of material suitable for burrow construction built burrows and used them, and showed significantly less wire-gnawing. In the 80 cm group, wire-gnawing was not observed during the study at all.

Why wire-gnawing was the measure It is not a quirk. Wire-gnawing is one of the standard behavioral measures used to assess housing quality in small rodents, which is why the study tracked it, and why "my hamster chews the bars" is a husbandry readout rather than a personality trait. The commercial default is nowhere near 40 cm, and most cages sold for hamsters cannot physically hold that depth, which makes cage choice and bedding depth the same decision.

Priority order

  1. Bedding deep enough to hold a real burrow, 40 cm as the floor and more if the enclosure allows
  2. An enclosure with the footprint and wall height to make that possible
  3. A correctly sized solid wheel
  1. Connected cover and buried tunnels, so the whole floor gets used
  2. Scatter feeding and foraging
  3. Novelty objects, last, and they are the cheap part

Cover, because a floor unused is a floor wasted

Hamsters are prey animals and cross open ground reluctantly. Connected cover, buried tunnel sections and scattered hides change how much of the enclosure gets used at all, which is measurable and is the point: a large enclosure the animal will not cross is a small enclosure. This is the reason space and cover are one item rather than two.

What not to do Don't house Syrians together, since they are solitary and this is one species where companionship is the wrong answer. Don't use a wire or mesh wheel, or one small enough to arch the back. Don't read heavy wheel running as proof the setup is working, because the study found the opposite pattern. Don't use cotton-style fluffy nesting material, which wraps around limbs. And don't add accessories to a shallow setup and call it enriched, because the behavior that changed in the study changed with depth.

Wet tail

Proliferative ileitis. The condition every hamster owner needs to recognize on sight rather than look up afterwards.

Why this has its own page A large veterinary study tracking causes of death in pet hamsters found wet tail to be both the most commonly diagnosed condition in the species and its leading specific cause of death, present in roughly 7 to 8% of the hamsters studied. It can be fatal within 24 to 48 hours without treatment. A condition that common and that fast is worth knowing before you need to.
CauseBacterial, and tied to stress. Transport, a new home, overcrowding, poor hygiene, or a sudden diet change are the usual triggers, which is why it clusters around the first days after purchase. It hits young Syrians between 3 and 10 weeks old hardest
SignsWatery diarrhea. A wet or matted rear end, which is where the name comes from. Lethargy. A hunched posture. Appetite loss, often the first thing you notice. A smell. In a long-haired hamster the wetness can be less obvious, so check by looking rather than assuming
ResponseSame-day vet, immediately. Do not wait to see if it improves. Treatment is antibiotics plus fluids and supportive care, and it works far better started early than started late. Ask which antibiotic, and check it against page 18 before the first dose. Keep the hamster warm and quiet on the way, and take the enclosure's details with you
RecoveryGenuinely possible with prompt treatment, and genuinely poor without it. Expect follow-up care and a period of supportive feeding. Full enclosure disinfection afterwards, and do not reuse the bedding

Prevention, which is mostly the first week

Minimise stress in the first week home: no handling, no visitors, no rearranging (page 28)
Keep the diet exactly as it was for a fortnight, then change it gradually
Spot-clean the latrine corner daily rather than leaving it to a weekly clean
Keep the journey home short, dark and quiet, and go straight home rather than shopping afterwards
Look at the rear end every day for the first fortnight. It takes two seconds
Never house two Syrians together, since crowding is on the trigger list (page 10)
Not every wet bottom is wet tail Diarrhea from a sudden diet change, too much fresh food, or a leaking water bottle can look similar and is less dangerous. That distinction is a vet's to make, not yours, and the cost of treating a mild case as urgent is one appointment. The cost of treating wet tail as mild is the hamster.

Overgrown incisors (malocclusion)

CauseHamster teeth grow continuously and are worn down by chewing. A lack of appropriate chew items is the usual reason they overgrow, and less commonly a genetic or jaw problem, or a tooth broken on cage bars. Once the teeth stop meeting each other properly, the overgrowth accelerates
SignsDropped food, or food picked up and put down again. Drooling, or a permanently damp chin. Weight loss. Pawing at the mouth. Respiratory infection & diabetes

Respiratory infection

CauseDraughts, damp conditions, and dusty bedding, with sawdust, cedar and pine especially implicated. The aromatic oils in cedar and pine irritate the airway directly, and a dusty enclosure does the mechanical version of the same thing. Stress and a cold room lower the resistance that would otherwise cope
SignsSneezing, wheezing, an audible click, labored breathing, and discharge from the eyes or nose. Lethargy and reduced appetite alongside. Open-mouth breathing is urgent at any hour
ResponseAlways a vet, since these do not resolve on their own in an animal this small. Antibiotics and supportive care, with the drug checked against page 18. At the same time, fix the cause: switch to clean, dust-free paper-based bedding, move the enclosure out of the draught, and confirm the room is not running colder or damper than page 9 allows

Diabetes, mainly in dwarf species

CauseCampbell's and Chinese dwarf hamsters have a documented high susceptibility. Sugary treats, honey sticks, and fruit-heavy diets are the typical culprits, and a treat-heavy seed mix does the same job more slowly
SignsExcessive drinking and urination, which shows up as a water bottle emptying faster and bedding that is wet more often. Weight loss despite eating. Lethargy, and a rough coat. In a small animal these are easy to miss without the weekly weigh-in

Torpor, and telling it from death

A cold hamster can look exactly like a dead one. Read this before you ever need it, because you will not want to be learning it in the moment.

What torpor is

Cold, short days and a thin food supply together produce a hibernation-like state: minimal activity, little or no eating, breathing so shallow and slow it is hard to see, and a body that feels cool and stiff. Merck and LafeberVet both put the temperature below 41°F (5°C), and LafeberVet names short days as a trigger in its own right. A room in the fifties will not usually do it alone, but that plus a short winter day plus a hamster eating poorly can. It is distinct from true hibernation, which pet hamsters do not undergo, and it is dangerous rather than restful. It is also one of the reasons a hamster stops eating (page 14). Check the room temperature first, because that is the single most useful piece of information you have.

Telling the difference

SignTorpor
Body temperatureCool to the touch, but not cold and not hard
BreathingPresent but extremely slow and shallow. Watch the flanks for a full minute in good light
WhiskersOccasional faint twitching, sometimes only in response to gentle warmth
BodyLimp or slightly stiff, but not rigid. Rigor mortis produces a genuine stiffness that torpor does not, and sets in within a couple of hours in an animal this small
ContextA room that has dropped well below the target range, often overnight or in a cold snap, and usually a short winter day as well

What to do

  1. Warm the room, gradually. Move the enclosure somewhere warmer rather than applying heat directly to the animal. Aim to get back into the 65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C) range over a couple of hours, not minutes.
  2. Warm the hamster gently. Cupped in your hands, or on a towel over a covered warm-water bottle, never in direct contact with a heat source and never in water. Do not shake, do not submerge, and do not bury.
  3. Wait. Rousing can take an hour or several. Movement usually returns before alertness does, and a hamster coming out of torpor is groggy and uncoordinated.
  4. Offer water and food once it is properly awake. Not before, because a barely conscious animal can aspirate.
  5. Then call the vet anyway. An episode means the room got too cold, and it can also uncover a hamster that was already unwell. If you are still unsure after two or three hours of warming, take the hamster in rather than deciding at home. Vets see this and will not think the question is foolish.
Prevention is one thermometer Put a digital thermometer next to the enclosure and read it, especially through autumn and winter, and especially if the hamster lives in a room that is not heated when nobody is in it. Torpor is entirely preventable and the prevention costs about ten dollars, which makes it one of the better trades in this book.

Lifespan, ageing & the end

This is where hamsters differ most from nearly every other pet, and it is worth thinking about before you buy rather than after.

The number A large veterinary study tracking hamster deaths found a median age at death of just 1.75 years, with a range extending to about 3.65. Syrians tend toward the higher end at 2 to 3 years, dwarfs often shorter at 1.5 to 2.5. That is a genuinely brief commitment compared with almost any other pet on this site, and it changes what good care means: there is no long run in which to fix things.

What ageing looks like

  • Thinning coat and bald patches, often over the hips and back.
  • Less running, and eventually no interest in the wheel at all.
  • Sleeping more, and being up for shorter stretches at night.
  • Weight loss, which is why the weekly weigh-in matters most at this end of life.
  • Stiffness, difficulty climbing, and reluctance to use levels or ramps.
  • Cloudy eyes, and less certainty about where things are.
  • Lumps appearing, which is the tumor risk on page 20.
  • Longer, less tidy nails, since a less active hamster wears them down less.

Adjusting the setup for an old hamster

Lower or remove high platforms and steep ramps, and take out anything a stiff hamster could fall from
Move food, water and the sand bath closer to the nest, so nothing requires a journey
Keep the bedding deep, but flatten a route between the nest and the essentials
Offer softer food if the teeth are worn or sore, and keep the room at the warmer end of range

The end

Most hamsters do not die peacefully in their sleep, whatever we would prefer. The honest version is that a hamster in obvious decline, refusing food, unable to move around its enclosure, or with an advanced mass, is suffering in a body that cannot recover, and a vet can end that gently. Euthanasia for a hamster is quick, humane, and inexpensive, and choosing it early is a kindness rather than a failure. Ask what it costs and what it involves at the wellness visit rather than at the emergency one.

Two years is not a small thing A short life is not a lesser one, and it is not a reason to invest less. It is the argument for getting the enclosure, the bedding and the diet right on day one rather than improving them over years you do not have. A hamster given a proper burrow for eighteen months has had most of its life the way the research says it should be, and that is a better outcome than a decade of gradual improvement would be here.

Where hamsters are not legal

The most legal exotic-adjacent pet in the United States. One state says no, and two more clear only the golden hamster by name.

Hawaii is the only ban

Mesocricetus auratus appears on Hawaii's List of Restricted Animals under Part A, which is headed "for research and exhibition," and that heading is the whole answer: Part A animals may be brought in by a university or a licensed exhibitor under permit, and may not be kept as pets. There is no separate pet permit to apply for. The reason is not about hamsters specifically. Hawaii runs an inverted rule under which introduction of live animals is prohibited except for those on the approved lists, so everything is barred unless something clears it. The concern is escape and establishment, the same reason the state bans every snake.

Power outages, travel & transport

A hamster needs no equipment running, so a blackout is purely a temperature problem. A journey is the one that carries real risk.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Read the thermometer rather than judging by feel, and keep reading it. Below 65°F (18°C) the room is out of range, and a room heading for the forties is torpor territory (page 22).
  • Move the enclosure to the warmest room, away from windows and exterior walls.
  • Add bedding rather than heat. Deep bedding is insulation, and a hamster in a deep burrow copes far better than one on a liner (page 7).
  • Drape a towel over part of the enclosure, leaving ventilation clear. Keep feeding normally.
  • In a hot outage instead, move to the coolest room, add airflow, offer a ceramic tile in the shade, and put out water in a second container in case one is knocked over or fouled.

Don't

  • Don't put an unregulated heat pad, a hot water bottle, or a hand warmer into the bedding or against the hamster. It cannot move away fast enough and it will burn.
  • Don't use a candle, a gas ring, or a paraffin heater near the enclosure.
  • Don't dig the hamster out to check on it. You would be removing the insulation that is doing the work.
Outage kit A spare bag of bedding, a towel, a torch, and a battery thermometer. That is the whole kit.

Leaving town

Trip lengthWhat to do
1 to 2 nightsFine alone, with the water bottle full and topped up right before you go, food scattered generously, and a room that will hold its temperature while the heating is off
3 to 5 nightsA sitter to check the water bottle, add food, glance at the rear end, and confirm the room temperature. Every other day is enough for a healthy adult
Over 5 nightsA sitter every other day who has done a practice visit with you, or better, the whole enclosure moved to their house if it can travel in one piece. Leave page 32 filled in

Transport and moving

  • A small, secure, ventilated carrier with a couple of inches of the hamster's own bedding, some food, and a piece of cucumber for moisture rather than a sloshing water bottle. Small and snug travels better than roomy.
  • Keep the journey short, dark and quiet, and go straight there. Transport is a documented wet tail trigger, which is the reason the first week home matters as much as it does (page 19).
  • Never leave the carrier in a parked car, hot or cold, and never in a car boot. Wedge it in a footwell where the temperature is even.
  • Moving house: move the enclosure intact if you can, bedding and all. If you cannot, bring as much of the old bedding as possible to rebuild with, so the scent survives the move. Expect a quiet, hidden hamster for several days, and watch the rear end daily for a fortnight.

Glossary

Cheek pouchThe internal pocket either side of the mouth that holds roughly a fifth of a hamster's body weight in food. It is why "hasn't eaten today" needs reading carefully.
CrepuscularMost active at dawn and dusk. Hamsters are this as well as nocturnal, which is why evening is the time to handle one.
Diabetes-prone dwarfsCampbell's and Chinese dwarf hamsters, which have a documented high susceptibility. It is why the sugar rules on page 13 are stricter for them.
Dwarf hamsterWinter White, Campbell's, Roborovski (all Phodopus) and the Chinese (Cricetulus). Smaller, faster, and generally more skittish than a Syrian.
FossorialBurrowing. Hamsters are a burrowing species, which is the whole basis of the bedding depth argument on page 7.
HoardingCarrying food to a cache rather than eating it where it is found. Normal, and the reason to check the stash before worrying about appetite.
Impaction, cheek pouchSoft or moist food clumping and lodging in a pouch. Shows as one-sided facial swelling with drooling, and needs a vet.
Lab blockA formulated complete diet pressed into a hard block. Nutritionally complete, and it doubles as something to gnaw.
MalocclusionTeeth that no longer meet correctly, so they overgrow. Almost always from too little to chew (page 20).
Proliferative ileitisThe clinical name for wet tail. The most commonly diagnosed condition in pet hamsters and their leading specific cause of death (page 19).
Sand bathA dish of dust-free, non-clumping sand used to manage coat and skin oils. A species essential, not an accessory.
SyrianMesocricetus auratus, the golden or teddy bear hamster. The largest, the most handleable, and strictly solitary.
TorporA cold-induced hibernation-like state in which a hamster feels cool and stiff and appears dead. Distinct from true hibernation, and dangerous (page 22).
Wet tailSee proliferative ileitis. Fatal within 24 to 48 hours untreated, and the reason page 19 exists.
Wire-gnawingChewing the bars. One of the standard behavioral measures used to assess housing quality in small rodents, which is why the bedding study counted it (page 17).
