The species, size & lifespan

What you are buying, how big it gets, and how long it stays.

Five pet species

Five species are kept as pets: the Syrian (the largest and the most common), the Winter White, the Campbell's, the Chinese and the Roborovski. The Winter White, Campbell's and Roborovski are the "dwarfs" of the pet trade, and the Chinese hamster is sold alongside them though it does not share their tolerance for company. Temperament varies more by species than most people expect: a Syrian and a Roborovski, both sold under the same label, are genuinely different pets to hold. Where the species differ, the page says which one it means.

In the wild a Syrian is a solitary burrower that digs extensive tunnel systems and is fiercely territorial toward other hamsters, a trait that carries into captivity and is why a Syrian lives alone as an adult.

Color and coat vary: short-haired, long-haired, satin and curly "rex". They rarely change the price, and this is not a species with morph-driven pricing. The one care difference is the coat: some long-haired hamsters need weekly brushing to take out mats, bedding and debris.

Section 02 · Housing & Environment

Enclosure size, bar spacing & where it goes

Bigger than the starter kit, and the first thing to get right because it is expensive to redo.

Floor space

Current welfare guidance calls for a genuinely large, unbroken floor, with more always better. A Syrian needs roughly 700 to 775 sq in (4,500 to 5,000 cm²), and a dwarf species around 600 sq in (3,900 cm²). A 40-gallon breeder tank suits a dwarf, and a Syrian does better in something larger still, such as a 75-gallon tank or its equivalent. Buy the right size once instead of planning to upgrade later.

SyrianDwarf (Winter White, Campbell's, Roborovski, Chinese)
Unbroken floor700 to 775 sq in (4,500 to 5,000 cm²)About 600 sq in (3,900 cm²)
Tank equivalent75 gallons or similar40-gallon breeder
Wire bar spacing, at most1/2 in (13 mm)1/4 in (6 mm), tighter for a Roborovski

Types of enclosure

A bin, a wire cage or a glass tank can all work. Whichever you choose, it has to hold the bedding depth that hamsters need, and that is where most commercial cages fail: they cannot physically hold the depth, which makes cage choice and bedding depth the same decision. A wire cage needs bar spacing no wider than the figures above, because a Roborovski especially can squeeze through surprisingly small gaps.

Where the cage goes

  • A quiet area, away from running water and from other pets.
  • A room where the lights go off at about the same time each night, because a hamster is active at night and needs a routine.
  • Not a bedroom. A hamster is noisy when it wakes.
  • Away from drafts, since a draft or damp bedding is a bigger cold risk than a cool room.
  • Not right beside another hamster's cage, and not near the cage of any other rodent, because their active hours differ and each disturbs the other.
Never Buy the starter kit and plan to upgrade later. The classic small wire cage falls well short of current welfare recommendations for floor space and for bedding. Also never house two Syrians in one enclosure, in any size (page 19).
What the cage costs The enclosure is the biggest line in the setup: budget $30 to $90, and go bigger than a basic kit offers. The price is the reason the animal is the cheapest part of keeping a hamster properly.

Bedding depth & the study behind it

The single highest-value thing you can change, and the one no starter kit can accommodate.

What to use

Dust-free paper-based bedding or aspen shavings. Mixing a burrow-holding material with a looser one helps tunnels stay up instead of collapsing, which is what separates a hamster that digs from one that just redecorates. Avoid cedar and pine entirely: the aromatic oils in both irritate the respiratory system. Never use cotton-style fluffy nesting material, which wraps around limbs.

How deep

DepthWhat it gets you
6 in (15 cm)The minimum, and a welfare requirement rather than decoration. This is the floor, not the target.
8 in (20 cm) or moreBetter, because it leaves room for a deeper layer.
4 in (10 cm)The shallow group in the study: significantly more wire-gnawing and higher wheel running.
40 cm, about 15 inThe depth at which every hamster in the study built a burrow and used it. The number to aim for.
40 to 80 cm, 15 to 30 inThe range the researchers concluded seemed to improve welfare.
80 cm, about 31 inThe deepest group, in which wire-gnawing was not observed at all.
The study, in three numbers Researchers assigned 45 male golden hamsters to groups with 80, 40 or 10 cm of wood shavings and measured their behavior, including responses to acute stressors. Hamsters on 10 cm gnawed the cage wire significantly more and ran the wheel harder than the others. Every hamster given 40 cm or more built a burrow and used it, with significantly less gnawing. In the 80 cm group, no wire-gnawing was seen. Depth is the enrichment, and everything else is secondary to it.

Why it matters in practice

  • Wire-gnawing is one of the standard behavioral measures of housing quality in small rodents. A hamster that chews the bars is reporting on the setup, and in a shallow cage it is a depth problem before it is a toy problem.
  • The commercial default is nowhere near 40 cm, and most cages sold for hamsters cannot hold it. Choose the enclosure by how much bedding it can carry.
  • Accessories added to a shallow setup do not make it enriched, because the behavior that changed in the study changed with depth.
  • Heavy wheel running is not proof the setup works. The hamsters on shallow bedding ran more.
What it costs A starting load of paper bedding runs $20 to $35, and replacing it through the year runs $60 to $100. Bedding is the largest share of the monthly cost, and the reason the monthly figure is not zero.

Temperature, humidity & light

No heat lamp, no UVB, and one cold threshold that genuinely matters.
ReadingWhat it means
65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C)The comfortable range, kept stable. A normal indoor room needs no supplemental heat.
Below 65°F (18°C)Out of range. Appetite and activity start to drop.
Below about 41°F (5°C)A hamster can slip into torpor, a hibernation-like state that can be dangerous, especially with short days. It does not fatten up first, so one that fails to wake to eat can starve (page 22).
Above 80°F (27°C)Heat stress. A hamster cannot cool itself like a person, and the room is the whole climate system.
Humidity 40 to 60%The target for the room.

Keeping it steady

  • A digital thermometer and humidity gauge in the room turn "seems fine" into a reading. A hamster is too small to judge by how the room feels to you.
  • Cold is less dangerous than heat in most rooms, but a draft or damp bedding is the realistic cold risk. Keep the cage out of drafts and keep the bedding dry.
  • In a hot spell, move the cage to the coolest room with some air movement, and keep fresh water in front of it.
  • Below 41°F, keep the room warmer. This is the one hard floor for the species.

Lighting

No special lighting and no UVB is needed. A normal day and night cycle is enough. The thing to remember is that this is a nocturnal species: bright light during its active hours is more disruptive than helpful, so a lamp left on over the cage in the evening is working against the animal. Put the cage in a room where the lights go off at about the same time each night.

Torpor is not hibernation Pet hamsters do not undergo true hibernation. What cold produces is torpor, which can look alarmingly like death.

The wheel, the sand bath & the furnishings

Four items that are essentials, not extras.

The wheel: solid, and sized to the species

SpeciesWheel diameter
Syrian8 to 11 in (20 to 28 cm)
Dwarf6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm)

A solid running surface with no rungs is essential, because wire and mesh wheels cause foot injuries. A wheel that is too small forces an arched running posture that is hard on the spine over time, so a Syrian should run with a flat back. The caveat comes from the bedding study: the hamsters on shallow bedding ran more, so heavy wheel use is not a welfare score. Provide the wheel, and do not read the odometer.

The sand bath

A dust-free, non-clumping sand bath is a genuine species essential. A hamster works coat and skin oils out in it the way a bird uses a dust bath, and it is also where most hamsters choose to toilet, which does more for hygiene than any amount of bedding changes. Use a shallow dish heavy enough that the hamster cannot tip it and deep enough to roll in. Sift the sand when you spot-clean, and replace it when it clumps or smells. Chinchilla bathing dust is a far finer powder that irritates the eyes and airways, so it is the wrong product.

Hides, cover and tunnels

A hideout is not optional: a hamster with nowhere to hide is a chronically stressed one. Hamsters are prey animals and cross open ground reluctantly, so connected cover, buried tunnel sections and scattered hides change how much of the enclosure gets used at all. A large cage the animal will not cross is a small cage.

The rest of the list

  • Water: a bottle with a valveless sipper tube, checked daily for leaks, or a bowl. Never a glass tube for a Syrian, which bites through glass (page 14).
  • Food dish, though much of the food is better scattered (page 12).
  • Chew items, because a lack of them is the usual reason the front teeth overgrow (page 26).
  • A digital thermometer and humidity gauge, for the room.
  • A kitchen scale in grams, for the weight checks.

Cleaning & hygiene

Spot daily, deep less often, and never a total strip-out.

A hamster's cage smells of its own scent marks and its food stores, and a cleaning routine has to keep enough of both to leave the animal settled.

How oftenWhat to do
DailyRemove soiled bedding and uneaten fresh food. Wash and rinse the water bottle or bowl, and check the sipper for leaks.
Weekly, small cageMove the hamster to a carrier. Take out the bedding, wash the cage and accessories with a small-animal habitat cleaner, rinse off every trace of it, and let everything dry fully before fresh bedding goes in.
Every few weeks, large deep-bedded enclosureThe same full clean, with spot cleaning in between. Deep bedding is replaced regularly to stay clean and dust free.
Every timeSet aside a handful of clean nesting material and any dry food hoard, and put them back afterward. Leave an established burrow in place during spot cleans.
Do not strip it out A total strip-out erases the scent marks and the hoard at once, and it can leave a hamster unsettled for days. Return the nest, wheel and bowls to the same spots, since a hamster relies on the layout staying put. Clean in the evening, once the hamster is already awake.

The sand bath and the latrine corner

Most hamsters toilet in the sand bath, so sifting it when you spot-clean does more than any amount of bedding changes. Replace the sand when it clumps or smells.

Washing your hands

Small mammals can carry germs that make people ill, and pet food, equipment and bedding can carry them too. Wash your hands with soap and running water after handling the hamster or its bedding. Keep the hamster and its equipment out of the kitchen and anywhere food is prepared, stored or eaten, and clean its supplies outside the home if you can. Children under 5 are more likely than anyone to get sick from small-animal germs, and a bite or scratch can spread germs even if the wound does not look deep. Page 30 covers the one germ specific to hamsters.

Poor hygiene and wet tail Poor hygiene is one of the triggers of wet tail, the biggest threat to the species. Spot-cleaning the soiled corner daily is cheap insurance, and it does not disturb the burrow.

Diet, the schedule & weaning

One staple, a disputed schedule, and a few firm numbers around it.

The staple

A nutritionally complete, species-appropriate hamster pellet or lab block (a formulated complete diet pressed into a block), not a loose seed mix on its own. Given a mix, a hamster picks out the sugary, fatty bits and leaves the fiber-rich pieces, which contributes to dental problems and obesity. A strict seed-only diet is too high in fat and nutritionally poor. Hamsters are genuinely omnivorous,, so small amounts of fresh food and protein sit alongside the staple.

The schedule is a real disagreement

There is no single right schedule, and both approaches are legitimate. Pick one and stay consistent, with fresh water always available either way.

ApproachWhat it looks like
Always availablePelleted food stays in the cage all the time. Hamsters naturally hoard surplus and rarely overeat.
Twice-daily mealsMeasured feedings with a daily target: roughly 2 tablespoons for a standard-size hamster and about 1 tablespoon for a dwarf. A broader range of 1/8 to 1/3 cup shows up elsewhere.

The firmer numbers

FoodHow much

Treats: seeds, nuts, popcorn1 to 2 times a week, under 5 to 10% of the total

Diet by age

  • Pups: weaned at about 20 days, and as late as 3 to 4 weeks by some counts. Syrian pups are the group wet tail hits hardest, between 3 and 10 weeks old, so a sudden diet change is the last thing a weaned pup needs (page 25).
  • A new hamster: stays on the food it was already eating. Any change is made over about ten days, a little of the new food mixed into the old.
  • Adults: the staple, the small fresh amounts above, and scattered food in place of a full bowl.
  • Dwarf species prone to diabetes: the sugar rules matter more, since Campbell's and Chinese hamsters have a documented high susceptibility.

Scatter it, don't serve it

Hamsters are genuinely omnivorous in the wild and eat insects, so mealworms or a little boiled egg are a reflection of the natural diet rather than an indulgence. Scatter or hide food around the cage instead of relying only on a bowl: it encourages the natural foraging and hoarding behavior that is core to the species, and it turns a two-minute event into a foraging session that costs nothing.

Cheek pouches and caches

A hamster's cheek pouches can hold roughly a fifth of its own body weight and stretch back as far as the shoulders. It carries food away and caches it around the enclosure, so you may not see it eat for a day or more while it quietly works through a stash. Some hamsters can go 3 to 4 days on a hoard they provisioned earlier. That is different from a hamster visibly refusing fresh food that is on offer.

Keep the hoard when you clean Set aside any dry food hoard and put it back after cleaning. Removing it all at once is one of the ways a clean unsettles a hamster for days (page 10).
What this costs Pellets or lab blocks run about $30 to $50 a year and vegetables about $20 to $40. Most of the monthly cost is bedding, not food.

Water & why a hamster stops eating

Cheek pouches make this harder to read than it looks.

Water

Fresh water must be there all the time, from a bottle with a valveless sipper tube, checked daily for leaks. A hamster drinks about 9 to 12 ml a day (about 2 teaspoons), so a bottle that has barely moved in a day is worth a look at the spout. Glass water tubes are the wrong choice for a Syrian, which bites through glass.

Why a hamster stops eating

  1. Dental problems. The single most common cause. Overgrown or broken front teeth make chewing painful, so it avoids food even while hungry (page 26).
  2. Wet tail. A bacterial gut infection that can be fatal within about 48 hours if untreated. Appetite loss is an early sign, alongside a soiled, wet rear end (page 25).
  3. Cold-induced torpor. Below about 41°F (5°C), particularly with short days, with minimal activity and little or no eating (page 22).
  4. Stress. A new environment, handling or cage changes cause a temporary, lower-urgency dip.
  5. Respiratory infection or other illness, usually with labored breathing, discharge or lethargy alongside.
  6. Diabetes in predisposed dwarf species, as appetite and energy changes.
  7. Cheek pouch impaction. Uncommon, but soft or moist food can lodge in a pouch.

When to worry

12 to 24 hours, or any refusal with lethargy or diarrheaCall the vet.
24 hours with no food intake at allCritical, given how fast a hamster's metabolism runs.

See a vet now, whatever the hour count Labored breathing or wheezing · wet or soiled hindquarters · discharge from the eyes or nose · a swollen abdomen · seizures · noticeable weight loss · a hunched, lethargic posture. Any of these alongside refused food skips the table above.

Growth, weight, body condition & aging

A hamster's life is short, so the stages come fast.

Weight

A healthy adult Syrian weighs roughly 3 to 5 oz (85 to 140 g), females heavier than males, and every dwarf species stays under 100 g. A kitchen scale and a small container make a weigh-in a thirty-second job, and a steady number is the plainest sign of a hamster that is eating and well. Weigh it regularly and write the number in the log on page 39, because an unexpected gain or loss is often the first thing that changes. A hamster has a high metabolic rate and loses condition quickly once it is unwell.

Reading condition

A sick hamster often isolates itself and can show weight loss, a hunched posture, lethargy, rough fur and labored breathing. A healthy one has clean, clear, bright eyes and a clean coat with no fur loss, flaky skin, scratches or wounds. Check the body for lumps, bumps and scabs while you weigh.

Life stages

AgeWhat it means
About 20 daysPups are weaned (as late as 3 to 4 weeks by some counts).
3 to 10 weeksThe window in which wet tail hits young Syrians hardest. Keep stress, transport and diet changes to a minimum.
7 to 8 weeksA Syrian can breed. Sex is worth checking before this age (page 21).
1.5 yearsA hamster counts as elderly.
About 2.5 years and olderOvergrown nails, hair loss, more sleeping and drinking, and slower movement.

Aging

Tumors are common in older hamsters, particularly females, and can grow quickly given how fast a hamster's metabolism runs. A lump anywhere always warrants a vet visit. A hamster that digs and runs wears its own nails down, so there is no trim schedule, only a check for overgrowth in an older or less active animal.

Food and condition Diet sits behind several problems: obesity, diabetes in dwarf species, and the diarrhea that follows a sudden food change. A plain staple, small fresh amounts and treats kept to 1 to 2 times a week protect the weight you are measuring.

Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Most hamster bites are not a mean pet. They are a startled, disoriented nocturnal animal defending itself.
The commonest way owners get bitten Reaching into the cage during the day while the hamster is deeply asleep. From the hamster's side that is an ambush, not a greeting. Let it know you are there, and wake it gently and slowly before you try to touch it.

The pickup

  • Approach calmly and let the hamster wake up and orient itself before you reach in. Evening, once it is up, goes far better than daytime.
  • Let it investigate your hands in its own time. Then cup it between two hands and open them so it sits on your joined palms.
  • Scoop from below. Never grab from above, and never corner or trap one that is trying to get away.
  • Stay low, over something soft, and hold it no more than a few inches over a table or the floor. A hamster is quick, and a fall can injure it.
  • Do not handle one that is resting or sleeping unless it is necessary.

Taming, on the hamster's clock

Set the cage up before collecting the hamster, with a handful of its used, dry bedding moved in so the place smells familiar. Leave it alone for the first 24 hours apart from food, water and a look for any sign of illness or injury. Then, in the evening once it is up and active:

  1. Hand in the cage, no touching. Rest a hand inside so your smell and presence become ordinary.
  2. Floor sessions. In a secure room, sit on the floor and put a short tunnel in front of the hamster. Let it walk through and come out to investigate you. For the first few weeks, let it walk over your hands and sit in your lap.
  3. Stop while it is calm. Put it back while it is still relaxed, not once it gets irritable, or it learns that fussing is how a session ends.
  4. Short lifts. Once it is comfortable in your lap, practice cupping it up and setting it back down. After one to two weeks of this, most hamsters are happy to be picked up and hand fed.

A short session every day works better than long sessions at weekends with nothing in between.

Species matter

A Syrian is the easiest to tame and generally the least likely to nip once settled. Winter White, Campbell's and Chinese dwarfs are quicker and more easily startled, with a real chance of nipping when cornered. A Roborovski is the fastest and least suited to regular handling, and its tiny size and sudden movements make a drop likely, so most are better watched than held.

Out of the cage Time out is always supervised, in a pen or a hamster-proofed room with cables covered and no hazardous plants. Exercise balls are not recommended: they can be stressful, and the hamster cannot reach food, water or a hiding place while inside one.

Torpor: cold, short days & telling it from death

The one seasonal problem a pet hamster has, and it looks like the worst outcome.

What it is

Below about 41°F (5°C), particularly with short days, a hamster can enter torpor, a hibernation-like state with minimal activity and little or no eating. It is distinct from true hibernation, which pet hamsters do not undergo. Torpor can look alarmingly like the hamster has died.

Why it is dangerous

A Syrian hamster does not fatten up first, so one that fails to wake periodically to eat will starve. That is why the floor is a hard one: keep the room above 41°F, and well above it, since the comfortable range starts at 65°F (18°C).

What to do

  1. Do not assume it has died. A cool, still hamster in a cold room may be in torpor.
  2. Warm it gradually. Gradual warming is the first response.
  3. Check the room reading. A thermometer at cage height tells you how cold it really got.
  4. Watch it eat. A hamster that has been that cold and still is not eating once warm is an appetite-loss case, so follow the hours table for a hamster that stops eating.

Seasonal checks

WhenDo this
AutumnMove the cage away from windows and doors, which means drafts, and put a thermometer in the room at cage height.
WinterKeep the room at 65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C) and above 41°F (5°C) at all times, including overnight and during an outage. Keep the bedding dry and the cage out of drafts.
SummerWatch for the room passing 80°F (27°C). Move the cage to the coolest room and keep fresh water in front of it.

Wet tail

Proliferative ileitis. The condition every hamster owner needs to recognize on sight.
A same-day emergency Wet tail can be fatal within 24 to 48 hours without treatment. Do not wait to see if it improves. Get to a vet immediately.
Why it has its own pageA large veterinary study of 4,504 pet hamsters under veterinary care in the United Kingdom found wet tail in 7.33% of them, the most common specific diagnosis in the species, and the leading recorded cause of death at 6.27%.
CauseBacterial, and tied to stress. Stress, transport, overcrowding, poor hygiene or a sudden diet change are the usual triggers. It hits young Syrians of 3 to 10 weeks hardest.
SignsWatery diarrhea, a wet or matted rear end, lethargy and a hunched posture. Appetite loss is an early sign.
ResponseA vet at once. Treatment involves an antibiotic, and the choice matters in a hamster (page 29).

Not a single disease

The study's authors note that wet tail is frequently used in primary-care practice as a catch-all term for a soiled rear end in hamsters, from several sources, including diarrhea or urinary and reproductive tract discharge. It describes what the rear end looks like rather than naming one cause. That is a reason to get a wet-bottomed hamster seen, not a reason to diagnose it yourself.

Prevention is mostly the first weeks

  • Keep the first days quiet: leave a new hamster alone for 24 hours, then start gently.
  • Keep the diet it came on, and change anything over about ten days.
  • Spot-clean the soiled corner daily instead of leaving it to a weekly strip-out.
  • Never house two Syrians together, since overcrowding is on the trigger list.
  • Look at the rear end whenever you handle it.
  • Keep handling gentle and the environment calm. Rough handling and a stressful setting are part of the picture.
Check the rear end by looking A vet checks the area under the tail for stains or discharge, and so can you at every handling session. In a long-haired hamster the wetness can be less obvious, so look rather than assume.

The conditions of age, of fights, and of sugar.

Tumors

Tumors are common in older hamsters, particularly females, and can grow quickly given how fast a hamster's metabolism runs. Antibiotics: ask before the first dose

The same drug a dog gets can kill a hamster.

A hamster is a hindgut fermenter: it digests plant fiber by fermenting it in an enlarged gut full of bacteria, and that bacterial population is overwhelmingly gram-positive. Certain common antibiotics wipe it out, and toxin-producing Clostridium bacteria take over. The result is a fatal diarrhea called antibiotic-associated enterotoxemia. It is a well-documented reaction, not a rare allergy, and it looks a lot like the wet tail being treated. Hamsters can develop it three to five days after an antibiotic dose, with severe intestinal inflammation on top of the diarrhea.

If it happens

Any diarrhea, appetite loss or lethargy that starts during or shortly after an antibiotic course is not something to monitor overnight. It is a call-the-vet-now situation, and stopping the antibiotic is usually the first step the vet will want to take. Do not wait for the next scheduled dose. Route matters too: oral dosing carries more risk than an injectable form of the same drug, because it passes directly through the gut where the bacteria live.

Ask, do not refuse Small mammals get real bacterial infections that need real antibiotics, and an untreated infection kills too. The aim is only that the right drug is picked the first time, particularly at a practice that mostly sees dogs and cats.

Power outages, heat waves, travel & transport

Power outage

An outage takes the air conditioning, the fan and the heat at once. The thresholds do not move: 80°F (27°C) is the heat line and about 41°F (5°C) the cold one. You are now the climate system. Keep fresh water in front of the hamster and keep feeding it.

Summer: freeze bottles of water so it can lie up against them, use cool wet towels, shade any open side with cloths, darken windows against direct sun, and put water in more than one container. Cucumber, melon and apple help. Move the cage to the coolest room, such as a tiled bathroom or a basement, add whatever air movement you can, and check the thermometer rather than guessing.

Winter: the problem is drafts and damp, not the thermometer. Keep the cage in a draft-free spot away from windows and doors, off the floor, keep the bedding dry, and check the water has not frozen.

Relocate once the room stops responding and you cannot bring it back: a friend's house, a relative's basement, or a vet with power. Decide at hour two, not hour eight.

Heat wave

The same plan as a summer outage. Heat stress starts with rapid or open-mouth breathing and drooling, and the first sign, not the fourth, is the point to move. Cool gradually, and never plunge it into cold or ice water.

Transport to the vet

  • A sturdy, rigid, ventilated, hard-sided carrier the animal cannot chew through, with room to lie down and turn around. A wire cage is a poor travel choice.
  • A hiding place inside, such as a towel over the carrier or a small tunnel, and a handful of clean bedding from its own cage.
  • Strap it in with a seatbelt, or wedge it in the footwell, with a side facing the direction of travel.
  • A cool, well-ventilated car. Never direct sun, never a closed trunk, and never a parked car, even briefly.
  • A hamster travels alone. Keep the trip short, and choose a vet close to home where you can.
