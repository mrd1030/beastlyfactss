Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Red flags & finding a vet

Contact an avian vet promptly if you see Lethargy or appetite loss · feathers held fluffed for long stretches, or sitting on both feet with eyes closing in the day · sitting on the cage floor instead of perching · tail bobbing with each breath, open-mouth breathing or wheezing · discharge from the eyes, nose or mouth · a hen straining, wide-legged, or with a swollen abdomen · bleeding · a change in droppings that lasts more than 24 hours · any mouth contact from a cat or dog · any shift in normal behavior.

Why "wait and see" does not apply to birds

A prey animal that shows weakness is the one that gets taken, so a lovebird conceals illness until concealment stops working. By the time a bird looks unwell to a person across the room, it has usually been sick for a while and has burned through the little reserve a 50 g body holds. That is why the threshold in this package is deliberately low: any clear deviation from that bird's normal is a call, not an observation to make again tomorrow. Tail bobbing in particular is a late sign. It means the bird is working to breathe.

Finding a vet before you need one

Not every small-animal clinic sees birds well. The minimum to ask for is a vet who is a member in good standing of the Association of Avian Veterinarians, whose find-a-vet directory lists members by location. Find one while the bird is well, and write the number on the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}}) so the first exam does not wait on the search.

Psittacine beak & feather disease and polyomavirus

Two viruses, both best handled by keeping them out of the house. Both can hide in a bird that looks completely healthy, which is why quarantine and testing exist (page {{P:arrival}}).

Psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD)

Lovebirds are particularly susceptible. A circovirus attacks the fast-growing cells behind the beak and the feather follicles, so both grow visibly wrong. The signs are abnormal pin feathers and mature feathers that look stunted, club-shaped or pinched and change symmetrically across the body, feather loss in places a bird cannot reach, like the top of the head, and a beak that can grow flaky or overgrown. The virus sheds readily in feather dust and droppings. There is no cure and no vaccine, and most affected birds die of secondary infections once the virus has suppressed their immune system, sometimes within months, sometimes over years. PCR (polymerase chain reaction) can find it in a bird that still looks healthy, and a vet diagnosis is the only way to tell it from plucking, a bad molt, malnutrition or trauma to a follicle, and guessing wrong in either direction is expensive.

Avian polyomavirus

Lovebirds are among the species commonly affected when young. In unweaned chicks signs appear 7 to 10 days after exposure and include lethargy and a crop that stops emptying, and death follows within 24 to 48 hours of the signs starting. In budgerigars, mortality can reach 100% in birds under 15 days old, and many juvenile and newly weaned parrots die quickly with no signs at all. Survivors can show permanent feather dystrophy. Adult birds generally resist the disease but can shed the virus for up to 90 days before clearing it, so a healthy-looking adult is still a risk to a resident bird. A vaccine exists for selected psittacine birds. In the studied schedule the first dose went in at 35 days of age with a booster 2 to 3 weeks later, but an avian vet sets the schedule for your bird, its age and its exposure.

Quarantine every new bird. This is the whole prevention strategy. A separate air space, separate dishes and tools, established birds cared for first, hands washed between birds, and a real vet visit with PCR screening for PBFD, polyomavirus and psittacosis. Skip any one piece and the others do not fully cover for it. Birds that have been to a show, fair or exhibition get the same 30 days.

Where exposure happens

  • Bringing home a second bird without quarantine, the commonest route, and one that is easy to skip on the second or third addition.
  • Birds from many sources sharing air and surfaces, as at a shop that puts new stock straight onto the floor.
  • Feather dust, dried droppings and dander on your hands and clothing after handling someone else's bird.
  • Wooden perches, rope toys and nest material from a sick bird, which cannot be disinfected reliably and are thrown out.

Respiratory disease & psittacosis

The category that moves fastest, in the animal with the least reserve. Watch for repeated sneezing, discharge from the nostrils or eyes, a change or loss of voice, fluffed posture, and a tail that bobs with every breath. Open-mouth breathing, wheezing and clicking are signs of a bird in trouble: an emergency, not an appointment next week.

Rule out the air before the bird Before assuming infection, account for what the bird has been breathing: an overheated nonstick pan, an aerosol or air freshener, a scented candle, new paint, smoke. Fume exposure and infection look alike from across the room and are treated completely differently (page {{P:hazards}}). Vitamin A deficiency can also cause sneezing, discharge and labored breathing.

Psittacosis, and why it matters to you as well as the bird

Psittacosis is caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. Sick birds and apparently healthy ones both shed it in droppings and secretions, so a bird can carry it with no signs at all, and some infected birds shed only intermittently. In the bird, bright lime-green droppings and urates are one sign, alongside respiratory trouble. PCR is the test, and a confirmed case is treated with doxycycline, typically for 45 days, because the bacterium needs a long course. A short course is not a substitute for quarantine, and a bird is not treated preventively.

This one is zoonotic: people catch it from birds People usually catch it by breathing dust from dried droppings or secretions, and less often from a bite or mouth contact. Symptoms typically start 5 to 14 days after exposure, occasionally later: a dry cough, fever and chills, headache and muscle aches, which can progress to pneumonia. If anyone in the house develops them after contact with a bird, tell the doctor about the bird. Dampen droppings rather than sweeping dry dust into the air, and wash your hands after the cage (page {{P:cleaning}}).

Supportive care on the way to the vet

  • Warmth, around 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), in a quiet room. Birds recover and eat better at the upper end of that range, and a covered cage in the warmest interior room works.
  • Quiet and dim, in a small carrier with a low perch or a folded towel on the floor.
  • Stop adding heat if the bird pants with its beak open, holds its feathers sleek and flat, or holds its wings away from its body. Those mean it is too hot.

Feather plucking & behavioral health

Plucking is a symptom, not a diagnosis, with medical and behavioral causes that look identical across the room. Rule out the medical causes first, then fix the environment. Lovebirds are prone to it: in a survey of pet parrots in Japan, about a quarter of lovebirds showed feather-damaging behavior, against 4.9% of budgerigars, and adults were about three times as likely as juveniles.

The reach test

A bird can only chew, pull or over-preen feathers it can reach. Damage across the chest, back or under the wings with a full, undisturbed head is very likely self-inflicted. Feather loss on top of the head, where the beak cannot reach, is not plucking and points to PBFD or another cause. In lovebirds the damage is often aimed at the thin skin of the wing membranes.

Medical causes to rule out

  • Vitamin A deficiency, which lists feather picking among its signs
  • Infections, and parasites such as giardiasis
  • PBFD, which looks identical to a habit at first glance
  • Polyfolliculosis, several feathers from one follicle, which itches
  • Liver disease from a high-fat diet

Behavioral causes to fix

  • Boredom, and a cage with nothing to destroy
  • Insufficient sleep, under 10 hours of darkness
  • A cage too small to move in
  • Sexual frustration and hormonal triggers
  • Loneliness, or separation anxiety in a single bird
Start with the basics before anything complex Cage size (page {{P:enclosure}}), 10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness (page {{P:climate}}), a pellet-forward diet (page {{P:diet}}) and daily interaction and foraging (page {{P:enrichment}}). A surprising share of cases trace to one of those four. Add foraging before you add toys.

The other boredom behaviors

Plucking is one of three signals the same problem produces. The other two are repetitive pacing along the same stretch of perch and screaming that is new, louder, or well beyond normal dawn and dusk calling. Treat all three the same way: a vet check to exclude a medical cause, then a hard look at sleep, foraging, cage size and daily contact. In that survey, hours alone and having a cage mate did not change the odds; separation anxiety did. The aim is a lovebird that can settle alone.

Do not assume a bird is just stressed Plucking always deserves a medical rule-out first, because an illness and a viral disease with no cure can produce feather loss that looks like a habit. Weeks spent rearranging the cage while an underlying cause goes untreated are weeks the bird stays unwell.
Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Nutritional disease, gut problems & injuries

Less dramatic than the last pages, and far more likely to be what you actually deal with. Most of this page starts in the food bowl or in the cage furniture.

Vitamin A deficiency and calcium shortfall

All-seed diets, and half-seed, half-pellet diets that are not portion controlled, are low in vitamin A. It shows as nasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eyes, conjunctivitis, labored breathing, increased thirst and urination, poor feather quality, feather picking and appetite loss. Seed is also poor in calcium relative to phosphorus, and indoor birds make less vitamin D3, so a shortfall shows as weakness, tremors, seizures and fractures with no obvious injury. The fix is pellets, orange and dark green vegetables, and UVB light (pages {{P:supplements}} and {{P:veg}}).

Obesity and fatty liver disease

High-fat food, meaning seed, nuts and table food, plus too much of it and too little exercise, leads to obesity, and fatty liver follows. Lameness and breathing trouble are what a keeper sees. It is corrected with a pelleted diet, portion control, and exercise through a larger cage, more than one feeding station and climbing perches. An overgrown beak is not a grooming problem: it points to malnutrition, liver disease, mites or past trauma, so it needs a vet workup rather than another trim.

Avian gastric yeast and the other conditions the species gets

Avian gastric yeast can leave a bird eating normally, with smooth feathers, and still losing weight. Whole seed or pellets in the droppings is a sign, and one fecal test can miss it, so a vet decides. Giardiasis, polyfolliculitis, poxvirus and so-called lovebird eye disease, marked by discharge from the eyes, depression and weight loss, also turn up in the species and need a vet.

Injuries

  • Broken blood feather: firm pressure, powder on the broken tip only, never pulled at home (page {{P:flight}}).
  • Cat or dog contact: a same-day vet visit even with no wound, because bacteria in the mouth can kill a small bird within hours (page {{P:proofing}}).
  • Window or fan collision, a toe caught in a loose fiber or open clip: a vet call, since a bird this size carries very little blood.
  • Nails: there is no fixed trim interval. Rough, varied perches wear nails down. The quick, the blood vessel and nerve inside the nail, shows as a pink core on a pale nail and is found on a dark one by backlighting with a flashlight. Take a little at a time. If you cut it, pinch the toe side to side above the nail, press styptic powder or cornstarch onto the cut end, and keep the bird away from the water dish for several hours.
The common thread Diet, sleep, light and an annual wellness exam prevent more of this list than any treatment cures.

Reading droppings, urates & hydration

The cheapest daily health check you have, and a small bird gives you dozens of chances at it: most pet birds pass a dropping every 20 to 30 minutes. Line the cage with plain white paper, change it daily, and glance at it at the same time each day, so you already know your bird's normal before anything changes.

One dropping, three parts

The feces are the solid, formed, worm-like part, green to brown depending on diet. The urates are the white to cream, chalky part, the bird's solid urine. The urine is the clear liquid ring around the rest. All three leave through one opening, which is why you see them together.

The 24-hour rule Once you know your bird's normal, any deviation that lasts more than 24 hours is a same-day call to an avian vet. Blood, black or tarry droppings, a bird that has stopped producing droppings near its normal rate, or any change with a fluffed, quiet or off-food bird does not wait the 24 hours. One noticeably larger dropping first thing in the morning is normal: it is the night's worth held over.

Hydration

  • Fresh, clean water at all times, with the dish washed daily rather than topped up, and changed more often if the bird bathes or drops food in it.
  • Note anything unusual with a date, or take a phone photo: one odd dropping described accurately is worth far more to a vet than a vague memory.
