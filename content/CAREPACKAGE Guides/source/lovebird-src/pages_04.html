Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling, taming & the bite problem

The name oversells the temperament. Lovebirds are affectionate and bold, but they are very territorial and aggressive when frightened or threatened, especially hens and especially during hormonal periods, and a lovebird will readily chase a much larger bird, or the family dog or cat, out of what it considers its space. That does not make it a bad pet. It makes it a bird that has to be handled on its own terms, and a lovebird has to be handled daily to stay tame.

Taming, in order

  • Talk softly and move slowly around the cage, and keep doing it until the bird stops backing away from you.
  • Teach step up on a stick or dowel first: hold it gently against the bird's chest while offering a treat, so stepping onto it pays. A stick is safer and faster than a hand, and it gives you a way to move an angry bird without a fight.
  • Do the stick work in a neutral room, outside the cage, where a defensive bird is calmer.
  • Move to a finger only once the stick is reliable.
Cage aggression has a mechanical fix Most cage aggression is territorial, so the quickest wins are structural: remove any nest-like object, fit exterior food dishes so your hand never goes in to feed, and take the bird to a neutral room before asking anything of it. The aggression often eases away from the cage. Reaching into the cage of an already defensive bird is a reliable way to get bitten.

Where to touch

Head and neck only. Stroking the back, under the wings or around the vent reads to a hen as a mate's touch and encourages laying, so keep contact to the head and neck, and keep sessions shorter during a laying episode (page {{P:hormones}}).

Where lovebirds sit against other small parrots

A lovebird is beakier and more aggressive for its size than a budgie or a cockatiel, and unlike a budgie it should never share space with another species: the pair-bonding drive that makes it devoted turns into real aggression toward a mismatched cage mate. It is also a more dedicated chewer, which matters when choosing toys. A bird handled from young is no harder than any other small parrot, but one that is not can be a difficult bird.

Body language, normal noise & when it is not

Small parrots have feathers, feet, eyes and a beak to speak with, and no single cue settles anything. Much of this rests on clinical experience rather than controlled studies, so the habit that pays is knowing your own bird's ordinary day, so that a change registers as a change.

What to checkFluffed and relaxedFluffed and possibly ill
DurationMinutes, then the feathers sleek back downHours, or most of the day
FeetOne foot tucked up into the belly feathersStanding flat on both feet
EyesOpen and alert, or briefly closed mid-nap and open readily to soundStay closed, or slow and reluctant to open
ActivityStill interested in food, toys and peopleQuiet, withdrawn, slow to respond
PositionNormal perch height, uprightSitting low, or on the cage floor

A bird matching two or three items in the right-hand column, especially both feet down plus sitting low or on the floor, has moved past a normal rest posture. That is a same-day call to an avian vet, not an overnight wait (page {{P:redflags}}).

The cues, one by one

  • Beak grinding is a soft scraping sound a comfortable bird makes, often just before sleep. Hearing it is a reasonable sign the bird feels safe.
  • Eye pinning, the rapid shrinking and widening of the pupils, marks arousal. It shows up around play and excitement and also around aggression and territorial defense, so a pinning bird is worked up, not necessarily happy. Reaching toward its face carries real bite risk.
  • Tail fanning spreads the tail flat like a hand fan, briefly, with excitement. Tail bobbing pumps up and down with every breath and means the bird is working to breathe, a same-day vet call.
  • Head bobbing can be impatience, or the prelude to regurgitating onto a favorite person or toy. That is a recognized affection behavior, distinct from vomiting, which comes with visible distress.
  • Crest position does not apply: lovebirds have no crest to read.

Normal noise, and when it is not

Lovebirds are vocal at dawn and dusk, and that contact calling is flock behavior that cannot be trained away, only accommodated. You can reduce nuisance screaming by ignoring the noise and rewarding quiet; silence is not realistic. What deserves attention is a change: screaming that is new or louder through the day, pacing the same stretch of perch, or a bird that has gone quiet and still (page {{P:plucking}}).

Where this meets weight Fluffing says something may be off today. A downward weight trend says it has been off for a while. Run both checks together, never one as a substitute for the other (page {{P:growth}}).
Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

One bird or two: the decision that shapes everything

Parrots are flock animals and need a social bond with something. A lovebird forms that bond with a person or with another lovebird, and once two lovebirds bond to each other you cannot easily undo it to get an interactive pet back. Make the choice before you buy.

ChoiceWhat you actually getWhat it costs you
One hand-raised birdBonds closely to its person and becomes tame and interactive: rides on shoulders, steps up reliably, often affectionateHandling and time out of the cage every day to stay that way, for 10 to 15 years or more
A bonded pairBond to each other. Entertaining to watch, busy, and largely uninterested in you. Closer to keeping a pair of finches than a tame parrotA bigger cage and double the food and toys, and birds that are rarely tame or reliably handleable
Two birds, not matchedThe risky middle: a pair that is not a true match can turn on each otherA second cage kept ready, and slow, supervised introductions
Aggression between mates is real Aggression between mates is common, usually the hen bullying the male, and in a bad case it ends in injury or death. Different lovebird species fight when housed together. Triggers include a poorly introduced new bird, a cage that is too small and breeding-season hormones. When it happens, separate cages settle it, so budget for two cages from the start.

If you choose a single bird

No study has put a number of hours on a lone lovebird. What a single bird needs is set in days: interaction and playtime outside the cage, every day, not a long weekend session to make up for a quiet week. The risk is specific to this bird. In a survey of 2,331 pet parrots in Japan, lovebirds had almost seven times the odds of feather-damaging behavior that budgerigars did, and birds showing signs of separation anxiety were more likely to do it. The hours a bird spent with nobody home, and whether it had a cage mate, did not change the odds. A weekday has three pieces: a supervised session out of the cage, short training sessions folded into it, and foraging set up before you leave. A keeper who cannot cover the first on most days is the case for a second lovebird.

If you choose a pair, or add a second bird later

The second bird must be a lovebird of the same species. Size the cage for the pair from the start, and give each bird its own toys and foraging, since a cage mate solves loneliness, not boredom. Even two of the same sex usually bond to each other and become less interested in people.

  • A vet exam for the newcomer, then quarantine in a separate room (page {{P:arrival}}), where the resident can hear the new bird but not see it.
  • Cages in the same room, visible to each other but out of reach, moved closer over days while both stay calm.
  • The first meeting on neutral ground, in a room neither bird owns, both out and supervised. A hen defends her cage hardest.
  • Separate cages again at the first sign of real injury. Squabbling is normal; blood is not.

Wing clipping & flight

A decision nearly every new owner is asked to make in the first week, usually by whoever is selling the trim. A clip is cutting some of the primary flight feathers, along the outer edge of the wing, so the bird cannot gain or hold altitude. It is not an amputation, it touches no tissue, and it grows back when those feathers are next molted out and replaced. Molt follows no fixed calendar, so check the wings, not a date.

The case for clippingThe case against
Reduces the risk of flying into windows, mirrors or ceiling fans, and of escaping through an open doorFlight is the bird's main exercise. A bird that cannot fly loses cardiovascular and muscular conditioning that flapping in place does not replace
Can make early taming and step-up training more manageableA bird that is more accepting of handling because it is less independent has not been trained, it has been made less able to leave
Some clipped birds are less brazen and more reliant on the ownerIt guarantees nothing: a gust, an incomplete trim or regrown feathers can still lift a bird out of a door
It is not a behavior tool Clipping does not treat biting, screaming or hormonal behavior. A bird that bites out of fear before a trim bites out of fear after one, and is merely less able to fly away first. An over-clipped bird drops instead of gliding and can injure its keel or beak on landing.

If it is done, it is done right

Only fully mature feathers are cut. The standard trim takes four to seven of the outermost primaries on both wings, cut below the coverts that overlay them, and leaves the secondaries closer to the body alone, since those are what let a bird slow down and land. A heavier bird needs fewer taken and a lighter, more agile one more. Clipping only one wing is not recommended. A correct trim leaves the bird able to flutter down under control, not drop. Have an avian vet or an experienced groomer do it, and watch the first one.

A broken blood feather is a first-aid situation A blood feather is a growing feather with a live blood supply in the shaft, and cutting or snapping one bleeds heavily in a bird this small. Apply firm, steady pressure with clean gauze, and put styptic powder, cornstarch or flour on the exposed broken tip of the shaft, never down into an open follicle, which can damage it permanently. If it has not stopped within two to three minutes, call an avian vet. Do not pull the feather at home: it leaves the shaft open and can cost more blood than it saves.

Whether to keep the bird flighted comes down to the room rather than the bird: how bird-proof the home really is, and whether you can supervise. Neither answer is wrong, and the decision is revisited at each molt (the room checklist is on page {{P:proofing}}).

Enrichment & common mistakes

A wild lovebird spends much of its day searching for food, and a cage with a single bowl removes nearly all of that. Boredom in parrots is linked to feather plucking, repetitive pacing and screaming that goes beyond normal vocalizing. Enrichment comes after the right cage, diet, sleep and company, never instead of them. In this order:

PriorityWhat to do
1. ForagingHide part of the daily ration instead of serving all of it: pellets wrapped in a paper cup, or a foraging box the bird has to open. Parrots often work for food while a free bowl sits nearby, which tells you the work is the point. Change the method every few days so it stays a puzzle
2. ShreddingUntreated paper, cardboard tubes and bird-safe wood. Balsa and other soft woods disappear fast, so keep a cheap rotating supply rather than one expensive toy
3. Daily time and trainingA supervised session out of the cage every day with interaction and play, plus short positive-reinforcement training sessions: step up, targeting, recall
4. RotationThree to five toys at a time, swapped weekly, mixing something to forage in, to shred and to chew. A toy that has been away a few weeks reads as new again

Reading the warning signs

Plucking, pacing the same stretch of perch, and new or louder screaming are the clearest signs current enrichment is not enough. In lovebirds the damage is often aimed at the thin skin of the wing membranes, so check under the wing, not just the chest. Lovebirds also get polyfolliculosis, several feathers growing from one follicle, which itches and can start feather damage on its own. The same signs can be medical, so a vet check comes first (page {{P:plucking}}).

The mistakes that cost the most

  • A happy hut, nest box or mirror in the cage. The most avoidable trigger for hormonal aggression and chronic egg laying, and it arrives packaged as a kindness.
  • Seed as the base of the diet. The cause behind much of the health section.
  • Two birds, expecting a tame one. A bonded pair bonds to each other. You do not get both.
  • One bird without daily time. A single lovebird that stops getting interaction is a candidate for screaming and plucking.
  • The cage in the kitchen. The most likely place for a sudden death from nonstick fumes.
  • Bar spacing chosen by eye. A wide gap can trap a head.
  • Lights on past bedtime. Too little darkness feeds both plucking and laying.

Enrichment does not stand in for sleep, a correct diet or daily contact with a single bird. A cage full of toys will not stop a bird plucking if any of those is missing.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a lovebird, quarantine & the first vet visit

Choosing the bird

Lifespan is the commitment people underestimate: 10 to 15 years, up to about 20. Hand-raised birds are already socialized and usually tame faster. A reputable breeder lets you see the parent birds, says whether a chick was hand-raised or parent-raised and when it left the nest, and answers questions about diet and any health issues without getting defensive. A shop is the easiest first contact but mixes birds from several sources, so ask how long the bird has been there, whether a vet has seen it, and whether new arrivals are kept apart from the floor birds. A rescue can hold adults with a known temperament, though ask about the bird's history.

A healthy bird is lively, alert and interactive, tracks movement and sound, and has smooth feathers. A bird that stays fluffed and still, sits with its eyes half or fully closed in the daytime, or ignores everything around the cage is not the one to take home, whatever the price or the selection.

Quarantine every new bird

A new bird can look normal for weeks while carrying something that would spread through a household in days. Thirty days is the floor, and the plan is 30 to 45 days in a separate room with its own air, while the bird is screened for chlamydia, salmonella, polyomavirus and psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD). A household or aviary with several birds should lean toward 90 days, because an infected adult can shed polyomavirus intermittently for up to 90 days before clearing it. It applies even if this is your only bird now, since the second bird will need it.

  • Its own dishes, perches, toys and cleaning tools, washed separately from everything else.
  • Established birds fed, cleaned and handled first, the new bird last.
  • Hands washed with soap and water between birds, and a designated shirt for the quarantine room if you can.
  • No preventive antibiotics: doxycycline treats a confirmed case and is not a shortcut around time and testing.

The first vet visit

Book it before the bird arrives, inside quarantine, within one to two weeks of coming home. Expect a weight, a head-to-tail exam, bloodwork, a fecal check for parasites, a Gram stain for abnormal bacteria or yeast, sometimes X-rays, and PCR (polymerase chain reaction, a lab test that finds an infection's genetic material) screening for PBFD, polyomavirus and psittacosis. PCR can find disease in a bird that looks perfectly healthy. The Association of Avian Veterinarians keeps a find-a-vet directory; a vet who is a member in good standing is the minimum to ask for. Checkups after that are once or twice a year, and the first exam gives a healthy baseline to compare against later.

Sexing, hens & chronic egg laying

Lovebirds are a textbook case of birds that look alike: nothing about plumage, size or shape reliably separates a male from a female, and visual sexing tips are anecdote. A DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) test is the actual answer, run on a blood sample or a plucked feather through a lab or an avian vet. Knowing the sex matters because a hen needs the management on this page, and an owner who assumes a bird is a cock will miss a laying cycle starting.

Why a lone hen lays at all

A hen does not need a male, or even another bird, to lay. Lovebirds are among the species that most often become chronic layers. Each clutch drains calcium faster than a hen can replace it, which turns a normal clutch into a soft-shelled egg, a fracture, or a bird that cannot pass the next one.

TriggerWhat to do about it
Long day lengthProtect 10 to 12 hours of dark. For a hen already laying, cut light to 8 hours a day (8 to 10 at most) on a vet's advice, then lengthen it back gradually
A nest siteRemove the happy hut, tent, nest box, and any dark enclosed place she can burrow into or shred paper into
A mate, or something treated as oneRemove mirrors and any toy she has bonded to. Separate a male into another room. Stop stroking her back, wings and vent
Abundant, rich foodMove fully to pellets and cut rich treats and surplus food
A secure territoryMove the cage to a different spot, which can help
Do not take the eggs away as they are laid Pulling eggs one at a time keeps her body reading the nest as incomplete, so she lays more. Let her sit on the full clutch for a complete incubation, which for a lovebird is 18 to 24 days (the 21 to 28 days quoted for parrots in general is longer). Then remove the eggs gradually, one every one to two days, since clearing them all at once can restart the cycle. Hard-boiled eggs, frozen eggs or craft-store fakes of a matching size can stand in so no real egg spoils.

When the environment is not enough

A vet has options for a hen who keeps cycling: leuprolide acetate by injection, which typically halts laying for only 3 to 4 weeks and is repeated on a schedule, or a deslorelin implant, which can hold laying off for 3 to 6 months or longer. Surgery is the last resort. Both drugs buy time while the light, diet and nest changes take hold, and neither replaces them. A hen who is laying heavily, thin, and slow to bounce back between clutches deserves a vet visit before anything gets stuck.

Egg binding

The likeliest emergency this species presents you with, and one where hours decide the outcome. An egg that cannot pass lodges in the oviduct and compresses circulation and the air sacs. Small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg bound. There is no home remedy that safely dislodges the egg, and no version where waiting until tomorrow is right. Lovebirds are named alongside cockatiels and budgerigars as the hens this happens to most.

What you seeWhat it means
No egg passed in 48 hours by a hen you know is layingOn its own it is enough to make the call, before anything else on this list appears
Sitting low or on the cage floor, weak, not perchingA bird that cannot perch is seriously compromised
Straining as if to pass a dropping, a wide-based stance, tail wagging or bobbingShe is trying to pass an egg and cannot. This is already the emergency
A swollen abdomen, over-fluffed feathersThe egg, or the effect of it
Tail bobbing with each breath, labored breathingThe egg is limiting how far the air sacs can expand
An egg bulging at the vent, or tissue prolapsing from itLate, and it does not mean the egg is about to pass. Same-hour vet
Cannot stand or grip the perch, or a leg that dragsPressure on the nerves to the legs. It is compression, not clumsiness

What causes it

Calcium deficiency is the biggest cause, which is why an all-seed diet sits under most cases: a hen short of calcium cannot contract the oviduct hard enough to push the egg out. Obesity, a lack of exercise, vitamin deficiencies, hereditary factors, old age and an improper laying environment all raise the risk (page {{P:supplements}}).

What the vet does

Heat, injected fluids, calcium and vitamin D3, and oxytocin to stimulate contractions. If the egg still does not pass, it can be removed under sedation, drawn out with a needle, or taken out surgically. Most birds treated promptly go on to live normal lives.

Call the vet now, not in the morning Put her in a small carrier on a folded towel, keep her warm, dark and quiet, and go. Around 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C) is the recovery range for a sick bird, and birds recover and eat better at the upper end. Watch for overheating, which shows as panting with an open beak, sleek flat feathers and wings held away from the body. Afterward she needs rest from laying triggers for at least 2 to 4 weeks, and preferably a whole breeding season.

Molt, hormonal seasons & seasonal behavior

Molting

Birds normally lose and replace most of their feathers at least once a year, and many have a smaller partial molt about six months after the main one. A normal molt is slow, made of whole undamaged feathers, and never produces bald skin. New feathers arrive as pin feathers, each in a waxy sheath that dries and flakes away as the feather matures, which a bird works loose by preening. Molting takes a lot of a bird's resources, and the pins can itch while they grow in, so a molting bird is often quieter, touchier and shorter-tempered. Do not read the grumpiness as a training problem.

Bathe or mist a molting bird regularly: the added humidity keeps the skin from drying out, may lessen plucking, and helps the bird work the sheaths off (daily baths are on page {{P:cleaning}}).

Hormonal seasons

Long days, plentiful food and a secure territory push a lovebird toward breeding condition. Birds under reproductive hormones are often less friendly, more vocal and more cage-protective and territorial than the same bird outside that state, and in the extreme, screaming and feather-destructive behavior follow. Regurgitating onto a favorite person or toy is a recognized affection behavior. This is the season to be least ambitious about handling and most disciplined about the triggers on page {{P:hormones}}: protect the dark hours, take out nest-like objects, keep contact to the head and neck, and wait it out.

The two-line summary The first needs nothing, the second needs a vet and an environment audit, and the third needs a PCR test. Any feather loss that is not a slow, whole-feather molt is worth a call.
