Section 03 · Feeding

Diet: pellets, seed & portions

A formulated pellet should be about 75 to 80% of what a lovebird eats, with vegetables and a little fruit filling the remaining 20 to 25%. Seed is the part keepers get backwards. It fails a lovebird for the same reasons it fails any parrot: short on vitamins, minerals and protein, and a bird eats selectively from a mix instead of across it, so the diet it actually eats is worse than the one on the label. It is also short in vitamin A and calcium, the two gaps this species runs into (pages {{P:supplements}} and {{P:nutrition}}).

ComponentShare of the dietHow to offer it
Formulated pellets75 to 80%In the dish all day. Small-parrot pellets
Fresh vegetables and a little fruitThe remaining 20 to 25%Washed and cut small. Weaving it through the bars or hiding it in a toy turns it into a foraging job (page {{P:veg}})
SeedMeasured, never free-fedNo more than 1 to 2 teaspoons a day. It goes further as a foraging-toy reward than as a bowl
TreatsA thumbnail-sized pieceA thumbnail-sized piece of any fresh food counts as a dinner-plate serving for a lovebird
GritNoneLovebirds shell their own seed. Grit is unnecessary and can cause a blockage
WaterAlways availableFresh and clean, with the dish washed daily. Changed more than once a day if the bird bathes in it

How much is a day's food

Pellets can stay in the dish all day, so the measured foods are seed and treats. To find what one bird really eats, weigh its food on a gram scale when it goes in, weigh what is left 24 hours later, and average the difference over seven days. That is its daily intake, and the amount to keep offering throughout a diet change. Weigh the bird too: losing more than 1 to 2% of its body weight in a week means a change is running too fast.

Why the proportions matter for a hen A single hen lays clutch after clutch with no mate, and each egg costs her calcium twice: once for the shell and again for the muscle work of passing it. Where that calcium comes from is decided months earlier at the food dish. Seed is poor in calcium and heavy in phosphorus, the worst combination for the job, so the deficit builds instead of resetting (page {{P:hormones}}).

Converting a seed eater to pellets

A lovebird that has only ever eaten seed may not recognize a pellet as food, and some will go without rather than experiment. That is the whole risk of a conversion: a small bird has almost no fat reserve and can go downhill in a day, so never starve a bird onto pellets, and never remove the seed dish outright and expect hunger to do the work.

ScheduleHow it runs
Gradual, 3-day steps75% seed and 25% pellets for 3 days, then 50/50 for 3 days, then 25% seed and 75% pellets until the bird is converted
Faster, 10-day taperStart at 90% seed and 10% pellets on day one, 80/20 on day two, and so on until the bird is on pellets alone on day 10
If the bird stallsHold at 20% seed and 80% pellets until it converts rather than pushing the last step. Changing a bird's diet can take days, weeks or months

The limits that keep it safe

  • A small bird should never go more than 12 hours without eating.
  • Weigh the bird every day through the change, before the first feed, and write it down. A drop of more than 10% from the starting weight means stop and call an avian vet.
  • Check the droppings daily. Minimal or black droppings mean stop and call a vet too.
  • Check the tray to confirm the bird is actually eating the pellets and not just cracking and dropping them.
  • Take the seed out completely only once the bird is reliably eating pellets and fresh vegetables.

Tricks that help

  • Offer pellets first thing in the morning, before anything else, when the bird is hungriest.
  • Grind pellets to a powder and mix it into a little moist food, then coarsen the texture and cut the moist food back over time.
  • Scatter pellets on a flat surface for the bird to walk through and peck at, which can work where a new bowl does nothing.
  • Keep a second pellet brand in the cupboard. A bird that refuses one shape or flavor for weeks sometimes takes another within days.
If it stalls, or the breathing changes Weight dropping, a conversion that will not move, or a wheeze or clicking during the change is not something to push through on your own. A wheeze can be simple stress or a nutritional problem already at work, and only a vet exam tells which.

Safe vegetables, greens & herbs

Bright color is the shortcut: the orange, red and dark green vegetables are the ones carrying the vitamin A that a seed diet leaves out. Vegetables and a little fruit fill the 20 to 25% of the diet that is not pellets.

Iceberg lettuce, celerySparinglyNot harmful, but nearly all water, so they fill a small bird up without feeding it
Onion and garlicNeverHarmful to the blood. Never offer them, in any form. Concentrated forms like garlic powder or dry soup mix are the worst

Presentation is half the battle Wash everything well, because pesticide residue is a particular risk for birds. Cut it into pieces a small beak can pick up. Fresh or frozen is better than canned, which carries salt and sugar, or freeze-dried, which carries less nutrition. A food the bird refuses gets offered again over 3 to 5 days before it is written off. Weaving greens through the bars, clipping vegetables to a skewer and hiding pieces in a foraging toy all get eaten more reliably than the same food in a bowl.

Fruit, treats & the never-feed list

Fruit is the smaller part of the fresh share: sweet, watery, and easy to overfeed. A thumbnail-sized piece of any fresh food counts as a full serving for a lovebird.

FoodTierNotes
Mango, papaya, cantaloupeBestThe most useful of the fruits
Berries: blueberry, raspberry, strawberryRegularWell accepted
Apple, pear, grapes, kiwi, melon, peachRegularApple and pear with the core and seeds removed. Peaches and nectarines are stone fruit, so no pits
BananaSometimesSweet, so a small piece, and best used as a reward
Apple seeds and fruit pitsNeverApple seeds contain cyanide, and so do the pits of cherries, plums, apricots and peaches. Remove them before offering the flesh

Seed and other extras

  • Seed: a measured treat of no more than 1 to 2 teaspoons a day, best hidden in a foraging toy so the bird works for it (page {{P:diet}}).
  • Dairy: birds cannot digest lactose, so skip it.
  • Salty, fatty or sugary human food: chips, crackers and processed food are out. They are not all strictly toxic, but they cause serious health problems.
The never-feed list, in one place Avocado: every part of the plant holds persin, a toxin that harms the heart. Respiratory distress usually shows about 12 hours after it is eaten, and death can follow within one to two days. Onion and garlic. Chocolate, which carries theobromine and caffeine, and caffeine in coffee, tea and soda. Alcohol. Sugar-free candy, which can contain xylitol. Fruit pits and apple seeds. Peanuts, which can carry mold toxins. Mushrooms, since some are toxic. Rhubarb and nightshade leaves. Salty, fatty or sugary human food. Grit is not toxic but is unnecessary and can cause a blockage, so it belongs here too.

Darker, more bitter chocolate carries more risk than milk chocolate, and none of these needs to be a dramatic exposure: a countertop within flight range of an out-of-cage bird is enough.

Section 03 · Feeding

Supplements, calcium & the grit myth

A bird eating 75 to 80% pellets does not need a general vitamin supplement stacked on top. The pellets are the supplement, adding more is not extra insurance, and some nutrients cause problems in excess. Supplements are for seed-fed birds and for one particular case, the laying hen.

Calcium and the laying hen

Every egg draws down calcium: some goes into the shell, and some goes to the muscle contractions and nerve signaling that move the egg out. A hen laying repeatedly spends a reserve she has to rebuild between clutches, and on a seed diet she cannot rebuild it fast enough. Taken far enough, the result is a weak, lethargic bird with bones brittle enough to fracture in ordinary handling, prone to egg binding, and in severe cases at risk of seizures and death.

  • Dark leafy greens are a real dietary calcium source and belong in the daily vegetables.
  • A hen who is actively laying is one of the few clear cases for supplementing calcium directly, and that is worth arranging with a vet rather than guessing at.
  • Vitamin D3 is what lets a bird absorb the calcium, and it comes from UVB light (page {{P:climate}}).
Dusting seed with vitamin powder does nothing A lovebird hulls a seed and eats the kernel inside, so powder sprinkled over a seed mix rides out with the husks. The bird eats around it entirely, and the keeper reasonably believes the supplement is being taken. If a powdered supplement is in use, during a pellet conversion for instance, it goes on moist food, where it stays put and is eaten.

Vitamin A and calcium shortfalls

ShortfallWhat it looks likeThe fix
Vitamin ANasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eyes, conjunctivitis, labored breathing, increased thirst and urination, poor feather quality, feather picking and appetite lossPellets, and orange and dark green vegetables. Injected vitamin A is a vet treatment, not a home one
CalciumWeakness, tremors, seizures, and fractures with no obvious injury. In a laying hen, thin-shelled or soft eggsPellets, dark greens, UVB light, and a vet's help for a laying hen

Grit

Grit is not part of how this species processes food. A bird that shells its own seed has no grinding job for grit to do, and eaten in quantity it can cause a serious gastrointestinal obstruction.

Weight, body condition & the daily scale

A lovebird hides illness until it is genuinely sick, and weight is the measurement that moves first. A bird's feathers hide body condition extremely well, so weight loss shows up on a scale days to weeks before the bird looks thin.

The scale

Buy a kitchen scale that reads in grams, not ounces: the swings worth catching in a small bird are often a handful of grams. A scale with a removable perch lets the bird stand on something it would land on anyway. Weigh every day before breakfast, at the same time, and write the number down. One reading says almost nothing; the logged trend says everything. Weigh daily from the first week to set the baseline.

MeasureWhat to know
Healthy adult weightAbout 40 to 60 g (1.5 to 2 oz), and healthy birds are reported up to 70 g. What matters is your own bird's steady number, not where it sits in the range
Length, beak to tail tip5 to 6.5 in (13 to 16.5 cm)
During a diet changeLosing more than 1 to 2% in a week means the change is too fast. A drop of 10% from the starting weight means stop and call a vet (page {{P:convert}})
A downward trend at any other timeWeight is the earliest signal of illness. A steady fall across several weigh-ins is a vet call even when the bird looks well

Body condition: feel the keel

The keel is the blade of breastbone running down the center of the chest, where the flight muscles attach on either side. Feel it with a fingertip; scoring is by feel, not by eye. Birds are scored from 1 (emaciated) to 5 (obese), and obesity generally means a bird about 20% over its ideal weight, or a score of 4 out of 5. This is not the 9-point dog and cat scale, which does not transfer to birds.

Underweight

The keel is a sharp, prominent ridge with little muscle either side. With a falling weight, this is a vet visit, not a feeding adjustment.

Ideal

A subtle ridge, with muscle rounded out on either side of it. Not sharp, not buried.

Overweight

The keel is hard to find because it is cushioned in fat. Usually a seed-heavy diet plus too little flying.

Track the trendA keel that has gotten sharper since last month is the signal, more than any single score.

A rounded chest and a keel you cannot find is where fatty liver disease starts (page {{P:nutrition}}). It is corrected with a pelleted diet, portion control, and exercise through a larger cage and room to fly.
