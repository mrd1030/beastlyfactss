Feed what the bird was already eating. Diet changes wait until it has settled
Book the first avian vet exam for within one to two weeks, and ask about PCR screening for PBFD, polyomavirus and psittacosis
Audit the cage against the checklist (page {{P:checklist}}): bar spacing measured, no hut, tent, nest box or mirror
Bird-proof the room for out-of-cage time
Set a fixed bedtime: 10 to 12 hours of darkness

Weeks 2 and 3

Start the pellet conversion if the bird is on seed, weighing every day (page {{P:convert}})
Begin taming: talking softly, then the stick in a neutral room (page {{P:handling}})
Start the owner log with a daily or weekly weigh-in (page {{P:ownerlog}})

Week 4

Review the weight trend. A steady adult weight is the goal, and a downward trend is a vet call
Check the fresh food is being eaten, not just shredded. Offer rejected foods again, cut differently
Introduce the first foraging toy and start the toy rotation (page {{P:enrichlog}})

Days 31 to 90

Finish quarantine at 30 days at the least, 30 to 45 planned, up to 90 for a household with several birds, and only then share air and space
Book any follow-up the vet recommended, and record test results (page {{P:equiplog}})
Watch for hormonal signs, especially in a hen: shredding paper, sitting low, nesting (page {{P:hormones}})
Look back at the log for slow drift in weight, appetite or droppings
Fill in the pet-sitter sheet before you need it (page {{P:sitter}})

Power outages, heat, travel & transport

A lovebird needs no heat lamp, so an outage is mostly a question of room temperature and of what people start burning for light and warmth. A reptile keeper stops feeding when heat fails. For a bird the rule runs backward: a small parrot has almost no energy reserve, so food and water stay in front of it the whole time.

Do

  • Keep feeding on the normal schedule, refilling by hand if anything depends on power.
  • Move the cage to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Cover most of the cage with a thick towel or blanket, leaving a gap for airflow.
  • Use a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel near the cage, never against it, in a genuinely cold house.
  • For a visibly cold bird, hold it against your body under a loose shirt for short stretches.

Don't

  • No candles, kerosene or propane heaters, gas stove or oven for heat in any room the bird is in. Use flashlights or battery candles.
  • Run a generator outside, at least 20 ft (6 m) from doors, windows and vents, never in or near a garage, house or shed.
  • Do not assume an open window or a cracked garage door is enough ventilation.
  • Do not stop feeding.

The target for a sick or recovering bird is 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C), with a working CO detector in any room with gas heat, a gas range or a generator nearby. Carbon monoxide is odorless to everyone, and a bird may be in trouble before a person feels anything.

Transport and vet trips

  • Use a travel carrier, not the main cage: compact, a solid or padded floor, and a low perch or a folded towel. Take loose toys and swings out first.
  • Secure the carrier with a seat belt or strap in the back seat, away from an active airbag, and cover part of it with a light breathable cloth.
  • Skip a water dish for the drive, since it spills under braking. A slice of fruit or a leafy green covers hydration on short trips.
  • A bird is never left in a parked car, not for a minute. A closed car can climb 20°F in 10 minutes and 50°F in an hour, and a bird that lives indoors should spend under 20 minutes in direct sun, with shade it can reach.
  • Overheating shows as panting with the beak open, sleek flat feathers and wings held away from the body. Move it to shade or air conditioning, mist it with room-temperature water, never cold, and call a vet on the way.

Why bother logging Weight is the one number that moves before a lovebird looks unwell, and a bird that hides illness gives you nothing else to work with. Weigh at the same time of day, before the first feed, and write it down even when nothing has changed. Sixteen rows is four months of weekly entries, long enough to see a real trend, and it is the most useful thing you can hand a vet.

Enrichment safety Check every toy weekly for loose fibers a toe can wrap in, small detachable parts, open metal clips and any galvanized metal. Replace shredded items before the bird works its way down to the hardware. And remember what enrichment cannot do: it does not substitute for sleep, for a correct diet, or for daily contact with a single bird.
