Cage size, bar spacing & where it goes

18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) is the least that is adequate for a single lovebird, and 24×18×24 in (61×46×61 cm) for a bonded pair. Treat those numbers as the least you can responsibly buy, and a larger floor of 3×2×2 ft (90×60×60 cm) as the target. Length matters more than height: lovebirds fly side to side rather than climbing, so a wide flight-style cage beats a tall, narrow one of the same volume.

Bar spacing is a safety limit 3/8 to 5/8 in (10 to 16 mm), with half an inch (13 mm) the ideal middle. The narrow end is the safer end, down to about 3/8 to 7/16 in (0.95 to 1.1 cm). Wider gaps let a lovebird trap its head between the bars, which can be fatal. Measure the cage yourself rather than trusting the box. Skip round cages, which give a bird no corner to retreat into.

Material

Powder-coated or stainless steel only. Homemade cages, cages with wooden components and galvanized wire all carry a real risk of zinc or other toxic exposure, and lovebirds chew constantly. The metals themselves are on page {{P:proofing}}.

Never Buy a cage in the $30 to $50 bracket. Cheap cages rust, and a rusting cage introduces a genuine zinc poisoning risk, which is a real veterinary emergency and not a minor concern.

Where the cage goes

  • A room the household actually uses, so the bird is not alone for most of the day.
  • Off the floor, at about eye level, in a well-lit spot out of drafts and away from doors and windows.
  • Never in the kitchen. An overheated nonstick pan can kill a bird with no warning (page {{P:hazards}}).
Buy the cage before the bird Set it up before the bird comes home. A cage that is too small or poorly laid out on day one gets used as it is far more often than it gets fixed later.

Perches, dishes & what to leave out

Perches

Natural wood branches work better than uniform dowels for foot health, because a bird gripping the same diameter all day loads the same points of the foot. Perches run about 3/8 to 1/2 in (10 to 13 mm) across, in wood, natural branch, rope or concrete, with at least two in the cage and many keepers fitting four. Sandpaper perch covers stay out of the cage. Place perches so that nothing sits directly above a food or water dish.

Dishes and liner

Exterior-mounted food dishes are worth the extra few dollars: you can feed without putting a hand inside the cage of a territorial bird, which is the commonest way owners get bitten (page {{P:handling}}). A plain paper liner under a removable metal grate is the simplest hygienic floor, because droppings fall through, the bird never stands in them, and you can read them every day. Avoid anything that holds moisture and grows mold. Lovebirds need no grit.

Furnishings checklist

Perches of varied diameter and material
Exterior-mount food and water dishes
Shreddable items: untreated paper, cardboard tubes
A foraging toy that hides part of the daily ration
Soft wood chew items, replaced as they disappear
A plain, breathable cage cover for night
Leave these out, deliberately A nest box, a happy hut or any enclosed, tunnel-shaped tent, and a mirror. Nest-like furnishings are the most common avoidable trigger for hormonal aggression and chronic egg laying in this species, and a mirror can work as a mate. This is not a style preference. Unless you are deliberately breeding, they stay out (page {{P:hormones}}). If you want the bird covered at night, a plain breathable cover over the outside of the cage does the job without the hormonal downside.

Toy safety

Lovebirds are aggressive chewers, so the toy list is stricter than for a calmer bird. Avoid small detachable parts, loose strings, open metal clips a beak could pry apart, and anything galvanized. Check the chew toys at every weekly clean: a toy worn down to a loose string or an exposed clip comes out.

Temperature, light & sleep

Target°F°C
Room temperature65 to 8018 to 27

A lovebird is comfortable at normal household temperatures, with no basking lamp, heat mat or thermostat. What it cannot take is a draft or a sudden swing, so keep the cage out of drafts and out of direct sun, where it cannot move away from the heat.

Sleep is a husbandry requirement

A lovebird needs 10 to 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness every night, at about the same clock time each night. Sleep deprivation is one of the recognized behavioral contributors to feather picking, and a lit evening reads to a bird as an endless summer. The cage in the living room is often the wrong sleep location: a cover blocks the light but not a nearby television or people walking through, so a covered cage may not give optimal rest. A smaller, plain sleeping cage in a quiet, dark room, moved to at the same time every night, solves both problems.

The photoperiod lever A day longer than 12 hours of light is a documented risk factor for excessive egg laying, so protecting the dark hours is the first lever against it. For a hen already laying repeatedly, cutting daylight or room light to 8 hours a day, or 8 to 10 at the outside, can reduce the stimulus. That is a managed intervention to discuss with an avian vet, not a permanent setting, and it can take weeks to work (page {{P:hormones}}).

Light quality and vitamin D3

Window glass filters out the UVB (ultraviolet B) wavelengths a bird uses to make vitamin D3, which lets it absorb calcium, so a sunny window gives warmth and daylight but not that. An avian-specific UVB lamp is worth adding if the bird gets no regular safe outdoor time. It runs 10 to 12 hours a day, mounted about 12 to 18 in (30 to 46 cm) from where the bird perches, and is replaced about every six months.

Household hazards: fumes & air quality

A bird's lungs move air in one direction through a system of air sacs, which is why a bird pulls far more air, and far more of whatever is in it, through its body than a person in the same room does. The practical rule: if you can smell it, it may harm a bird's airways. Smaller birds go down faster on the same exposure because they have less body mass to buffer it.

Nonstick coating fumes kill birds with no warning PTFE, short for polytetrafluoroethylene, is the nonstick coating on many pans. Stable at normal cooking temperatures, it breaks down past 536°F (280°C) into gases that cause direct, caustic damage to a bird's lungs. An empty pan on a hot burner, or one preheated too long, can cross that line in minutes without looking scorched or smelling wrong to you. Sudden death is often the only sign. Irons, ironing board covers, some self-cleaning ovens and certain heat lamps can carry it too.

The fix is ordinary: cook with stainless steel or cast iron, keep the cage out of the kitchen, run the exhaust fan during high-heat cooking, and never run a self-cleaning oven cycle with a bird anywhere in the house.

Everything else you can smell

Aerosol sprays, hairspray, spray cleaners, spray paint, carpet fresheners, perfumes, deodorizers, votive candles, wax melts, essential oil diffusers and vaporizers all irritate a bird. So do plastics melted or burned in a microwave, and cigarette, cigar and pipe smoke, from the air and from residue that settles on feathers and gets preened off. Move the bird to a well-ventilated room before using any of them.

Smoke and carbon monoxide

Cooking smoke, a fireplace and carbon monoxide (CO) from any gas appliance are airborne risks too. CO is odorless and colorless to everyone in the room, and a bird sharing the air has no buffer, so it may be in trouble before a person notices anything. A working CO detector in any room with gas heat or a gas range is worth having in any home, and matters more with a bird in it.

Feather dust

Lovebirds do not produce anywhere near the dust that cockatoos, African greys and cockatiels shed, so the dust and lung concerns that come with those species are not a lovebird issue.

If a pan scorches Get the bird out of the house, open every window, and call an avian vet before anything else. Do not wait for signs.

Countertops cleared of avocado, chocolate, onion and anything else on page {{P:fruit}}
Any mouth contact from a cat or dog is a same-day vet visit Cat saliva carries Pasteurella bacteria, and at a bird's body temperature it can multiply fast enough to cause fatal sepsis within hours, from a bite that may not even break the skin visibly through the feathers. Do not wait to see how the bird is tomorrow. A vet will usually flush the wound and start antibiotics straight away. Dog bites add crushing injury to the same bacterial risk. Supervised time is the real rule in a mixed-pet household, since a cat that has never shown interest can still react to one startled, fast movement.

Zinc and lead: the two metals that poison slowly

Zinc, sometimes called new wire disease, comes mainly from galvanized cage hardware, toys, chains and mesh. Chewing galvanized wire has caused zinc poisoning, because zinc can leach from hot-dipped galvanizing without the bird swallowing a flake. Electroplated galvanizing is considered safe, but hot-dipped, the more common and cheaper kind, is not. Early signs are stomach upset and appetite loss, then weakness, pale gums and discolored droppings. Powder-coated or stainless steel hardware avoids it.

Lead tastes sweet, which is part of why a bird that finds it keeps chewing. Signs range from weakness and stomach upset to circling, tremors and leg paralysis, and by then the bird is often seriously ill. Both metals respond to chelation therapy, a vet treatment that binds the metal so the body can pass it, if caught early, so walk a room for galvanized hardware, old paint and vintage glass before the bird has access.

Plants

A bird chewing leaves out of curiosity cannot tell a safe plant from a toxic one. Check any plant against a current toxic-plant list before it comes into a room the bird can reach.

Cleaning, bathing & hygiene

Daily

Take out soiled paper and discarded food, and wash the food and water bowls. A lovebird bathes in any water it can reach, so the drinking dish needs changing whenever it has been used as a bath, which is often more than once a day. Every bowl is emptied, scrubbed with soap and water, rinsed, soaked in disinfectant and rinsed again before it goes back in. Changing the paper each day also gives a daily look at the droppings (page {{P:stool}}).

Weekly: the full clean

  • Move the bird to a travel cage in another room, so it is in a separate air space while anything is sprayed or scrubbed.
  • Strip out the paper, perches and toys. Take every dropping and scrap of food off with detergent first, because disinfectant does little against organic debris.
  • Disinfect with a bird habitat cleaner, or freshly mixed household bleach at 1:32, about half a cup per gallon. Most disinfectants need 5 to 10 minutes of wet contact. Never mix one product with another.
  • Rinse in clean running water until no trace of the cleaner, or its smell, is left, and let everything dry completely before the bird goes back in.
  • Spray the floor under the cage with water before sweeping, so dried droppings and feather dust stay down instead of lifting into the air.

A pair soils the cage faster, so the liner is changed more often than weekly when two birds share it. Perches and toys are washed and completely dried whenever they are soiled. Wooden perches, rope toys and nest material cannot be disinfected reliably, so after a sick bird they are thrown out. Only products formulated for use around pets belong near the cage.

Bathing

Offer a bath every day, in the morning so the feathers are dry before night. A dish of lukewarm water large enough to bathe in works, and so does a gentle mist from a plant mister for a bird that dislikes dishes. Plain water only, with no commercial bathing products, and bottled water if the tap water is chlorinated. Bathing keeps feathers and skin in condition and encourages preening, which matters most in dry indoor air, and it helps a molting bird work the sheaths off its new feathers (page {{P:molt}}).

Wash hands around the cage Wash your hands with soap and water after handling the bird or cleaning its cage, and between any two birds. Psittacosis, which people can catch, is spread by dust from dried droppings, so dampen droppings instead of sweeping them dry (page {{P:respiratory}}).
