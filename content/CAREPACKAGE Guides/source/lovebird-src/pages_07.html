Glossary, A to L

AgapornisThe genus of the nine lovebird species. Page {{P:species}}.
Avian gastric yeastA yeast, Macrorhabdus ornithogaster, that damages a bird's true stomach so it can keep eating and still lose weight. Whole seed in the droppings is one sign. Page {{P:nutrition}}.
Blood featherA growing feather with a live blood supply in the shaft. Cutting, breaking or pulling one causes real bleeding. Page {{P:flight}}.
Bonded pairTwo lovebirds paired to each other. A bonded pair bonds to each other rather than to a keeper. Page {{P:pair}}.
CereThe fleshy patch above the beak where the nostrils sit. Staining or crusting there can be a respiratory sign. Page {{P:respiratory}}.
ChelationA vet treatment that uses drugs to bind a metal such as zinc or lead so the body can pass it. Page {{P:proofing}}.
Chronic egg layingClutch after clutch of eggs beyond an occasional egg, driven by light, food, nest sites and a perceived mate, and hard on a hen's calcium. Page {{P:hormones}}.
CO (carbon monoxide)An odorless, colorless gas from burning fuel. A bird in the same air may be in trouble before a person feels anything. A detector belongs in any room with gas heat. Page {{P:hazards}}.
Contra-freeloadingThe documented tendency of parrots to work for food even when identical food sits free in a bowl, which is why foraging toys matter. Page {{P:enrichment}}.
Deslorelin implantA hormone implant placed under the skin by a vet that can hold off laying for 3 to 6 months or longer. Page {{P:hormones}}.
DNA testA lab test of deoxyribonucleic acid, a bird's genetic material, from a blood sample or a plucked feather. The only reliable way to sex a lovebird. Page {{P:hormones}}.
DoxycyclineAn antibiotic given by a vet for a confirmed case of psittacosis, typically for 45 days. Not a preventive. Page {{P:respiratory}}.
Egg bindingAn egg that cannot pass. A life-threatening emergency that needs a vet the same day. Page {{P:binding}}.
Fatty liver diseaseFat building up in the liver from a high-fat diet and too little exercise. Page {{P:nutrition}}.
Fecal testA vet's check of a dropping sample under the microscope for parasites. Page {{P:arrival}}.
ForagingSearching for and working at food. Hiding part of the daily ration in a toy replaces what a food bowl removes. Page {{P:enrichment}}.
GiardiasisA gut parasite infection, on the list of conditions lovebirds get. A vet finds it with a fecal test. Page {{P:nutrition}}.
GritGravel some cage birds are given to grind food. Lovebirds shell their own seed and do not need it, and it can cause a blockage. Page {{P:supplements}}.
Happy hutAn enclosed, tent or tunnel-shaped hide sold for cage birds. A nest-like trigger for hormonal behavior and laying, and not for a pet lovebird. Page {{P:perches}}.
HypocalcemiaLow blood calcium. In a hen it follows repeated laying on a calcium-poor diet and causes weakness, tremors and fractures. Page {{P:supplements}}.
IUCNInternational Union for Conservation of Nature, which keeps the global list of threatened species. Page {{P:profile}}.
KeelThe ridge of breastbone down the center of the chest. Feeling it either side is how body condition is judged. Page {{P:growth}}.
Leuprolide acetateA hormone injection a vet gives to pause laying, usually for 3 to 4 weeks at a time. Page {{P:hormones}}.
Reference

Glossary, M to Z

MoltThe normal replacement of feathers, slow, with whole feathers shed and new ones already coming in. Page {{P:molt}}.
PBFDPsittacine beak and feather disease: an incurable circovirus that damages feathers, the beak and the immune system. Psittacine means parrot. Page {{P:viruses}}.
PCRPolymerase chain reaction: a lab test that finds an infection's genetic material, so it can detect disease in a bird that looks healthy. Page {{P:arrival}}.
PhotoperiodThe daily light and dark cycle. 10 to 12 hours of darkness is routine, and 8 hours of light is the level used to break a laying cycle. Page {{P:climate}}.
Pin featherA new feather still in its waxy sheath, which the bird flakes off by preening. Page {{P:molt}}.
PolyfolliculosisSeveral feathers growing from one follicle. It itches and can start feather damage. Page {{P:plucking}}.
PolyomavirusA virus most dangerous to young birds, with a vaccine available. Adults can carry and shed it. Page {{P:viruses}}.
PolyuriaExcess urine: a watery dropping whose solid part is still formed. Different from diarrhea. Page {{P:stool}}.
PsittacosisInfection with Chlamydia psittaci, treatable with doxycycline, and transmissible to people. Page {{P:respiratory}}.
PTFEPolytetrafluoroethylene: the nonstick coating on many pans, which breaks down into lung-damaging gases above 536°F (280°C). Page {{P:hazards}}.
QuarantineKeeping a new bird apart on separate air, with its own dishes and tools, for at least 30 days before it meets resident birds. Page {{P:arrival}}.
QuickThe blood vessel and nerve inside a nail. Cutting into it bleeds. Page {{P:nutrition}}.
Styptic powderA clotting powder that stops bleeding from a nail or a broken blood feather shaft. Never packed into an open follicle. Page {{P:flight}}.
Unitsg grams, oz ounces, in inches, ft feet, cm centimeters, mm millimeters, °F degrees Fahrenheit, °C degrees Celsius, % percent. Explained at the start of the book.
UratesThe chalky white to cream part of a dropping, the bird's solid urine. Bright yellow or lime-green urates point to liver disease. Page {{P:stool}}.
UVBUltraviolet B: the light a bird uses to make vitamin D3. Window glass filters it out. Page {{P:climate}}.
Vitamin A deficiencyA shortfall from seed-heavy diets that shows in the airways, eyes, skin and feathers. Page {{P:supplements}}.
Vitamin D3The vitamin a bird makes under UVB light and needs to absorb calcium. Page {{P:supplements}}.
Zinc toxicosisPoisoning from zinc leaching off galvanized wire, toys or hardware, also called new wire disease. Page {{P:proofing}}.
ZoonoticPassed from animals to people. Psittacosis is the one that matters in this species. Page {{P:respiratory}}.
