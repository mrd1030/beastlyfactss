First 30 days checklist

Day 1

Weigh the bird in grams and photograph it and the setup for a baseline
Leave it alone. Food, water, quiet, and no handling. Talk to it from across the room
Fill in the numbers on page {{P:emergency}} and the equipment dates on page {{P:equiplog}}
If you already keep birds, the new one is in a separate room on separate air (page {{P:pbfd}})

Week 1

Feed exactly what the bird was already eating. Diet changes wait until it has settled
Book a wellness visit with an avian vet, and ask about PCR screening for PBFD, polyomavirus and psittacosis
Audit the cage against page {{P:checklist}}: bar spacing measured, no hut, tent, nest box or mirror
Bird-proof the room you will use for out-of-cage time (page {{P:hazards}})

Week 2 to 3

Start the pellet conversion on one of the two schedules on page {{P:diet}}, weighing every few days
Begin taming: sitting beside the cage, then millet through the bars, then stick training in a neutral room (page {{P:handling}})
Set the bedtime and stick to it, 10 to 12 hours of darkness every night
Start the Owner Log on page {{P:ownerlog}} with a weekly weigh-in

Week 4

Review the weight trend. A stable adult weight is the goal, and a downward trend is a vet call
Check the fresh food is being eaten, not just shredded. Try rejected items again a different way
Introduce the first foraging toy and start the enrichment rotation on page {{P:enrichlog}}

Days 31 to 90

Finish quarantine at 30 days minimum, 45 to 60 in a multi-bird house, and only then share air and space
Book any follow-up the vet recommended, and record the test results on page {{P:equiplog}}
Watch for the first hormonal signs, especially in a hen: paper shredding, floor sitting, tail lifting (page {{P:eggs}})
Look back at the Owner Log for slow drift in weight, appetite, or droppings
Fill in the pet-sitter sheet on page {{P:sitter}} before you need it

Daily, weekly & seasonal routine

Daily

Fresh pellets and clean water, dish washed rather than topped up
Chopped fresh vegetables in the morning, removed within about three hours
Change the paper liner and read the droppings before you bin it (page {{P:droppings}})
1 to 2 hours out of the cage, and about 2 hours of interaction for a single bird
Cover or lights out at a fixed time, for 10 to 12 hours of darkness
Quick visual check: posture, breathing, eyes, nostrils, feet, feather condition

Weekly

Weigh in grams and log it on page {{P:ownerlog}}
Rotate the toys, keeping 3 to 5 in the cage
Offer a bath two or three times, or mist the bird
Scrub the dishes and wipe the perches and bars

Monthly

Full cage deep clean, including the tray and grate
Check perches for wear and replace any that have been chewed thin
Review the Owner Log for slow trends, not just single readings

Every 3 months

Check the nails and beak, and book a vet trim if the nails catch on fabric
Re-audit the room for new hazards: a new candle, a new pan, a new houseplant

Seasonal

As daylight lengthens in spring, hold the bird's night at 10 to 12 hours with a cover or a timer
Expect a hormonal season and a molt. Ease off handling, keep the diet up, offer more baths (page {{P:molt}})
Check heating and cooling changes have not put the cage in a draft or in direct sun

Yearly

Wellness exam, with a blood panel for any bird that has been on a seed-heavy diet
Replace supplements and the cuttlebone, and refresh the emergency kit on page {{P:outage}}
The three-hour rule Fresh food spoils fast at room temperature, faster under a warm cage light. Put it in each morning and take it out within about three hours, before it can grow anything. Moldy food is a real cause of avian illness, and the tray under the cage is where it hides.

Power outages, travel & transport

A lovebird needs no heat lamp, so an outage is mostly a question of room temperature and of what people start burning for light and warmth.

When the power goes out

Do

  • Move the cage to the warmest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Cover three sides and the top with blankets, leaving one side open for air. Trapped body heat does most of the work at this size.
  • Keep feeding normally. A 50 g bird has almost no reserve and skipping meals is a bigger risk than being cool.
  • Keep the day and night cycle roughly normal with a battery lantern rather than leaving the bird in the dark all day.

Don't

  • Don't use candles, kerosene, gas stoves, generators indoors, or any open flame near the bird. Smoke and combustion fumes are the real danger of an outage for a bird, not the temperature.
  • Don't use a camping heater, a self-cleaning oven cycle, or a hair dryer to warm the room. Several carry PTFE-coated elements.
Outage kit Battery lantern, blankets, a hand warmer wrapped in a towel and taped outside the carrier, a week of pellets in a sealed tub, bottled water, and the carrier itself kept where you can reach it in the dark.

Leaving town

Trip lengthSingle birdBonded pair
1 nightPossible with full dishes, but a visit is better. The risk is a spilled water dish, not hungerSame, and they at least have each other for company
2 to 3 nightsA daily sitter. Fresh food and water every day, and eyes on the birdA daily sitter
Over 3 nightsA daily sitter who has done a practice visit with you, or boarding with an avian vet or a bird-specific shop. Leave the sheet on page {{P:sitter}} filled in.

A single hand-raised lovebird is bonded to you, so a long absence is a social problem as much as a husbandry one. Expect a period of loud contact calling when you get back, and some birds pluck after a long separation.

Transport and vet trips

  • A small hard-sided carrier with a low perch, or a folded towel on the floor for a sick bird, is safer than the cage. Cover it: a covered bird travels calmly.
  • Belt the carrier in, keep the car quiet, and never run the air conditioning or heat straight onto it.
  • Take the morning's cage liner to the appointment. It is the sample the vet actually wants.
  • Never leave the bird in a parked car, hot or cold, even briefly. A car interior kills a small bird faster than it would a dog.
  • Clip a spare wing check and a photograph of the bird into the carrier lid, in case it gets out at the clinic door.

Enrichment checklist & log

Setup

3 to 5 toys in the cage, mixing forage, shred and chew rather than duplicates
At least part of the daily ration served inside a foraging toy, not in the dish
Perches at varied heights and diameters, with room to fly between them
A dedicated bird-proofed room for daily out-of-cage time

Rotate the toys weekly, and put retired ones away rather than binning them
Change the foraging method every few days so it stays a puzzle
Two or three short training sessions a week: step up, target, recall

Enrichment safety Check every toy weekly for loose fibers a toe can wrap in, small detachable parts, open metal clips, and any galvanized metal. Replace shredded items before the bird works its way down to the hardware. And remember what enrichment cannot do: it does not substitute for sleep, for a correct diet, or for daily contact with a single bird, and a cage full of toys will not stop a bird plucking if any of those three is missing.

Glossary

AgapornisThe genus of the nine lovebird species, from the Greek for "love bird," after the pair bond.
Blood featherA growing feather with a live blood supply in the shaft. Cutting, breaking or pulling one causes real bleeding.
Bonded pairTwo lovebirds paired to each other, often for life. A bonded pair bonds to each other rather than to a keeper.
CereThe fleshy area above the beak containing the nostrils. Staining or crusting there is a respiratory sign.
Chronic egg layingRepeated clutches beyond an occasional egg, driven by hormonal triggers and hard on a hen's calcium reserves.
Contra-freeloadingThe documented tendency of parrots to work for food even when identical food is freely available, which is why foraging toys matter.
Egg bindingAn egg that cannot pass. A life-threatening emergency requiring same-day veterinary care.
Happy hutAn enclosed, tent or tunnel-shaped fabric hide sold for cage birds. A documented hormonal trigger, and not for a pet lovebird.
Hepatic lipidosisFatty liver disease, usually from a high-fat diet and too little exercise.
HypocalcemiaLow blood calcium. In hens it follows repeated laying on a calcium-poor diet, and causes weakness, tremors and fractures.
KeelThe ridge of breastbone down the center of the chest. Feeling it either side is how body condition is judged.
KnemidocoptesThe mite that causes scaly face and leg disease, seen as white crusty encrustation on the cere, beak corners and legs.
MoltThe normal replacement of feathers, symmetrical on both sides, with new feathers emerging before old ones drop.
PBFDPsittacine beak and feather disease, an incurable circovirus affecting feathers, beak and the immune system.
PhotoperiodThe daily light and dark cycle. 10 to 12 hours of darkness is routine; 8 hours of light is the level used to break a laying cycle.
Pin featherA new feather still in its keratin sheath, which the bird flakes off by preening.
PolyomavirusA virus most dangerous to young birds, often fatal under four months. A vaccine exists.
PolyuriaExcess urine: a watery dropping whose solid part is still formed. Different from diarrhea, and usually less serious.
Powder downFine feather dust that normally keeps a parrot's beak looking matte. Its loss is an early PBFD sign.
PsittacosisInfection with Chlamydia psittaci, treatable with a 45-day course of doxycycline, and transmissible to people.
PTFEThe nonstick coating that releases a gas lethal to birds when a pan is overheated to about 536°F (280°C).
QuarantineKeeping a new bird separate, ideally on separate air, for 30 days minimum before it meets resident birds.
UratesThe chalky white portion of a dropping, made of uric acid. Yellow or green urates point to liver disease.
ZoonoticTransmissible from animals to people. Psittacosis is the one that matters in this species.

  • Diet split. Pellet shares between 60 and 80% appear in circulation. This package uses the 75 to 80% pellets and 20 to 40% fresh figures from VCA's lovebird feeding guidance, which is the species-specific source.
