Fun facts worth knowing

  • "Budgie" is short for budgerigar. In the wild, budgerigars are nomadic, moving across the Australian interior in large flocks in search of food and water.
  • Budgies are strong mimics, and many individuals build a real vocabulary of words and sounds, though not every bird chooses to talk.
  • The cere, the fleshy patch above the beak, is one of the more reliable ways to sex an adult budgie by sight: typically blue on males, and tan, brown, or pale pink/white on females.

Section 02 · Full Care Guide

Cage size & bar spacing

18×18×18 in is the absolute minimum footprint for a single budgie; 30×18×18 in for a pair. That's a floor, not a target, bigger is always better. Bar spacing needs to be half an inch (0.5 in) or less: wider spacing risks a head or wing getting caught, or a curious bird squeezing through entirely.

Never Use bar spacing wider than half an inch (0.5 in). This is a known head-entrapment and escape risk, regardless of how roomy the rest of the cage looks.

What the diagram doesn't show

  • Perches should vary in diameter and material, natural wood branches rather than uniform dowels, to exercise the feet and prevent pressure sores.
  • Keep perches from sitting directly above food and water dishes. Droppings falling into either is a daily contamination problem, not an occasional one.
  • Leave open space for a full wing stretch and a short flight hop between perches. A cage packed wall-to-wall with toys defeats the purpose of the extra size.
  • Position the cage away from the kitchen, direct all-day sun, and cold drafts.

Diet & feeding

Budgies are seed-eating birds by ancestry, but "seed-eating" is not the same as "seed-only." A pellet-forward diet with fresh vegetables is what current guidance recommends, with seeds kept as a smaller part of the mix rather than the base of the diet.

ComponentRole in the diet
Formulated pelletsThe base of the diet. Should make up the majority of what your budgie eats day to day.
Fresh vegetablesOffered daily alongside pellets. Adds moisture, fiber, and nutrients pellets alone don't fully cover.
SeedsA smaller part of the mix, not the foundation. Seed-only diets are a major cause of fatty liver disease, see page 10.
Fresh waterChanged daily. Check that it hasn't been fouled by droppings or discarded food.
Never feed seed-only Seeds are high in fat and don't cover the broader nutrient profile a budgie needs. Seed-only feeding is one of the most common, and most preventable, causes of fatty liver disease in pet budgies. See page 10.

Making the switch to pellets

A budgie raised on seed can be reluctant to try pellets at first. Offer both side by side rather than switching overnight, and expect the transition to take patience. A bird that stops eating entirely during a diet change is a vet visit, not a wait-and-see situation.

Foods to avoid

Avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and anything high in salt or sugar. None of these belong in a budgie's diet in any amount.

Keep it simple A consistent pellet-forward routine with a small rotating selection of vegetables is easier to stick to, and easier to notice appetite changes against, than a diet that changes every day.

Handling & social needs

Budgies are highly social flock birds. In the wild they live and travel in large groups, and that instinct doesn't switch off in a cage. A single budgie needs substantial daily interaction from you, ideally spread across multiple short sessions, or is better off with a compatible budgie companion for company when you're not around.

Building trust with a flighted bird

Handling a budgie is nothing like handling an animal that just needs to tolerate being picked up. A budgie can leave the interaction entirely, so the goal is trust, not restraint. Move slowly, keep your voice calm and consistent, and let the bird choose to approach rather than reaching in to grab it.

Building trust Let your budgie come to you. Reaching into the cage to grab a bird reads as a predator strike, and it can cost you weeks of trust-building in a few seconds.

Step-up training

Most owners start with a finger or small perch offered at chest height, paired with a consistent cue word, and reward calm curiosity rather than pushing for immediate contact. Short, frequent sessions build confidence faster than long, infrequent ones.

If you have, or want, more than one budgie

Two budgies of similar size and temperament, introduced gradually in neutral territory, often do better together than a single bird kept alone. A companion doesn't replace your own interaction time, it supplements it.

First days in a new home

Give a newly arrived budgie a few quiet days to settle before attempting any handling beyond feeding and cage maintenance. Talk to it calmly near the cage so it gets used to your presence and voice before you ever open the door.

Common mistakes & enrichment

Mistakes that cause the most harm

  • Keeping a single budgie with minimal daily interaction and no companion. Flock birds do not thrive in isolation.
  • Feeding a seed-only diet because "that's what budgies eat," leading to fatty liver disease over time.
  • Sizing the cage to the bare minimum and never upgrading, or choosing a cage on footprint alone without checking bar spacing.
  • Placing the cage in the kitchen, in a drafty spot, or in direct all-day sun.
  • Skipping toy and perch rotation, leading to a bored, understimulated bird.
  • Assuming a fluffed-up, quiet bird is just resting instead of recognizing it as a possible early illness sign.
  • Missing early signs of egg binding in a female because straining can look like normal nesting behavior at first glance.

Enrichment, by priority

Foraging

Hiding pellets or vegetable pieces inside foraging toys turns eating into a natural search-and-work behavior instead of a passive activity.

Chewing

Bird-safe wood, cardboard, and shreddable toys satisfy a natural chewing drive and help keep the beak in good shape.

Perch variety

Different diameters and textures across several perches exercise the feet and prevent pressure sores from a single uniform surface.

Training sessions

Short step-up and target-training sessions are mental exercise as much as physical, and they build the trust described on page 7.

Supervised out-of-cage time

Time outside the cage in a bird-proofed, supervised room lets a budgie stretch its wings. Never unsupervised, and never in a room with open windows, ceiling fans, or other pets.

Companionship

For a single budgie, your own daily interaction is the single biggest enrichment input there is. A compatible companion bird is the next best thing.

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags

What to watch for, and what to actually tell the vet.

Contact an avian vet promptly if you see Open-mouth breathing or tail-bobbing with each breath, a fluffed-up and unusually quiet bird, crusty or honeycomb-textured patches around the beak or legs, an overweight body condition paired with lethargy, or a female straining without producing an egg.

What to tell the vet

Diet breakdown (pellets vs. seeds vs. vegetables) over the last two weeks
When symptoms started and whether they're worsening
Changes in droppings, appetite, or activity level
Whether the bird is female and currently laying, or has laid recently

Why diet and cage setup come up first

Most of the serious conditions in this section trace back to diet or setup more often than anything else: fatty liver from seed-only feeding, and egg binding linked in part to calcium and vitamin D deficiency. A vet will ask about food and setup before anything else for exactly that reason.

Normal vs. concerning

NormalConcerning
Fluffed feathers briefly while resting or sleeping, then sleek again once activeFluffed up and puffy for extended periods during normal waking hours
A quiet, low-key stretch of the day, especially around middayPersistent lethargy, or a bird that won't leave the cage floor or a single perch
A healthy-weight, active female laying an occasional egg without difficultyStraining, tail-bobbing, or lethargy around egg-laying, a possible sign of egg binding (page 11)

Fatty liver & scaly face mites

Fatty Liver & Obesity

Cause: seed-only or seed-heavy diets over time; seeds are calorie-dense and don't cover the broader nutrient profile a pellet-forward diet provides. Signs: an overweight body condition and lethargy. Response: diet correction under avian vet guidance, not a crash diet attempted on your own. Related conditions: lipomas and xanthomas, fatty growths that can develop alongside chronic obesity, are worth mentioning to your vet if you notice any lumps.

Prevention beats treatment A pellet-forward diet with fresh vegetables (page 6) prevents nearly all diet-driven fatty liver cases. This is the single highest-leverage decision in this whole package.

Scaly Face & Leg Mites

Cause: a mite infestation, spread through direct contact between birds. Signs: visible crusty, honeycomb-textured patches around the beak or legs. Response: vet-prescribed treatment. This condition is highly treatable once diagnosed, it's not a reason for panic, but it does need an actual vet visit rather than a home remedy.

Don't wait it out Crusty patches around the beak or legs don't resolve on their own. The sooner treatment starts, the more comfortable your budgie is in the meantime.

Respiratory disease & egg binding

One applies to every budgie. The other only to females, but it's important enough to include.

Respiratory Disease

Signs: open-mouth breathing, tail-bobbing with each breath, and discharge from the eyes or nares. What it can include: a range of bacterial, fungal, and viral causes, among them psittacosis, a bacterial infection that is zoonotic (transmissible to people), part of why any respiratory symptom in a budgie deserves a prompt response rather than a wait-and-see approach. Response: a prompt avian vet visit. Don't try to diagnose the specific cause yourself, treatment differs by cause and a vet exam is how that gets sorted out.

Egg Binding

Female-only, and one of the true emergencies in this package. What it is: a condition where an egg cannot pass through the reproductive tract. Linked to: calcium and vitamin D deficiency and obesity, both diet and husbandry issues you have real control over. Signs: straining, lethargy, and tail-bobbing. Response: an immediate emergency vet visit. This is not a condition to monitor overnight and see how it looks in the morning.

Emergency, not a wait-and-see If you keep a female budgie and see straining combined with lethargy or tail-bobbing, treat it as an emergency immediately. Egg binding can be fatal within hours to days if untreated.

Other conditions worth knowing by name

Megabacteriosis, also called avian gastric yeast, is a yeast infection of the digestive tract that a vet can test for. Goiter, an enlarged thyroid gland caused by iodine deficiency, is another condition worth naming if you notice changes around your budgie's neck or voice. Neither is covered in depth here, but knowing the name helps you describe what you're seeing.

The common thread Diet and cage setup drive more of this health section than anything else: seed-only feeding, calcium and vitamin D shortfalls, and obesity. Get pages 5 and 6 right and you meaningfully lower the odds of ending up here.

Setup checklist & temperature targets

Essential setup checklist

18×18×18 in cage minimum, single bird (30×18×18 in for a pair)
Bar spacing 0.5 in or less
Multiple perches, varied diameter and material
Food and water dishes positioned away from perches above them
Foraging and chewable toys
Consistent light/dark cycle, roughly 10 to 12 hrs darkness nightly

First 30 days & days 31 to 90

Week 1

Confirm cage size and bar spacing meet the minimums on page 12, not just "looks about right"
Let your budgie settle: minimal handling, focus on feeding and observing appetite and droppings
Schedule a wellness visit with an avian vet within the first couple of weeks

Week 2 to 3

Begin short trust-building and step-up sessions if your budgie is eating and active normally
Establish a consistent pellet-forward feeding routine
Start the Owner Log on page 18

Week 4

Review appetite, droppings, and activity trends from the Owner Log
Introduce first enrichment items from page 19
Confirm your light/dark routine is consistent, roughly 10 to 12 hours of darkness nightly

Days 31 to 90

Book any follow-up wellness check your avian vet recommended
Look back at the Owner Log for any slow drift in weight, appetite, or activity level
Start rotating enrichment on the monthly schedule from page 19
If your budgie is alone and you're considering a companion, introduce gradually in neutral territory (page 7)

Glossary

BudgerigarThe full name for a budgie, a small parrot native to Australia's interior and commonly kept worldwide as a companion bird.
CereThe fleshy area above a budgie's beak; typically blue on adult males and tan, brown, or pale pink/white on adult females.
PhotoperiodThe daily cycle of light and darkness. Budgies do best with roughly 10 to 12 hours of darkness at night.
Fatty liver diseaseA buildup of fat in the liver, most often caused by a seed-only or seed-heavy diet.
Scaly face/leg mitesA mite infestation causing crusty, honeycomb-textured patches around the beak and legs. Highly treatable with vet-prescribed medication.
PsittacosisA bacterial infection that can accompany respiratory disease in birds and is zoonotic, meaning it can pass to people.
Egg bindingA life-threatening, female-only condition where an egg cannot pass through the reproductive tract.
MegabacteriosisAlso called avian gastric yeast; a yeast infection of the digestive tract.
GoiterAn enlarged thyroid gland caused by iodine deficiency.
