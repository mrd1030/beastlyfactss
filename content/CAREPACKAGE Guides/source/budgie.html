Cage size, bar spacing & placement

The purchase that is hardest to undo, and the one number that is a safety limit.

Two credible veterinary sources give different cage figures, and both are printed here rather than averaged. LafeberVet gives a minimum of 18 in (46 cm) wide and long and says cage length matters more than height. The MSD Veterinary Manual's cage table gives 20×20×30 in (51×51×76 cm) for a budgerigar. Treat 18×18 in as the floor below which a cage is not adequate, and 20×20×30 in or wider as what you should actually buy. Nobody has ever regretted the bigger cage.

Shape matters as much as volume. A budgie flies in short lateral bursts rather than climbing, so a tall narrow cage sells you height the bird will not use. Given 30 in of width or 30 in of height at the same price, take the width every time.

Bar spacing: 3/8 to 1/2 in (10 to 13 mm) LafeberVet gives approximately 3/8 in (0.95 cm); the MSD Veterinary Manual gives 0.5 in for a budgerigar. Anything wider is a head-entrapment risk, and a trapped budgie panics and injures its neck long before you find it. Measure the cage yourself rather than trusting the box. Avoid round cages, which give a bird no corner to settle in, and check the finish: galvanized bars and hardware are a zinc source, covered on page 8.

Perches, dishes & what to leave out

What goes in the cage, where, and the four things not to add.

Perches

A budgie spends essentially its whole life standing, so perch variety is a foot-health decision rather than a decorative one. Provide a mix of diameters and textures: natural wood branches of different thickness, at least one rope perch, and no more than one abrasive or cement perch positioned away from the favorite sleeping spot. A foot that has to adjust its grip through the day stays healthy; a foot gripping one uniform dowel at one diameter develops pressure sores and arthritis.

  • Place the highest perch where the bird can sit upright without its head or tail touching the cage.
  • Leave a clear flight line between two perches. A cage packed wall to wall with toys is a smaller cage.
  • Skip sandpaper perch covers. They do not shorten nails usefully and they abrade the foot.
  • Replace chewed wood perches rather than treating the chewing as a problem. Chewing is the point.

Dishes and water

Position food and water dishes so that nothing perches directly above them. Droppings in a water dish are a daily contamination problem, not an occasional one. Change water daily and wash dishes daily, and keep a spare set so a dirty one never stays in the cage while the other dries. Budgies do not need grit or gravel: some birds overconsume it and obstruct the gut, and a parrot husks a seed rather than swallowing it whole, so it has no use for it in the first place.

Leave these out Nest boxes and happy huts. Any enclosed dark cavity is a nest site to a hen, and it is the single easiest way to start a chronic laying cycle. Page 17.
Mirrors. A budgie treats its reflection as a mate it can never resolve, and mirror-bonded birds regurgitate at the glass and become territorial about it.
Rope or fabric with loose fibers. Frayed strands wrap toes and cut off circulation. Trim rope perches and toys as they wear.
Anything with lead or zinc. Galvanized hardware, cheap bells, chain, and mirror backings. Page 8.

Cuttlebone and mineral block

Keep a cuttlebone or mineral block in the cage at all times. It gives the beak something appropriate to wear against, and it is a calcium source that matters most for a hen who may lay whether or not there is a male in the house. It is a supplement to a good diet, not a replacement for one: a bird eating mostly pellets is already getting its calcium, and a bird eating mostly seed is not going to be rescued by a cuttlebone alone.

Line the tray with plain paper Newspaper or plain paper towel, changed daily, is better than any loose substrate. It costs nothing, and it lets you read the droppings every morning, which is the cheapest health check a bird owner gets. Page 26 explains what to look for. Loose litter hides exactly that information and gets eaten.

Light, sleep & household climate

Day length is a health control, not a lighting preference.

A budgie reads day length the way it would in the Australian interior: long days mean the season to breed, short days mean it is not. A bird sitting in a living room lit until midnight is living through an endless summer, and the consequences show up as hormonal behavior, chronic egg laying in hens, aggression, and sleep debt that feeds feather plucking. This page and the egg pages are the same subject seen from two ends.

SettingTarget
Darkness, every night10 to 12 hours, uninterrupted. Cover the cage or move the bird to a quiet sleep cage in an unused room
DaylightA normal room's light on a consistent schedule. Indirect natural daylight is ideal; a bird does not need a special lamp to be healthy
Hormonal interventionCutting light to 8 hours a day is the standard first step in breaking a laying cycle, alongside removing nest sites; LafeberVet gives the same intervention as 8 to 10 hours. Merck names a photoperiod over 12 hours as a documented risk factor for excessive laying, which is the line a lit living room crosses. Page 17
Room temperature70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C). A healthy budgie tolerates a wider range than that comfortably; it is the sudden change and the draft that cause problems, not the number
HumidityNormal household humidity is fine. Very dry winter air suits a bird poorly, so bathing matters more in a heated house

What uninterrupted actually means

Covered but in a room where the television is on is not darkness, and a bird that wakes six times a night is sleep-deprived even if the total hours look right. Noise wakes a budgie as reliably as light does. If the cage lives in the main room of the house and the household stays up late, the honest fix is a second small cage somewhere quiet, used only for sleeping. It costs less than the main cage and solves a problem that no amount of covering solves.

Bathing

Offer a shallow dish of room-temperature water two or three times a week, or a light mist from a spray bottle aimed above the bird rather than at it. Some budgies bathe enthusiastically and some never do; both are normal. Bathing keeps feather condition good, helps in dry heated air, and takes the edge off a molt. Never use soap or any additive, and let the bird dry in a draft-free room rather than with a hair dryer, which is itself a nonstick-coated appliance in some models. Page 8.

Sleep debt looks like a behavior problem A budgie that is irritable, bites more than it used to, screams in the evening, or has started plucking is worth investigating from the sleep side before anything else. It is the cheapest thing on the list to fix and it is wrong in a large share of households, because nobody thinks of a light switch as husbandry.

Household hazards & bird-proofing

The most important page in this book. A bird's lungs make ordinary household air lethal.

A bird's respiratory system moves air through the lungs in one direction and extracts oxygen on both the inhale and the exhale. It is far more efficient than a mammal's, and that efficiency is exactly why airborne poisons that a person would merely find unpleasant kill a budgie in the same room. Nothing else in this book has as short a distance between a normal household act and a dead bird.

PTFE: nonstick cookware Overheated nonstick surfaces release a gas at around 280°C (536°F), which is well within reach of an empty pan left on a burner. In a small bird, acute death is often the only sign there was ever a problem. Keep birds out of the kitchen entirely, never run a self-cleaning oven with a bird in the house, and check the other sources Merck names: irons and ironing board covers, some self-cleaning ovens, heating elements in some reverse-cycle heat pumps, and some heat lamps.

Airborne

  • Aerosols of any kind. Hairspray, deodorant, air freshener, oven cleaner, spray sunscreen, cleaning sprays.
  • Scented candles, incense, plug-in air fresheners, and burned or melted plastic. All named in the veterinary literature as irritant or toxic to birds.
  • Cigarette and cannabis smoke, and fireplace or wood-stove smoke. Keep a working carbon monoxide detector in any room with gas heat or a gas range: CO is odorless to you, and the bird has no margin.
  • Fresh paint, varnish, and new carpet. Move the bird out of the house, not into another room, and ventilate for days rather than hours.

Heavy metals

A budgie chews everything it can reach, which turns ordinary hardware into a poisoning route. Lead sources named in the veterinary literature include window blinds, costume jewelry, mirror backings, some bird toys, hardware cloth and curtain weights, and lead tastes sweet, which is why a bird that finds it keeps chewing. Zinc sources include galvanized toys, chains, mesh, bells, keys and pennies minted after 1982; electroplated galvanized metal is considered safe, but hot-dipped, the cheaper and commoner coating, leaches zinc without the bird swallowing a visible flake. Signs of either are non-specific and easy to miss: appetite loss, weight loss, regurgitation, diarrhea, weakness, and increased drinking and urination. Buy cage hardware and toys that state stainless steel or powder-coated, and treat an unlabeled shiny metal bell as a maybe rather than a yes.

Physical hazards during out-of-cage time

  • Ceiling fans off, always, before the door opens.
  • Windows and mirrors covered or curtained. A budgie flies into glass at full speed.
  • Open water: toilet lid down, sinks empty, pots covered, mugs finished.
  • Hot surfaces: stove, kettle, candles, a just-used iron.
  • Other pets shut out of the room, with no exceptions for the friendly ones.
  • Toxic houseplants moved out. If you cannot identify a plant, it goes.
  • Doors and windows shut, and everyone in the house told the bird is out.
  • Sticky traps, mouse bait and insect sprays removed from the room entirely.
A cat or dog bite is a same-day emergency with no visible wound Cat saliva carries Pasteurella, which at a bird's body temperature can multiply fast enough to cause fatal sepsis within hours, and the puncture is usually invisible under feathers. Any contact at all between a cat's mouth and a bird is a vet visit today, not a wait-and-see. The same goes for a dog's grab, where the crushing injury is internal.

One budgie or two, and the talking trade-off

The decision that shapes every other one, and the honest version of the trade.

Budgies are flock birds. In the wild they move across the Australian interior in flocks that can number in the thousands, and none of that instinct switches off in a cage. A budgie alone is not automatically miserable, but the company has to come from somewhere, and there are only two places it can come from: another budgie, or you, several hours a day, every day, for the bird's whole life. Deciding which honestly, before you buy, is worth more than any other page here.

One budgieA pair
What it needs from youSeveral hours of genuine daily interaction, split across sessions, indefinitelyDaily interaction still, but the bird is not alone when you leave the house
TamenessUsually tamer and faster to tame, because you are the flockSlower and often less complete. Two birds bond to each other first
TalkingFar more likely, and mostly in males. A single male bonded to one person is the setup that produces a talking budgieUnlikely. Birds that have each other to talk to have no reason to learn your words
NoiseQuieter, though never silentChattier all day, which most owners come to like
CostBaselineRoughly half again on food and toys, plus a wider cage
Risk it addsLoneliness if the daily time does not happen, which shows up as plucking and screamingA male and female pair will breed given any nest site, and a hen that starts laying is a real health problem. Page 17
The honest recommendation If you work away from home, or you want a bird because it looks low effort, get two. If you are home most of the day and want a tame bird that might talk, one is genuinely the better outcome for both of you, provided the time is real. Two hens or two cocks avoids the breeding question entirely; a mixed pair with no nest box and a controlled photoperiod is manageable, but it is a thing you have to actively manage rather than a thing that takes care of itself.

Adding a second budgie later

Budgies are also the one small parrot commonly kept in groups rather than in ones and twos. A colony is a different problem from a pair, not a bigger one. No verified space-per-bird or sex-ratio figure exists in a source worth printing, so what actually works is more feeding stations than birds, perches and sightlines that stop one bird controlling the space, and the understanding that any mixed-sex group with a nest site available is a breeding setup whether or not you meant one.

This works, and it is the commonest reason a settled single bird suddenly has company. Two things make or break it. Quarantine the new bird for 30 to 45 days in a separate room first, which is not optional and is covered on page 27. Then introduce in separate cages side by side for a week or more before any shared space, and make the first shared space neutral rather than the resident bird's cage. A resident budgie defends its cage; the same two birds meeting on a play stand often get along immediately.

What a lonely budgie looks like

Feather plucking, pacing the same stretch of perch over and over, screaming beyond normal contact calls, and obsessive attachment to a mirror or a toy. All four are also signs of other problems, so treat them as a prompt to check both the social side and the health side rather than a diagnosis. Page 24 covers the differential.

Diet: pellets, seed & converting a seed eater

The single biggest lever on how long your budgie lives.

Wild budgies eat grass seed, which is true and is the reason most pet budgies are fed badly. Wild seed is eaten green and milky, in enormous variety, by a bird flying miles a day for it. A bowl of dry sunflower and millet in a cage is a different food eaten by a different animal. An all-seed diet is deficient in protein, vitamins and minerals including calcium and vitamin A, and the conditions that follow from it, obesity, fatty liver disease, and the tumors on page 20, are the reason a budgie dies at five instead of twelve.

Four ways to convert a seed eater, from a veterinary source

  1. Offer pellets first thing in the morning, before anything else goes in, when the bird is hungriest.
  2. Spread pellets on a flat surface such as a shallow tray or a sheet of paper, which mimics ground feeding and reads as food rather than as an object.
  3. Grind pellets to powder and coat a moist food with it, then increase the particle size over days until the bird is taking whole pellets.
  4. Mix pellets into the seed, then decrease the seed and increase the pellets a little each day.

Expect it to take days, weeks or months. That is the published range, not a failure on your part.

Never starve a bird onto pellets A budgie weighs about 30 g and has no meaningful energy reserve. Pulling the seed and waiting is how birds die during a diet change. Weigh the bird on a gram scale through the conversion (page 16), keep offering the old food until the new food is genuinely being eaten, and check the droppings daily: fewer and smaller droppings mean less food is going in, whatever the dish looks like. Lafeber's limit for a bird this size is firm: never let a budgerigar go more than 12 hours without eating, and stop and call an avian vet at a 10% drop from its starting weight.

Safe vegetables, greens & herbs

The 20 to 25% that pellets do not cover, weighted toward vegetables rather than fruit.

Bright yellow, red and orange vegetables carry the most vitamin A, and vitamin A deficiency is one of the commonest nutritional problems in seed-fed birds. It shows up as sneezing, nasal discharge, swelling around the eyes, poor feather quality and feather picking, so a chart that looks like a garnish list is doing real work. Chop small enough for a 30 g bird to manage, offer fresh in the morning, and take it out after a few hours in a warm room before it spoils.

Wash everything, and prefer organic where you can Birds are very sensitive to pesticides, chemicals and sprays, and a budgie eats a large amount relative to its body weight. Wash produce thoroughly under running water even when it is labeled pre-washed, and treat anything from a garden that has been sprayed as off the list entirely.

Getting a suspicious bird to try vegetables

Most budgies refuse new food the first several times it appears, and most owners stop before the bird starts. Persistence over days and weeks is what works, not variety on any single day. Three things that help:

  • Offer it clipped to the bars at perch height, not in a dish on the floor of the cage. Food a budgie has to work at is food it takes seriously.
  • Eat it in front of the bird. Budgies are flock feeders and copying what the flock eats is how a young bird learns what food is.
  • Chop it into pellet-sized pieces and mix a little into the pellet dish. Familiar shape, new content.

Sprouting

Sprouted seed is the closest a home kitchen gets to what a wild budgie actually eats, and the sprouting process shifts a dry seed's balance away from fat. Rinse thoroughly twice a day, sprout for one to two days only, refrigerate, and throw away anything that smells sour or looks slimy. Spoiled sprouts grow bacteria and fungus fast, and a bird this size has no margin for a gut infection, so if you are not going to be careful about it, skip sprouting and stay with fresh vegetables.

A chop, made once a week Dice a mix of the daily-tier vegetables, portion it into an ice cube tray, and freeze. Thaw one cube each morning. It removes the daily preparation excuse, which is the real reason fresh food stops appearing in most households after the first month.

Fruit, treats & the never-feed list

The smallest part of the diet, and the part where the real dangers are.

Fruit is sugar with vitamins attached. It belongs inside the 20 to 25% fresh share rather than on top of it, and the orange-fleshed fruits earn their place better than the sweet pale ones because of the vitamin A. A budgie is small enough that a grape is a meal, so portion by the piece, not by the handful.

TierFoods
Best choicesMango, papaya, cantaloupe. All three are high in vitamin A and are the fruits worth offering most often
Fine, in small piecesApple with the seeds removed, pear, berries, banana, melon, kiwi, orange and other citrus in small amounts, grapes cut in half
Treats, not foodMillet spray in small quantities once or twice a month. A little plain cooked pasta or unsweetened whole-grain cereal. Nothing sweetened, salted or fried
NeverAvocado, onion, garlic, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, anything very salty, and processed food cooked with butter or oil
The never list, and why Avocado carries persin in every part of the plant, and smaller birds are the more sensitive. Respiratory distress typically appears about 12 hours after ingestion, with death possible inside one to two days. No part of it, including guacamole on a fingertip.
Onion and garlic contain chemicals that may damage a bird's red blood cells.
Chocolate, caffeine and alcohol are never fed to a bird in any amount.
Salt. A budgie's kidneys are handling a fraction of a person's food volume. A single salted chip is a large dose.
Apple seeds and stone-fruit pits come out before the fruit goes in. Remove cherry pits specifically.
Anything moldy. Fruit left in a warm cage grows mold within hours, and a budgie will eat it.

Fresh food and the clock

Fresh food goes in when you get up and comes out a few hours later, sooner in a warm room. Leaving a wet vegetable in the cage all day is how a good diet turns into a bacterial problem, and it also removes the one useful signal you get from a food dish: whether the food was eaten or ignored.

What about the bird that only wants millet

Almost every budgie prefers millet to everything else on offer, in the way a child prefers dessert, and the veterinary guidance to hold it to small quantities once or twice a month is exactly because the preference is so strong. Use it deliberately: a millet head is the most effective taming currency you have (page 14) and it is wasted as a dish filler. Break a spray into short pieces and hand-feed those rather than clipping a whole one into the cage.

A bird eating mostly pellets does not need vitamin drops Once 75 to 80% of the diet is pellets, supplemental vitamins are not needed, and water-soluble vitamin additives foul the water and encourage bacterial growth in the dish. A bird still on seed is the one with the deficiency, and the answer there is the conversion on page 10, not a bottle.

Foraging first

A wild budgie spends most of its active day looking for food. A cage with an open dish removes that entire behavior and gives nothing back, which is where cage boredom, pacing and plucking start. Enrichment that restores foraging does more than any single toy purchase, and it costs nothing: wrap part of the daily ration in a twist of plain paper, put some in a shallow tray under a layer of shredded paper, thread a few pellets into a cardboard tube, or hide small portions in three places instead of one. Change the method every few days so it stays a puzzle rather than a memorized route.

The rest of the enrichment picture

Destructible, and cheap

Plain paper, cardboard, soft untreated wood, palm leaf, yucca. Budgies destroy these fast, which is the point. A rotating supply of cheap shreddables beats one expensive toy that survives a year.

Rotation, not accumulation

Keep three to five toys in the cage and swap two of them weekly. A toy ignored for a week becomes interesting again after a fortnight away. A cage crammed with twenty permanent toys is visually busy and behaviorally empty.

Flight is enrichment Supervised out-of-cage flight in a bird-proofed room does more for a budgie's physical condition than every toy on this page combined, and it is the single best obesity intervention available to an owner. Handling, taming & the bite problem

Trust with an animal that can leave. There is no restraint-based version of this.

Where the bird came from sets the starting line. A hand-raised budgie may step up on day one; a parent-raised or aviary-raised bird has never been handled and needs weeks. Neither is better as a pet, but pushing a nervous bird at a hand-raised bird's pace sets you back rather than forward.

Settle first, for a week or two

Do nothing but feed, change water and clean for the first week or more. Sit near the cage and talk. Position the cage at or near eye level and against a wall: movement above or behind a caged bird triggers a genuine predator response, and a bird spending its energy on that has none left for you.

The progression

  1. Hand in the cage, doing nothing. Rest your hand inside, not reaching, until the bird stops reacting to it. This step is longer than people expect and it is the one that actually works, because it hands the bird control of the timing.
  2. Millet from your fingers. Hold a short piece still and close enough that the bird has to come to it. Do not follow the bird with it.
  3. Step up. Once food is being taken calmly, press a finger gently against the lower chest just above the feet and give a consistent cue word. The pressure tips the bird's balance backward onto your finger; you are not lifting it.
  4. Out of the cage. Only in a bird-proofed room, page 8, and only once step-up is reliable inside.

Short and frequent beats long and forced. Five minutes three times a day is worth more than an hour on Sunday.

No dominance, no punishment, no grabbing Advice about establishing dominance over a bird is outdated and actively counterproductive. Budgies are prey animals: a hand that reaches in and grabs is a predator strike, and it costs weeks of trust in seconds. Never punish a bite. Punishment teaches a prey animal that hands are dangerous, which is the opposite of the thing you are trying to build.

Biting, and what it actually means

A budgie bite is almost always one of four things: fear, cage territoriality, hormones, or the bird using its beak as a third foot to test whether a perch is stable. The last is not a bite at all and is the commonest thing new owners misread. Hens tend to be more territorial and bite harder than cocks, particularly in breeding condition, which is normal for the species.

  • Cage aggression: take the bird out to a neutral perch first, and do not reach in to retrieve it.
  • Hormonal biting: page 17. Light, nest sites and petting down the back are the drivers.
  • Fear biting: you moved too fast. Go back a step.

Stress signals, and the towel

Frantic flapping, panting or open-mouth breathing, and repeatedly fleeing from your hand all mean stop and try later. If you genuinely have to restrain a budgie for medication or to check an injury, use a small towel rather than bare hands, so the bird associates the unpleasant part with the towel instead of with you. Restrain around the shoulders, never around the chest: a bird's breathing depends on the chest wall moving, and a firm grip suffocates it.

Wing clipping & flight

You will be asked about this in week one, usually by whoever would do the trim.

Clipping means shortening some of the primary flight feathers so a bird cannot gain height. It is not permanent: the cut feathers are dead tissue and the bird regains flight at its next molt. There is no fixed interval to quote, because Merck notes molt timing varies with nutrition, daylight and humidity, so check the wings rather than the calendar. It is also not a training tool. A clipped budgie is not tamer, quieter or better behaved; it is a budgie that cannot get away from anything, which is a different thing from a budgie that has chosen not to.

The case for clipping

  • A household with doors opening onto a street, or people who will not reliably check before opening one.
  • Large windows and mirrors that cannot be covered.
  • A bird that panics and repeatedly flies into things during the settling-in period.
  • A brief clip while a specific, temporary risk is fixed.

The case against

  • Flight is the main exercise a caged budgie gets, and obesity is this species' biggest killer. Page 20.
  • A clipped bird still gets startled and still leaves the perch, but lands hard. Keel and leg injuries follow.
  • Falling repeatedly makes some birds more fearful, not calmer.
  • A bird that escapes while clipped cannot climb, cannot gain height, and cannot return.
If you do clip, these are the rules Only primary flight feathers are trimmed, and Merck's method is four to seven of the outermost primaries on both wings, cut below the coverts that overlay them, with the secondaries closer to the body left alone because those are what let a bird slow down and land. The number depends on the individual bird's weight and flying ability: a heavier bird needs fewer feathers cut, a lighter one more. Trimming only one wing is not recommended, because it leaves the bird unable to steer and it crashes. Have it done by an avian veterinarian or an experienced technician the first time, and watch, rather than learning on your own bird. A cut into a blood feather, one still growing and still supplied with blood, bleeds heavily and needs pressure and a same-day vet.

What a correct clip leaves the bird able to do

Glide gently to the floor from perch height without dropping like a stone, and keep its balance. If the bird plummets, too much was taken. If it can climb and circle the room, nothing useful was achieved. The window between those is narrow, which is one honest argument for leaving a budgie flighted and fixing the room instead.

The recommendation this book makes

Default to flighted, and make the room safe (page 8) rather than the bird incapable. Clip only for a specific, named risk that you cannot otherwise remove, and revisit the decision at each molt rather than treating it as a standing arrangement. A budgie that flies confidently around a safe room is fitter, lands better and is easier to keep at a healthy weight than one that cannot.

Leg bands and microchips Most budgies come with a closed leg band carrying the breeder's code and year. Leave it on unless a vet advises otherwise; it is the only identification the bird has, and it is what gets a lost budgie home. Check under it during the weekly handling check for swelling or a caught fiber, which is the one real risk a band carries.

Sexing by cere, weight & body condition

One of the few parrots you can sex by looking, and the vital sign that is a number.

The cere

The cere is the fleshy patch above the beak where the nostrils sit, and in adult budgies it is colored by sex. Cocks show a blue to purple-blue cere. Hens show tan, brown or pale white, thickening and roughening in breeding condition. Juveniles of both sexes show pink or pale purple that has not settled yet, which is why sexing a very young bird by cere alone does not work; it resolves somewhere between 6 and 12 months, the same window as sexual maturity. Some color mutations, notably the recessive pied and lutino and albino birds, keep a pinkish cere into adulthood and are the genuine exceptions.

A brown crusty cere means different things in the two sexes In a hen it is usually breeding condition, and it smooths and lightens again when she comes out of it. In a cock the same texture change is not hormonal and is worth a vet visit: scaly face mites give a crusty porous surface (page 23), and a cere changing color in a mature male is also associated with reproductive tumors. Sudden change, bleeding, or a change alongside lethargy or labored breathing is a vet appointment whatever the sex.

Weight is this species' vital sign

You cannot see a budgie's condition through its feathers, and a bird hides illness until it cannot. Weight is the one number that moves before anything is visible, and a digital kitchen scale reading in single grams costs less than a bag of pellets. Train the bird to step onto a perch fixed to the scale, or use a small tub.

Feeling the keel

The keel is the ridge of breastbone running down the center of the chest. Rest a fingertip either side of it with the bird held gently in a towel or sitting on your hand. Hens, hormones & chronic egg laying

A single hen with no male in the house can lay, and repeatedly laying is what harms her.

Budgerigars are one of the three species named most often in the veterinary literature for chronic egg laying, alongside cockatiels and lovebirds. A hen lays because her body has read the environment as a breeding season, and a male is not required for that. Each clutch draws calcium out of her, and a hen who lays clutch after clutch runs into calcium depletion, egg binding, and prolapse. One clutch in a lifetime is a normal event; a hen laying every few weeks is a medical problem that needs the environment changed, not the eggs removed.

Reference figure
Sexual maturity6 to 12 months
Typical clutch4 to 6 eggs, laid roughly every other day
IncubationAbout 18 days from the start of sitting

The triggers, in the order worth removing them

  1. Any dark enclosed cavity. Nest box, happy hut, a tent, a covered food dish, a cardboard box on the floor, the gap behind the sofa during out-of-cage time. This is the strongest and easiest trigger to remove.
  2. Long day length. Reducing daylight to 8 hours a day is the standard intervention, and it is the lever this whole page turns on. Page 7.
  3. A mate. That includes a mirror, a favorite toy the bird regurgitates to, and in some birds a person. Remove the object; separate a paired bird if the laying continues.
  4. Petting down the back and under the tail. Restrict affection to the head and neck, which is where a budgie preens a flock-mate rather than a mate.
  5. Abundant rich food and constant access to it. Rising food availability is a breeding signal. Conversion to a formulated pelleted diet is part of the standard management.
Do not take the eggs away Pulling a clutch tells the hen the nest failed, and she lays a replacement clutch, which is exactly the cycle you are trying to stop. Leave the eggs, or swap them for dummy eggs, and let her sit out the roughly 18 day incubation until she loses interest. That is the budgerigar's own figure from LafeberVet, not the 21 to 28 days quoted as a general parrot range: use the species number, because it is the bird's biology that decides when she gives up on a clutch. Removing the environment's breeding cues is the intervention; removing the eggs is the mistake.

When the environment is not enough

A hen who keeps laying after the nest sites are gone, the light is at 8 hours, the mate object is removed and the diet has changed is a veterinary case, not a husbandry one. Hormonal treatment exists and works: leuprolide acetate is given by injection at 800 mcg/kg every three weeks for three doses and then as needed, and a deslorelin implant placed under the skin or into muscle is the longer-acting option. Both need an avian vet, and neither substitutes for fixing the environment, because the cues will simply restart the cycle when the drug wears off.

Calcium, before it becomes an emergency A laying hen is spending calcium she may not have. Keep a cuttlebone or mineral block available at all times, and get the diet onto pellets, which carry a workable calcium to phosphorus ratio where a seed mix does not. A calcium-deficient hen lays thin-shelled eggs, and thin-shelled eggs are what get stuck. That is page 18.

Egg binding

A hen who cannot pass an egg has hours, not days. This is the page to know by heart.

Egg binding is a failure to pass a formed egg, and in a bird this small it kills quickly. The egg presses on the kidneys and the major vessels, and it obstructs the single opening the bird uses for both droppings and eggs, so a bound hen stops passing droppings as well. Calcium deficiency and obesity are the two commonest predisposing factors, which is why the diet pages and this one are connected.

Signs, roughly in the order they appear Straining, tail bobbing or pumping with each effort, and a wide-legged stance. Then a hen sitting on the floor of the cage rather than perching, depressed, with her eyes closed. Then labored breathing. A swelling low in the abdomen may be visible or palpable. Sometimes an egg is visible at the vent, or tissue prolapses from it. Late on, one or both legs go weak, because the egg presses on the nerves running to them. Fewer droppings, then none, is the sign that decides it. VCA adds a marker worth holding to on its own: no egg passed in 48 hours by a hen you know is laying. Any hen on the cage floor and fluffed is an emergency whether or not you knew she was laying.

What to do, in order

  1. Call the avian vet now. Before anything else. Say the words "possible egg binding" so the practice treats it as an emergency slot rather than an appointment.
  2. Warmth and humidity while you travel. Move her to a small carrier lined with paper towel, keep the ambient temperature up at around 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C), which sits inside the 80 to 90°F (27 to 32°C) range LafeberVet gives for a bird under supplemental heat where VCA puts a recovering bird's ideal at 75 to 80°F (22 to 25°C), and add humidity if you can. Back off if she shows flat sleek feathers, wings held out from the body, or open-mouth breathing, which mean she is too hot. Warmth alone resolves some mild cases and buys time in all of them.
  3. Do not stop offering food and water. A 30 g bird has no reserve. Leave both within reach in the carrier.
  4. Keep her quiet and dark in transit, and do not keep checking on her.
Things people try that make it worse Never massage or squeeze the abdomen. A broken egg inside the bird turns a treatable obstruction into peritonitis and sharp fragments.
Never try to pull the egg out or insert anything into the vent.
Do not put oil into the vent. It is a folk remedy that soaks the feathers, chills the bird and achieves nothing.
Do not wait overnight to see if she passes it. Overnight is the whole window.

What the vet will actually do

Fluids, injectable calcium, pain relief, and a warm humid environment resolve a large share of cases without anything invasive; the calcium restores the contractions that were failing. If that does not work, the egg can be collapsed and removed under anesthesia, and in a hen with a badly adhered egg or repeated episodes the surgical option is removal of the reproductive tract, which also permanently ends the laying problem. Ask about that last option directly if this is not the first episode.

Preventing the next one

Every prevention step is on page 17, and the two that matter most are calcium and day length. A hen who has been bound once is likelier to be bound again, so treat the first episode as the signal to change the environment properly rather than as bad luck. If she is on seed, the conversion on page 10 is not optional after an episode of binding.

Obesity, fatty liver & tumors

The condition group that shortens more budgie lives than everything else on the site combined.

Obesity and hepatic lipidosis

Obesity in a bird is defined as roughly 20% over ideal weight, or a body condition score of 4 out of 5 on the keel. An obese budgie is at increased risk of fatty liver disease, in which fat accumulates in the liver and displaces working tissue, and also of atherosclerosis and heart disease. The cause is almost always the same: a calorie-dense seed diet in a cage that offers no flight. It is the single biggest preventable problem in pet budgies, and it is preventable by two things that are free, converting to pellets (page 10) and letting the bird fly (page 13).

Weight loss is done slowly, and with a vet Crash-dieting a 30 g bird mobilizes fat into a liver that is already struggling, which makes it worse rather than better. Lipomas and xanthomas

Lipomas are benign fatty growths, most often on the chest or belly, and xanthomas are yellow thickened areas of skin loaded with cholesterol, both associated with high-fat seed-heavy diets. A budgie with one is telling you about its diet. They are not usually urgent, but a growing lipoma interferes with flight and can ulcerate, and any new lump is worth a vet appointment rather than a decision made at home about what kind of lump it is.

Tumors, and the sign nobody expects

Budgerigars have an unusually high rate of tumors for a pet bird, and kidney tumors in particular are noted as common in this species. The presenting sign is often not a lump at all: a tumor sitting against the nerves that run past the kidney causes lameness or paralysis in one leg or both, because the nerve supply to the leg is compressed. A budgie that starts limping, drags a foot, or cannot grip a perch on one side is a vet visit, and it is a mistake to assume the bird knocked itself during a flight.

The cere is a tumor sign in cocks Reproductive tumors change the hormone balance, and in a mature male budgie one of the visible results is a cere shifting from blue toward brown. A hen's cere turning brown is ordinary breeding condition; a cock's is not, and it earns an appointment. Page 16 covers the normal version so you can tell them apart.

Any breathing sign in a budgie is urgent. Psittacosis, or parrot fever

Caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci, and especially common in cockatiels, budgerigars and small parrots. In the bird the signs are non-specific: lethargy, nasal and eye discharge, weight loss, diarrhea, and characteristically lime-green or yellow-green droppings, because the organism attacks the liver. It is treatable. The treatment is doxycycline, given orally or by injection. Forty-five days has been the standard course, but the public-health compendium makes a budgerigar-specific exception: 30 days is effective in this species, and recent work puts 21 to 30 days within reach for psittacines generally, with a retest two to four weeks after the course ends. The length is still the point: a short course does not clear it and the bird relapses. Every bird in the house is treated, not just the sick one.

This one infects people Psittacosis spreads to humans by inhaling dried droppings and respiratory secretions, so cleaning a cage is the exposure. In people the incubation is 5 to 14 days and the illness starts abruptly with fever, chills, headache, aching muscles and a dry cough, and it can progress to pneumonia. Fewer than 1% of properly treated patients die, but it is frequently misdiagnosed because nobody mentions the bird. Tell your own doctor you keep a bird. When cleaning a cage during a suspected case, dampen the surfaces first so nothing goes airborne, wear gloves and an N95 respirator, and never dry-sweep or vacuum dried droppings.

What you can do before you get there

Keep the bird warm and quiet, around 80 to 85°F (27 to 29°C), inside LafeberVet's 80 to 90°F range for a bird under supplemental heat where VCA puts a recovering bird at 75 to 80°F. Flat sleek feathers, wings held out, or open-mouth breathing mean too hot. Leave food and water within reach at perch height so it does not have to move to reach them, and stop any spraying, cooking or cleaning in the house. If a toxin is suspected, get the bird into fresh air first and call from outside. Do not give any human medicine, and do not use over-the-counter bird antibiotics sold without a prescription; they are the wrong drug at the wrong dose and they delay the visit that would have worked.

Polyomavirus, French moult & PBFD

Two viruses that both cause feather damage, and telling them apart matters.

Avian polyomavirus

This virus is so closely tied to this species that its other name is budgerigar fledgling disease. It hits young birds hardest. In unweaned chicks the presentation is an acute one: sudden lethargy, the crop stops emptying, and death follows within 24 to 48 hours, with signs appearing 7 to 10 days after exposure. VCA puts mortality at up to 100% in birds under 15 days old, and notes that many juvenile and newly weaned parrots die quickly with no clinical signs at all. Budgerigars that survive past about three weeks of age typically do not die of it but develop the feather form instead, which is where the old aviary name comes from. Budgerigars also have one transmission route no other affected species has: vertical transmission from an infected parent straight into the egg, which is why a clean-looking breeding pair is not proof of anything.

French moult The feather form of polyomavirus. Flight and tail feathers are dystrophic: they fail to grow properly, break, or drop out, leaving a fledgling that cannot fly. Aviculturists have called these birds runners or, where the down persists without proper flight feathers, feather dusters. Many affected birds go on to live normal lives as non-flying pets, but the bird is a virus source for other birds in the house. A vaccine exists: manufacturers recommend the first dose once a chick is over 35 days old, with a booster 2 to 3 weeks later. That is a breeder's decision more than a pet owner's, and it is a fair question to ask the breeder you buy from.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

PBFD is caused by a circovirus and is the other feather-destroying virus people worry about. The species it is reported in most are African grey parrots, eclectus parrots, lovebirds, lorikeets and historically cockatoos, so it is a much less likely explanation for a scruffy budgie than polyomavirus is. When it occurs, the picture is abnormal feathers that are pinched or clubbed at the base and shed easily with slow or absent regrowth, beak lesions, and immune suppression that lets other infections in. Diagnosis is a PCR test on feces, feather dander or blood, and the test detects infected birds that look completely healthy, which is exactly why it belongs in a quarantine panel. Page 27.

Scaly face mites, megabacteriosis & goiter

Three conditions this species gets more than most, and all three are treatable.

Scaly face and leg mites

Caused by the mite Knemidocoptes pilae, and one of the few budgie problems visible from across the room. It produces white, porous, proliferative crusts at the corners of the mouth, on the cere and beak, and sometimes around the eyes, on the legs or at the vent. The honeycombed texture is characteristic. Left alone it deforms the beak permanently, so it is worth catching early, and it is contagious to other birds in the house.

Treatment Ivermectin at 0.2 mg/kg by mouth or by injection, or moxidectin at 0.2 mg/kg by mouth or applied to the skin, repeated after two weeks. This is a prescription from an avian vet at a dose calculated for a 30 g bird, not a pet-shop mite spray and not a dog product measured by eye. Clean and replace wooden perches and porous cage furniture while treating, and have any other birds in the house checked.

Megabacteriosis, or avian gastric yeast

Despite the old name, the organism, Macrorhabdus ornithogaster, is a yeast rather than a bacterium, and it is one of the small pet birds it most often affects, alongside cockatiels, lovebirds, parrotlets and finches. Budgerigars carry the longest history with it, though at least one peer-reviewed case series found it more often in the cockatiels it examined than in the budgies. It lives in the proventriculus and interferes with digestion. The classic picture is the "going light" bird: a budgie eating normally or even ravenously while steadily losing weight, sometimes regurgitating, sometimes passing whole undigested seed in the droppings. Mortality across affected birds runs anywhere from 10 to 80% depending on the bird and the strain, which is why the weekly weighing on page 16 earns its place: this disease is a falling number long before it is anything you can see.

  • Diagnosis is by finding the organism in a fresh dropping under the microscope, or by a PCR test on droppings. It sheds intermittently, so one clear sample does not rule it out.
  • Treatment with the best published success rate is amphotericin B at 100 mg/kg by mouth twice daily for 30 days. Shorter courses fail. Voriconazole at 10 mg/kg twice daily has been reported anecdotally.
  • During and after treatment expect at least one repeat fecal exam while the course runs and another once it ends, because a bird can look and act recovered while still shedding the organism.
  • The uncommon acute form is worth knowing: budgerigars in otherwise good condition have been documented collapsing and dying within 12 to 24 hours, usually from bleeding into the proventriculus. Any of the signs above plus sudden weakness is a same-day visit, not a wait-and-see.

Goiter, from iodine deficiency

A seed diet is iodine-poor, and without iodine the thyroid enlarges. The gland sits at the base of the neck, right next to the airway, so the sign is respiratory rather than a visible swelling: a stridor, a wheeze or a repeated click when the bird breathes, sometimes with regurgitation as the enlarged gland presses on the crop and esophagus. It is much less common now that pellets are normal, and it remains one of the clearest arguments for them. Treatment is Lugol's iodine, one drop per 250 mL of drinking water, until the bird is converted onto a pelleted or properly fortified diet, which then fixes the underlying cause.

Overgrown beak and nails are a symptom, not a grooming task A healthy budgie on good perches and a cuttlebone maintains its own beak. A beak that keeps overgrowing points at liver disease (page 20), at scaly face mites, or at a past injury to the growth plate. Trimming it addresses the shape and not the cause, so a bird needing repeated trims needs a work-up. Never trim a beak yourself: it carries a blood supply and nerves.

Feather plucking & behavioral health

Rule out the medical causes first. Most owners work this list backwards.

Feather destructive behavior is a symptom with a long list of causes, and the mistake that costs the most time is treating it as a boredom problem from the start. Budgies pluck less often than the larger parrots do, so a plucking budgie deserves a proper look rather than another toy. Work the list in this order.

CheckWhat it looks like, and what to do

What the enrichment research actually supports Work on captive parrots links consistent environmental enrichment to less feather-destructive behavior compared with birds kept without it, and foraging opportunities specifically stand out as one of the higher-impact changes available. Other behavior worth reading

  • Beak grinding, on one foot, eyes half closed. Contentment, almost always just before sleep, and it worries every new owner exactly once.
  • Eye pinning, the pupils flashing wide and narrow. High arousal rather than happiness: it comes with aggression as readily as with play, so read the rest of the bird before putting a hand near the beak.
  • Pacing the same stretch of perch repeatedly. Usually a cage that is too small or too empty, sometimes frustration at something visible outside it.

  • Regurgitating onto a mirror, a toy or a person. Affection, in budgie terms, and a hormonal signal. Remove the object. Page 17.
  • Head bobbing and beak tapping in a cock. Courtship display, entirely normal, and a signal that the bird is in breeding condition.
  • Sudden fear of a person the bird liked. Something changed: glasses, a hat, a hair color, a nail color. Budgies are strongly visual and it usually resolves in days.

Molt & seasonal behavior

Normal, uncomfortable, and misread as illness roughly every time it happens.

A budgie replaces its feathers gradually rather than all at once, and a molting bird looks worse and behaves worse than a healthy bird should. Knowing what a normal molt looks like saves an unnecessary vet visit, and more usefully it stops an abnormal one from being dismissed.

Normal moltNot a molt

Helping through a molt

  • Bathe more often. Water softens the sheaths and makes preening them off easier. Page 7.
  • Feed properly. Feathers are protein, and a molt is the most expensive thing a budgie's body does. It is the worst moment to be on a seed diet.
  • Expect it more than once a year. Merck's picture is a full molt at least annually, with many birds also running a smaller partial molt about six months after the main one.
  • Handle less, and expect grumpiness. Pin feathers on the head are genuinely tender, and a bird that bites during a molt is not regressing.
  • Never pull a pin feather. They carry a blood supply while growing.
  • Keep the room warm and draft-free. A part-feathered bird loses heat faster.
A broken blood feather bleeds seriously A growing feather has a blood supply, and a snapped one can bleed enough to matter in a bird this size. Apply firm, steady pressure with clean gauze, and put styptic powder or cornstarch on the exposed broken tip of the shaft, never down into an open follicle, which can damage the follicle permanently. If it has not stopped within two to three minutes, call an avian vet. Do not pull the feather: VCA's current guidance is that pulling one at home is not recommended, because it leaves the shaft open and can cost more blood than it saves, and even in a clinic it is a last resort.

Seasonal behavior

Budgies breed opportunistically rather than to a fixed calendar, which is why an indoor bird under household lighting can come into condition at any time of year and often does. Expect chattier, more territorial, more restless behavior when a bird is in condition, and in hens a browner, thicker cere. It is normal biology. It only becomes a problem when the environment keeps a hen in that state, which is the entire subject of page 17. If seasonal behavior in your house is really year-round behavior, look at the light before anything else.

Reading droppings & hydration

The cheapest daily health check you have, and the reason the cage tray gets plain paper.

A bird dropping is three things at once, and separating them is what makes the check useful. The fecal part is the solid green or brown coil. The urates are the chalky white portion, uric acid, which is how a bird excretes nitrogen while conserving water. The urine is the clear liquid. Each of the three changes independently and each means something different, which is why "the droppings look wrong" is not a useful thing to tell a vet and "the urates have turned green" is.

Plain paper on the tray, changed every morning. Glance at it while you change it: number, size, and the three colors. That is the whole routine and it takes five seconds. A photograph travels to the vet; a description does not.

Hydration

A healthy budgie passes a dropping roughly every 20 to 30 minutes, and one noticeably larger dropping first thing in the morning is normal rather than a problem: it is the night's worth held over. Page 23.

Budgies come from an arid environment and are efficient with water, so dehydration is not obvious. Sunken eyes are late. Wet vegetables contribute real water, which is one more argument for the fresh share of the diet, and a bird that has stopped eating has usually stopped drinking too. Quarantine & adding a second bird

Thirty days of inconvenience against a disease that can take the whole household.

Every bird that enters your home is quarantined, with no exceptions for a bird that looks perfectly healthy, came from a good breeder, or is only visiting. Looking healthy is the specific thing this species is good at, and the two diseases that spread fastest between pet birds, psittacosis and polyomavirus, both have carriers with no signs at all. The public health guidance is to isolate newly acquired birds, including birds that have been to shows, exhibitions and fairs, for at least 30 days, and to test them before adding them to a group. Routine prophylactic antibiotic treatment is explicitly discouraged in that same guidance, because it can harm the bird and breed resistant strains: quarantine and a test, not a course of doxycycline just in case. Merck's figure for adding a bird to an aviary or an established multi-bird house is 90 days with testing, since an infected adult can shed polyomavirus intermittently for up to 90 days.

  3. Neutral ground first. A play stand or a table, never inside the resident bird's cage. A budgie defends its cage and will meet the same bird peaceably on a perch it does not own.
  4. Shared cage last, and supervised. Two food dishes, two water dishes and two of every favorite perch. Most resource fights are about there being one of something.

Power outages, travel & transport

For a bird, the danger in a blackout is usually what people light, not the cold.

A budgie does not depend on powered equipment the way a reptile or a fish does. There is no heater to fail and no filter to stall. What actually kills birds during an outage is the response: candles, kerosene and propane heaters, generators run too close to the house, a gas oven left open for warmth, and a fireplace lit in a room that has not been swept. Every one of those puts combustion products into the air of a small animal with an exceptionally efficient respiratory system.

During an outage No candles, no kerosene or propane heaters, no gas oven as a room heater. A generator runs outside and at least 20 feet from any door, window or vent, which is the CDC's figure; the CPSC rules out running one in or near an enclosed space, so an open garage door does not qualify. Keep a working carbon monoxide detector in the house. Use flashlights and battery lanterns. If the only available warmth is a combustion source, the bird goes to a friend's house or to a boarding facility instead, and that is not an overreaction.

Keeping a budgie warm without power

  • Move the cage to the smallest interior room, away from windows and exterior doors.
  • Cover the cage on three sides with blankets, leaving one side open for air. A covered cage holds the bird's own heat well.
  • Keep feeding. A 30 g bird burns through its reserves in hours, and a bird that is cold needs more food, not less. This is the opposite of the advice for a reptile.
  • Hot water bottles wrapped in towels, placed outside the cage, are safe. Chemical hand warmers are not: some release fumes, and none should be inside a cage a bird can chew.
  • A healthy budgie tolerates a cool house better than most owners expect. Draft and damp are worse than temperature.

Glossary

Terms used earlier in this book, in the sense they were used.
Blood featherA feather still growing, with a blood supply running into the shaft. Breaking one bleeds seriously in a bird this small
CereThe fleshy patch above the beak holding the nostrils. Blue in adult cocks, tan or brown in hens, pink or pale in juveniles
Chlamydia psittaciThe bacterium that causes psittacosis, or parrot fever. Infects people as well as birds
CropA storage pouch at the base of the neck where food waits before digestion. A crop that stops emptying is a serious sign
Dystrophic featherA feather that has grown abnormally: bent, clubbed, pinched at the base, or failing to emerge
French moultThe chronic feather form of avian polyomavirus in budgerigars, leaving a fledgling unable to fly
GoiterAn enlarged thyroid, from iodine deficiency. Presents as a breathing noise rather than a visible swelling
Hepatic lipidosisFatty liver disease. Fat accumulating in the liver, most often from a seed diet
KeelThe ridge of breastbone down the center of the chest, used to score body condition by feel
Knemidocoptes pilaeThe mite that causes scaly face and scaly leg in budgies
LipomaA benign fatty tumor, usually on the chest or belly, associated with high-fat diets
Macrorhabdus ornithogasterAvian gastric yeast, once called megabacteria. The classic cause of a budgie eating well and losing weight
MoltThe ordinary, gradual replacement of feathers. Symmetrical, with pin feathers rising before old ones drop
MonomorphicA species whose sexes look alike. Budgies are not: the cere sexes them, which is unusual among parrots
Pin featherA new feather still in its waxy sheath. Tender, and preened out by the bird or its partner
PolyuriaToo much liquid urine, with the solid fecal part still formed. Different from diarrhea, and often confused with it
PhotoperiodHours of light per day. The main lever on hormonal behavior and egg laying
PBFDPsittacine beak and feather disease, a circovirus causing progressive feather and beak damage
PTFEPolytetrafluoroethylene, the nonstick coating. Releases a gas lethal to birds at around 280°C (536°F)
PsittacineOf the parrot family. Budgies are small psittacines, not finches
QuarantineIsolating a new bird in a separate room, on separate equipment, for 30 to 45 days
SalpingohysterectomySurgical removal of a hen's reproductive tract. The definitive answer to repeated egg binding
StridorA harsh breathing noise caused by narrowed airway. In budgies, classically goiter
UratesThe chalky white part of a dropping: uric acid, how a bird excretes nitrogen while conserving water
XanthomaA yellow, thickened, cholesterol-laden patch of skin. Another sign of a high-fat diet
