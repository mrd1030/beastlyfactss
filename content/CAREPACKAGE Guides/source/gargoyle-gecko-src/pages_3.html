Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Shedding, seasonal slowdown & behavior

Most of what worries a new owner is normal. This page sorts the normal from the worth-a-look.

The shed cycle

Reptiles shed their outer skin, a process called ecdysis, to grow and to clear old, damaged skin. Juveniles shed far more often than adults because they are growing faster, and there is no universal schedule. What matters is whether each shed comes off cleanly and completely, especially on the toes and the tail tip. The gecko often skips a meal or two in the days before a shed. Humidity is the biggest single factor in a clean shed, and rough surfaces such as cork and textured branches give it something to rub against.

Seasonal slowdown, not brumation

Some reptiles go through brumation, a true winter dormancy. Gargoyle geckos show something lighter: for a few weeks, usually in winter, they eat less, climb less and hide more. It needs no deliberate cooling. Weigh through it. A slowdown with a steady weight is a season; a slowdown with a falling weight is a health problem, page {{P:redflags}}.

Color, activity and the breeding season

Wild-type gargoyles are light to dark gray with patches of orange or red, in reticulated or striped patterns, and selectively bred lines run from saturated red to white and black. They are sleepier by day and active after dark. Wild gargoyles live surprisingly low for a climbing gecko, mostly within a few meters of the ground, and come down to the forest floor to hide or to cross between plants. Males often go off food at the start of the breeding season.

What helps most

The evidence comes from the gargoyle's closest studied relatives. A study comparing two nocturnal (night-active) gecko species found that crested geckos which engaged with new objects sooner, and for longer, had lower baseline levels of stress hormones. That is a link, not proof that novelty calms a gecko, and it is still the best steer available for this group. In leopard geckos, five kinds of enrichment were tested and ranked: temperature and feeding ranked highest, visual enrichment lowest.

Priority order

1. Vertical structure it can genuinely climb
2. Cover available at every height
3. Humidity and live planting
4. Feeding both ways: powder and hunted insects
5. Feeding spots placed so it has to climb
6. Occasional novelty, last
Feed both halves Put the powder in shallow dishes at different heights, so getting to food means climbing. Then release live insects into the planting, which is the part a dish cannot do: hunting is what gets removed when everything arrives in a bowl. A new piece of cork or a moved branch now and then is cheap and reasonable.
Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a gecko, quarantine & the law

The healthiest gecko is chosen well, kept apart for months and seen by a vet early.

Choosing one

Every gargoyle for sale should be captive-bred. Once among the rarest lizards in captivity, the species is now bred in large numbers, so an animal offered as wild-caught, or with no breeder history at all, is a reason to walk away. Color and pattern set the price, not sex or personality: reticulated gray, black and white is the cheapest, striped and red- or orange-striped run higher, and saturated "super red" or selectively bred white and black lines reach $500 to $1,000 or more. Look the gecko over in good light before paying.

CheckHealthyWalk away or ask more
EyesClear and alertSwollen, sunken, cloudy or crusted
Nose, breathingDry nostrils, quiet breathingBubbles, mucus, wheezing, open-mouth breathing
VentCleanStained, pasted with droppings, or swollen
BodyRounded body, a full tail baseHips, spine or ribs showing; a thin tail
Skin, shedIntact, no old shedWounds, or rings of old shed on toes or tail tip
MitesNoneTiny dark, red or orange specks, especially around the eyes

Ask four things: the hatch date, whether it was captive-bred, what it eats and when it last ate, and whether it has been treated for anything. A good breeder answers without looking it up.

Quarantine: three to six months

If you keep any other reptile, the new gecko is kept apart for 3 to 6 months; 60 to 90 days is only the floor. A separate room, not a second tank on the same shelf. Paper towel substrate, so mites, droppings and regurgitated food are visible. Its own dish, tongs and spray bottle. Care for the established animals first and this one last, washing your hands between. Book a first vet exam within 72 hours of bringing it home, and no later than two weeks, with a fecal exam (a microscope check of a fresh dropping for parasites). One clear fecal does not end quarantine. If anything flags, the clock restarts.

Where it is legal

Captive-bred gargoyle geckos are legal to keep in most of the United States, and this package carries no state-by-state table. A few rules that cover lizards as a class are worth knowing. Hawaii allows an animal only if it appears on its approved lists and bars anything never written down, so check whether this species is listed before buying there. The District of Columbia permits no lizard as a private pet. Some states tie their gecko clearances to a species' conservation status, and West Virginia requires an importation permit for any reptile brought in from outside the state. Your state wildlife agency is the answer that counts, and a city or county can be stricter than its state.

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Sexing, single housing & pairs

One gecko per enclosure, and a pair only when you mean to breed.

Telling male from female

A male grows a clear bulge at the base of the tail, just behind the vent (the single rear opening that droppings and eggs pass through), where the hemipenes, the paired male reproductive organs, sit. A female stays smooth below the vent. The bulge shows once a male reaches roughly 18 to 25 g, and that is when sex is confirmed. Femoral pores, a row of small pores in front of the vent, can be checked earlier using a jeweler's loupe (a small magnifier), but a young female can show a slight bulge and a pore reading on a juvenile is not always right.

MaleFemale
Tail baseA clear bulge behind the vent, from about 18 to 25 gSmooth
MaturityCan breed at about a year, if large enoughLater: around 40 g and 12 to 18 months
If bredCommonly loses its tail during matingLays eggs through the season

Why one per enclosure

  • Territorial. Males fight when housed together.
  • Tail-nipping. Gargoyles are known for nipping tails in shared enclosures, and juveniles kept together can nip and eat each other's tails.
  • Hatchlings get eaten. An adult can eat a hatchling.
  • Mating is rough. Rough enough that a breeding male with a tail at all is unusual. Pairing a small young male with a much larger female invites aggression.
  • Unlike the mourning gecko, a small gecko kept in colonies, gargoyles are not a colonial species. Solo is the safe default.

Before you house any two together

Both are sexed at a size where the bulge is clear, both have finished quarantine on page {{P:arrival}}, and you have a plan for the eggs and the hatchlings, which need their own tubs. Two females do not avoid the egg question: a lone female can still produce eggs.

Gargoyle or crested, side by side Gargoyle: regrows its tail, bite can break skin, climbs glass poorly and needs branches, juveniles jumpier, insects required. Crested: tail gone for good, bite rarely breaks skin, sticks to glass with ease, insects optional. Same islands, same powder, different animals.
Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Females, eggs & egg binding

A female kept alone can still produce eggs. This page is for every female owner, not only breeders.

Eggs without a male

A single female with no exposure to a male can still become gravid, carrying eggs. That is why most reptiles brought to a vet for egg binding are single females. A gravid female may refuse food and still be bright and active, which is normal; a female that is off food and also weak is not.

What a breeding female does

FactFigure
ClutchUsually 2 eggs, one from each ovary
Clutches a season4 to 5, about 4.5 to 6 weeks apart
Where she laysShe buries them, so she needs a lidded laying box of damp substrate with an entry hole cut in the top
IncubationRoom temperature around 70°F (21°C) with no added heat; hatching about 70 to 90 days after laying
HatchlingsSmall tubs of their own, with a humid hide and climbing foliage. Never back in with adults
Laying yearsFemales produce eggs for about 6 years

Egg binding: when she cannot lay

Cause: egg binding, called dystocia, is a female unable to pass her eggs. Poor husbandry is often behind it: no suitable place to lay, wrong temperatures, humidity or lighting, too little calcium. Age, injury, infection and oversized or misshapen eggs also play a part. Signs: a gravid female that has stopped eating and is weak, with a swollen belly. Many cases progress to severe lethargy, and the gecko can become unresponsive if it is not treated early. Unlike in mammals and birds, it is rarely sudden: eggs can be held for weeks or even months past the normal laying time. Diagnosis: a reptile vet, by feeling the belly and with x-rays and blood tests that tell egg binding apart from a normal pregnancy. Treatment: fluids, calcium and hormone injections at doses the vet sets, a quiet, private nest site, and in many cases surgery to remove the eggs. For a single female, the vet may suggest spaying, surgically removing the ovaries. Recovery: early treatment improves the outcome; waiting makes surgery more likely.

Same-day call A female heavy with eggs that is off food and weak, straining with nothing produced, or with any tissue showing at the vent. Do not wait to see whether she lays overnight.
Prevention Correct temperatures, humidity and UVB, enough calcium in the diet, water every day, and a laying box in place before she needs it. Weigh a laying female before and after each clutch, and raise clutch after clutch with the vet rather than accepting it as normal.
Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Health red flags & finding a vet

Most of this section traces back to two husbandry errors: humidity that never cycles, held too wet or too dry around the clock, or an enclosure without enough places to hide.
Call a reptile vet today if The air has passed 86°F (30°C) and the gecko is weak, limp or unresponsive. Open-mouth breathing, mucus or bubbles at the nose, clicking or wheezing. A soft jaw, a kinked or wavy tail, curved limbs, or tremors. A gravid female off food and weak. Any tissue showing at the vent. A toe that has turned dark or is tightly ringed with old shed. No water access for 2 to 3 days.
Book this week if Weight falling across several weigh-ins, or a thinning tail. Not eating for about two weeks with weight loss, or about a week alongside any other sign. Loose, smelly droppings more than once. A tail still bending over the back after you have added branches and hides. Film or swelling over the eyes. Yellow, orange or gritty urates.

Finding the right vet

Not every practice treats reptiles. The ARAV (Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians) runs a find-a-vet directory, and the vet should at least be a member. When you call, ask how many reptiles the practice sees, whether it runs fecal exams in house, and who covers reptile emergencies after hours. A routine exotic exam runs about $50 to $150; an illness visit with tests, $150 to $600; imaging or surgery, $800 to $2,500 or more. These are general exotic reptile figures.

Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Metabolic bone disease

A calcium problem, and one this species may develop earlier than a crested gecko.

Metabolic bone disease, MBD for short, is the everyday name for nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism: when the diet runs short of usable calcium, the body pulls calcium out of the skeleton to keep the blood level up, and the bones soften and bend. Gargoyle geckos may be more prone to early MBD than crested geckos, which is why the supplement routine on page {{P:insects}} matters so much.

MBD
CauseToo little calcium or vitamin D3, often with no UVB. In practice: powder that is served but not eaten, insects fed without dusting or gut-loading, a calcium-to-phosphorus balance tipped the wrong way, or a UVB tube long past its date. Too cool an enclosure plays a part too
SignsA kinked or wavy tail, a soft or rubbery jaw, curved limbs, tremors or twitching, loss of coordination, lethargy and appetite loss. Advanced cases bring a swollen or distorted jaw, broken bones and a cloacal prolapse (tissue pushed out of the vent).
DiagnosisA vet: blood tests for calcium and vitamin D3, x-rays to show how dense the bones are, and a look at the calcium sacs at the back of the roof of the mouth. Bring the supplement jars and the UVB tube's install date
TreatmentCorrecting the diet and husbandry is the core of it, with fluids, nutritional support and calcium as the vet directs. Any calcium or vitamin D3 dosing during treatment is set by the vet
RecoveryEarly cases often resolve within months once diet and supplements are corrected, and the outlook is best while the gecko is still eating. Deformities already present are usually permanent
A soft jaw is a vet visit, not a supplement change A jaw that feels soft, any tremor, or a gecko that has stopped climbing when it used to is an appointment this week rather than something to watch. Meanwhile lower the highest perches so a weak gecko cannot fall from height.

Prevention is three habits

  • A complete powder that is actually eaten, not just served. Check the dish, page {{P:powder}}.
  • Insects gut-loaded and dusted, plain calcium on most feedings and D3 periodically.
  • Low-level UVB, replaced on schedule, page {{P:uvb}}.
More is not safer Vitamin D3 builds up in the body, and the powder already carries it. Dusting D3 on every insect, or stirring supplements into the powder, does not prevent bone disease any better, and a vet should set any change while a gecko is being treated.

Floppy tail syndrome & tail loss

Two very different tail problems. One is caused by the enclosure; the other is a defense.
Floppy tailFloppy tail syndrome (FTS)
CauseRepeatedly sleeping hanging upside down on bare glass, which over time deforms the base of the tail. A gargoyle grips glass poorly, and in a sparse enclosure the glass is all it has
SignsThe tail hangs over the gecko's back, or flops to one side, when it rests upside down
Why it mattersOver time it can deform the base of the tail and the hips, which can make it hard for the gecko to pass droppings or, in a female, lay eggs
DiagnosisA reptile vet confirms it, and checks for a calcium problem behind it, since bone disease also affects the tail
TreatmentAlmost entirely the enclosure: enough branches, cork and hides that the gecko stops choosing the glass. Severe cases may need the vet to remove the tail to stop the spine and hips deforming further
RecoveryCaught early, clutter stops it getting worse. A tail that stays bent after you have added hides and branches is a vet visit
The prevention test Look at where the gecko sleeps. If it is in a cork tube, behind a leaf or on a branch, the enclosure is doing its job. If it is flat on the glass, upside down, night after night, it needs more clutter at the top, today. Page {{P:substrate}}.

Tail loss, and the tail that grows back

Tail dropAutotomy
CauseA defense, not a disease. The gecko drops its tail if grabbed by it, badly startled, restrained roughly, or in a fight. Mating is rough enough that breeding males often have no tail at all
SignsA dropped tail and a break at the tail base
ResponsePut the gecko back in a clean, quiet enclosure and leave it alone. A stump that swells, oozes or smells goes to the vet
RecoveryUnlike a crested gecko, a gargoyle regrows its tail. The new one is prehensile, with cartilage rather than bone, and somewhat uneven scales, and it works
Do not confuse the two A dropped tail is a stress response with a good outcome. A bent tail is a husbandry problem with a slow, poor outcome if nothing changes. The first needs calmer handling; the second needs more branches.

Stuck shed, toe loss & respiratory infection

The two opposite humidity mistakes. Too dry costs toes; too wet costs lungs.
Stuck shedDysecdysis (a bad shed), and toe or tail-tip loss
CauseMostly low humidity: the most common gargoyle husbandry failure. Mites, poor nutrition, illness and nothing rough to rub against can also cause it
SignsRings or patches of old skin left on the toes or the tail tip after a shed. Left on, the ring tightens like a tourniquet and cuts off blood flow until the toe dies and drops off
ResponseEarly stuck shed often softens with a gentle warm soak: shallow, chin-deep water at enclosure temperature for about 30 minutes, supervised the whole time, then rub it off gently with a damp cotton swab. Never peel dry shed. A tight band you cannot shift, or a toe that has turned dark, goes to the vet
RecoveryA lost toe is gone for good. Prevention is the daily wet-dry cycle on page {{P:humidity}}, with a heavy evening mist, not a permanently damp tank
RespiratoryRespiratory infection
CauseAn enclosure kept constantly wet with poor ventilation, the opposite mistake from stuck shed. Too cool a setup, dirty conditions and other illness add to the risk
SignsLabored or open-mouth breathing, mucus or bubbles around the nose, a clicking or wheezing sound, lethargy and appetite loss
DiagnosisA vet, who may take a sample to grow and identify the bacteria so the right antibiotic is chosen
TreatmentAntibiotics prescribed by the vet, with the gecko kept toward the warm part of its normal range and the husbandry fixed at the same time: humidity dropping back between mistings, and real airflow
RecoveryIt can be fatal if untreated, and a long, untreated case can spread into the blood. Fixing the humidity and airflow is part of every recovery
Breathing trouble is a same-day call Open-mouth breathing, bubbles at the nose or a clicking sound is a vet call today, not a wait-and-see. Do not raise the heat past the normal basking range to "help": this species' ceiling is 86°F (30°C) whatever is wrong.
One hygrometer, two problems prevented Both conditions are settings, not bad luck. Read the hygrometer an hour after the evening mist and again the next afternoon. A high reading that never drops points at respiratory infection; a low reading that never climbs points at stuck shed.
Section 06 · Health & Common Issues

Overheating, dehydration & reading droppings

The emergency this gecko's low ceiling creates, and the free health check in the droppings.

Overheating

Air above 86°F (30°C) is dangerous for this species. The usual causes are sun on the glass, a bulb with no thermostat or a thermostat that has failed, and a room that got away from you in a heat wave. Act on the thermometer rather than waiting for the gecko to look unwell.

If the reading is over 86°F (30°C)Do this
FirstTurn the heat source off and get the enclosure out of any sun
ThenCool the room: shade the window, open the screen top fully, run a fan across the room rather than into the enclosure. Mist the surfaces so there are droplets to drink
If the gecko is weak, limp or unresponsiveMove it in a ventilated tub with a damp paper towel to the coolest room, and call the vet now
AfterwardFind what did it, and fix that before the heat goes back on. Page {{P:outage}} has the heat-wave plan

Dehydration

Signs of dehydration in a reptile include loose skin and sunken eyes. Because this gecko drinks mostly from misted surfaces, a missed evening mist matters more than a stale dish. Correct the misting and check the hygrometer. A gecko whose skin stays loose once the cycle is right needs a vet, who can give fluids by injection.

Reading the droppings

A reptile passes solid stool and urates together: the urate is the white, chalky paste of uric acid, the reptile version of urine.

Parasites, blockages, mouth rot & more

Less common, and still worth recognizing early.
ParasitesInternal parasites
CauseWorms and protozoa (single-celled parasites), most often arriving with a new animal
SignsOften none at first. Then weight loss despite eating, repeated loose or smelly droppings, and a gecko that fails to thrive
DiagnosisA fecal exam on a fresh sample. That is why every new gecko gets one inside quarantine, page {{P:arrival}}
Treatment and recoveryMedication chosen and dosed by the vet, treated only when the exam finds a real burden, with daily cleaning of droppings while it works

Cryptosporidiosis, a protozoal gut infection known in other geckos, causes marked weight loss and slow wasting, is diagnosed with a special stain on fresh droppings, and has no reliably effective treatment. It is the strongest argument for the full quarantine window.

BlockageImpaction
CauseA gut blockage, linked to swallowing substrate along with food. The risk sits with sand, wood chips and gravel rather than with soil or coconut fiber, and a climbing gecko that lives off the floor is at lower risk than a ground-dweller
SignsStraining, a swollen belly, or no droppings for longer than its normal pattern, with appetite loss
Response and recoveryA vet. Switch to paper towel meanwhile. Recovery depends on what is blocking and how it is cleared
Mouth rotInfectious stomatitis
CauseA bacterial infection of the mouth and gums
SignsTiny purplish-red spots inside the mouth at first, then cheesy material building up along the rows of teeth. Mouth sores often come with appetite loss
Response and recoveryA vet visit, without waiting: severe cases can spread into the jawbone
ProlapseTissue pushed out of the vent
CauseThe cloaca (the shared chamber behind the vent), the colon, the oviduct (egg tube) in a female or a hemipenis in a male can be pushed out. Causes include egg binding, rough mating, infection, bone disease and straining
SignsTissue showing at the vent that does not go back in
Response and recoveryA same-day vet call. Keep the gecko on clean paper towel so the tissue picks up no substrate. Outcome follows the cause and how fast it is treated
Mites, and why paper towel helps Lizard mites show as tiny orange, red or dark specks, often around the eyes, and are hard to see in small numbers. For any gecko under watch, plain paper towel shows every mite, dropping and leftover insect, and lets you tell a vet exactly what came out and when.
