How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

If you haven't bought a gecko yet

Read pages {{P:profile}} through {{P:cleaning}} in order before buying anything, then the feeding section. Two decisions are the expensive ones to get wrong. The first is the enclosure on page {{P:enclosure}}: it has to be tall, and it has to be packed with branches, because this gecko grips branches better than glass and sleeping on bare glass is what bends its tail. The second is the heat on page {{P:temperature}}: a gentle warm spot, and a room that never pushes the air past 86°F (30°C). Settle both, then use the Budget and Shopping List on page {{P:budget}} to buy once instead of twice.

If your gecko is already home

Start with the Setup Checklist on page {{P:checklist}} to audit what you have. Then read the humidity cycle on page {{P:humidity}}: an enclosure that never dries out causes respiratory infection, and one that stays too dry causes the stuck shed that costs this species its toes. Then weigh the gecko and start the log on page {{P:ownerlog}}, because a falling weight is usually the first sign of trouble. If anything already looks wrong, go straight to Health Red Flags on page {{P:redflags}}.

Why this package exists The gargoyle gecko is usually sold as "a crested gecko with horns", and the two do share an island, a powdered diet and a tall enclosure. The differences are what catch new keepers out. This gecko needs live insects as a real part of its diet, not a treat. It grips branches better than glass. Its tail grows back, and its bite can break skin. And the most repeated advice about it, that it needs no heat at all, is wrong: it needs a gentle warm spot, with a ceiling it must never pass. Every figure here is cross-checked against the Beastly Facts guides and the veterinary and species sources listed on page {{P:sources}}, and every temperature and dimension is given in both imperial and metric.

Cost overview

Initial setup

The gecko is $50 to $300 for a common reticulated (net-patterned) or striped animal, $500 to $1,000 or more for a premium color line. Itemized with the heat lamp and thermostat, the equipment comes to $301 to $553. With the gecko, food, supplements and a first vet exam, plan on $436 to $1,048. Page {{P:budget}} has every line.

Ongoing

Diet, insects, supplements, the UVB tube, electricity and a yearly checkup run $245 to $500 a year. Food alone is about $10 to $25 a month. An illness visit with tests runs $150 to $600.

The number that should drive the decision Not the price. The lifespan. A gecko bought for a ten-year-old is very likely still alive when that child is in their late twenties. At $245 to $500 a year for 15 to 20 years, the routine care alone comes to roughly $3,675 to $10,000, before a single emergency.
Section 02 · Housing & Environment

The vertical enclosure & where it goes

A climbing gecko measures its home in height, and the height only counts once it is filled.

18×18×24 in (46×46×61 cm) is the minimum for one adult, with the 24 in as height, and 24×24×24 in (61×61×61 cm) is the ideal. Vertical space matters more than floor space for an arboreal species. Hatchlings and juveniles under about 12 g do better in a smaller grow-out enclosure for a while, where they find food easily and you can find them. A screen top and real airflow matter as much as the size: the enclosure should dry out within about 24 hours after misting.

One gecko per enclosure. Gargoyle geckos are territorial, they can eat hatchlings, and they are known for nipping each other's tails. Page {{P:sexing}} covers why pairs are a breeding decision, not company.

Where the enclosure goes

Away from sunny windowsRoom heat is what most often pushes this gecko past its 86°F (30°C) ceiling, and a glass box in sun traps it. A summer heat wave is a bigger risk than a cold snap.
Off radiators, heat vents and draftsEach one swings the temperature and dries the air, which fights the humidity cycle on page {{P:humidity}}
Not in the kitchenReptiles carry Salmonella, a germ that gives people food-poisoning-type illness, without looking sick, and the hygiene rules on page {{P:cleaning}} start with keeping them away from food

The two bold lines are limits, not targets. Air above 86°F (30°C) is dangerous for this species, a lower ceiling than almost any other lizard in the hobby.

Why it still needs heat

"Gargoyle geckos need no heat" is the most repeated mistake about this species, and it is wrong. A gecko is ectothermic: its body runs at the temperature of its surroundings, and it warms or cools itself by moving between warmer and cooler spots. A room that suits people gives it no gradient to choose from. A gecko kept too cool also digests poorly, and a warm side that runs cold is a common, fixable reason it stops eating. What is different about this species is not the need for heat but how little room there is above the basking spot.

The heat source

PartHow it is used
Low-wattage bulbA low-wattage basking bulb or a ceramic heat emitter (a bulb-shaped heater that gives heat and no light), in a dome over one side of the screen top, outside the enclosure, so the top branches reach the basking range
ThermostatThe bulb runs through one, so a hot day or a bulb that is too strong cannot push the enclosure past the ceiling. Its probe goes at gecko level, on or beside the top basking branch
Two readingsDigital, one high at the basking spot and one low at the cool end. Check both against each other daily: a thermostat stuck on, or a probe that slipped and reads room air, is the failure that matters
Watch the room, not just the bulb In summer the room itself can do the heating. If the enclosure reads above 82°F (28°C) with the bulb off, the bulb stays off, and page {{P:outage}} has the heat-wave plan. Never leave a dome lamp running on a timer alone with no thermostat behind it.

The humidity cycle, misting & airflow

Most of this gecko's health problems come from getting this page wrong in one direction or the other.

Aim for 50 to 70% on average. Mist heavily in the evening, as the gecko wakes, and then let the enclosure dry back toward 50% before misting again. A heavy evening mist can briefly push humidity to 80 to 100%, and a light mist in the morning is optional. The swing matters more than hitting an exact number. An enclosure that never dries causes respiratory infection; one that stays too dry causes stuck shed, which can strangle a toe.

Water: droplets first, a dish as well

Gargoyle geckos mostly drink droplets off leaves and walls rather than from a dish, so the evening mist is the main drink of the day. A shallow dish of fresh, dechlorinated water (tap water treated to remove its chlorine) still goes in, emptied and refilled daily. Water runs on a short clock: no gecko should go more than 2 to 3 days without water, whatever its appetite.

Holding the cycle in practice

  • Read it twice. A digital hygrometer (humidity meter) mid-height, away from where you spray: once an hour after the evening mist, and again the next afternoon. Those two numbers are the cycle.
  • Mist the surfaces, not the gecko. Leaves, cork and glass, until they bead.
  • Never dries back? More airflow, less misting, and look for soggy substrate. Condensation all day, a musty smell or mold are the signs.
  • Too dry by afternoon? More live plants, or cover a small part of the screen. Change one thing, then wait two days to judge it.
Ventilation is half of humidity Veterinary husbandry guidance warns that cutting ventilation to hold in humidity or heat frequently causes skin and respiratory disease. A screen top is not a flaw to be covered up; it is how the enclosure dries out within about 24 hours after misting.

UVB light & the day length

Survivable without, because the diet carries vitamin D3. Better with, at a low level.

A gargoyle gecko can survive without UVB, because a complete powdered diet supplies vitamin D3, the vitamin a gecko needs to absorb and use calcium. Low-level UVB still brings real digestive and immune benefits, and lack of UVB is one of the listed causes of metabolic bone disease. This is a gecko that spends its days in leaf shade, so the right tube is a weak one.

SettingNumber
TubeA 12 in (30 cm) low-output T5 HO tube, such as a 5.0 rating. T5 is the slim fluorescent tube size, 5/8 inch across, and HO means high output, the tube format; the low rating is what keeps it gentle. In a reflector fixture across the top
Strength at the geckoA UV index (UVI), the standard scale for UVB strength, of roughly 1.0 to 2.0 at the basking area, if you own a UV meter. Fluorescent UVB has to sit fairly close to work: within about 12 in (30 cm) of where the gecko basks
Day length12 hours on, 12 hours off, on a timer. No light overnight, colored night bulbs included
Replace the tubeEvery 6 to 12 months, on the maker's schedule, whether or not it still lights. The budget on page {{P:budget}} assumes once a year
Glass and plastic block it Most glass and clear plastic filter out UVB, so a tube sitting on a glass lid, or an enclosure near a sunny window, delivers far less than it seems to. Mount the fixture directly over open mesh. A window is never the UVB source: through glass it is only a heat trap, and heat is this gecko's real risk.

The tube that still glows

A UVB tube keeps producing visible light long after its UVB output has dropped. That is why it is replaced on a date rather than when it looks tired, and why the date it went in belongs on the equipment log on page {{P:equiplog}}.

UVB and the supplement jar are one decision

The complete diet powder already carries calcium and vitamin D3, and a gecko under a working tube also makes its own D3. So the calcium dusted onto insects is mostly plain calcium without D3, with a D3 product only periodically. Vitamin D3 builds up in the body rather than washing out, so more is not safer. The schedule is on page {{P:insects}}.

Light needs shade beside it A gecko that sleeps by day needs somewhere to get out of the light. Broad leaves and cork are what make a lit enclosure livable, and a gecko that is always fully exposed under the tube needs more cover, not less light.

Substrate, clutter & furnishings

Sparse decor is the wrong instinct for this species. Clutter is health care.

Substrate

ChoiceVerdictNotes
Coconut fiberGoodHolds humidity well. Loose substrate with no cleanup crew is spot cleaned daily and replaced monthly
Bioactive soil mixGoodA living, planted floor over a drainage layer, with a cleanup crew of springtails and isopods (tiny soil animals and woodlice) that break down waste. The substrate stays in place
Paper towelSimpleBest for hatchlings, a sick gecko and quarantine: easy to clean and to check droppings on
Sand, wood chips, gravelNeverThe substrates that carry a real risk of impaction, a gut blockage from swallowed particles. Page {{P:minor}}

Soil and coconut fiber are a different case from sand and chips: this is a climbing species that spends most of its time off the floor, and soil-based and bioactive substrates are the standard recommendation for it.

Why clutter is not optional

Gargoyle geckos climb smooth glass noticeably worse than crested geckos do, so grippable branches matter more here. And a gecko with nowhere better to rest ends up sleeping hanging upside down on bare glass, which is linked to floppy tail syndrome, a tail that bends over the back and can deform the hips. Branches, vines and cork give it somewhere else to sleep. The test: can the gecko get from the floor to the top without touching glass?

Furnishings

  • Branches at every angle. Horizontal, diagonal and vertical, so it can climb, jump and rest at different heights. Anchor every one: a perch that shifts mid-jump is a fall.
  • Cork slabs and tubes. Vertical slabs, and tubes mounted high as well as low, so it can hide wherever it happens to be. A background with ledges turns dead wall into climbing surface.
  • Dense planting, live or artificial, at every level. Broad leaves and moss also hold droplets after misting.
  • The bottom third. Wild gargoyles spend time near and on the ground, so leaf litter, a low cork hollow and ground-level plants give it a reason to come down.
  • A feeding ledge mounted high and a shallow water dish low.
Every piece earns its place Each item should do at least one of three jobs: a place to hide, a place to climb or jump, or a surface that holds water droplets after misting. If the gecko is always in plain view, there is not enough in there.
