Section 03 · Feeding

Diet by age

Live insects, no formulated diet, and a schedule that matters much less than most keepers expect.

StageFeeding
Sling
under about 2 in (5 cm)		Every 2 to 3 days, on pinhead crickets, fruit flies or other tiny prey no larger than the sling's own abdomen.
Juvenile
about 2 to 4 in (5 to 10 cm)		Every 4 to 7 days at the young end of this stage, stretching to every 7 to 14 days as it grows. Growth slows here, so treat the number as a starting point and adjust it on abdomen size and appetite (page {{P:condition}}).
Subadult and adultCommonly every 1 to 2 weeks, but the real need depends on the species. A slow-metabolism rose hair may need only four or five crickets a month, while a fast-growing tropical species can eat several times a week.
Mature maleAfter his final molt, many eat markedly less or stop almost entirely, and some never eat again. A natural end-of-life pattern, not a health problem (page {{P:sexing}}).
Before and after a moltTake prey out well before a molt and offer none during it. Afterward, wait for the fangs to harden: 3 to 7 days for slings and juveniles, 7 to 14 days for adults (page {{P:molting2}}).
Two rules at every stage Prey is never larger than the tarantula's own abdomen, and anything uneaten comes out within 24 hours. A live cricket left in the enclosure can injure a freshly molted spider that cannot yet defend itself.

How a tarantula eats

It injects digestive enzymes through its fangs to liquefy the prey's insides outside its own body. A muscular sucking stomach then pumps the liquid meal into the gut, and the empty exoskeleton is left behind as a small pellet called a bolus. Offer prey live and whole, never chopped, and lift the bolus out with tongs when you see it. A single cricket can take days to fully digest.

How to offer it

Release, don't present. Put the prey in and let the spider hunt it. The ambush is what the animal is built for, and it is free enrichment.

Vary the timing, and log it. Feed on an irregular schedule, and write down what went in and whether it was taken (page {{P:ownerlog}}). In an animal that can fast for months, the log tells a normal fast from a decline.

Feeder insects & the never-feed list

Staples every feeding, extras for variety, rare treats, and the items that never go in.

PreyTierNotes
CricketsStapleThe usual default: high protein, low fat, easy to digest, and sold in every size from pinhead up.
Dubia or discoid roachesStapleA good alternative, especially for larger spiders, because they are more nutrient-dense and digest more slowly. Crush the head first on larger roaches, which can bite.
MealwormsExtraUseful as variety rather than a whole diet.
SuperwormsExtraCrush the head first. They bite, and a loose superworm is a hazard to a molting spider.
WaxwormsTreatFine occasionally. Too fatty for regular use.
Pinky mouseRareOnly for the largest species, and never necessary for a healthy diet.
Wild-caught insects of any kindNeverA pesticide and parasite risk that is not worth taking. Buy from a breeder or a pet store.
FirefliesNeverA hard no across tarantula keeping. The strongest toxicity evidence is in reptiles, and the precaution is consistent enough to follow.
Anything from a sprayed garden or lawnNeverInsecticide residue on a feeder reaches the spider with the meal.
Processed human foodNeverNot part of a tarantula's diet in any form.
Prey larger than the abdomenNeverThe size rule overrides curiosity, at every age.
Why wild-caught is out even from clean countryside A 2021 study by Brühl and colleagues trapped flying insects inside 21 nature conservation areas in Germany and found residues of 47 current-use pesticides, with each sample carrying 16.7 on average. Insects from protected land, not sprayed fields, still carried them.
The 24-hour rule, because it matters Anything not eaten within 24 hours comes out. Crickets and roaches will attack a spider that is molting or freshly molted and cannot defend itself. Keepers lose spiders to this every year, and a look in the enclosure the next day prevents it.
Section 03 · Feeding

Gut-loading, not supplements

The page a reptile keeper expects to find here is about calcium powder. For a tarantula it is about the feeder's last meal.

No dusting

Skip calcium and vitamin dusting entirely. That is a reptile practice, built for animals with bones, and a tarantula has none. Keepers broadly agree dusting has no place in a tarantula enclosure, and there are anecdotal reports linking it to bad molts. There is no strong scientific evidence that it causes harm, so treat this as a consistent precaution rather than a proven fact, and leave the powder out.

Gut-loading is the only route in

Gut-loading means feeding the feeder insects a nutritious diet for a day or two before they are offered, so that the nutrition reaches the spider. Whatever the cricket or roach has eaten is the whole nutritional content of the meal, which makes gut-loading the one lever a keeper has.

Feed the feedersNotes
Collard, mustard, turnip and dandelion greensCalcium-dense and low in the compounds that block calcium
Butternut squashSame group as the greens above
A dry diet sold on its calcium contentLook at the stated calcium, not a label that just says gut-load
Carrot and potatoKeep insects alive and hydrated, but add little else
Spinach, beet greens and Swiss chardSkip them: high in oxalic acid, which binds calcium so it can't be absorbed

Timing

Insects start emptying their gut once they are taken off the food. A cricket left in an empty container for a day has already lost much of what you fed it, so offer feeders within a few hours of taking them off the gut-load. If you keep a standing tub of crickets or a roach colony, leave the gut-load food in all the time.

Where the feeders come from

Commercially farmed feeders from a breeder or pet store only. A cricket from the garden can carry pesticide or parasites into an animal that has no way to deal with either (page {{P:prey}}).

In short No powder, ever. Feed the feeders well for a day or two, offer them within hours, and buy them farmed.
Section 03 · Feeding

Why a tarantula stops eating

The one pet where skipping meals for weeks is normal. Here is how to tell a healthy fast from a sick spider.

  1. Premolt fasting. By far the most common reason. A darkening, plumping abdomen and reduced activity come first. Slings might fast a few weeks, adults several months, with rare cases reported well past a year (page {{P:molting}}).
  2. Post-molt recovery. The new exoskeleton and fangs are soft: no food for 3 to 7 days for slings and juveniles, 7 to 14 days for adults.
  3. A mature male. After his final molt, many males eat little or nothing as they focus on finding a mate.
  4. A new enclosure or stress. A rehouse, a move, or a husbandry detail that is off (temperature, humidity, no hide) can put a spider off food for a stretch.
  5. The species. Rose hairs are known for long, seemingly unprompted fasts.
  6. Prey pickiness. Refusing one prey type, or prey that is too large, is not a health concern by itself.
  7. Illness, injury or dehydration. The one that matters. Appetite loss with not drinking, a visible injury, uncoordinated legs, or legs curled tightly under an upright body points to a real problem (page {{P:redflags}}).

An older spider refusing food with no premolt signs

Most of these spiders are healthy. Laboratory tarantulas have survived up to two years without food. Water is what kills quickly, so check in this order:

1. The abdomenPlump and round means reserves and time. Shrunken or wrinkled means dehydration, and water comes before anything else (page {{P:dehydration}}).
2. The water dishFull, clean, and shallow enough to reach. A spider seen drinking is not in trouble from the fast.
3. The mouthA white, sticky mass around the mouthparts, palps held tucked under the fangs, a wet underside from drooling, or long stretches by the water dish are the signs of oral nematodes (page {{P:parasites}}).
4. The sexA male just through his final molt often eats little or nothing from then on, and that is normal for him.
5. Recent changesA rehouse, a move or a new room explains weeks of refusal on its own.

If all five come back clean, keep the water dish full, offer one prey item every week or two, and take it out within 24 hours when it is ignored. Log each offer. A fast that ends in a molt shows plainly in the log, and so does a spider losing condition.

When to worry A juvenile refusing food for more than 6 to 8 weeks with weight loss or lethargy is worth a vet consult. Adults can safely fast for weeks to several months. At any age, refusal together with a shriveled abdomen, uncoordinated legs or a body it cannot lift is the emergency, and it can kill within days.
Section 03 · Feeding

Body condition & growth

The abdomen is the gauge, and the molt is where growth happens.

Reading the abdomen against the carapace

The carapace is the hard plate over the front half of the body. Compare the abdomen with it at its widest point, from above, and let that set the feeding schedule.

What you seeWhat to do
Abdomen thinner than the carapaceFeed more often
Abdomen about as wide as the carapaceKeep the schedule as it is
Abdomen wider than the carapaceFeed less often. A fat tarantula's real danger is its abdomen rupturing in a fall
Plump, darkening, with a bald patch on topPremolt: stop offering food (page {{P:molting}})
Shrunken and wrinkledDehydration, not hunger: water first, today

Photograph the abdomen from above while the spider is healthy, and keep the picture with the log. A spider that is already round gets fewer meals, not more.

Growth happens at the molt

An exoskeleton cannot stretch, so a tarantula grows only by shedding the whole thing and hardening a larger one. Growth comes in steps, one per molt, and the steps space out with age.

StageHow often it molts
Slings and juvenilesEvery few weeks to a couple of months
Mature adultAbout once a year
Very old femalesSome go several years between molts
Mature malesNever again after the molt that makes them mature

Measuring and logging

Leg span is the standard measure: diagonally from the tip of a front leg to the tip of the opposite back leg. Never handle the spider to measure it. Log each molt date and the size you can see, and keep the shed skin, which is also how a spider is sexed (page {{P:sexing}}). Over a female's life, that molt log becomes the record of the animal.
