Glossary, A to H

AbdomenThe rear, rounded section of the body, covered by thin cuticle. Its size is the feeding gauge, and its shape is the hydration gauge. Pages {{P:condition}}, {{P:dehydration}}.
ArborealTree-dwelling. An arboreal tarantula such as the pink toe needs a tall enclosure, vertical cork and strong cross-ventilation. Page {{P:arboreal}}.
BolusThe small pellet of empty exoskeleton left after a meal. Lift it out with tongs. Page {{P:diet}}.
Book lungsThe paired breathing organs on the underside of the abdomen. They take air straight from the room, which is why insecticide fumes reach a spider. Pages {{P:sexing}}, {{P:pesticides}}.
Captive-bredHatched from parents kept by people rather than taken from the wild. Less likely to carry oral nematodes. Page {{P:arrival}}.
CarapaceThe hard plate covering the top of the front half of the body. Mold on it is a serious sign. Page {{P:parasites}}.
CITESThe Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora: the treaty that controls trade in listed animals between countries. Its Appendix II lists species whose trade is regulated. Pages {{P:species}}, {{P:legal}}.
Cross-ventilationVentilation holes on opposite sides of an enclosure, so air moves through it instead of sitting. Pages {{P:humidity}}, {{P:arboreal}}.
Death curlLegs curled tightly under an upright body: a sign of advanced dehydration and an emergency. Not the same as a spider molting on its back. Page {{P:dehydration}}.
Deli cupA clear plastic food cup with a lid, used to house slings. Sold by volume in ounces. Page {{P:arboreal}}.
DKSDyskinetic syndrome: a poorly understood loss of motor coordination, often fatal, with no established treatment. Its signs overlap with pesticide poisoning. Page {{P:pesticides}}.
DramA unit of volume used to size small plastic vials for slings. Page {{P:arboreal}}.
ExuviaThe shed exoskeleton left after a molt, a hollow copy of the spider. The best way to sex it. Pages {{P:molting}}, {{P:sexing}}.
Gut-loadingFeeding the feeder insects well for a day or two before they are offered. The only route nutrition has into a tarantula, since dusting does not apply. Page {{P:gutload}}.
HardeningThe days after a molt while the new exoskeleton and fangs firm up: 3 to 7 days for slings and juveniles, 7 to 14 days for adults. No food, no handling. Page {{P:molting2}}.
HemolymphThe tarantula equivalent of blood. It flows freely through the body cavity rather than in vessels, so a ruptured abdomen is so often fatal. Page {{P:injury}}.
Reference

Glossary, I to Z

Leg spanThe standard measure of a tarantula: diagonally from the tip of a front leg to the tip of the opposite back leg. Enclosures are sized to it. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
Mature maleA male after his final molt, with bulbs on his palps and, in many species, hooks under the first legs. He never molts again and has a short time left. Page {{P:sexing}}.
MoltShedding the whole exoskeleton to grow. The molt itself takes 15 minutes to several hours. Page {{P:molting}}.
New World, Old WorldTarantulas from the Americas, and from Africa and Asia. New World species defend with urticating hairs; Old World species have none and rely on a stronger bite. This package covers New World species. Page {{P:species}}.
Oral nematodesMicroscopic parasitic worms around the mouthparts, mostly in wild-caught spiders, with no known treatment. Page {{P:parasites}}.
PalpsShort for pedipalps: the pair of short, leg-like feelers beside the fangs. A mature male's end in swollen bulbs. Page {{P:sexing}}.
PremoltThe weeks or months before a molt: refused food, a dark and swollen abdomen, a bald patch, heavy webbing. Page {{P:molting}}.
QuarantineKeeping a new spider apart from any other invertebrate for at least three months, with its own tools. Page {{P:arrival}}.
SlingA spiderling: a young tarantula under about 2 in (5 cm), housed in a vial or deli cup. Page {{P:arboreal}}.
SpermathecaeA female's sperm-storage organs. Their lining shows in her shed skin as a flap between the front book lungs. Page {{P:sexing}}.
TerrestrialGround-dwelling. A wide, low enclosure mostly full of substrate. Most species in this package. Page {{P:enclosure}}.
ThermostatA switch that holds a heater at a set temperature by reading a probe. Required on any heat mat. Page {{P:temperature}}.
Units°F, degrees Fahrenheit, and °C, degrees Celsius, for temperature; in (inches) and cm (centimeters) for size; % (percent) for humidity; drams and ounces for sling containers. Page {{P:budget}} lists prices in United States dollars.
Urticating hairsBarbed hairs a New World tarantula kicks off its abdomen at a threat. They irritate skin, eyes and airways. Pages {{P:handling}}, {{P:hairs}}.
USDA APHISThe United States Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service, which permits the movement of plant-feeding invertebrates between states.
USPSThe United States Postal Service, which does not accept spiders for mailing.
