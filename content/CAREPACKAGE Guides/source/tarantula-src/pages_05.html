Section 07 · Health & Common Issues

Red flags & finding a vet

Veterinary care for tarantulas is limited, so prevention does most of the work and knowing what is normal matters most.

Act today if you see A shriveled, wrinkled abdomen with lethargy · legs curled tightly under an upright body · a spider that cannot lift its body off the ground · clear fluid leaking from the abdomen or a joint, or a split abdomen · a molt stuck half on for hours with no progress · jerky, uncoordinated or twitching legs · a white, sticky mass around the mouthparts · a white or yellowish fuzzy plume on the spider itself · mites clustered on the spider · a juvenile off food more than 6 to 8 weeks with weight loss or lethargy. Check the water dish and humidity first, every time.

What to have ready for a vet or an experienced keeper

The species in Latin, and the spider's sex if known
The last molt date, and whether premolt signs came before this
What it last ate and when, from the Owner Log
Measured temperature and humidity, and the substrate
When the signs started, and anything that changed: a rehouse, a move, a spray used nearby
Clear photos of the spider from above and of anything unusual

Finding a vet

Many areas have no vet who treats invertebrates, so look before the first problem. Search for exotics practices, ask each one whether it sees tarantulas, and write the number on the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}}). Where no vet will, experienced keepers are the realistic second line. They are useful, but they are not a diagnosis. Treatment options for a tarantula are limited even with a vet, which is why the habits in this book carry so much weight.

The distinction that does the work A plump spider refusing food is almost always fine. A shriveled spider refusing food is an emergency. In this animal, hydration is the red flag, not hunger. Look at the abdomen before you look at the calendar.

Dehydration & reading the abdomen

The most common health problem in pet tarantulas, and almost always preventable with a dish of water.

CausesA missing or empty water dish, usually together with too much ventilation. A spider that has stopped eating also loses the water its prey carried. Tarantulas survive long fasts but die quickly without water.
SignsA shriveled, wrinkled, raisin-like abdomen; lethargy beyond the normal stillness; legs curling tightly underneath the body, sometimes called the death curl; and at the end, a spider that cannot lift its body off the ground. It can be fatal within days.
The fixA large, shallow, open dish of water straight away, where the spider can reach it. A dehydrated spider lowers the front of its body into the water and drinks for minutes or even hours. The abdomen starts to fill out within a few hours, and activity usually picks up overnight.
AfterwardCut back the ventilation a little and keep part of the substrate damp, so it cannot happen again. Mild cases often resolve once conditions are corrected.

Reading the abdomen

The abdomen is the fuel gauge for water and food at once. Learn what your spider's looks like when it is well, and photograph it.

AbdomenWhat it means
Plump, smooth and roundFed and hydrated. Compare its width with the carapace to set the feeding schedule (page {{P:condition}}).
Plump, darkening, with a bald patch on topPremolt. Normal. Stop offering food.
Shrunken, wrinkled, obviously undersizedDehydration. Water now.
Shriveled, with legs curling beneath the bodyAdvanced dehydration. Water now, and treat it as an emergency.
Two postures not to confuse Upside down with legs folded over is a spider molting: leave it alone. Upright with legs curled tightly underneath is the death curl: water immediately.
Prevention is one habit A shallow dish, kept full, changed daily, for every spider of every species, plus humidity in its range (page {{P:humidity}}). When a tarantula stops eating, check the water first.

Falls, injuries & stuck molts

The two physical emergencies, both mostly prevented by not touching the spider.

Falls and trauma

Why it's seriousThe abdomen is covered by a thin cuticle with none of the reinforcement of the legs or front body. Hemolymph, the tarantula equivalent of blood, is not held in vessels; it flows freely through the body cavity and bathes the organs, so even a moderate rupture can turn fatal quickly.
SignsA split or dented abdomen, or clear hemolymph leaking from a wound or a joint.
ResponseA ruptured abdomen or a visible leak is a real emergency. A minor leak can sometimes be stemmed with a small amount of cornstarch applied to the wound. Anything beyond minor bleeding needs specialist attention if you can find it, and how fast you act often decides the outcome.
Where falls happenMostly during attempted handling, and during rushed rehousing or a slide inside a car. A fall from waist height can rupture the abdomen.

Stuck and failed molts

A stuck or incomplete molt can be fatal, and prevention matters more than any response: humidity in the species' range through premolt, and no disturbing or feeding during the molt. The molt itself runs anywhere from 15 minutes to several hours, and a long, quiet stretch inside that window is normal.

Stuck looks likeA spider half in and half out of the old skin for hours with no progress, struggling that goes nowhere, or legs left twisted at the end.
ResponseCall an experienced keeper or an exotics vet. Never pull at the old skin yourself; an untrained attempt to help is how a survivable molt becomes a fatal one.
Usual causesToo little humidity in premolt, physical disturbance, or an underlying health problem.
After any injury Keep the water dish full and within easy reach, keep the enclosure quiet, and take out any live prey. A spider that cannot defend itself is a target for a cricket.
The prevention is two sentences long Keep a ground-dweller's enclosure low and mostly full of substrate, so there is nothing to fall from. Don't handle the spider, and move it only with a cup and card, low over a tub (page {{P:rehousing}}).

Oral nematodes, mites & mold

One serious parasite, and two problems that are mostly about cleaning and airflow.

Oral nematodes

Parasitic nematodes are microscopic worms that arrive mostly on wild-caught spiders and can spread through a whole collection. The spider behaves normally until late in the infection, which is why the signs matter:

  • A white, sticky mass around the mouthparts, the clearest sign
  • Palps held permanently tucked under the fangs
  • A wet underside from drooling, beyond normal cleaning
  • Long stretches beside the water dish, restlessness, or unusual amounts of silk
  • A sweet smell from the enclosure
  • Loss of appetite

Isolate the spider from any others at once, with its own tools, and contact an exotics vet if one can be found. No treatment is known to work. A confirmed case is usually euthanized before the worms can spread, which is why captive-bred spiders and a three-month quarantine matter (page {{P:arrival}}).

Mites

Most mites in an enclosure are harmless scavengers living on prey remains, and the fix is removing the remains and restoring ventilation. The ones that matter are on the spider: clustered around the mouth, the leg joints or the book lungs. That calls for a vet with invertebrate experience, never a chemical. Nearly every product sold to kill mites kills the spider too (page {{P:pesticides}}).

Mold

In the enclosureFuzzy growth on the substrate, on leftover prey, or in a damp corner. A husbandry problem: remove it with the substrate under it, cut back the misting, and improve airflow. Replace substrate that keeps molding.
On the spiderMore serious. A white or yellowish plume on the carapace, the underside of the abdomen, or the leg tips. Once it reaches the book lungs and the organs it is usually fatal. It hits spiders kept damp with poor airflow, especially burrowers that spend their lives out of sight.
ResponseCaught early: move the spider into a clean, dry, well-ventilated container with a water dish, away from the spores. The affected area is dabbed with a povidone iodine antiseptic.
Small flies are a clue Flies hovering over the enclosure mean something is rotting in it. Find the prey remains or the soaked corner, remove it, and the flies leave within a week.

DKS & pesticides

A poorly understood condition, and the household hazard that can kill a well-kept spider overnight.

Dyskinetic syndrome (DKS)

DKS, short for dyskinetic syndrome, is a poorly understood loss of motor coordination. It is often fatal, and there is no well-established treatment. Its signs, jerky or uncoordinated movement and tremoring legs, are also the signs of pesticide poisoning, so the first job is ruling out a pesticide source.

Why insecticide reaches a spider

A tarantula is an arthropod, and every insecticide sold for home use is built to kill arthropods. The safety comparison on the labels is insects against people and pets, and a tarantula is on the wrong side of that line. Direct testing on tarantulas barely exists, so this is a strong precaution drawn from how these products act on insect nerves rather than a measured dose. A spider's book lungs draw air straight from the room, and a lid does not keep fumes out.

Household routeWhy it matters
Aerosol bug spraysDrift and settling residue reach an enclosure even from the next room.
Total release foggers (bug bombs)Built to fill a whole room's air with insecticide. The worst of all.
Plug-in insect devicesRelease insecticide into the room's air for days or weeks.
Flea and tick treatment on a dog or catCan transfer to fur and furniture in the same room, especially before a spot-on dries.
Ant and roach baits, pest control visitsDrawn from the same insecticide classes.
Wild-caught feedersInsects from the outdoors can carry many residues (page {{P:prey}}).

If exposure happens

  • Move the spider and its enclosure away from the source at once, to a room with a door that closes, and open the windows.
  • Don't add water, dust or anything else to the enclosure to neutralize residue; no safe method is known.
  • Watch for uncoordinated movement, tremoring legs, an unusual posture, or no response to vibration.
  • Call an exotics vet if one is available, knowing the options are very limited.
Prevention is the only part that works Keep the enclosure in a room that is never sprayed, fogged or baited, with no plug-ins. Tell every pest control company in advance that you keep invertebrates, and ask for that room to be skipped. Treat dogs and cats for fleas in another room, and let spot-ons dry fully first. Move the spider out of the building for any building-wide treatment.

Urticating hairs, bites & your own safety

The most common real injury in tarantula keeping is not a bite. It is hairs in an eye.

Urticating hairs are tiny barbed hairs on the abdomen that a New World tarantula kicks off with its hind legs toward a threat. A rehouse is a common trigger, which is exactly when a keeper's face is closest. In a 2012 case report, Mangat and Newman showed under an electron microscope why these hairs are dangerous in an eye: they are barbed, so they migrate through the cornea and, in some cases, into the eye itself. Another published case, a keeper with a hair lodged deep in his cornea, needed steroid eye drops for nine months to control the inflammation, and recovered full vision.

WhereWhat to do
SkinPress tape onto the skin and lift it away, repeating until most of the hairs are out. Don't rub or scratch. If irritation continues or spreads, see a doctor.
EyesDo not rub. Cover the eye if you can without pressing on it, and get to an eye doctor or an emergency department the same day. No rinse is documented for an embedded hair, and early removal lowers the risk of lasting inflammation.
AirwayWheezing, throat tightness or real difficulty breathing after hair exposure is a same-day emergency room visit. Severe cases have needed airway management.

Bites

Bites are uncommon and rarely medically serious, roughly a hornet sting. There is no antidote, and the venom of the North American species typically causes only pain at the bite.

  • Put the spider down on a low surface before reacting. The reflexive drop kills more spiders than bites hurt people.
  • Wash the bite well with soap and water.
  • A cool compress and an over-the-counter pain reliever such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen manage the pain.
  • Check that the bitten person's tetanus shot is current.
  • A poison center can advise on any bite: 1-800-222-1222 in the United States.
  • Have the species name ready: "a tarantula" is not enough for a clinician.
Protect your eyes, every time Keep your face out of the open enclosure, and never lean over a spider that is already kicking hairs. Wear safety glasses for rehousing and full substrate changes, when hair-laden substrate and old molts get moved around. A dust mask is a sensible addition for anyone rehousing New World species regularly, with the room ventilated.
