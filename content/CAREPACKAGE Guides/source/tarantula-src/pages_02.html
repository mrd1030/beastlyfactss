Section 02 · Housing & Environment

Enclosure shape, size & where it goes

Most pet tarantulas are terrestrial, and their enclosure is horizontal, wider than it is tall, to limit how far the spider can fall. A reasonable adult minimum is about 20×10×10 in (51×25×25 cm), roughly a 10-gallon tank, sized to about three times the leg span. An adult rose hair spans about 6 in (15 cm) and a curly hair about 6.5 in (17 cm), which is why three times the leg span lands near that 20 in (51 cm) floor. Keep the overall height under about 12 in (30 cm).

The height rule is not fussiness. A short fall that most pets would shrug off can rupture a tarantula's abdomen and kill it, so everything about a terrestrial setup comes back to not letting the spider fall. Deep substrate is what takes the height away.

The lid

A secure lid, but never a mesh top: a tarantula's feet can catch in mesh and the spider can be badly hurt. Acrylic with drilled ventilation holes is the better choice. Holes must be small enough that the spider cannot fit through.

One spider per enclosure

Most species are solitary and cannibalistic. Cohabitation is not a workable setup at any enclosure size.

Where it goes

  • Out of direct sun. Glass and acrylic heat fast, and the spider cannot leave.
  • In a room that is never sprayed, fogged or treated for insects, with no plug-in insect devices (page {{P:pesticides}}).
  • At ordinary room temperature, which suits most tarantulas with no added heat (page {{P:temperature}}).
  • Off the floor and somewhere you will look at it. Watching is most of the husbandry.

Arboreal enclosures & housing a sling

Two setups that break the low-and-wide rule: a tree-dweller, and a spider too small for any adult enclosure.

The pink toe, a tree-dweller

A pink toe lives in trees, and its enclosure turns the terrestrial rules on their side. An adult Guyana pink toe reaches up to about 6 in (15 cm) across the legs. It gets a taller enclosure with vertical cork bark tubes and branches to anchor its silk retreat up high. Substrate fills about a third of the enclosure instead of half to two-thirds, leaving the height for climbing.

Cross-ventilationMore important here than for any ground-dweller. Holes on opposite sides keep air moving through, because stagnant, humid air is what harms this species. Airflow comes first, ahead of any humidity number.
HumidityAround 60 to 70%, from a large water dish on the floor, a smaller second dish up near the retreat, and an occasional light misting.
Temperature70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C), the same as a terrestrial.
Moving itBring the cup to the spider at its own level on the decor, or move it already sealed in a container it has retreated into (page {{P:rehousing}}).

Housing a sling

A sling, or spiderling, never goes in the adult enclosure. Most terrestrial species start out around 1/4 in (0.6 cm) across the legs, and in a big tank the sling and its prey never find each other, and nobody can tell whether it is eating or molting.

ContainerA 40-dram vial raises most slings to about a 1 in (2.5 cm) leg span. After that, a container two to three times the spider's leg span across. A 16-ounce deli cup with a mesh insect lid ventilates far better than a vial and holds more substrate: 12 to 16 ounces for terrestrial species, 32 ounces for arboreal ones.
SubstrateFill about halfway. Moisten it until it just clumps when squeezed in a fist; if water runs out, it is too wet. Err toward dry: even slings of humid-forest species die quickly in wet, stagnant containers. Poke a 1 in (2.5 cm) starter burrow near the side so the spider is visible.
WaterSlings are too small for a dish and get their water from prey, as long as the substrate never dries out completely. A 2-liter bottle cap becomes the first dish once the leg span matches it. No damp sponges, which breed bacteria.
HeatRoom temperature of 72 to 78°F (22 to 26°C) raises most species. Where extra heat is needed, keep the containers off the heat source and below 82°F (28°C).
Arboreal slingsA taller container only a quarter filled with substrate, with a sprig of artificial plant or a piece of bark to climb. A light weekly misting that dries within a day or two, never sprayed onto the spider.
Two sling rules Take out uneaten prey the next morning, since a live cricket can kill a sling that is fasting before a molt. Move a sling with a soft artist's paintbrush, never by hand.

Temperature, heat & light

The easiest part of tarantula keeping, and the one most often overcomplicated.

Room temperature is the target

Room temperature, roughly 70 to 80°F (21 to 27°C), is fine for most homes with no supplemental heat, and most tarantulas stay comfortable anywhere from 70 to 85°F (21 to 29°C). There is no basking spot and no hot and cool end to build, as a reptile needs. Slings do best at 72 to 78°F (22 to 26°C) (page {{P:arboreal}}).

If your home runs cold

Use a heat mat mounted on the side of the enclosure, never the bottom, and run it through a thermostat. A thermostat is a switch that holds the mat at a set temperature by reading a probe, a small sensor on a wire. Place the probe where your thermostat's manual says, hold it with a probe clip or a suction cup, never tape, and set the thermostat inside the range above. A heat source with no thermostat is one the spider cannot get away from.

Reading the numbers

A digital thermometer and hygrometer combination reads temperature and humidity at once, so you are reading numbers rather than guessing. Put it where the spider lives, low in the enclosure, and check it weekly.

Light

None is required. Most pet tarantulas are nocturnal, and bright light or direct sun is something to avoid rather than add. Ordinary room light on a normal day and night cycle is all they need. A tarantula has no use for the ultraviolet lamps a reptile depends on.

Never A heat mat under the enclosure floor, a heat source with no thermostat, a probe taped in place, or an enclosure in a sunny window.

Humidity, water & ventilation

A full water dish does most of the work, and airflow keeps damp from turning into mold.

Humidity by species

SpeciesTarget
Rose hair and other dry-adapted ground-dwellers40 to 60%, the low end of a published 40 to 75% range. Ventilation matters more than the number, and stagnant damp breeds mold.
Pink toeAround 60 to 70%, with strong cross-ventilation (page {{P:arboreal}}).

The steadiest way to hold it is a reliable water dish plus lightly moistened substrate, with a fine mist spray used sparingly. Overflowing the dish to dampen one corner gives a moist patch the spider can choose. Heavy misting mostly adds mold, with no clear benefit.

The water dish

Dehydration is the most common health problem Tarantulas survive long fasts but die quickly without water. A shallow dish, kept full, prevents most of it, for every species including the dry-adapted ones (page {{P:dehydration}}).
  • Shallow. A deep dish can drown a tarantula. A bottle cap for a small spider; a shallow dish for an adult.
  • Changed daily. Fresh water at all times. Substrate, silk and prey remains all end up in it.
  • No sponges. A damp sponge breeds bacteria.
  • Buried dishes. A spider that buries its dish is working the substrate. Dig it out, refill it, and leave the rest.
  • Slings drink from their prey and damp substrate until they are big enough for a cap.

Ventilation

Airflow is the counterweight to humidity, and it is what keeps mold and mites down. Holes on opposite sides of the enclosure, not only in the lid, move air across it instead of letting it sit. Too much is a problem too: too much ventilation together with a missing water dish is the usual cause of dehydration. If a spider dries out, a little less airflow and a damp patch of substrate are part of the fix.

The habit that prevents most of this book Look at the water dish every day and top it up. It takes seconds and prevents the leading cause of death in captive tarantulas.

Substrate, hides & furnishings

Deep enough to remove the fall height and hold a burrow, and furnished low.

Substrate

Coconut fiber, peat, or organic pesticide-free soil, or a mix. All three hold moisture well and support burrowing. Use at least 3 in (7.5 cm), filling roughly half to two-thirds of a terrestrial enclosure, because that depth is what takes away the fall height. A pink toe gets about a third (page {{P:arboreal}}).

Never sand Sand is the one substrate to leave out of a tarantula enclosure.

Many wild ground-dwelling tarantulas spend much of their time in burrows they dig themselves or take over from other animals. Depth lets a pet express that instinct even though it will never need to escape a predator. Species that are obligate burrowers need far more depth than any spider in this package, which is one reason they are not covered here.

A retreat

Every tarantula wants somewhere to be out of sight. Cork bark, a half-log, or half of a terracotta pot all work. Lay cork down as a starter burrow for a terrestrial, or stand it upright for an arboreal species to web a tube against.

Let it remodel

A tarantula that rearranges its substrate, webs up the entrance to its hide, or buries its water dish is doing the closest thing to construction this animal does. Don't tidy it back. An enclosure it can't alter is one less thing for it to do.

Low structure

Cork flats, leaf litter, a low branch, moss or sphagnum, all anchored so nothing can shift onto the spider. Keep it low for terrestrial species: structure to move through, not to climb up and fall from.

Terrestrial

Wide and low, substrate half to two-thirds full, cork laid flat. The rose hair, curly hair, Brazilian black, Chaco golden knee and red knee.

Arboreal

Tall, substrate about a third full, vertical cork and branches, strong cross-ventilation. The pink toe.

Cleaning, hygiene & escapes

An enclosure that never looks dirty still needs a routine, and the usual mistake is cleaning too much.

TaskHow oftenWhy
Uneaten live prey outWithin 24 hoursCrickets and roaches attack a molting or resting spider
Prey remains and droppings outAs seen, at least weeklyThe food source for every mite and mold problem
Water dish refilled with fresh waterDaily, kept fullStagnant water is where flies and mites start
Mold spot removed with the substrate under itAs seenCaught early, a spoonful of substrate; late, the whole enclosure
Substrate replacedEvery 6 to 12 months, or at the first moldLonger is fine for a dry, well-ventilated enclosure
Full strip-down and disinfectOnly after mites on the spider, a nematode case, or a deathOtherwise a disturbance with no benefit

A tarantula can sit on the same substrate for years. Every problem in the enclosure comes down to prey remains and dampness without airflow: take the remains out, keep air moving, and neither mites nor mold take hold. Small flies over the enclosure mean something is rotting in there. Find the remains or the soaked corner, remove it, and the flies leave within a week. Time any substrate change away from a molt (page {{P:molting}}).

Hygiene for you

Wash your hands after any enclosure work, and keep them away from your face and eyes until you have. Never share tongs, dishes or other tools between a new arrival in quarantine and any other animal (page {{P:arrival}}). Wear safety glasses for a full substrate change, when hair-laden substrate gets moved around (page {{P:hairs}}).

Escapes

Prevention. A sturdy, sealed enclosure with a locking lid, and ventilation holes too small for the spider, which for a sling means smaller than you would think. Most escapes happen during care, with the lid set aside for a moment.

The search. Ground-dwellers seek dark, warm, moist places: under furniture, dark corners, under the bed, piles of clothes or shoes. A pink toe climbs, so check walls, curtains and shelves. Within an hour or two it is almost certainly still in that room: close the door. Search at night with a flashlight, and set out a water dish or damp paper towel as bait.

Recapture is a clear cup set over the spider and a stiff card slid underneath, carried back upright. No hands, since a fall from waist height can rupture the abdomen.
