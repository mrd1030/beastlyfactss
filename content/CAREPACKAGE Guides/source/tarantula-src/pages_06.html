Emergency & quick targets

Act now if The abdomen is shriveled, wrinkled or raisin-like · the legs are curled tightly under an upright body · the spider cannot lift its body · clear fluid is leaking from the abdomen or a joint · it is stuck half out of a molt for hours with no progress · its legs move in a jerky, uncoordinated way · there is white material around the mouth · mold is growing on the spider. First 30 days

Most of this list is things not to do, which is a fair description of tarantula keeping.

Day 1

Move the spider from its shipping cup with the cup and card, low over a tub (page {{P:rehousing}})
Water dish full; lid on; leave it alone in a dark, quiet room. No food on day one
Ask the seller for the last molt and feeding dates and write them down
Fill in the emergency card (page {{P:emergency}})
Start quarantine if any other invertebrate lives here (page {{P:arrival}})

Week 1

Top up the water dish daily. That is the whole daily routine
Photograph the abdomen from above while it looks healthy (page {{P:condition}})
Read the thermometer and hygrometer, and fix anything out of range

Weeks 2 to 4

Offer one prey item of the right size; take it out within 24 hours if ignored. Refusal now is ordinary (page {{P:fasting}})
Start the Owner Log, one row per offer (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Leave the webbing and any rearranged substrate as the spider left it
Watch for a darkening abdomen and a bald patch: the first premolt may come within weeks (page {{P:molting}})

Days 31 to 90

Look at the abdomen from above and adjust the feeding interval
Keep the first shed skin and have it sexed (page {{P:sexing}})
Keep quarantine going until it has fed, drunk and settled, before sharing tools or a room
Fill in the pet-sitter sheet before you need it (page {{P:sitter}})

Daily, weekly & yearly routine

The shortest routine of any pet, and the daily part is the part that matters.

Daily

Look at the water dish, change it and top it up. This prevents the leading cause of death
Look at the spider: where it is, the shape of the abdomen, whether it moves normally
If prey went in yesterday, take out whatever is left

Weekly

Feed on the interval for the spider's stage, unless it is in premolt (page {{P:diet}})
Lift out boluses, prey remains and droppings with tongs
Read the thermometer and hygrometer
Log offers, refusals and anything noticed (page {{P:ownerlog}})
Check the abdomen's shape, and look for a bald patch

Monthly

Dampen a corner of the substrate if the enclosure has dried out
Check that the lid still locks and the ventilation holes are clear
Look for mold spots and remove them with the substrate under them

Every 6 to 12 months

Replace the substrate in a damp setup, or at the first mold, timed away from any molt; a dry, well-ventilated one can run for years (page {{P:cleaning}})
Check the enclosure is still at least three times the leg span

Every molt

Log the date, keep the shed skin, and have it sexed if you don't know yet (page {{P:moltlog}})

Always

No insect spray, fogger, plug-in or pet flea treatment in the spider's room (page {{P:pesticides}})
No direct sun on the enclosure in any season

Power outages, travel, transport & shipping

A tarantula needs nothing running, so an outage is a non-event in most homes. The real risk is in a car or a shipping box.

When the power goes out

Pet tarantulas are comfortable across roughly 70 to 85°F (21 to 29°C), which describes an ordinary room for most of the year. An outage only matters if the house turns cold in winter. Then put warm blankets over the enclosure, leaving the ventilation clear, and move it to the warmest interior room. Treat a multi-day cold snap, not the outage itself, as the emergency. Don't add a heat source with no thermostat, and don't feed while it is cold.

Leaving town

A healthy adult already fasts through every molt, so missed meals are not the constraint. Water is. Refill the dish with fresh water the morning you go, check that the lid is secure, and don't pre-feed extra insects, which only leaves live prey loose with nobody watching. Slings dry out faster and eat more often, so get someone in sooner for them.

Transport and moving

  • A secure, ventilated, escape-proof container, never a loose box or an open cup, lined with soft substrate or folded paper towel so it cannot slide on a bare surface.
  • Wedge it upright so it cannot tip or roll, and keep it in the passenger area with you, never in a trunk.
  • No hot or freezing parked car, even briefly. The temperature swing inside one happens faster than most people expect.
  • By hand, move slowly and low, over a bed or table, never over a hard floor.
  • Never move a spider in premolt, mid-molt or freshly molted if it can be postponed.
  • Moving house: set the enclosure up at the new place first, then move the spider in its travel container and rehouse it there.

Shipping

USPS, the United States Postal Service, will not carry a tarantula: its mailing standards make all spiders nonmailable. The hobby ships through other carriers' live-animal programs instead, each with its own packaging and timing rules, so check the carrier's current terms yourself. Two federal frameworks can apply by species: USDA APHIS (the United States Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service) permits for moving plant-feeding invertebrates between states, and the injurious wildlife list kept under the Lacey Act. State law adds its own layer. When buying, ask the seller how and when they ship and what their live-arrival policy is.

Please don't

  • Touch, handle, move or "help" the spider for any reason.
  • Open the enclosure to look closer, or lean your face over it. It can flick irritating hairs into your eyes. Take photos through the side instead.
  • Feed anything not written on this sheet.
  • Mist, add water anywhere but the dish, or change anything in the enclosure. Overwatering is a more common sitter mistake than underwatering.
  • Tidy the webbing, the rearranged soil or a buried water dish. That is the spider's own work.
  • Use any bug spray or flea treatment in this room.
  • Worry if it hasn't moved. Sitting still for days is normal.
