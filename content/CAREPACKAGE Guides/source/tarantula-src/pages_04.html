Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling: why the answer is no

If you want a pet you can hold, a tarantula is the wrong choice, and it is better to know that before buying one than after.

The risk to the spider

A fall from your hand, your shoulder or a couch cushion can fatally injure a tarantula. Even a short drop, the kind that would not worry you with almost any other pet, can rupture the abdomen, and the hemolymph inside is not held in vessels, so the damage does not stay local. There is no margin for error, which is the same reason the enclosure is built low and wide.

The risk to you

New World species, every one in this package, defend themselves by flicking urticating hairs: tiny barbed hairs that irritate skin, airways and eyes. Bites are uncommon and rarely medically serious, roughly a hornet sting. The bigger problem is what follows a bite: the handler jerks or drops the spider, which brings back the fall risk, and the spider that was only defending itself often dies. Hairs in an eye, not bites, are the more common real injury in this hobby (page {{P:hairs}}).

What interacting looks like instead

  • Watching. A tarantula remodeling its burrow or taking prey is the point of keeping one.
  • Long tongs for rearranging decor and lifting out a bolus or a shed skin.
  • A catch cup and a stiff card for every move, low over a lined tub, with tongs or a soft paintbrush to steer the spider into the cup. A gentle brush stroke tends to calm a spider rather than irritate it (page {{P:rehousing}}).
  • Enclosure maintenance done without touching the spider at all.
  • Washing your hands after any enclosure work, and keeping them away from your face and eyes until you have.
Never handle A tarantula in premolt, mid-molt or freshly molted, when its new exoskeleton is soft. Disturbing, handling or feeding it in that window is one of the few absolute rules in tarantula keeping (page {{P:molting2}}).
The honest takeaway A tarantula is a rewarding animal to watch and a poor animal to hold. Handling adds risk for the spider and for you and gives the spider nothing it wants. Nobody should buy a tarantula with the goal of handling it.
Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Body language & normal behavior

Half of tarantula keeping is knowing which alarming-looking things are completely normal.

Defensive signals

Rearing up on the hind legs, flicking hairs toward a perceived threat, and displaying the fangs are all defensive signals. None of them means aggression the way that word works with a mammal. They are the responses of an animal that would much rather be left alone, and the right answer to every one of them is to close the enclosure and come back later. Never lean over a spider that is already kicking hairs.

What you seeWhat it means
Sitting motionless for daysNormal. In the one controlled housing study, inactivity was nearly all of the observed behavior.
Hiding all day, active after darkNormal. Most pet tarantulas are nocturnal.
Rearranging substrate, webbing over the hide entrance, burying the water dishNormal. The spider is building. Dig out and refill the dish, and leave the rest.
Refusing food, abdomen dark and swollen, a bald patch on topPremolt. Stop offering food (page {{P:molting}}).
Lying on its back, legs folded overMost likely molting. Leave it completely alone.

Front of the body lowered into the water dish for a long timeDrinking. A dehydrated spider can drink for minutes or hours.
A male with swollen tips on its palps, wandering, off foodA mature male after his final molt. Normal for him (page {{P:sexing}}).
Jerky, uncoordinated or twitching legsNot normal. Possible pesticide exposure or dyskinetic syndrome (page {{P:pesticides}}).

A spider on its back is not dying

A tarantula often molts on its back. A spider lying upside down with its legs folded is almost certainly mid-molt and must be left alone. The posture that looks similar and means the opposite is the death curl, where the legs curl tightly underneath an upright body. Learn to tell those two apart before you ever need to.

Don't read stillness as anything at all

A still spider looks the same whether the setup is right or wrong. Judge the husbandry on substrate, humidity, ventilation and water rather than on how busy the animal looks.

Enrichment & common mistakes

There is exactly one controlled tarantula housing study, and it found less than enthusiasts hope and more than skeptics expect.

Stalter, Dorleus, Milone, Sincage, Skurski and Leeds published a preliminary study of Brazilian black tarantulas in December 2024. They compared Standard housing against Complex housing, which was about six times larger with six times as many structural elements. All four findings belong together:

ActivityDid not change. Inactivity was nearly all of the observed behavior in both enclosures.
Space useDid change. Home ranges were 77% larger and core areas 113% larger in the complex enclosure.
BoldnessIn a new environment, spiders from the complex enclosure had 150% greater odds of being active.
PreferenceNone. Offered both at once, the spiders split their time roughly evenly.

The authors concluded that complexity may improve welfare, while noting the small sample. Go larger than the minimum floor where you sensibly can, with two limits: never add fall height for a ground-dweller, and don't make the enclosure so large that a small spider is hard to find and feed.

Priority order

  1. Substrate depth and enclosure type matched to the species
  2. Water, always available
  3. A retreat the spider can web into and modify
  4. Correct humidity and ventilation
  5. Floor area beyond the minimum, without fall height
  6. Low structure to move through
  7. Live prey released, at varied times

Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

Choosing a spider, quarantine & finding a vet

Buy captive-bred, look at the abdomen and the mouth, and keep it apart for three months.

Choosing a seller

Buy captive-bred. Captive-bred spiders are less likely to carry oral nematodes, the parasite that turns up mostly in wild-caught imports and can spread through a whole collection, and buying them adds nothing to the capture of wild animals.

Choosing the spider

Look for a plump abdomen, and the species name in Latin on the label or the receipt. A "sexed female" should come with a photo of the molt she was sexed from (page {{P:sexing}}).

Walk away from a shrunken, wrinkled abdomen, which means dehydration and is common in spiders kept badly in shops, and from any white mass around the mouth, the nematode sign.

Quarantine: at least three months

A new tarantula lives apart from any other invertebrate for at least three months, ideally in a different room, with its own tools: not the tongs, not the water dish, not your hands between enclosures without washing. The problems that arrive with a new spider are slow. Mites build over weeks, mold takes hold in a damp corner, and nematodes in a wild-caught import show only once the spider is settled.

Use a plain enclosure with paper towel or bare substrate, a water dish and a hide. Feed lightly, leave a molt alone if one comes, and look at the spider, the substrate and the prey remains every few days. A first and only spider still gets the three months of watching: it is the window in which a problem shows up while the setup is simple enough to see it. For a shipped spider, the first days are about a dark, quiet enclosure with water, not food.

Finding a vet before you need one

There is no routine first vet visit for a tarantula, and many areas have no vet who treats invertebrates at all. Search now for an exotics vet who will see one, ask whether they do, and write the number on the emergency card. Where none exists, experienced keepers are the realistic second line, though they are not a diagnosis. What a vet will want to hear is on page {{P:redflags}}.

Rehousing: the cup and card method

Moving a spider is two jobs: everything ready before the lid comes off, then a slow, low transfer with no hands.

When

When the spider outgrows its container, when a sling moves up a size, when a shipped spider comes out of its cup, or when quarantine ends. Never in premolt, mid-molt or freshly molted, whatever the reason (page {{P:molting2}}).

Before the lid comes off

New enclosure set up and running a few hours ahead: substrate in, decor placed, water dish filled
Door shut, gaps under furniture checked, drawers and floor vents covered
Pets and children out of the room
Catch cup, a spare cup, and a stiff card or thin rigid plastic within reach
A large lined tub, or a bathtub for big spiders, under the whole job
Safety glasses on, room ventilated, face out of the enclosure

The method

  1. Lower a clear, smooth-sided cup over the spider, with room for it to stand normally.
  2. Slide the card along the surface under the cup until it seals the opening.
  3. Lift cup and card together as one sealed unit, keeping the card pressed flat.
  4. Carry it low and level to the new enclosure, set it down inside, slide the card back and lift the cup away. Let the spider walk out on its own; never tip or shake it out.

Work a few inches above the tub floor, not a few feet above open floor. That height is the single biggest thing you control. For a pink toe, bring the cup to the spider at its own level on the decor, or move it already sealed in a container it has retreated into. Steer a sling with a soft paintbrush.

If it bolts

  • Don't grab it. A reflexive grab is how a keeper ends up with a fall, a bite or a face full of hairs.
  • Stay still and let it run. Most stop once they reach cover or a corner.
  • The tub is why it has a floor and not a fall. Re-approach slowly, cup first.
  • If it gets out of the tub, dim the lights, close the door, and give it a few minutes to settle before trying again (page {{P:cleaning}}).

Molting: the cycle

A tarantula grows by shedding its whole exoskeleton. It is the most vulnerable thing that happens to it, and it happens over and over.

What is going on

An exoskeleton does not stretch. To grow, the spider builds a new, larger one underneath, sheds the entire old one, including the linings of parts such as the fangs and book lungs, and then hardens the new one. The shed skin left behind, the exuvia, is a hollow copy of the animal, and it is how a spider is sexed (page {{P:sexing}}).

Reading premolt

SignWhat it means
Refusing food, sometimes for weeks or monthsNormal and expected. Stop offering food rather than persisting.
Abdomen dark and swollenThe new exoskeleton forming beneath the old one.
A bald patch on top of the abdomenHairs kicked off. A clear early sign.
Reduced movement, a dulled lookNormal. Leave it be.
Heavy webbing, a silk mat, or sealing itself inPreparation. Leave the web exactly as it is.

How long it takes

Premolt

A few weeks of fasting for a sling, several months for an adult, rarely past a year. There is no way to hurry it.

The molt itself

15 minutes to several hours, often on its back. A long, quiet stretch inside that window is normal.

Hardening

3 to 7 days for slings and juveniles, 7 to 14 days for adults, before the fangs are hard enough to eat with.

How often

Juveniles molt every few weeks to a couple of months. A mature adult may molt once a year, and very old females of some species go several years between molts. Females keep molting for life. A male stops for good at the molt that makes him mature, which is why he lives so much shorter than she does.

Log the cycle Note the date you first see premolt signs, the molt date, and the date it ate again (page {{P:equiplog}}). After two or three cycles the next one becomes predictable, and a fast stops being a worry.

Molting: what to do and never do

The one absolute rule in tarantula keeping Do not handle, disturb, move or feed a tarantula in premolt, mid-molt, or while the new exoskeleton hardens. Not to check on it, not to rearrange the enclosure, not for a better photo. Physical disturbance during or just after a molt is one of the most common preventable causes of death.

During premolt: do

  • Take out any prey already in the enclosure, well before the molt.
  • Keep the water dish full.
  • Keep humidity in the species' range. Too little humidity in premolt is linked to failed molts.
  • Leave the webbing as the spider built it.

During premolt: don't

  • Don't rehouse. If the enclosure needs changing, wait until the spider has hardened.
  • Don't keep offering food because it has been a while.
  • Don't mist the spider directly.
  • Don't "help" by pulling at the old skin.

After the molt

  • Leave it alone for the hardening window: 3 to 7 days for slings and juveniles, 7 to 14 days for adults. The fangs are soft, and it cannot eat or defend itself.
  • Keep the water dish full throughout.
  • At the end of the window, offer one prey item. If it is refused, take it out within 24 hours and try again in a few days.
  • Lift the shed skin out with tongs once the spider has moved away from it, and keep it for sexing.
  • Log the molt date.

Slow is not the same as stuck

NormalStuck
A molt that takes several hours, with long quiet stretchesHalf in and half out of the old skin for hours with no progress
A pale, soft spider lying still after it climbs outStruggling that goes nowhere, or legs left twisted at the end
When a molt goes wrong Call an experienced keeper or an exotics vet. Never try to pull the old skin off yourself, which tears limbs. A failed molt is far less common than a normal one, and most trace back to low humidity in premolt, disturbance, or an underlying health problem (page {{P:injury}}).

Sexing, mature males & eggs

The sex decides how long you have this animal: a female rose hair can outlive a male by a decade or more.

The mature male

Young males and females look almost identical. The difference shows when a male makes his final molt: he grows bulbs on the tips of his palps (the pair of short, leg-like feelers beside the fangs) and, in many species, hooks under the first pair of legs. A spider already showing those is a mature male with a short time left. Many stop eating, and most die within about a year of reaching maturity and mating. That is natural, not illness.

Sexing from the shed skin

MethodHow it works and how far to trust it
The shed skinThe best method. A female sheds the lining of her spermathecae, the sperm-storage organs, with the rest of the exoskeleton. Opened out and laid flat, the abdomen of her molt shows them as a flap between the front pair of book lungs; a male's molt shows only a plain line there. From about a 2 in (5 cm) leg span, an experienced keeper can read a molt under a stereo microscope. On large adult molts the structures are visible to the naked eye. Soften the molt before opening it out.
The live spider's undersideThe backup. Males carry a small patch of extra spinnerets just in front of the furrow between the front book lungs. Less reliable, and hard to read at all on young spiders.
Mature male featuresConclusive, but late: the palp bulbs and leg hooks appear only at the final molt.

What that means when you buy

  • Unsexed slings are the cheapest route, and any one of them may turn out male.
  • A "sexed female" should come with a photo of the molt she was sexed from. A guess from the underside or from body shape is not the same product.
  • Sexing your own later is free: keep each molt, soften it, and have it checked.

Eggs

A mated female lays her eggs, sometimes hundreds at once, sealed inside a silk egg sac, and gives no care once the spiderlings emerge and disperse. Breeding is outside the scope of this package, which is written for one spider kept alone.

Section 06 · The Law

Is a tarantula legal where you live?

Legal with no permit in most of the United States, because most wildlife laws stop at animals with backbones.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet tarantula is legal with no permit in 41, barred in 4, permit-only in 1, limited to named species in 1, legal on a condition in 1, and unclear in 4. Most state wildlife codes stop at mammals, birds, fish, reptiles and amphibians, so a spider is invisible to them.
