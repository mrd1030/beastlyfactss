Section 04 · Handling & Behavior

Handling & taming

Most hamster bites are not a mean pet. They are a startled, disoriented nocturnal animal defending itself.
The commonest way owners get bitten Reaching into the cage during the day while the hamster is deeply asleep. From the hamster's side that is an ambush, not a greeting. Let it know you are there, and wake it gently and slowly before you try to touch it.

The pickup

  • Approach calmly and let the hamster wake up and orient itself before you reach in. Evening, once it is up, goes far better than daytime.
  • Let it investigate your hands in its own time. Then cup it between two hands and open them so it sits on your joined palms.
  • Scoop from below. Never grab from above, and never corner or trap one that is trying to get away.
  • Stay low, over something soft, and hold it no more than a few inches over a table or the floor. A hamster is quick, and a fall can injure it.
  • Do not handle one that is resting or sleeping unless it is necessary.

Taming, on the hamster's clock

Set the cage up before collecting the hamster, with a handful of its used, dry bedding moved in so the place smells familiar. Leave it alone for the first 24 hours apart from food, water and a look for any sign of illness or injury. Then, in the evening once it is up and active:

  1. Hand in the cage, no touching. Rest a hand inside so your smell and presence become ordinary.
  2. Floor sessions. In a secure room, sit on the floor and put a short tunnel in front of the hamster. Let it walk through and come out to investigate you. For the first few weeks, let it walk over your hands and sit in your lap.
  3. Stop while it is calm. Put it back while it is still relaxed, not once it gets irritable, or it learns that fussing is how a session ends.
  4. Short lifts. Once it is comfortable in your lap, practice cupping it up and setting it back down. After one to two weeks of this, most hamsters are happy to be picked up and hand fed.

A short session every day works better than long sessions at weekends with nothing in between.

Species matter

A Syrian is the easiest to tame and generally the least likely to nip once settled. Winter White, Campbell's and Chinese dwarfs are quicker and more easily startled, with a real chance of nipping when cornered. A Roborovski is the fastest and least suited to regular handling, and its tiny size and sudden movements make a drop likely, so most are better watched than held.

Out of the cage Time out is always supervised, in a pen or a hamster-proofed room with cables covered and no hazardous plants. Exercise balls are not recommended: they can be stressful, and the hamster cannot reach food, water or a hiding place while inside one.

Normal behavior & body language

Packed cheeks, then a hidden stash in a cornerNormal hoarding. A hamster stores food in its cheek pouches and deposits it to eat later, so a day without seeing it eat can be nothing.

Nights, fights and company

Wild Syrians are solitary burrowers and fiercely territorial toward each other. Fights between hamsters happen at night, which is why a pair of any kind is checked for bite wounds every morning. Hamsters do not play-fight, so any real fight means permanent separation, with no attempt to reintroduce them.

What stress looks like in the setup

  • A hamster with nowhere to hide is a chronically stressed one, so a hideout is not optional.
  • Rough handling, a startled wake-up, a new environment and cage changes are the usual triggers.
  • Stress and poor hygiene are directly linked to wet tail.
Reading a bite A bite is nearly always information about the approach, not about the animal. Being woken suddenly, startled or handled roughly are the usual triggers, so change the approach, not the hamster.

Enrichment & common mistakes

Most small-pet enrichment advice is a list of things to buy. For hamsters, depth reorders the list.

Priority order

  1. Bedding deep enough to hold a real burrow: 6 in (15 cm) as the floor, and 40 cm (about 15 in) or more where the cage allows (page {{P:bedding}}).
  2. A cage with the footprint and wall height to make that possible.
  3. A correctly sized solid wheel.
  4. Connected cover and buried tunnels, so the whole floor gets used.
  5. Scatter feeding and foraging toys, instead of a bowl.
  6. Novelty objects, last. They are the cheap part.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happens
Buying the starter kitToo small for the floor space and too shallow for the bedding, and both are expensive to redo.

If handling and interaction matter to you, choose a Syrian. If you mainly want an active pet to watch, a dwarf species, a Roborovski especially, fits that role better than regular handling. Neither is wrong, but picking on what you want out of the relationship matters more with hamsters than with most small pets. Rescues rehome hamsters too, and a Syrian costs about $25 at a pet store chain while dwarfs run $15 to $25.

When dwarfs can share

Roborovski, Campbell's and Winter White dwarfs can sometimes share a cage as a pair or trio, when both of these hold:

  • The group is one species and one sex, formed early in life, ideally from littermates.
  • The cage is large, with plenty of enrichment and several shelters, so each hamster can reach food and water and get away from the others.

No new adult is added to an established group later. If it cannot be avoided, the two live in separate cages side by side for about a week first, and are not left together overnight until they have clearly settled. Unfamiliar hamsters otherwise should not live in cages side by side, since the scent of a stranger is stressful. The same goes for two males, or a male near a female.

Never house two Syrians together Syrians are strictly solitary, in any enclosure size. Buying "two for company" is one of the most common and preventable hamster-owner mistakes. Even a settled dwarf pair can fall out as it ages, so have a second cage ready before a pair ever comes home.
The safe default None of these species needs company to be happy, and a single hamster is the safe default for all of them.
Section 05 · Arrival & Life Stages

A healthy hamster, the first days & the first vet visit

Almost all of the early decisions are made before it comes home.

Choosing a healthy one

Look at the hamster in the evening, when it should be alert, active and interested in food.

CheckA healthy hamster
EyesClean, clear and bright, with no cloudiness or discharge.
TeethFront teeth light yellow, smooth and wearing evenly.
Coat and skinClean, with no fur loss, flaky skin, scratches or wounds.
BreathingNo sneezing, wheezing or labored breathing.
BodyNo lumps, bumps or scabs.
Rear endDry, with no stains or discharge. A wet or soiled bottom is the first sign of wet tail, which hits young Syrians hardest (page {{P:wettail}}).

Before it comes home

  • Set the cage up first, and move a handful of used, dry bedding from the old cage into the new one so the place smells familiar.
  • Register with a local vet, so someone is on the list to call, and hand over the medical record you are given at collection as soon as you can.
  • Put the cage in a quiet area, away from running water and other pets (page {{P:enclosure}}).

The first days

Leave the hamster alone for the first 24 hours, apart from food, water and a look for any sign of illness or injury. Keep it on the food it was already eating, and change anything gradually. Stress, transport, overcrowding, poor hygiene and a sudden diet change are the usual wet tail triggers, which is why the first days are for quiet. Then start the taming steps (page {{P:handling}}).

Keeping a new hamster apart

A new hamster lives alone in its own cage. Do not put it in a cage side by side with another hamster, or near another rodent's cage. For a single hamster the practical rule is to watch it closely for illness from the first day and to keep it away from other animals.

The first vet visit

A vet can confirm a hamster's sex at the first checkup, which matters because hamsters breed early and fast. It is also the moment to find out whether the practice sees rodents. A routine small-mammal wellness exam costs about $80 to $115.

Pet shop and breeder Wet tail hits young Syrians of 3 to 10 weeks hardest, and a move to a new home is one of its triggers, so a young hamster is the one to watch most closely in the first weeks.

Sexing, breeding & litters

Hamsters breed early and fast, which is why it pays to know the sex.

Telling males from females

In an adult hamster the sex shows on the outside. Hold the hamster securely on its back in cupped hands and compare the gap between the anus and the genital opening: short in a female, clearly longer in a male. The testicles can be drawn up into the body, so not seeing them does not prove a female. In very young hamsters that gap is the most reliable guide, and a vet can confirm it at the first checkup.

Why it matters

FactFigure
Pups weanedAbout 20 days (as late as 3 to 4 weeks by some counts)
Able to breed7 to 8 weeks old
Pregnancy16 to 19 days
Litter sizeAn average of nine pups

A pair of dwarfs sold as two females that turns out to be a male and a female can produce a litter before anyone has checked. Confirm the sex, and have a second cage ready regardless.

Fights and bite wounds Fights between cage mates happen at night, so both animals are checked for bite wounds every morning. An abscess, a pus-filled swelling from an infected bite, needs a vet.
What this package does not cover Breeding is not a goal of this package, and it does not cover the care of a nursing mother. The facts above are here so a surprise litter is not a surprise.

Torpor: cold, short days & telling it from death

The one seasonal problem a pet hamster has, and it looks like the worst outcome.

What it is

Below about 41°F (5°C), particularly with short days, a hamster can enter torpor, a hibernation-like state with minimal activity and little or no eating. It is distinct from true hibernation, which pet hamsters do not undergo. Torpor can look alarmingly like the hamster has died.

Why it is dangerous

A Syrian hamster does not fatten up first, so one that fails to wake periodically to eat will starve. That is why the floor is a hard one: keep the room above 41°F, and well above it, since the comfortable range starts at 65°F (18°C).

What to do

  1. Do not assume it has died. A cool, still hamster in a cold room may be in torpor.
  2. Warm it gradually. Gradual warming is the first response.
  3. Check the room reading. A thermometer at cage height tells you how cold it really got.
  4. Watch it eat. A hamster that has been that cold and still is not eating once warm is an appetite-loss case, so follow the hours table for a hamster that stops eating.

Seasonal checks

WhenDo this
AutumnMove the cage away from windows and doors, which means drafts, and put a thermometer in the room at cage height.
WinterKeep the room at 65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C) and above 41°F (5°C) at all times, including overnight and during an outage. Keep the bedding dry and the cage out of drafts.
SummerWatch for the room passing 80°F (27°C). Move the cage to the coolest room and keep fresh water in front of it.
Section 06 · The Law

Is a hamster legal where you live?

Legal almost everywhere in the United States, with one ban and one unclear rule.

This page covers all 50 states, the District of Columbia and New York City, which has its own rules: 52 jurisdictions. A pet hamster is legal with no permit in 50, barred in 1 and unclear in 1.

WhereStatusThe rule
HawaiiBarredThe Syrian hamster is on the Restricted Animal List, Part A (Hawaii Administrative Rules § 4-71-6), which is for research and exhibition only. No route for a household and no pet permit.
OregonUnclearThe not-wild list (Oregon Administrative Rules 635-056-0020(14)) names the "Common Hamster, Cricetus cricetus", a large wild European hamster, not the pet Syrian (Mesocricetus auratus). An unlisted mammal is barred by default (635-056-0130(1)). Pet hamsters are sold openly there, so the exemption plainly means the pet one, but ask the state wildlife agency before you buy.

Arkansas, Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, VermontThe golden hamster and at least one dwarf group, by name.
Alaska, WyomingThe golden hamster only, on a closed list. Dwarfs are not named.
New JerseyThe golden hamster is on the exempt list (New Jersey Administrative Code 7:25-4.4). The dwarf Phodopus species fall under the separate rule at 4.5, not the exemption.
Colorado, Georgia, Kentucky, New York City, UtahThe Syrian or golden hamster, by genus or species.
