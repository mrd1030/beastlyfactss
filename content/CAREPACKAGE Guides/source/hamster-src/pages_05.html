Red flags & finding a vet

Hamsters are hardy for their size, but a short life means problems progress fast.
Speed is the whole problem A hamster's metabolism runs fast, so a condition can go from a first sign to an emergency in hours, and a life of two years or so leaves little margin for a wait-and-see week. Find a vet who sees small mammals before you need one, and write the number on the emergency card.

Wet tail

Proliferative ileitis. The condition every hamster owner needs to recognize on sight.
A same-day emergency Wet tail can be fatal within 24 to 48 hours without treatment. Do not wait to see if it improves. Get to a vet immediately.
Why it has its own pageA large veterinary study of 4,504 pet hamsters under veterinary care in the United Kingdom found wet tail in 7.33% of them, the most common specific diagnosis in the species, and the leading recorded cause of death at 6.27%.
CauseBacterial, and tied to stress. Stress, transport, overcrowding, poor hygiene or a sudden diet change are the usual triggers. It hits young Syrians of 3 to 10 weeks hardest.
SignsWatery diarrhea, a wet or matted rear end, lethargy and a hunched posture. Appetite loss is an early sign.
ResponseA vet at once. Treatment involves an antibiotic, and the choice matters in a hamster (page {{P:antibiotics}}).

Not a single disease

The study's authors note that wet tail is frequently used in primary-care practice as a catch-all term for a soiled rear end in hamsters, from several sources, including diarrhea or urinary and reproductive tract discharge. It describes what the rear end looks like rather than naming one cause. That is a reason to get a wet-bottomed hamster seen, not a reason to diagnose it yourself.

Prevention is mostly the first weeks

  • Keep the first days quiet: leave a new hamster alone for 24 hours, then start gently.
  • Keep the diet it came on, and change anything over about ten days.
  • Spot-clean the soiled corner daily instead of leaving it to a weekly strip-out.
  • Never house two Syrians together, since overcrowding is on the trigger list.
  • Look at the rear end whenever you handle it.
  • Keep handling gentle and the environment calm. Rough handling and a stressful setting are part of the picture.
Check the rear end by looking A vet checks the area under the tail for stains or discharge, and so can you at every handling session. In a long-haired hamster the wetness can be less obvious, so look rather than assume.
What the vet visit costs An emergency exam runs about $200 before any tests or treatment, and some hospitals add an after-hours fee. It is the cost most worth budgeting for.

Overgrown incisors & cheek pouch problems

Two mouth problems that both show up as a hamster that will not eat.

Overgrown incisors (malocclusion)

CauseA hamster's teeth grow continuously and are worn down by chewing. A lack of appropriate chew items is the usual reason they overgrow, and less commonly a genetic or jaw problem.
SignsDropped food, drooling and weight loss. Overgrown teeth lead to pain and difficulty eating, so a hamster may avoid food while hungry.
Healthy teethThe front teeth light yellow, smooth and wearing evenly.
ResponseA vet, for trimming. It is not a home job.
PreventionAppropriate chew items in the cage, and timothy hay as a chewing supplement.
The most common reason a hamster stops eating Dental problems are the single most common cause of appetite refusal. Weighing the hamster regularly catches it early, because a hamster in mouth pain loses weight.

Cheek pouch problems

A pouch full of food swells the face on either side and can look like a growth, but it is normal and empties again. A hamster's pouches stretch back as far as the shoulders, and impaction is one of the conditions a vet looks for, so the mouth is checked for impacted pouches and overgrown teeth together.

CauseUncommon, but soft or moist food can clump and lodge in a pouch.
SignsSwelling that stays on one side, face-pawing, drooling, and a sudden loss of interest in collecting food.
ResponseA vet. Any of those signs needs one.
Telling a full pouch from a problem A full pouch swells both cheeks and goes down once the hamster has stashed the food. A swelling that stays on one side, with drooling or pawing, does not.

Tumors, skin problems & diabetes

The conditions of age, of fights, and of sugar.

Tumors

Tumors are common in older hamsters, particularly females, and can grow quickly given how fast a hamster's metabolism runs. They are fairly common in an elderly hamster and one of the more frequent causes of death in an aging one. A lump anywhere always warrants a vet visit. Check for lumps, bumps and scabs during handling, and again when you weigh.

Abscesses, mites and skin problems

An abscess is a pus-filled swelling, usually from an infected injury or bite. Mites, fungal infections or allergies cause itching, redness and hair loss. Fur loss, irritated skin, heavy dandruff or visible mites need a vet, as does any lump or wound found at a health check. After a night's fight between cage mates, a morning check for bite wounds is the way an abscess is caught early.

Diabetes, more common in dwarfs

WhoMore common in dwarf species. Campbell's and Chinese hamsters have a documented high susceptibility.
SignsExcessive drinking and urination alongside weight loss, with appetite and energy changes.
ResponseA vet. It is manageable with a vet-guided low-sugar diet.
PreventionAvoid sugary treats, honey sticks and fruit-heavy diets as a matter of course. A plain formulated pellet or lab block as the diet base is the simplest way not to feed into the risk.
Plan for the old age A hamster of 1.5 years or more already counts as elderly. From about 2.5 years expect overgrown nails, hair loss, more sleeping and drinking, and slower movement. A steady weight log makes every one of these changes easier to see.
The husbandry pattern worth remembering Stress and poor hygiene are directly linked to wet tail. Dusty or aromatic bedding causes respiratory problems. A sugar-heavy diet raises diabetes risk in dwarfs. All three are fully within an owner's control, and given how short a hamster's life already is, the basics matter even more than they would with a longer-lived pet.

Respiratory infection & heat stress

Both start with the air in the room and the bedding in the cage.

Respiratory infection

Sneezing, wheezing, labored breathing and discharge are the signs. Drafts, damp conditions and dusty bedding, sawdust, cedar or pine especially, are the usual causes. A thermometer and humidity gauge confirm the room is not running colder or damper than it should, and clean, dust-free paper bedding removes one of the most common triggers outright. Always see a vet for this.

Heat stress

A hamster does not sweat the way a person does and has no cooling equipment of its own, so the room is its whole climate system. Heat risk begins around 80°F (27°C). The signs appear in a rough order:

  1. Rapid or open-mouth breathing, faster and shallower than normal.
  2. Excess drooling, sometimes leaving the fur around the mouth wet.
  3. Weakness and reluctance to move, a normally active animal becoming lethargic or hard to rouse.
  4. Severe signs: tremors, convulsions, collapse or unconsciousness. A genuine emergency.
Do this immediatelyDo not
Move the hamster to the coolest available room, with air conditioning or good airflow away from direct sun.Plunge it into cold or ice water. That can send a stressed small mammal into shock.
Offer cool, not ice-cold, water. Do not force it.Assume it will recover on its own once it looks calmer. Heat stress can look like it is resolving and then relapse.
Cool it gradually, with a cool, damp towel or a light wipe of cool water on the ears and feet.Skip the vet. It is a genuine emergency, not wait-and-see.
Get to a vet immediately.
Do not leave it in a parked car A closed car crosses into heat stress territory well before it feels uncomfortably warm to a person standing outside it. Never put the carrier in direct sun or a closed trunk.

Antibiotics: ask before the first dose

The same drug a dog gets can kill a hamster.

A hamster is a hindgut fermenter: it digests plant fiber by fermenting it in an enlarged gut full of bacteria, and that bacterial population is overwhelmingly gram-positive. Certain common antibiotics wipe it out, and toxin-producing Clostridium bacteria take over. The result is a fatal diarrhea called antibiotic-associated enterotoxemia. It is a well-documented reaction, not a rare allergy, and it looks a lot like the wet tail being treated. Hamsters can develop it three to five days after an antibiotic dose, with severe intestinal inflammation on top of the diarrhea.

The antibiotic classes to ask about

Class or drugDocumented risk
Penicillins (penicillin, ampicillin, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate)Contraindicated in hamsters
Lincosamides (clindamycin, lincomycin)Contraindicated in hamsters
Macrolides (erythromycin, tylosin)Unsafe for rodents generally
CephalosporinsThe same gram-positive mechanism as penicillins
BacitracinUnsafe for rodents, including in topical ointments if licked
StreptomycinOn the do-not-give list for rodents
What to ask "Is this antibiotic one of the ones known to cause fatal diarrhea in this species, and have you prescribed it to this species before?" Any drug aimed at gram-positive bacteria carries the risk, so the mechanism matters more than the brand. A vet experienced with exotics has an answer at once. One who does not may need to look it up, and that is reasonable to ask.

If it happens

Any diarrhea, appetite loss or lethargy that starts during or shortly after an antibiotic course is not something to monitor overnight. It is a call-the-vet-now situation, and stopping the antibiotic is usually the first step the vet will want to take. Do not wait for the next scheduled dose. Route matters too: oral dosing carries more risk than an injectable form of the same drug, because it passes directly through the gut where the bacteria live.

Ask, do not refuse Small mammals get real bacterial infections that need real antibiotics, and an untreated infection kills too. The aim is only that the right drug is picked the first time, particularly at a practice that mostly sees dogs and cats.

Reading the signs, hydration & germs you can catch

A hamster hides illness, so the check does the noticing.

The routine health check

Look atWhat you want to see
EyesClean, clear and bright, with no cloudiness or discharge.
TeethFront teeth light yellow, smooth and wearing evenly.
Coat and skinClean, with no fur loss, flaky skin, dandruff, scratches or wounds.
BreathingNo sneezing, wheezing or labored breathing.
Rear endNo stains or discharge.
WeightSteady on the kitchen scale. An unexpected gain or loss is worth acting on.
BodyNo lumps, bumps or scabs.

Hydration

A hamster drinks about 9 to 12 ml a day. Dehydration shows as dull, sunken eyes, slow movements, very concentrated urine (dark color and a strong odor) and a skin tent, skin that stays up when it is gently lifted. A bottle that has barely moved in a day, a blocked spout and a hot room are the first things to check.

Droppings and waste

Diarrhea is the sign to act on: watery stool, or a wet or matted rear end, is a same-day vet call because of wet tail. Diarrhea that starts during or after antibiotics is a call too, and so is any change in appetite alongside it.

Germs you can catch

Syrian hamsters have a reputation as carriers of lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV), a virus that can cause neurological disease in people, and an infected hamster can look completely well and still pass it on for at least 8 months. The routine is the same for every small mammal: wash hands with soap and running water after handling the hamster or its bedding, keep it and its equipment out of the kitchen and anywhere food is prepared or eaten, and clean its supplies outside the home if you can. Children under 5 are more likely than anyone to get sick from small-animal germs, and a bite or scratch can spread germs even when it does not look deep.
