Section 03 · Feeding

Diet, the schedule & weaning

One staple, a disputed schedule, and a few firm numbers around it.

The staple

A nutritionally complete, species-appropriate hamster pellet or lab block (a formulated complete diet pressed into a block), not a loose seed mix on its own. Given a mix, a hamster picks out the sugary, fatty bits and leaves the fiber-rich pieces, which contributes to dental problems and obesity. A strict seed-only diet is too high in fat and nutritionally poor. Hamsters are genuinely omnivorous,, so small amounts of fresh food and protein sit alongside the staple.

The schedule is a real disagreement

There is no single right schedule, and both approaches are legitimate. Pick one and stay consistent, with fresh water always available either way.

ApproachWhat it looks like
Always availablePelleted food stays in the cage all the time. Hamsters naturally hoard surplus and rarely overeat.
Twice-daily mealsMeasured feedings with a daily target: roughly 2 tablespoons for a standard-size hamster and about 1 tablespoon for a dwarf. A broader range of 1/8 to 1/3 cup shows up elsewhere.

Diet by age

  • Pups: weaned at about 20 days, and as late as 3 to 4 weeks by some counts. Syrian pups are the group wet tail hits hardest, between 3 and 10 weeks old, so a sudden diet change is the last thing a weaned pup needs (page {{P:wettail}}).
  • A new hamster: stays on the food it was already eating. Any change is made over about ten days, a little of the new food mixed into the old.
  • Adults: the staple, the small fresh amounts above, and scattered food in place of a full bowl.
  • Dwarf species prone to diabetes: the sugar rules matter more, since Campbell's and Chinese hamsters have a documented high susceptibility.

Scatter it, don't serve it

Hamsters are genuinely omnivorous in the wild and eat insects, so mealworms or a little boiled egg are a reflection of the natural diet rather than an indulgence. Scatter or hide food around the cage instead of relying only on a bowl: it encourages the natural foraging and hoarding behavior that is core to the species, and it turns a two-minute event into a foraging session that costs nothing.

Cheek pouches and caches

A hamster's cheek pouches can hold roughly a fifth of its own body weight and stretch back as far as the shoulders. It carries food away and caches it around the enclosure, so you may not see it eat for a day or more while it quietly works through a stash. Some hamsters can go 3 to 4 days on a hoard they provisioned earlier. That is different from a hamster visibly refusing fresh food that is on offer.

Keep the hoard when you clean Set aside any dry food hoard and put it back after cleaning. Removing it all at once is one of the ways a clean unsettles a hamster for days (page {{P:cleaning}}).
What this costs Pellets or lab blocks run about $30 to $50 a year and vegetables about $20 to $40. Most of the monthly cost is bedding, not food.

Water & why a hamster stops eating

Cheek pouches make this harder to read than it looks.

Water

Fresh water must be there all the time, from a bottle with a valveless sipper tube, checked daily for leaks. A hamster drinks about 9 to 12 ml a day (about 2 teaspoons), so a bottle that has barely moved in a day is worth a look at the spout. Glass water tubes are the wrong choice for a Syrian, which bites through glass.

Why a hamster stops eating

  1. Dental problems. The single most common cause. Overgrown or broken front teeth make chewing painful, so it avoids food even while hungry (page {{P:teeth}}).
  2. Wet tail. A bacterial gut infection that can be fatal within about 48 hours if untreated. Appetite loss is an early sign, alongside a soiled, wet rear end (page {{P:wettail}}).
  3. Cold-induced torpor. Below about 41°F (5°C), particularly with short days, with minimal activity and little or no eating (page {{P:torpor}}).
  4. Stress. A new environment, handling or cage changes cause a temporary, lower-urgency dip.
  5. Respiratory infection or other illness, usually with labored breathing, discharge or lethargy alongside.
  6. Diabetes in predisposed dwarf species, as appetite and energy changes.
  7. Cheek pouch impaction. Uncommon, but soft or moist food can lodge in a pouch.

Growth, weight, body condition & aging

A hamster's life is short, so the stages come fast.

Weight

A healthy adult Syrian weighs roughly 3 to 5 oz (85 to 140 g), females heavier than males, and every dwarf species stays under 100 g. A kitchen scale and a small container make a weigh-in a thirty-second job, and a steady number is the plainest sign of a hamster that is eating and well. Weigh it regularly and write the number in the log on page {{P:ownerlog}}, because an unexpected gain or loss is often the first thing that changes. A hamster has a high metabolic rate and loses condition quickly once it is unwell.

Reading condition

A sick hamster often isolates itself and can show weight loss, a hunched posture, lethargy, rough fur and labored breathing. A healthy one has clean, clear, bright eyes and a clean coat with no fur loss, flaky skin, scratches or wounds. Check the body for lumps, bumps and scabs while you weigh.

Aging

Tumors are common in older hamsters, particularly females, and can grow quickly given how fast a hamster's metabolism runs. A lump anywhere always warrants a vet visit. A hamster that digs and runs wears its own nails down, so there is no trim schedule, only a check for overgrowth in an older or less active animal.

Food and condition Diet sits behind several problems: obesity, diabetes in dwarf species, and the diarrhea that follows a sudden food change. A plain staple, small fresh amounts and treats kept to 1 to 2 times a week protect the weight you are measuring.
