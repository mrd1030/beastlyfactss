Enclosure size, bar spacing & where it goes

Bigger than the starter kit, and the first thing to get right because it is expensive to redo.

Floor space

Current welfare guidance calls for a genuinely large, unbroken floor, with more always better. A Syrian needs roughly 700 to 775 sq in (4,500 to 5,000 cm²), and a dwarf species around 600 sq in (3,900 cm²). A 40-gallon breeder tank suits a dwarf, and a Syrian does better in something larger still, such as a 75-gallon tank or its equivalent. Buy the right size once instead of planning to upgrade later.

SyrianDwarf (Winter White, Campbell's, Roborovski, Chinese)
Unbroken floor700 to 775 sq in (4,500 to 5,000 cm²)About 600 sq in (3,900 cm²)
Tank equivalent75 gallons or similar40-gallon breeder
Wire bar spacing, at most1/2 in (13 mm)1/4 in (6 mm), tighter for a Roborovski

Types of enclosure

A bin, a wire cage or a glass tank can all work. Whichever you choose, it has to hold the bedding depth that hamsters need, and that is where most commercial cages fail: they cannot physically hold the depth, which makes cage choice and bedding depth the same decision. A wire cage needs bar spacing no wider than the figures above, because a Roborovski especially can squeeze through surprisingly small gaps.

Where the cage goes

  • A quiet area, away from running water and from other pets.
  • A room where the lights go off at about the same time each night, because a hamster is active at night and needs a routine.
  • Not a bedroom. A hamster is noisy when it wakes.
  • Away from drafts, since a draft or damp bedding is a bigger cold risk than a cool room.
  • Not right beside another hamster's cage, and not near the cage of any other rodent, because their active hours differ and each disturbs the other.
Never Buy the starter kit and plan to upgrade later. The classic small wire cage falls well short of current welfare recommendations for floor space and for bedding. Also never house two Syrians in one enclosure, in any size (page {{P:choosing}}).
What the cage costs The enclosure is the biggest line in the setup: budget $30 to $90, and go bigger than a basic kit offers. The price is the reason the animal is the cheapest part of keeping a hamster properly.

Bedding depth & the study behind it

The single highest-value thing you can change, and the one no starter kit can accommodate.

What to use

Dust-free paper-based bedding or aspen shavings. Mixing a burrow-holding material with a looser one helps tunnels stay up instead of collapsing, which is what separates a hamster that digs from one that just redecorates. Avoid cedar and pine entirely: the aromatic oils in both irritate the respiratory system. Never use cotton-style fluffy nesting material, which wraps around limbs.

How deep

DepthWhat it gets you
6 in (15 cm)The minimum, and a welfare requirement rather than decoration. This is the floor, not the target.
8 in (20 cm) or moreBetter, because it leaves room for a deeper layer.
4 in (10 cm)The shallow group in the study: significantly more wire-gnawing and higher wheel running.
40 cm, about 15 inThe depth at which every hamster in the study built a burrow and used it. The number to aim for.
40 to 80 cm, about 15 to 31 inThe range the researchers concluded seemed to improve welfare.
80 cm, about 31 inThe deepest group, in which wire-gnawing was not observed at all.
The study, in three numbers Researchers assigned 45 male golden hamsters to groups with 80, 40 or 10 cm of wood shavings and measured their behavior, including responses to acute stressors. Hamsters on 10 cm gnawed the cage wire significantly more and ran the wheel harder than the others. Every hamster given 40 cm or more built a burrow and used it, with significantly less gnawing. In the 80 cm group, no wire-gnawing was seen. Depth is the enrichment, and everything else is secondary to it.

Why it matters in practice

  • Wire-gnawing is one of the standard behavioral measures of housing quality in small rodents. A hamster that chews the bars is reporting on the setup, and in a shallow cage it is a depth problem before it is a toy problem.
  • The commercial default is nowhere near 40 cm, and most cages sold for hamsters cannot hold it. Choose the enclosure by how much bedding it can carry.
  • Heavy wheel running is not proof the setup works. The hamsters on shallow bedding ran more.
What it costs A starting load of paper bedding runs $20 to $35, and replacing it through the year runs $60 to $100. Bedding is the largest share of the monthly cost, and the reason the monthly figure is not zero.

Temperature, humidity & light

No heat lamp, no UVB, and one cold threshold that genuinely matters.
ReadingWhat it means
65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C)The comfortable range, kept stable. A normal indoor room needs no supplemental heat.
Below 65°F (18°C)Out of range. Appetite and activity start to drop.
Below about 41°F (5°C)A hamster can slip into torpor, a hibernation-like state that can be dangerous, especially with short days. It does not fatten up first, so one that fails to wake to eat can starve (page {{P:torpor}}).
Above 80°F (27°C)Heat stress. A hamster cannot cool itself like a person, and the room is the whole climate system.
Humidity 40 to 60%The target for the room.

Keeping it steady

  • A digital thermometer and humidity gauge in the room turn "seems fine" into a reading. A hamster is too small to judge by how the room feels to you.
  • Cold is less dangerous than heat in most rooms, but a draft or damp bedding is the realistic cold risk. Keep the cage out of drafts and keep the bedding dry.
  • In a hot spell, move the cage to the coolest room with some air movement, and keep fresh water in front of it.
  • Below 41°F, keep the room warmer. This is the one hard floor for the species.

Lighting

No special lighting and no UVB is needed. A normal day and night cycle is enough. The thing to remember is that this is a nocturnal species: bright light during its active hours is more disruptive than helpful, so a lamp left on over the cage in the evening is working against the animal. Put the cage in a room where the lights go off at about the same time each night.

Torpor is not hibernation Pet hamsters do not undergo true hibernation. What cold produces is torpor, which can look alarmingly like death.

The wheel, the sand bath & the furnishings

Four items that are essentials, not extras.

The wheel: solid, and sized to the species

SpeciesWheel diameter
Syrian8 to 11 in (20 to 28 cm)
Dwarf6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm)

A solid running surface with no rungs is essential, because wire and mesh wheels cause foot injuries. A wheel that is too small forces an arched running posture that is hard on the spine over time, so a Syrian should run with a flat back. The caveat comes from the bedding study: the hamsters on shallow bedding ran more, so heavy wheel use is not a welfare score. Provide the wheel, and do not read the odometer.

The sand bath

A dust-free, non-clumping sand bath is a genuine species essential. A hamster works coat and skin oils out in it the way a bird uses a dust bath, and it is also where most hamsters choose to toilet, which does more for hygiene than any amount of bedding changes. Use a shallow dish heavy enough that the hamster cannot tip it and deep enough to roll in. Sift the sand when you spot-clean, and replace it when it clumps or smells. Chinchilla bathing dust is a far finer powder that irritates the eyes and airways, so it is the wrong product.

Hides, cover and tunnels

A hideout is not optional: a hamster with nowhere to hide is a chronically stressed one. Hamsters are prey animals and cross open ground reluctantly, so connected cover, buried tunnel sections and scattered hides change how much of the enclosure gets used at all. A large cage the animal will not cross is a small cage.

The rest of the list

  • Water: a bottle with a valveless sipper tube, checked daily for leaks, or a bowl. Never a glass tube for a Syrian, which bites through glass (page {{P:water}}).
  • Food dish, though much of the food is better scattered (page {{P:fresh}}).
  • Chew items, because a lack of them is the usual reason the front teeth overgrow (page {{P:teeth}}).
  • A digital thermometer and humidity gauge, for the room.
  • A kitchen scale in grams, for the weight checks.

Cleaning & hygiene

Spot daily, deep less often, and never a total strip-out.

A hamster's cage smells of its own scent marks and its food stores, and a cleaning routine has to keep enough of both to leave the animal settled.

How oftenWhat to do
DailyRemove soiled bedding and uneaten fresh food. Wash and rinse the water bottle or bowl, and check the sipper for leaks.
Weekly, small cageMove the hamster to a carrier. Take out the bedding, wash the cage and accessories with a small-animal habitat cleaner, rinse off every trace of it, and let everything dry fully before fresh bedding goes in.
Every few weeks, large deep-bedded enclosureThe same full clean, with spot cleaning in between. Deep bedding is replaced regularly to stay clean and dust free.
Every timeSet aside a handful of clean nesting material and any dry food hoard, and put them back afterward. Leave an established burrow in place during spot cleans.
Do not strip it out A total strip-out erases the scent marks and the hoard at once, and it can leave a hamster unsettled for days. Return the nest, wheel and bowls to the same spots, since a hamster relies on the layout staying put. Clean in the evening, once the hamster is already awake.

The sand bath and the latrine corner

Most hamsters toilet in the sand bath, so sifting it when you spot-clean does more than any amount of bedding changes. Replace the sand when it clumps or smells.

Washing your hands

Small mammals can carry germs that make people ill, and pet food, equipment and bedding can carry them too. Wash your hands with soap and running water after handling the hamster or its bedding. Keep the hamster and its equipment out of the kitchen and anywhere food is prepared, stored or eaten, and clean its supplies outside the home if you can. Children under 5 are more likely than anyone to get sick from small-animal germs, and a bite or scratch can spread germs even if the wound does not look deep. Page {{P:signs}} covers the one germ specific to hamsters.

Poor hygiene and wet tail Poor hygiene is one of the triggers of wet tail, the biggest threat to the species. Spot-cleaning the soiled corner daily is cheap insurance, and it does not disturb the burrow.
