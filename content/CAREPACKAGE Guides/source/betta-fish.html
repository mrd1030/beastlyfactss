How to use this package

Three ways to get value from it, depending on where you are right now.

If you haven't set up yet

Read pages 4 through 12 in order before buying anything, and before buying the fish. The single most important thing in this book is that the tank is cycled before a betta goes in it, and cycling takes weeks, not an afternoon. Almost every betta that dies in its first month dies of something that started here. Use the Budget & Shopping List on page 25 to buy once, then start the cycle on page 7 while the fish is still in the shop.

If your betta is already set up

Go straight to the Setup Checklist and Water Targets on page 23 and audit what you have. If the tank has no heater, no filter, or has never been tested with a liquid test kit, fix that this week rather than reading further. If your betta already looks unwell, start at Health Red Flags on page 16, but test the water first: the answer is in the test tube far more often than it is in the medication cabinet.

Everything at a glance before you read the detail.

Adult size2.5 to 3 in (6 to 7.5 cm) body. Male finnage is fin, not extra size
Captive lifespan2 to 4 years, up to 5 with excellent water quality. Often months old when sold
Native rangeShallow rice paddies, floodplains and slow streams in Southeast Asia
Diet typeObligate carnivore, a surface ambush feeder. Pellet staple, never flakes

Temperature78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C), held steady. Never room temperature (page 5)
The one hard ruleAmmonia and nitrite read 0 ppm, always. Anything else is an emergency (page 8)

Handling difficultyBeginner, but observation only. Never bare hands (page 15)
Conservation statusVulnerable (IUCN) in the wild, despite huge numbers in captivity

Cost overview

Initial setup

$157 to $336 for a complete cycled setup including the test kit, plus $5 to $30 for the fish itself and up to about $55 for a rare variety.

Monthly ongoing

$10 to $25 for food, conditioner, test reagents, electricity, and a medication reserve. Roughly $120 to $300 a year.

Where the money actually goes Not the fish, and not the food. A betta eats a few pellets a day, so the recurring cost is a heater running around the clock and fresh test reagents. The upfront figure looks high next to a $6 fish because it includes the two things the pet-aisle bundle leaves out, a heater and a real filter, and those decide whether the fish lives two months or four years.

Fun facts worth knowing

  • They breathe air. The labyrinth organ above the gills takes oxygen straight from the surface, an adaptation to warm paddy water holding very little dissolved oxygen. It also means a betta blocked from the surface can drown.
  • Males build nests out of spit. A bubble nest is a raft of saliva-coated bubbles built at the surface to hold eggs. Captive males build them with no female present, and it reads as a comfortable, well-warmed fish.
  • The aggression is partly built, not born. Research found that the timing of isolation from an enriched environment shapes later aggression, so your fish's temperament was set before you bought it.

Tank, heater & lid

Five gallons (19 L) is the current minimum, and it is a floor rather than a target. Ten gallons (38 L) is the size most keepers wish they had started with: more water dilutes waste further, holds temperature more steadily, and gives you hours of margin when something goes wrong instead of minutes. A standard 5 gallon measures about 16×8×10 in (40×20×25 cm), a 10 gallon about 20×10×12 in (50×25×30 cm). Length and surface area matter more than depth for a fish that has to reach the top to breathe.

A betta cannot be kept at room temperature. It is a tropical fish from water that sits in the high seventies year round, and an unheated tank in a heated house still drifts several degrees every night. Cold water suppresses immune function and digestion, which is why so many "mystery" declines are really slow chilling.

Never Run a betta in an unheated container, or trust a preset "tropical" heater without checking it against a separate thermometer. A stuck-on heater cooks a 5 gallon tank in hours, and a stuck-off one chills it overnight. The thermometer is what tells you which is happening.

Filtration & the nitrogen cycle

The two most important pages in this package. Everything on them happens before the fish comes home.

The filter

A sponge filter driven by a small air pump is the standard for this species, for one specific reason: a betta's fins catch water like a sail, and the outflow a tetra ignores will exhaust a betta and fray its fins over weeks. A sponge filter provides biological filtration with almost no current. If you use a hang-on-back filter instead, baffle the outflow with a piece of filter foam or a cut plastic bottle until the surface is barely rippling.

Fit a check valve on the airline between the pump and the tank. Without one, a power cut lets water siphon back down the tube and out onto the floor, which is how a small outage becomes a flood and an empty tank.

What the filter actually does

Almost nothing you can see. The sponge is a surface for bacteria to live on, and those bacteria are the filter. They convert the ammonia in fish waste and uneaten food into nitrite, then into far less harmful nitrate, which you remove with water changes. That colony is what "cycled" means, and it is why a tank that looks spotless can still be lethal.

StageWhat is happeningWhat you see on a test
1. AmmoniaWaste and leftover food break down into ammonia, which burns gills at any levelAmmonia climbs, nitrite and nitrate at 0
2. NitriteNitrosomonas bacteria establish and convert ammonia to nitrite, which blocks oxygen uptake in the bloodAmmonia falls, nitrite spikes
3. NitrateA second group of bacteria converts nitrite to nitrate, which is mildly toxic and easy to diluteNitrite falls to 0, nitrate appears
4. CycledBoth colonies keep pace with the tank's daily waste loadAmmonia 0, nitrite 0, nitrate present
Cycle with the plants already in Put the plants, driftwood, and decor in before you start. Plants take up ammonia directly, the decor gains its own share of the colony, and you avoid disturbing a settled tank later. It costs nothing to do it in this order.

Fishless cycling, step by step

Weeks of work with no fish in the tank. This is the part that gets skipped, and it is the part that decides everything.

  1. Set the tank up completely: substrate, plants, decor, heater at 78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C), filter running. Warm water cycles faster, so the heater goes in on day one.
  2. Fill with tap water dosed with dechlorinator. Chlorine and chloramine kill the bacteria you are trying to grow.
  3. Add an ammonia source: pure household ammonia, no surfactants or perfume, dosed to 2 to 4 ppm. Test to confirm rather than counting drops.
  4. Add bottled nitrifying bacteria, or better, a squeeze of used sponge or gravel from an established healthy tank. Seeded media cuts weeks off this.
  5. Test ammonia and nitrite every 2 to 3 days. Re-dose ammonia back to 2 ppm whenever it falls to 0, so the growing colony keeps being fed.
  6. The tank is cycled when it can take a 2 ppm ammonia dose and read 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours later. Not "close to zero." Zero.
  7. Change enough water to bring nitrate under 20 ppm, temperature-matched, and only then bring the fish home.

Expect 3 to 6 weeks with bottled bacteria, longer without. There is no way to rush the biology, only ways to seed it.

If the fish is already home and the tank isn't cycled This is a fish-in cycle and it is a rescue, not a plan. Test ammonia and nitrite daily. Change 25 to 50% of the water any time either reads above 0, use a conditioner that detoxifies ammonia between changes, feed lightly every other day, and add seeded media if you can get it. Expect this to run several weeks and to need attention every single day.

When the cycle stalls

What you seeWhat is usually happening
Ammonia sits high for weeks, no nitrite at allChlorine or chloramine still present, an ammonia product with additives, or the tank is too cold. Re-dose dechlorinator, confirm 78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C)
Nitrite spikes and then stays thereNormal, and the longest stage. High ammonia holds it back, so stop re-dosing above 2 ppm until nitrite falls
Everything reads 0, including nitrateUsually nothing has started yet. Confirm the ammonia dose actually registered on a test before assuming the tank is cycled
pH crashes and the cycle stops deadThe bacteria consume carbonate hardness. Very soft water runs out of buffer, pH falls, and the colony stalls. A partial water change usually restarts it
It cycled, then broke after a filter cleanThe colony lives on the sponge. Rinsing it under the tap kills it. Rinse in old tank water only, and only when flow drops

Water quality & testing

Most betta illness is a water problem wearing a costume. These are the numbers behind that.

ParameterTargetWhy it matters
Temperature78 to 80°F (25.5 to 26.5°C)Cold water suppresses immune function and digestion, and slows recovery from everything else on this list
pH6.5 to 7.5Wide tolerance. Stability matters far more than the exact figure
Ammonia0 ppmEven 0.25 ppm causes gill damage. Any detectable reading is an emergency
Nitrite0 ppmBlocks oxygen uptake in the blood. Toxic at any measurable level
NitrateUnder 20 ppmThe normal end product of a healthy cycle, controlled by water changes
GH / KHModerate, stableSecondary. Very soft, poorly buffered water lets pH crash after a change, which is worse than a steady pH slightly out of range
Zero means zero Ammonia and nitrite are the only two numbers here with no safe nonzero baseline. Temperature and pH have ranges. These do not. A cycled, established tank reads exactly 0 ppm for both, consistently, and any other reading is something to act on today rather than watch.

Test with reagents, not strips

A liquid reagent kit is the only reliable way to know whether a tank is cycled. Paper strips are notoriously imprecise, especially at the low end of the ammonia and nitrite scales, which is exactly the range where the answer matters. One kit lasts a long time and works out cheaper per test than strips do.

WhenWhat to test
New or recently set up tankAmmonia and nitrite every 1 to 2 days until both read 0 for a full week
Established, cycled tankAmmonia, nitrite, and nitrate weekly, on the same day each week
Anything looks offTest immediately. Clamped fins, lethargy, and appetite loss usually show up in the water before they show on the fish
After a change over 30%A quick recheck confirms the new water didn't bring chlorine or chloramine in with it
After adding anythingNew decor, a new plant, a medication, or a filter change: test for the next few days

Water changes & keeping the cycle

The single most useful habit in fishkeeping, and the easiest one to do in a way that undoes itself.

The weekly water change

  1. Change 20 to 30% of the volume weekly. In a 5 gallon that is roughly 1 to 1.5 gallons (4 to 6 L).
  2. Vacuum the substrate as you siphon, working over a different section each week rather than the whole floor at once.
  3. Prepare the replacement water in a dedicated bucket, dose the dechlorinator into it, and match the temperature to the tank by feel and then by thermometer. A few degrees of difference is a real stressor at this size.
  4. Pour it in slowly against the glass or onto a plate rather than straight onto the substrate or the fish.
  5. Rinse the filter sponge in the bucket of old tank water, gently, and only when flow visibly drops. Never under the tap.

When to change more than 30%

SituationChange
Ammonia or nitrite above 0 ppm25 to 50% now, and again the next day if it has not come back to 0
Nitrate over 20 ppmChange more, or change more often, rather than doing one very large change
Overfeeding accident, food left on the substrateSiphon the food out first, then change 25%
Medicating, at the end of a courseFollow the label. Usually a large change plus fresh carbon once treatment is finished
Routine weekly maintenance20 to 30%. Bigger is not better here
Topping up is not a water change Water evaporates, dissolved waste does not. Adding fresh water to replace what evaporated raises the level and dilutes nothing, so nitrate keeps climbing while the tank looks full. Top up when the level drops, and change water on schedule regardless. Keep the lid on and there will be far less of both to do.
What resets a cycle A 100% water change, scrubbing or replacing the filter sponge, rinsing media in chlorinated tap water, or deep-cleaning the whole tank at once. Each of these throws out the bacteria colony and puts you back at day one of page 7, with a fish already in the tank. Buckets, siphons, and sponges used for the aquarium stay aquarium-only, and never meet soap.

Diet & feeding schedule

Overfeeding is the most common mistake in betta keeping, and it is the one that fouls the water too.

Bettas are obligate carnivores and surface ambush feeders: in the wild they take insects, larvae, and small crustaceans off the top of the water. In a tank the staple is a betta-specific high-protein pellet, not tropical flakes and not goldfish food, both of which have the wrong nutrient profile and far more filler. Look for a pellet that lists brine shrimp, krill, or fish meal near the top of the ingredients and runs roughly 30 to 35% protein and around 5% fat.

Portion, which matters more than frequency

A betta's stomach is roughly the size of its own eye. That is the actual basis for the standard guidance of 2 to 4 pellets per meal, and it is why a fish that looks hungry is usually just a fish that always looks hungry. Depending on pellet size and brand this can run anywhere from 2 to 3 up to 6 to 7, so the more reliable rule is whatever gets cleared within 1 to 2 minutes. Remove anything still floating after that.

The routine

Once or twice a day, at least 8 hours apart if you feed twice, with one full fasting day a week. Pick a schedule and hold it. Consistency does more here than the exact number of meals.

Pre-soak expanding pellets

If a pellet swells in water, it swells in the fish too. Soak it in a spoonful of tank water for a minute first. This alone prevents a large share of bloat and constipation cases.

Move the feeding spot

Drop food in a different part of the tank each day and rotate what you offer. It is the cheapest enrichment there is, and the fish general enrichment literature backs feeding variation directly.

Why a betta stops eating

Bettas refuse food for six ordinary reasons and one worrying one. Work them in order.

  1. Environmental stress. A new tank, a recent move, or a disturbance nearby suppresses appetite temporarily.
  2. Poor water quality. An ammonia, nitrite, or nitrate spike. Check this first, every time, before anything else on the list.
  3. Water too cold. Below roughly 76°F (24.5°C) metabolism and appetite both slow. Most unheated rooms sit here.
  4. Picky eating. Bettas reject an unfamiliar brand outright. Try a different food before assuming illness.
  5. Overfeeding, constipation, or swim bladder trouble. A swollen belly, stringy or absent feces, floating or sinking (page 20).
  6. Illness. Bacterial infection or parasites, usually alongside clamped fins, white spots, or labored swimming.
When a fast becomes a symptom A healthy adult can physically survive around two weeks without food, but treat that as an emergency ceiling, not a guideline. The point to investigate is 5 to 7 days of refusal after you have already checked water quality and temperature and tried a different food, and immediately at any point if a physical symptom from page 16 appears alongside it.

What to try, in order

  1. Test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate, and read the thermometer. Do this before anything else, every time.
  2. Change 25% of the water, temperature-matched, whether or not the test showed anything.
  3. Look the fish over properly: fins, belly, scales, gills, and how it holds itself in the water.
  4. Skip a day. A betta that has been overfed genuinely is not hungry, and a fast day is already part of the schedule.
  5. Offer something different: thawed brine shrimp or daphnia will tempt a fish that has gone off pellets.
  6. Check what changed around the tank. A new light, a mirror-bright window, a moved position, or a new reflection all suppress appetite.
Tempting a fussy betta Feed at the same time each day so hunger lines up with the routine. Pre-soak pellets, which softens them and releases scent. Drop food where the fish already is rather than expecting it to cross the tank. And give a new food three or four tries across a week before deciding it has been rejected: bettas often refuse something unfamiliar twice and then take it.

Enrichment: what the research says

The betta is the most-studied fish in the freshwater hobby, and the findings are inconvenient for the way it is usually sold.

Restricted space raised signs of stress, including hiding and inactivity. Tank size and furnishings both measurably affected behavior and welfare. And fish in aquaria planted with Sagittaria subulata showed the longest average active swimming time and fewer negative behaviors.

The counterintuitive part is the planting. Adding dense cover does not produce a fish you never see. It produces one that swims more, because being out in open water is only a risk when there is nowhere else to be. Live planting also works on the water chemistry and the tank's microbial community at the same time, so it earns its place twice.

Priority order

  1. Tank volume, decided before anything else goes in it
  2. Dense planting and cover, live where you can
  3. Surface resting spots and broken sightlines
  1. Varied feeding location and varied food
  2. Tannins from driftwood, and softened light
  3. Mirrors, at the bottom of the list or off it entirely
On group housing Research indicates bettas of either sex can be group-housed with lower aggression if they have been reared in groups since hatching under enriched conditions. That is a finding about rearing, not a license to combine adults. Two adult males in one tank will fight until one dies, whatever the tank is planted with.
Don't read a still fish as a calm one A betta sitting motionless in a bare tank is displaying the exact pattern the restricted-space study produced. Stillness in an empty box is not contentment, and it is the single easiest welfare problem in this species to miss.
Tannins and light Driftwood releases tannins, which tint the water faintly amber and soften the light. That is closer to the blackwater conditions this species comes from, and it is cosmetic rather than harmful. If the tint bothers you, a shorter photoperiod or a dimmer light gets you most of the same effect. A betta lit hard from above in a bare tank has nowhere to be out of the glare.

Reading a healthy betta

You will never handle this fish, so reading it from outside the glass is the whole skill.

Body condition, from directly above

Look down into the tank from the top, not through the side. A betta in good condition looks like a streamlined torpedo, with the "shoulders" just behind the gill covers the same width as the middle third of the body.

A healthy betta

  • Deep, even color that holds day to day
  • Fins carried open and flowing, edges intact
  • Active swimming through the whole tank, not one corner
  • Eager, immediate interest in food
  • Regular trips to the surface for air, then back down
  • Clear eyes, smooth scales, no lesions

Stress signals

  • Fins clamped tight against the body
  • Faded color, or horizontal stress stripes
  • Lethargy, hiding, or resting on the substrate
  • Rapid gill movement, or gulping at the surface constantly
  • Loss of appetite
  • Scratching or flashing against decor
Never bare hands A betta's slime coat is an active barrier against infection and parasites, and skin contact strips it. Move the fish with a net or, better, a cup, and only when you actually have to. When a new betta comes home, float the sealed bag for about 15 minutes to equalize temperature, then net or cup the fish across. Never pour store water into your tank.

Health red flags & first response

Almost every illness in this section traces back to water quality or water temperature. That is genuinely good news, because it means the first move is nearly always the same.

Test the water before you treat anything Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and temperature, in that order, before a single dose of medication goes in the tank. A huge share of what looks like disease is a water-quality problem that will not respond to treatment, and medication on top of bad water is two problems instead of one. Correcting the water is not "doing nothing" while you wait. It is the treatment.

Act today

Scales standing out from the body like a pineconeDropsy, a sign of organ failure and generally a poor prognosis (page 20)
A fine gold or rust-colored dust over the bodyVelvet, which moves fast and kills fast (page 19)
White cottony or grey saddle-shaped patches, rapid spreadColumnaris, highly contagious and fast (page 19)
Fins visibly shorter or more ragged than last weekFin rot, actively progressing (page 18)
Grains of white salt across the body and finsIch (page 18)
Unable to stay upright, stuck floating or stuck on the bottomSwim bladder disorder (page 20)

Section 03 · Health & Common Issues

Finding a vet & what to tell them

Most betta problems are solved at the test kit. The ones that are not need somebody who has seen a fish before.

Fish veterinarians are far rarer than reptile or avian vets, and most betta problems genuinely are managed by correcting water quality rather than by treatment. That said, "rare" is not "nonexistent." Search for an aquatic veterinarian in your region now, while nothing is wrong, and write the number on page 24. Many will do a phone or video consult and advise on medication choice, which is often exactly what you need.

What to have ready when you call

  • Current ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and pH, with the test kit type
  • Current temperature, and whether it has been stable
  • Tank volume, and how long it has been running
  • Date of the last water change and how much you changed
  • What the fish is doing, and when it changed
  • What it is eating and how much, from the log on page 32
  • Anything added recently: decor, plants, food, a tankmate
  • Any medication already used, at what dose, and for how long
A hospital tank is worth having A bare 2 to 3 gallon (8 to 11 L) container with a spare heater and a sponge filter seeded in your main tank lets you treat a fish without dosing medication into your plants, your snails, and your bacteria colony. It is the cheapest piece of equipment on this list and the one most keepers wish they had bought before they needed it.
Quarantine everything wet New fish, plants, snails, and even decor from another tank can carry ich, velvet, and columnaris. Quarantine a new fish for 2 to 4 weeks in a separate container before it goes anywhere near an established tank, and rinse and inspect plants. Never move a net, a siphon, or a bucket between tanks without drying it fully in between.
While medicating anything Remove activated carbon from the filter, it adsorbs the medication out of the water. Keep the biological media in place, though. Do not combine medications unless a label or a vet says the combination is safe. Increase aeration, because most treatments reduce dissolved oxygen. And keep testing: medication can stall a bacteria colony and produce an ammonia spike on top of the illness.

Fin rot & ich

Fin rot

CauseA bacterial infection of the fin tissue, almost always secondary to poor water quality, chronic cold, or a physical fin injury from sharp decor or a strong current
SignsFin edges frayed, ragged, or torn-looking. Black, brown, or milky discoloration along the margins. Fins visibly shorter week to week. In advanced cases the erosion reaches the body and the fin rays themselves
ResponseTest and fix the water first, this is the actual cure in mild cases. Increase water change frequency. Remove anything sharp. Aquarium salt at 1 teaspoon per gallon (per 3.8 L), dissolved in a cup of tank water before it goes in, is the standard mild-case treatment. Advanced cases, or any case not improving after about a week, need an antibacterial medication or veterinary antibiotics
RecoveryRegrowth begins as a clear or pale membrane at the fin edge, and takes weeks to months to fill back in. Regrown fin often comes back a different shade, sometimes clear, which is normal. If the edges stop receding, it is working
Aquarium salt: the limits Do not run salt for more than about 10 days continuously, it is hard on the kidneys and liver. Salt does not evaporate, so only replace what you remove in a water change, dosed to the volume replaced. It also harms most live plants, snails, and shrimp, which is what the hospital tank on page 17 is for. And aquarium salt is not table salt: no iodine, no anti-caking agents.

Ich (white spot disease)

CauseThe parasite Ichthyophthirius multifiliis, usually introduced on a new fish, plant, or unquarantined decor, and triggered into an outbreak by a chill or a stress event
SignsSmall white grains, like scattered salt, across the body and fins. Flashing and scratching against decor, often days before the spots appear. Clamped fins, rapid gill movement, and appetite loss
ResponseRaise the temperature gradually, no more than 1 to 2°F (0.5 to 1°C) per hour, to around 82°F (28°C), which speeds the parasite through the only stage of its life cycle that medication can reach. Add a copper or formalin-based ich treatment and follow the full course. Warmer water holds less oxygen, so increase surface agitation while the temperature is up
RecoverySpots fall away over roughly a week. Keep dosing for the full labeled course after the last visible spot, because the free-swimming stage is what you are actually killing. Then bring the temperature back down as slowly as you raised it
Why the temperature trick works Ich is only vulnerable while it is swimming free between hosts. Encysted on the fish, it is shielded from medication; in its reproductive stage on the substrate it is shielded too. Warmth shortens the whole cycle so more of the population passes through that exposed window while your treatment is in the water.

Velvet & columnaris

The two fastest killers on this list. Both are survivable if you catch them in the first day or two.

Velvet (gold dust disease)

CauseOodinium, a parasitic dinoflagellate. Usually introduced on a new fish and set off by stress, chilling, or poor water quality
SignsA fine gold, rust, or bronze dust over the body, easiest to see with a flashlight held at an angle in a darkened room. Clamped fins, flashing against decor, rapid gill movement, appetite loss, and lethargy. Often mistaken for ich, but the particles are far finer and the sheen is metallic rather than white
ResponseTreat immediately, this one kills in days. Darken the tank completely: the parasite is partly photosynthetic and light feeds it. Dose a copper-based or antiparasitic velvet treatment for the full labeled course. Raise the temperature slightly if the label calls for it, and increase surface agitation. Remove carbon from any filter while medicating, it strips the medication straight back out
RecoveryThe dust fades over several days. Keep the tank dark and finish the course after the last visible sign. Gills take the most damage, so expect laboured breathing to be the last symptom to resolve

Columnaris

CauseFlavobacterium columnare, a bacterium that is highly contagious between fish and between tanks on shared equipment. It becomes far more aggressive in warm water, which is what makes it so dangerous in a tropical tank
SignsWhite or greyish cottony patches, classically a saddle-shaped band across the back, or around the mouth, which is where the old name "mouth fungus" comes from. Frayed fin edges, ulcers, and pale gills. It is not a fungus, and it moves far faster than fin rot: hours to days rather than weeks
ResponseLower the temperature toward the bottom of the betta's tolerable range, around 76°F (24.5°C), gradually. This is the opposite of the ich response and getting it backwards accelerates the infection. Isolate the fish. Treat with an antibacterial medication effective against gram-negative bacteria, or veterinary antibiotics. Disinfect nets, siphons, and buckets before they touch another tank
RecoveryImprovement should be visible within a couple of days of correct treatment. The aggressive strains can kill within 24 to 48 hours, so if there is no change on day two, escalate rather than waiting out the course
Ich or velvet or columnaris? Distinct white grains, like salt, evenly scattered: ich. A fine metallic gold or rust sheen best seen under a torch: velvet. A white or grey cottony patch, often saddled across the back or around the mouth, spreading visibly by the hour: columnaris. When you are unsure between ich and velvet, treat as velvet, since it is the faster of the two and the treatments overlap. Never guess between ich and columnaris, because the temperature instructions point in opposite directions.

Swim bladder, dropsy & mycobacteriosis

Swim bladder disorder

CauseUsually overfeeding or constipation, a swollen gut pressing on the swim bladder. Sometimes infection, injury, or a defect in heavily bred strains
SignsBuoyancy problems: floating at the surface, sinking to the bottom, listing to one side, or swimming at an odd angle. Often with a visibly bloated belly and stringy or absent feces
ResponseFast for 2 to 3 days. Confirm the temperature is in range, since cold slows digestion. Then offer one small piece of blanched, shelled pea, or daphnia, to move things along. Lower the water level slightly if the fish struggles to reach the surface, and add a resting spot near the top
RecoveryMild constipation cases resolve in a few days. When they do, go back to page 10 and cut the portion permanently, because it will recur otherwise. Cases that persist beyond a week, or come with other symptoms, are more likely infection or a structural problem and need a vet

Dropsy

CauseNot a disease in itself. It is the visible result of fluid building up inside the body from organ failure, most often the kidneys, usually secondary to long-term infection or chronic poor conditions
SignsA swollen belly with the scales standing out from the body, giving the classic pinecone look when viewed from above. Lethargy, appetite loss, and often bulging eyes
ResponseIsolate, keep the water pristine and the temperature stable, and speak to an aquatic vet. By the time the pinecone sign appears the underlying failure is usually advanced and the prognosis poor. Humane euthanasia advised by a vet is a legitimate and kind outcome
RecoveryUncommon. What dropsy is genuinely useful for is as a warning about the tank: it is a long-term-conditions disease, so review pages 6 to 9 carefully before adding another fish

Mycobacteriosis (fish tuberculosis)

CauseMycobacterium species, present at low levels in many aquariums and becoming a problem in stressed or immunocompromised fish. Rare, but worth knowing because it is zoonotic
SignsSlow decline: wasting despite eating, a curved or S-shaped spine, open sores, faded color, lost scales. Weeks to months rather than days
ResponseThere is no reliable cure in home aquaria. Isolate the fish, keep the equipment for that tank strictly separate, and talk to a vet about prognosis and humane euthanasia. Do not move fish or equipment from that tank into another one
RecoveryRare. Manage this one as a containment problem rather than a treatment problem
Zoonotic: protect yourself Mycobacterium marinum transmits to people through aquarium water, especially through broken skin, causing a stubborn infection sometimes called fish tank granuloma. Wear waterproof gloves if you have cuts or a suspected case in the tank, wash your hands after every session regardless, and never siphon by mouth. If a persistent lesion appears on a hand or forearm, tell your doctor you keep an aquarium: it changes what they test for.

Flaring, and what to do about it

Flaring is the full threat display: gill covers spread, fins fanned, body turned side-on. It is normal behavior and a fish that never flares is not necessarily happier. What matters is what it is aimed at, and how often.

  • A few seconds at a passing shadow, at you, or at a new decor item is ordinary and passes.
  • Repeated flaring at one spot on the glass is a reflection. Move the light, put something behind the tank, or reduce the contrast between a bright tank and a dark room.
  • Flaring that runs for long stretches every day costs the fish energy and appetite. Treat it as a stressor to remove, not a display to enjoy.
  • A mirror is not enrichment. If you want to see the display once, a few seconds is enough, and it belongs at the bottom of the list on page 14.
The order to check things in Whatever the behavior, the sequence is the same: water first, temperature second, then what changed in or around the tank in the last week, then the fish itself. Working in that order stops you treating a fish for an illness it does not have.

Tankmates

A betta does not need company and is not lonely alone. Anything you add is for you.

Anything you add is a risk you are choosing to take on the fish's behalf, so add it deliberately or not at all. The absolute rules are short: one male per tank, never two, and never a male with a female outside a supervised, deliberate breeding attempt.

OptionVerdict
Alone, in a planted 5 to 10 gal (19 to 38 L)Recommended The default, and the setup this whole package is written around
Nerite snail or mystery snailUsually fine In 5 gal and up. Some bettas pick at snail antennae, so watch the first week
Amano or ghost shrimpRisky 10 gal and up with heavy cover. Some bettas treat shrimp as food and there is no undoing that
Small schooling fish, corydorasRisky 15 to 20 gal (57 to 76 L) minimum, never fin-nippers, and only with a genuine plan for separating them
Another male bettaNever They will fight until one dies. There is no tank size that solves this
A "sorority" of femalesNot for beginners Frequently ends in serious injury. The group-housing research is about fish reared together from hatching, not adults introduced to each other
Guppies or other long-finned fishNever A betta reads flowing fins as a rival, and guppies get shredded
If you do add anything Quarantine it for 2 to 4 weeks first (page 17), add it to the betta's tank rather than the other way around only if you must, rearrange the decor first so the territory feels new to both, and have a second cycled container genuinely ready before the introduction, not planned for afterward. Watch continuously for the first hour and then daily for a week.

Signs an introduction is failing

  • The betta shadows or follows the newcomer continuously rather than losing interest after the first hour.
  • Nipped fins, missing scales, or a snail withdrawn into its shell and staying there.
  • Either animal permanently occupying one corner, or the betta unable to reach the surface without passing a confrontation.
  • The betta stops eating, or its color fades, in the days after the addition.

Any of these means separating them, not waiting to see. Damage in a small tank happens over hours, and a betta that has decided something is a rival does not usually change its mind.

Power outages & the blackout plan

A betta breathes air, which buys you far more time in a blackout than most fish get. Temperature is the thing that will actually get you.

When the power goes out

Do

  • For the first hour or two, do nothing. A single betta in 5 gallons is a very light bioload and the water holds fine.
  • Insulate. Wrap the tank in towels or blankets, keep the lid on, and leave the front uncovered so you can still see the fish.
  • Heat gently: a sealed hot-water bottle or a hand warmer taped to the outside of the glass, checking the thermometer as you go.
  • From about the 8 hour mark, restore oxygen for an hour, then repeat every 8 hours. A battery or USB air pump on a power bank does it; otherwise scoop water out and pour it back from a few inches up, repeating every 10 to 15 minutes.
  • Unplug the filter at the wall so it cannot flush stagnant, rotting media into the tank when power returns.
  • Stop feeding entirely, and keep the tank dark and quiet.

Don't

  • Don't feed. Food raises oxygen demand and adds ammonia to water with no filter running.
  • Don't put a heat source in the water or in direct contact with the fish.
  • Don't do a water change during the outage, and don't restart a filter that has sat unpowered for hours until you have rinsed the media in tank water.
  • Don't let the recovery be abrupt: bring temperature back gradually, then test ammonia and nitrite before you feed again.
Blackout kit A battery or USB air pump with a spare air stone, a power bank, hand warmers, two thick towels, a jug, and the numbers from page 24.

Rough timeline

From 8 hoursRun oxygen for an hour, then repeat every 8 hours. If temperature has fallen more than a few degrees, start gentle external warming

Travel, transport & moving

A betta travels better than most fish, because it can breathe the air in the bag.

Leaving town

Trip lengthWhat to do
1 to 3 nightsFine alone. A healthy adult skips meals routinely and one fasting day is already part of the schedule. Water change the day before you leave, check the heater, and go. Do not use a holiday feeder block, which fouls water badly in a small tank.
4 to 7 nightsA sitter every 2 to 3 days to feed the pre-portioned meals, read the thermometer, and look at the fish. Or an automatic feeder loaded with exact portions, plus one sitter visit mid-trip.
Over a weekA sitter every 2 to 3 days who has done a practice visit with you, including one water change if the trip runs past a week. Leave page 31 filled in and the meals pre-portioned.

Transport and moving

  • Short trips, under about 6 hours: a bag or a sealed container, one third to halfway full with the fish's own tank water, the rest air. Bettas breathe from the surface, so the air gap is the point.
  • Longer trips: a lidded bucket with an air stone on a battery pump holds temperature and oxygen far better than a bag. A shop can also fill a transport bag with pure oxygen, which extends the safe window considerably.
  • Insulate the container in a cooler or a padded box, keep it out of direct sun, and do not run the car heater or air conditioning straight onto it. Do not feed for 24 hours before travel.
  • Moving house: transport as much of the original tank water and the filter sponge as you can, in separate sealed containers, keeping the sponge submerged the whole time. That sponge is your cycle. Set the tank up and get it warm before the fish goes back in, and test daily for the first week.

The first week after a move

Test ammonia and nitrite daily. Moving disturbs the substrate and the media, and a mini-cycle is common
Skip the first day's feeding, then feed lightly for several days
Check the heater is holding: a new room is a new ambient temperature
Expect faded color, hiding, and a refused meal or two. Give it a week before treating it as a symptom
Leave the decor arranged the way it was, if you can. Familiar layout helps a fish settle
Keep the tank out of direct sun and away from a draft or a radiator in the new spot
Never Move a betta in a sealed bag with no air space, transport it in water it has not been living in, or leave the container in a parked car. And never set the tank up with entirely fresh water because the old water "looked dirty." The filter sponge carries the cycle, but the tank water buys you a gentler transition.

Enrichment checklist & log

Setup

Dense planting, live where possible, soft silk where not
A surface resting spot: leaf hammock or a broad-leaved plant near the top
Driftwood or decor positioned to break the tank into zones and cut sightlines
At least one cave or fully enclosed hide with a smooth entrance

Monthly review

Move or add one item, without blocking the route to the surface
Rotate the food and the feeding spot, and try one new safe item from page 12
Check the fish is using more of the tank than last month, not less
Run a hand over every decor item. If it would snag a stocking, it will tear a fin

What not to do Don't use a mirror as a daily routine: what you are provoking is a threat display, and it is what researchers use to generate a measurable stress response. Don't use stiff plastic plants. Don't add so much decor that the fish cannot reach the surface, because air access outranks everything on this page. And don't change three things at once, or you will never know which one the fish responded to.

Glossary

Ammonia (NH3)The first and most toxic product of fish waste and decaying food. Target is 0 ppm, always.
Bubble nestA raft of saliva-coated air bubbles a male builds at the surface to hold eggs. Captive males build them with no female present, and it generally signals a comfortable fish.
ChloramineChlorine bonded to ammonia, used by many water utilities. It does not gas off the way chlorine does, so the dechlorinator has to be one that handles it.
Clamped finsFins held tight against the body instead of spread. One of the earliest visible signs of stress or a water problem.
CycledA tank whose bacteria colonies convert a 2 ppm ammonia dose to 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite within 24 hours. The single prerequisite for adding a fish.
DropsyNot a disease but a sign: fluid retention from organ failure, showing as a swollen body with scales standing out like a pinecone.
FlashingScratching or rubbing against decor and substrate. Usually the first sign of a parasite, often days before anything is visible.
GH and KHGeneral hardness (dissolved minerals) and carbonate hardness (buffering capacity). Secondary parameters, but very low KH lets pH crash after a water change.
Hospital tankA bare container with a spare heater and a seeded sponge, used to treat a fish without dosing medication into plants, snails, and the main filter colony.
Labyrinth organA folded, vascularized structure above the gills that lets a betta breathe air from the surface. It is why surface access is a physiological requirement.
Nitrite (NO2)The middle step of the nitrogen cycle. Blocks oxygen uptake in the blood. Target is 0 ppm.
Nitrate (NO3)The end product of a healthy cycle. Mildly toxic and removed by water changes. Keep under 20 ppm.
Ovipositor (egg spot)A small white spot between the ventral and anal fins on a female, the tube eggs pass through. A male can show a protruding vent that resembles it, so it is suggestive rather than conclusive.
ppmParts per million, the unit every test kit on page 8 reports in. Equivalent to milligrams per liter.
Seeded mediaFilter sponge or gravel taken from an established, healthy tank, carrying a live bacteria colony. The fastest legitimate way to shorten a cycle.
Slime coatThe mucus layer over a fish's body, an active barrier against infection and parasites. Bare hands strip it, which is why bettas are moved by net or cup.
Sponge filterAn air-driven filter whose sponge is the surface the bacteria live on. The standard for bettas because it filters without producing current.
Stress stripesDark horizontal bands appearing along a stressed betta's body. Distinct from the vertical bars a receptive female shows.
Swim bladderThe gas-filled organ controlling buoyancy. When compressed by a swollen gut, the fish floats, sinks, or lists.
