# Common Black Skirt Tetra Health Issues (and Why Ich Heat Stops at 79°F)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

The black skirt tetra (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi), also sold as the black widow tetra, comes from the Rio Paraguay basin of South America, and every fish in the trade today is captive bred. Generations of tank breeding have made it relaxed about water chemistry, but not about disease. It gets ich, fin rot and dactylogyrid gill flukes, and it is a recorded host of both mycobacteriosis and a microsporidian infection first described in it in 2021. Ich in this fish can turn fatal in two days.

## Signs That Need Action Today

A quick count at feeding time is the daily check on a school of 12. These are the signs that set the order of what happens next.

**Test the water first:**

- Two or more fish off their food or moving slowly at the same time.
- A fish found dead. Take the body out of the tank before anything else.
- The whole school gone sluggish and eating less. Read the thermometer along with the test kit, since a cold tank slows every fish in it.
- Fish piping at the surface or coughing. If the water tests clean, gill flukes are one cause.

**Start treatment today:**

- White spots on the body or fins.
- A fish that turns darker, keeps apart from the school and refuses food while the water tests clean.

**Watch and check again tomorrow:**

- A torn or ragged fin on a single fish.
- One fish eating less than the others but otherwise behaving normally.
- A slow run of losses, one fish at a time, with no clear cause.

Each fish on the watch list gets a close look every day until the sign clears or turns into one from the lists above. A slow run of losses is the pattern two of the diseases further down leave behind, and it is the point to involve a fish vet.

A fading black is not a sign of illness on its own. The black on a young skirt tetra is at its deepest early on and fades toward silvery gray as the fish ages, and from day to day it can fade or deepen with the fish's mood. A fish that stays darker and sits apart from the rest is a different matter.

## Water Quality Comes First

Captive breeding has given this species an easy range: 68 to 79°F (20 to 26°C), pH 6.0 to 7.5 and 5 to 19 dGH. Holding the tank steady inside it matters more than any one number. A tank that has finished cycling, the single most important step when it is first set up, and a liquid test kit kept beside it answer the first group of signs above before anything gets treated. Several fish turning lethargic, going off their food and dying in a new tank is the classic picture of a cycle that has not caught up.

## How Cold Is Too Cold

White skirt tetras, the pale form of the same species, have been cooled by 1°C a day from 68°F (20°C) to find where they fail. At about 63°F (17°C) they fed and moved less. Close to 54°F (12°C) they stopped eating, they stopped moving just above 50°F (10°C), and at 9.95°C, a fraction under 50°F, they lost their balance.

The bottom of the normal range sits only 5°F above the point where appetite drops, so a failed heater in a cool house can take the tank into that zone. A chilled fish is also an easier target for water molds, as the fin section below explains.

## Ich: Fast in This Fish, Treated Below 86°F

Ich, or white spot disease, can kill a black skirt tetra fast, as a 2025 case report shows. A batch of fluorescent widow tetras bought at a market in India was found heavily infected. The sick fish darkened, separated from the group, slowed down and quit eating. Fins had broken down, gill filaments were gone entirely, and the fish died within 48 hours of the first symptoms. The water at the time was 73°F (23°C) at pH 7.5, well inside the range the species is kept in, so a tank in good order does not hold ich off once an infected fish arrives.

That darkening fits the wider ich picture, along with lethargy and clamped fins, and losses can be rapid and catastrophic. When the parasite sits only on the gills there may be no spots at all, just a sudden run of deaths. It spreads by direct contact with infected fish and on nets and other shared gear, and survivors can pass it to fish that never had it.

Most strains of ich die in water above 86°F, but not every fish can stand that much heat. The black skirt's range ends at 79°F, 7°F short of that, so heat is a helper here and not the cure. Breeders do run a spawning pair at 82 to 86°F for a short stay, but no source shows a school coping with the days at 86°F an ich cure needs. Heat still helps up to 79°F, because the parasite multiplies faster in warmer water and the course of treatment moves along faster with it. Raise the tank slowly to 79°F, run an air stone, since extra oxygen is essential whenever the water is warmed, and let aquarium salt or an ich medication kill the parasite. Over-the-counter ich medications built on formalin and malachite green are effective, and at warm aquarium temperatures they go in daily, often three to seven times, as [the warm-then-treat course for fish below 86°F](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/) sets out. Cysts on the bottom shrug off the drugs, so a thorough gravel clean between doses removes them. Treatment repeated over several doses, together with the raised temperature, is how outbreaks are brought under control.

Salt has one exception in this species. In a tank holding newly hatched fry, salt at 1 and 3 parts per thousand killed black skirt fry in large numbers while they still carried their yolk sacs, yet barely touched fry that were already swimming. A breeding tank with young fry in it is treated with something else, or the sick adults move to a hospital tank. The stronger salt baths vets list for ich are meant for species known to tolerate them, and adult black skirts have never been tested.

## Fin Rot and Fungus: Damage First, Then Infection

The black skirt has a reputation as a fin nipper, mostly toward slow or long-finned tankmates. A school of 12 is the usual fix, since the fish then mostly keep their squabbling among themselves. Which tankmates are safe with it, and which to leave out, comes down to [temperament and pairing](/blog/black-skirt-tetra-handling-guide/).

Fin rot affects this species at both an early and a late stage, so a torn fin is worth watching until it either regrows or starts to erode. The difference between a clean tear and fin tissue being lost to a bacterial infection is what decides whether to treat. Ich can strip fins too, so a fish with ragged fins and white spots goes on the ich treatment first. Fin damage is also among the signs of mycobacteriosis, which makes a ragged fin on a fish that is also wasting a case for the section on lookalikes below.

Cottony growth on skin or fins is usually a water mold such as Saprolegnia, a fungus-like organism that settles on tissue already hurt by an injury or poor water, and low temperature is one of its triggers. Correct the water and the temperature first, then move the fish to the hospital tank, since the mold is a secondary problem and the original one still needs fixing.

## Internal Parasites and Gill Flukes

Capillaria and its relatives live in the gut of tetras, among other aquarium fish, and cause weight loss and a pot belly. Camallanus shows as red worms protruding from the vent, and Daphnia and Cyclops carry it as intermediate hosts, so the live daphnia this species enjoys should come from a reputable source. Tapeworms respond best to praziquantel. Organophosphates are a class of treatment kept away from some characins, a group the black skirt belongs to.

Gill flukes are the parasite most closely tied to this fish. A dactylogyrid fluke, Diaphorocleidus armillatus, was described as a new species from black skirt tetras kept as introduced aquarium fish in India, and later turned up on their gills there again. Fish carrying flukes pipe at the surface, cough and lose weight, and high numbers can cause catastrophic losses. Praziquantel and formalin are the treatments, and fresh smears or a gill biopsy under a microscope are how a vet tells flukes from ich or a water problem.

## Neon Tetra Disease and Its Lookalikes

The parasite behind neon tetra disease, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, is a microsporidian whose primary host is the neon tetra. It has been reported from some 20 species of freshwater fish in four orders. No report so far names the black skirt among them, which neither rules it in nor out. The microsporidian on record in this species is a different one.

### Tetra Disseminated Microsporidiosis

In 2021 researchers described a new microsporidian disease in two aquarium fish, the black tetra and the cardinal tetra. The parasite, named Fusasporis stethaprioni and placed in a new genus, spreads through most of the internal organs and into the gills. In black tetras it was linked to a steady, drawn-out run of deaths in a farmed population, while in cardinals it turned up in dying fish held in quarantine at a public aquarium.

It is a separate parasite from the one behind neon tetra disease, though both are microsporidians. The pattern it leaves in a tank is the slow trickle of losses on the watch list above, and that pattern is the point to take a fish to a vet who treats fish.

### Mycobacteriosis

The black tetra is a documented host of mycobacteriosis. Mycobacteria were described in it as early as 1940, and it appeared again among the tuberculosis-like cases in ornamental fish logged by a diagnostic team between 1995 and 2004. The signs in those fish ran from loss of appetite, lethargy, staying alone in a corner and floating at the surface to wasting, lost color, severe popeye, a swollen belly, raised or missing scales, skin ulcers and fin damage. One of the species involved, Mycobacterium smegmatis, has been recorded in black tetras and can infect people.

Outbreaks follow crowding and poor water, low pH and low dissolved oxygen above all, and fish can pick it up by eating dead tankmates. Infections respond poorly to antibiotics, no vaccine exists, and an affected group often has to be removed from the system altogether. The disease cannot be detected with nonlethal tests.

The early signs overlap with ich. A fish that turns darker, keeps apart and refuses food fits both, and the difference shows within days: ich brings white spots and kills fast, while mycobacteriosis is slow and moves on to wasting, scale loss, ulcers or popeye.

## White, Long-Fin, GloFish and Dyed Skirt Tetras

The plain black, white and long-fin forms are all the same fish. White skirts were produced by selecting a naturally occurring pale trait, and long fins are a naturally occurring trait bred up for the aquarium trade. No study has looked at the health of long-fin skirts, but long, trailing fins draw fin nippers, so a long-fin form gets the same calm company any long-finned fish needs. GloFish Skirt Tetras are this species too. No formal study has compared their disease risk with ordinary skirts, and the case that they are no more prone rests on the company's own veterinary statements.

Artificially dyed skirt tetras are a health problem from the start. The color is injected, or applied by stripping the slime coat with acid and then soaking the fish in dye, the process puts the fish under heavy stress, and it often cuts their lives considerably short. The color fades with time anyway. The plain black, white and long-fin forms are the same fish without the dye.

## Copper, Soft Water and Shrimp

Black skirts are not a soft-water fish, but a tank run at the low end of their range, near pH 6.0 on soft tap water, can still fall below the line where copper turns dangerous. The lower the total alkalinity, the harder copper hits fish, and once it drops below 50 ppm, about 2.8 dKH, the chance of a kill is high enough that copper treatment is not recommended. The alkalinity has to be known before any copper goes in. A strong treatment can also knock back the filter bacteria, and a temporary rise in ammonia and nitrite should be expected for weeks to months afterward, which puts the test kit back on daily duty.

Copper is extremely toxic to invertebrates and plants, which come out before the water is treated. Shrimp and snails leave the tank before any medication at all goes in, whatever the label claims, and the hospital tank is where the treatment happens.

## Quarantine Every New Fish

Every new black skirt lives [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before it joins the school, and quarantine is the single most important step for every fish added. A month is long enough for ich to show, and it gives a slow disease a chance to surface where only the newcomers are exposed. Thirty days is the minimum: a valuable fish is better held 30 to 60 days. Quarantine cannot catch everything, since mycobacteria cannot be detected without killing the fish, but that is no reason to skip the month.

## When a Fish Vet Is Worth It, and How to Find One

Gill flukes are confirmed under a microscope, and mycobacteriosis and the microsporidian in a lab. Wrong guesses with store-bought medicine are common, and stacking a second product on a failed first usually does the fish more harm than good.

Licenses do not cross state lines, but a vet anywhere can bring an aquatic specialist from another state onto the case, so the practice that sees the household's other animals can take a fish with that help. The American Association of Fish Veterinarians runs a finder that sorts fish vets by state or country, and for keepers beyond North America a fish vet's own guide suggests the World Aquatic Veterinary Medical Association. A treatment plan cannot legally rest on an online chat with no exam.

For the visit:

- **Water.** About a quart of tank water, drawn when the fish first act abnormally, before any water change, and kept cold. A water sample goes with every fish.
- **The fish.** A live fish near death tells a vet the most. A freshly dead one goes in a sturdy plastic bag on ice, and a fish that has been in the freezer is of limited use.
- **The history.** Tank size and age, how many fish of which species, recent additions, when the signs began, the food, the date of the last water change, and every treatment so far with its dose.

A black skirt that eats eagerly, swims with the school and keeps its fins whole in steady water is a healthy fish. Most of what goes wrong with the species arrives with a new fish or follows a slip in the water, and both can be caught early.
