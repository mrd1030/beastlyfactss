# Common Glowlight Tetra Health Issues (and Why the Ich Heat Cure Stops at 82°F)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

The glowlight tetra is a fish of about 1.6 inches from the Essequibo River in Guyana, and no disease belongs to it in particular. The problems recorded for it are the short list every small community tetra shares: fin rot, white spot, parasitic infestations and general bacterial infections. Where it differs from some relatives is temperature. The top of its range sits below the heat that kills ich, and that changes how the disease is treated, while the soft, acidic water that brings out its color is also where copper medicines do the most damage.

## Signs That Need Action Today

A head count at feeding time is the daily check in a school of 10. These are the signs that cannot wait for tomorrow.

**Test ammonia and nitrite first:**

- Fish gasping at the surface or breathing fast.
- Two or more fish showing the same sign at once.
- A fish found dead. Take the body out of the tank before anything else.

**Start treatment today:**

- White spots like grains of salt on the body or fins.
- Fins that look shortened, ragged or reddened along the edges, worse than the day before.
- Fast breathing that continues once the water tests clean, since ich can infect the gills.

**Watch closely and check again tomorrow:**

- Flashing: rubbing against plants, glass or substrate.
- A fish off its food or hanging listless.
- Fins held clamped against the body.
- Color fading to dull.

The last group are early signs that fit several problems, ich among them, so a fish showing them gets a close look every day until they pass. Gasping and a lost appetite are as often the water as a parasite, which is why [telling a water problem from an illness](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) starts at the test kit. A fish with an open ulcer, or illness moving through several fish in a tank, is past home observation and needs an aquatic veterinarian.

One behavior is easy to misread. Glowlights flick their fins as a visual alarm signal to the rest of the school when they detect a chemical alarm cue in the water, which is a different movement from flashing, where a fish rubs its body against objects.

The tank sets the baseline for color: glowlights show the most in acidic, dimly lit, tea-tinted water. The albino strain, widely sold, is cared for exactly like the standard form.

## Water Quality Comes First

Glowlights do best at 75 to 82°F, pH 5.5 to 7.5 and 2 to 15 dGH, and the stripe is more colorful in acidic water. Holding the tank steady inside those ranges matters more than hitting one number. A liquid test kit used before each water change catches a drift early, and in soft water [KH is what holds pH still](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/), the same reading that decides whether copper is ever an option.

Food is the other half. A glowlight takes micropellets or small flakes, and light portions keep uneaten food from fouling the water.

## Ich: Warm to the Ceiling, Then Treat

Ich, or white spot disease, is highly contagious and can kill, and it usually reaches a tank through infected water or a newly added fish. Every freshwater fish is considered susceptible. Outbreaks tend to start when the water temperature is changing quickly and hit hardest in crowded tanks. The spots tend to show long after the infection starts, so the watch list above is often the first warning. Because the parasite multiplies explosively, a fish carrying even one is treated straight away, and an outbreak left alone can kill every fish in the tank.

Most strains of ich cannot survive water above 86°F, and for a fish that lives comfortably that warm, holding the tank there clears an outbreak. The cardinal tetra tolerates water reaching 86°F in short spells, so for it the heat method is possible only with strong aeration and the fish watched closely. The glowlight's range ends at 82°F, four degrees short, so the heat method is not for it. Heat still helps up to the ceiling, because the parasite multiplies faster in warm water and a faster cycle brings each new generation into reach of the treatment sooner. At 75 to 79°F the whole cycle takes about three to six days, so treatment goes in daily, at least three to five times. Raise the tank slowly to 82°F, using a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">aquarium heater</AffiliateLink>, with an air stone running for the extra oxygen warm water needs, and let one of these do the killing:

- **Aquarium salt**, never table salt, at about a tablespoon per 5 gallons, added gradually. It suits scaled fish like this one for the length of the course only, and water changes take it back out once the infection clears. Stronger, longer salt baths are given only to species known to handle them, and no salt tolerance has been measured for the glowlight. Live plants may not tolerate raised salt either.
- **An ich medication**, dosed to the label or to a vet's directions, and in soft water one without copper, for the reasons in the copper section below. Heat and salt are weaker than a chemical treatment, because resistant strains can develop. Potassium permanganate is not recommended for ich.

Keep treating until every sign of illness has gone and the deaths have stopped, following [the full warm-then-treat course](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/) for fish whose ceiling falls short of 86°F. Survivors of an outbreak can stay carriers of the parasite, a reason to watch a recovered school for weeks after the spots clear.

## Fin Rot and Fungus: Damage First, Then Infection

Fin rot, in early and late stages, is on the species' recorded list. It begins as fraying at the fin edge and works inward. Treatment starts with correcting the water that let it take hold. A fish still losing fin after that needs an antibacterial medication, given in a hospital tank away from the display.

Cottony growths are a separate problem. Water molds such as Saprolegnia are fungus-like organisms that invade tissue already damaged by an injury, poor water or low temperature, so they are a second problem sitting on a first. A deeper fungal infection has also been described in a close relative, the pretty tetra: fish with a fungus in the swim bladder went off their food, developed popeye, swam erratically, lost scales, showed pale gills and swelled at the belly, and the fungi probably came in on wood decor and plants.

General bacterial infections and parasitic infestations by protozoa or worms complete the list. Sores and red patches on the body are the cue to get a diagnosis before choosing a medication.

## Internal Parasites

The worms and protozoa on the glowlight's list mostly show as a fish that thins or loses interest in food. Camallanus is the visible one, red worms protruding from the vent, and it travels inside Cyclops and Daphnia, so live crustaceans are a way in. Fenbendazole or levamisole treats it. Spironucleus causes weight loss and a lost appetite and is treated with metronidazole, and praziquantel is the drug for tapeworms. A wet mount or smear under a microscope is what tells them apart, which makes the drug a vet's or a lab's call.

## Neon Tetra Disease and Its Lookalikes

Neon tetra disease is caused by a microsporidian parasite, *Pleistophora hyphessobryconis*, named for its type host, the neon tetra. It has turned up in a broad range of aquarium fish across several families, some 20 freshwater species in four orders, so the name does not fence it in. A close relative in the same genus, Hemigrammus ocellifer, was recorded with it as early as 1942, though no report names the glowlight itself.

Fish catch it by eating spores. Spores released from dead fish are a likely source, and spawning can release them too, into eggs that tankmates are quick to eat. A dead fish comes out of the tank the moment it is found, whatever killed it, which also stops the ich parasites on it from leaving to multiply in the water.

Mycobacteriosis is the other disease that can pass for it. Tetras are among the families more prone to the infection, and its signs are variable and nonspecific, wasting, paleness and skeletal deformity among them. There is no effective treatment for an infected fish, so a glowlight that wastes away with no clear cause is a case for a lab and not a guessed medication.

## Copper, Soft Water and Shrimp

Copper sulfate is toxic to fish, above all in water of low alkalinity, and it is not to be used at all when total alkalinity is under 50 mg/L, about 2.8 dKH, or when the alkalinity is unknown. A glowlight kept soft and acidic for its color is the low-alkalinity case. Characins are the copper-sensitive group: in ion-poor Amazon water, all four characin species tested were more sensitive to copper than the catfish and cichlids, and the most sensitive was the rummy-nose tetra, another Hemigrammus at the time of the test.

Most invertebrates are highly sensitive to copper and will not survive a treatment. Shrimp sharing the tank come out before any medication goes in, whatever the label says, and treating in the hospital tank keeps every drug away from the display.

## Quarantine Every New Fish

New fish are the usual route for ich into an established tank, and keeping the parasite out is one of the main reasons every incoming fish is quarantined. In warm aquarium water, active disease in shipped fish often shows one to three weeks after arrival, so every new glowlight spends [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before it meets the school. Thirty days is the minimum, and longer periods may be needed. Quarantine is the single most important step for every fish added.

Nets and siphon hoses are not shared with the main tank unless they have been disinfected, and plants that sat with infected fish can carry the parasite's cyst stage across.

## When a Fish Vet Is Worth It, and How to Find One

Fish are often treated with the wrong over-the-counter medication, and a string of treatments tried one after another usually does more harm than good. The parasites, the bacteria and the fungi above are each identified under a microscope or in a lab, which is the work a fish vet does.

Licenses keep a vet to one state, but consultations do not: any vet can bring in an aquatic vet from any state, so a nearby exotic or small-animal clinic is a good first call. The American Association of Fish Veterinarians lists fish vets by state or country, and a practicing fish vet's guide points keepers outside North America to the World Aquatic Veterinary Medical Association. Online advice with no exam cannot form a legal treatment plan.

Bring:

- About a quart of tank water, collected as soon as the fish begin to act abnormally and before any water change, and kept cold. The water from a transport bag does not stand in for it.
- A live sick fish where possible. Dead fish are seldom useful, and frozen ones are not.
- The tank's record: size and age, the species and numbers, recent additions, when the signs began, the food and amounts, the last water change, and any treatment with its dose.

A glowlight that is eating, showing a bright stripe and moving with its school in steady water is a healthy one. Most of what goes wrong with the species begins in that water or arrives with the next fish.
