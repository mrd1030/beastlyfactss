# Glowlight Tetra Tank Setup: A 24 x 15 Inch Base, Acidic Water, and Planted Cover

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Glowlight tetras come from forested tributaries of the Essequibo River in Guyana, where the water is stained dark brown by tannins and often strongly acidic. An adult reaches about 1.6 inches (4 cm), and a tank for a school of 10 is built around floor space, soft acidic water and dense plants.

## Tank Size: A 24 x 15 Inch Base for Ten

Buy for the base. The tank for a school of 10 has a floor of at least 24 x 15 inches (60 x 38 cm), which works out to roughly 18 to 20 gallons. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">A 20-gallon long</AffiliateLink> covers the same floor area as that 24 x 15 base, so it fits the school too.

A 10-gallon tank is sometimes sold as enough for these fish, and it is the size for a group of six. Six is the least that works, and 10 is the school to buy. A school of that size fills the middle of the tank and, the larger it gets, works lower toward the floor. The larger base leaves that water open instead of crowding the fish against the glass and the plants.

## Temperature: 75 to 82°F, Held Steady

75 to 82°F (24 to 28°C) is the range, held by <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">a submersible heater</AffiliateLink>. Set the heater inside the band, since 82°F is the top of it and nothing is gained by running closer.

That is the same band as an ember tetra tank. A neon tetra tank runs cooler, at 70 to 77°F, so the two share only 75 to 77°F.

## Water Chemistry: The Acidic Side for Color

The target is a pH of 5.5 to 7.5 and a general hardness of 2 to 15 dGH. Captive-bred glowlights settle into a wide range of conditions and survive slightly alkaline water, but the fish color up best at the acidic end of the pH range, so a tank that lands near the middle or below is the better outcome than one pushed toward 7.5.

Test the tap water before the tank is filled. Water outside those numbers moves by [adjusting GH and KH together](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/), which carries the pH with it; a pH-down product on its own drags the number briefly and then lets it drift back. Every fill and water change is treated with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rg5gTT" product="Water conditioner">water conditioner</AffiliateLink> that removes chlorine and chloramine.

## Filtration: Four to Five Turnovers an Hour

Size the filter to turn the tank's whole volume over four to five times an hour, which for an 18-gallon tank means a filter rated at roughly 70 to 90 gallons an hour. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> sized for the tank is the simple choice. It does its work as a home for the bacteria that process waste, so the biological media is the part to check when choosing a size, and its output leaves the open water in the middle of the tank calm. A hang-on-back works as well.

## Substrate: A Thin Bed of River Sand

River sand is the floor for this species, with a few driftwood branches laid across it, and a thin layer of fine sand is all the bed needs to be.

The bed should be inert. A glowlight survives slightly alkaline water, so a calcareous floor is less dangerous here than in a cardinal tank, but it still drags the water away from the acidic side where the stripe looks its best. Even standard pea gravel carries enough calcium to raise pH and hardness, and coral sand pushes the pH past 8.0, beyond the 7.5 top of this fish's range.

About 1 inch (2.5 cm) of sand is plenty, since sand this fine tends to stagnate in a deep layer. A bed planted into runs 2 inches (5 cm) or more of gravel, with root tabs pushed in beside the rooted plants. Leaf litter on top suits glowlights, which are as much at home among wood and fallen leaves as among plants.

## Plants: Fine Leaves at Several Heights

Plant the tank with a wide variety of plants, and favor fine-leaved species at several heights so the school has cover at every level. Glowlights look their best in a heavily planted tank and can appear washed out in a spartan one. Leave a clear space in the center of the tank, because that open water is where the school swims. Floating plants add surface cover on top.

## Lighting: Fairly Dim, 8 to 10 Hours

Use fairly dim lighting, on for roughly 8 to 10 hours a day. Glowlights show their best color in soft water under dim light with a tannin tint, and floating plants do part of the dimming.

<FunFact source="U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, quoting Eigenmann (1917); The ETYFish Project">
The 1917 redescription of the glowlight tetra describes its stripe as a broad band without chromatophores, cherry red in life. The species name agrees: erythrozonus means red-banded.
</FunFact>

## Tankmates: Peaceful, Similar in Size

Keep the tank calm. The glowlight is peaceful and sometimes a little shy, one of the best tetras for a general community, and it suits companions of a similar size and temperament. Anything big enough to swallow a 1.6-inch fish does not belong. Every pairing also has to pass [the same four checks on temperature, water, size and fins](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) that any two species face before sharing a tank.

The companions that work:

- Most livebearers, danios, rasboras and other small tetras.
- Peaceful bottom dwellers such as Corydoras and kuhli loaches.
- Cherry barbs and small gouramis such as honey and sparkling gouramis.
- Dwarf cichlids in a larger tank, where a glowlight school makes a good dither fish.

Glowlights leave slow, long-finned fish alone, and aggression is rarely a problem. Angelfish are the one to rule out: they are notorious for eating glowlights.

## Blackwater or Clear Water

Either works. The wild streams are stained dark, but every glowlight sold is farm-bred, and captive-bred fish settle into a wide range of conditions, plain tap water included. The tint is about color: it brings out the stripe.

For the stained look, river sand goes down with <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">driftwood</AffiliateLink> and a few handfuls of dried beech or oak leaves. The wood and leaves color the water like weak tea, with old leaves pulled every few weeks before they rot and foul the water. A small bag of aquarium-safe peat in the filter, the old tannin source, is not needed: its collection is unsustainable and environmentally destructive.

## Cycle the Tank Before the School Goes In

A finished cycle is the single most important step when a glowlight tank is first set up. The tank is [cycled before any fish are added](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/): ammonia and nitrite both read zero, and nitrate has started to build. The whole school can go in together once the readings hold steady, with the water tested daily until they settle.

Cycle the tank at the tap water's own chemistry, and bring the pH down with leaves and wood only after the cycle has finished, so the water shifts once the bacteria are established.

Quarantine is the single most important step for every fish added. New fish spend 30 days in [a bare quarantine tank](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before they join a display tank that already holds fish.
