# Glowlight Tetras Are Never Handled, So the Trip Home and the Tankmates Matter

A glowlight tetra (*Hemigrammus erythrozonus*) is a lively, peaceful fish marked with a red stripe along each side, and an adult measures about 1.6 inches (4 cm). Nobody picks one up. The hands-on part of keeping it comes down to a bag, a net and the time it takes to move from shop water into tank water. After that, the choices that shape its life are the size of the school and which fish share the tank.

## Bagging and the Trip Home

Fill the transport bag with one part water to three parts air. The bag looks underfilled, but the air above the water is the oxygen supply for the ride. Set it in a dark container, or wrap it in a towel or a few sheets of newspaper.

Water in a bag deteriorates the longer the trip runs: ammonia rises and pH drops. That is the reason the bag water never goes into the tank, and a journey of more than a short drive calls for the preparation described in the [aquarium transport guide](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/).

## Acclimation, Step by Step

This is the routine for small tropical fish, and it suits a glowlight well.

1. **Turn the lights off.** New arrivals stay in the dark for the whole process, never under a bright tank lamp.
2. **Mix the water gradually.** Roll the top of the bag down and add a little tank water every few minutes, until the bag holds mostly aquarium water. Plan on 30 to 60 minutes.
3. **Net the fish across.** Lift them out and release them into the tank, and discard what is left in the bag. It carries waste from the trip and can carry pest snail eggs.
4. **Give them quiet.** An hour or two with the lights still off lets the school settle, and some take a day or two to start eating.

Wild glowlights come from soft, acidic water, and the tank should run at pH 5.5 to 7.5 and 2 to 15 dGH. Drip acclimation, which stretches the same job over about two hours, is meant for sensitive animals such as Caridina shrimp. For a glowlight it earns its time only when the shop's water and the home tank differ widely, which testing the tank's [pH and hardness](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) beforehand will show.

A quarantine tank is worth having for every new fish, and most of all for fish bought online. New arrivals spend [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before they join an established tank.

## How Many to Bring Home

Six is the floor, and ten or more is the number to buy. A school of 10 fills the middle of the tank with movement and color, and it needs a tank with a base of 24 x 15 inches (60 x 38 cm), roughly 18 to 20 gallons. The [glowlight tetra tank setup guide](/blog/glowlight-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) explains why that footprint is the recommendation over the 10 gallons sometimes sold for the species.

Ten fish arriving together add a sudden load of waste, so they belong in a tank that has finished its [nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) and has been running since.

## Temperament: Lively and Peaceful

Glowlights are active, colorful and peaceful, with aggression rarely a concern. They work the middle layer of the tank and use the open water in its center, which makes a school of them a natural fit for a calm community.

## Tankmates That Work

The test is a peaceful fish of similar size that cannot swallow a 1.6-inch tetra. Good matches include:

- Livebearers such as [guppies](/blog/guppy-handling-guide/) and mollies.
- Danios, rasboras, cherry barbs and other peaceful tetras.
- Bottom dwellers such as [corydoras](/blog/corydoras-catfish-handling-guide/), kuhli loaches and clown loaches.
- Honey and sparkling gouramis.

Larger fish that see a small tetra as a meal are the ones to avoid. Angelfish are the standard example: a mature one [will eat tankmates this size](/blog/angelfish-handling-guide/). Any candidate's adult size is worth checking against the [stocking and tankmates guide](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) before it goes in.

## Telling Males From Females

Mature females are rounder through the belly and tend to grow a little larger than males, while males are slimmer. The differences are subtle. A school of 10 needs no sorting, and the distinction matters only to a keeper planning to breed.

<FunFact>
The glowlight tetra was first described in 1909, from fish collected on a 1908 expedition to British Guiana run jointly by Indiana University and the Carnegie Museum.
</FunFact>
