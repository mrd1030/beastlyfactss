# Rummy-Nose Tetra Enrichment: The School Physicists Study

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The rummy-nose tetra (Petitella bleheri, formerly Hemigrammus bleheri) is a fish 1.5 to 2 inches long with a red head, and physics labs keep it to work out how a school of fish moves as one body.

<KeyTakeaway>
Rummy-nose tetras form one of the tightest schools among small tetras. The research on them shows that a bigger group holds its course and that the school needs light to function at all. Enrichment means **a school of 10 or more**, **a tank at least 36 inches long**, **fairly dim but steady light**, a blackwater layout of sand, wood and leaves, and varied small foods.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Life in the Rio Negro

The fish comes from the Rio Negro and Rio Meta basins of Brazil and Colombia. These are blackwater rivers: tannins leaching from rotting plant matter stain the water brown and make it strongly acidic, and aquatic plants are scarce.

Around Barcelos, on the middle Rio Negro, the fish is called rodostomo, and fishers find it in streams and in the igapós, the flooded forest. Asked how it lives, the fishers most often described a fish that travels in shoals, with 36 mentions. Seasonal migration came next with 32, while only 8 called it resident and 4 called it solitary. Rodostomo is one of the species in the arribação, the seasonal movement of fish that follows the rise and fall of the river.

So the wild picture is a group that stays together and keeps moving, through dark-stained water with little plant growth. The tank should give the school the same three things: company, room to travel, and that water.

<FunFact>
After the cardinal tetra, the rummy-nose is the fish Amazonas state exports most for the aquarium trade. It makes up about 6% of the 20 to 30 million fish the state ships each year.
</FunFact>

## Why the School Comes First

The target is 10 or more of this one species, and more is better. Rummy-noses shoal more tightly than most small tetras.

The research shows what group size changes. Rummy-nose tetras swam around a ring-shaped tank in groups of 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10 and 20. Every so often a group reversed direction, and the turn usually began with a fish at the front, then ran back through the group one fish after another. Group size set how often that happened. A school of 10 turned around about once every 20 minutes, roughly a hundred times less often than a pair.

For a keeper, that is the case for buying the full group at once. A larger school is a steadier one: it settles on a direction and holds it, while two or three fish keep reversing. The fish are very peaceful and lose out to boisterous or much larger tankmates, so the rest of the [stocking](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) should be calm and similar in size.

## Sight and the Lateral Line

A school needs each fish to keep track of its neighbors, and rummy-noses do it with two senses: their eyes, and the lateral line system.

Two experiments weigh those senses differently. In a 2010 study by Faucher and colleagues, rummy-nose tetras whose whole lateral system had been knocked out with an antibiotic treatment could not hold a shoal, and they only regained it as the system recovered over about a month. In a 2020 study by McKee and colleagues, rummy-noses with a compromised lateral line still schooled by sight alone, but each fish swam about 79% farther from its neighbors. Both studies found that the lateral line keeps the school tight, and the 2020 work found that sight is needed for schooling and is enough on its own.

A keeper cannot do much about either sense directly. The light the eyes depend on is another matter.

## Light: Enough to School By

Rummy-nose tetras are a blackwater fish and prefer fairly dim lighting. Dim is not the same as dark, though, and the research shows where the line falls.

In the 2020 study, groups of five needed more than about 1.5 lux to school. A 2023 study tested schools of about 50 fish across a range of 0 to 900 lux. In too little light the fish could not form a coherent, cohesive group. As the light rose, the school first lined up and swam in one direction, and at higher light it settled into a steady, regular circling pattern, the whole group wheeling around together.

The 1.5 lux threshold is tiny next to the top of that range, so a softly lit tank clears it easily. What the findings argue for is shade within a lit tank: driftwood and leaf litter under a light that stays on through the day. A timer keeps the day length regular; the 2023 study ran 14 hours of light to 10 of dark. At lights out, expect the school to loosen and drift apart, and expect it to form again when the light returns.

## A Tank Long Enough to Swim

Buy for length. A school of 10 needs a [tank at least 36 inches (90 cm) long](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-tank-setup-guide/), which in volume is a 30 to 40 gallon tank, because rummy-noses are active swimmers that cover the length of the tank in runs. Keep the middle and front open along the full length and put the decor at the back and ends.

## A Blackwater Layout

Model the tank on the river. River sand on the bottom, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">driftwood</AffiliateLink> and roots for structure, and a few handfuls of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01G78H5XS?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="SunGrow Indian Almond Leaves (Catappa), 50 Pack">dried leaves</AffiliateLink> spread over the sand, replaced every few weeks as they break down. A small net bag of aquarium-safe peat can go in the filter as well.

Plants are optional. They are not part of the fish's natural waters, but rummy-noses do well in a planted tank too, as long as it gets a [partial water change](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/) every week.

The water itself is soft and acidic: 73 to 79°F (23 to 26°C), pH 5.5 to 7.0 and 2 to 12 dGH. Their colors fade in alkaline water, so [test pH and hardness as separate readings](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before the fish arrive.

## Food the School Works For

Rummy-noses are omnivores that take almost anything offered, which makes it easy to vary what they get. Give a mix of dried flakes and granules alongside small live and frozen foods, all sized for a small mouth. Variety pays off in the fish: a mixed diet is what brings out the best color. Feed [small portions at least once a day](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-feeding-guide/).

## Priority Order

1. A school of 10 or more rummy-nose tetras, one species
2. A tank at least 36 inches (90 cm) long with open water along its length
3. Fairly dim, steady light on a timer, never full darkness by day
4. Soft, acidic water at 73 to 79°F, pH 5.5 to 7.0
5. Sand, driftwood, roots and dried leaves
6. Calm, similar-sized tankmates
7. Varied small foods, with live and frozen alongside flake and granules

## What Not to Do

Do not buy three or four and plan to add the rest later. Do not put a school of 10 in a short tank. Do not turn the light so low that the fish cannot see one another. Do not keep them in hard, alkaline water. Do not add them to a tank that has not finished [cycling](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/), and do not skip [quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) for new fish.

On the Rio Negro, a rummy-nose spends its life inside a moving shoal. Give it enough companions, enough length and enough light to see them by, and the school does the rest.
