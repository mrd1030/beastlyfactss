# Fish Color and Diet: What Brings It Out, and What No Food Can Change

A fish wears three kinds of color, and only one of them comes from its food. The black is a pigment the fish makes for itself. The blues, silvers and iridescent greens are light bouncing off crystals in its skin. The reds, oranges and yellows are built partly from carotenoids, pigments no fish can make and every fish has to eat.

## Where a Fish's Color Comes From

Fish color sits in four kinds of pigment cell in the skin. Melanophores hold melanin, the black and brown pigment, and they make it themselves. Xanthophores and erythrophores carry the yellows, oranges and reds, built from two kinds of pigment: carotenoids, which can only come from the diet, and pteridines, which the cells make on their own. Iridophores hold no pigment at all. They are packed with stacks of colorless guanine platelets, and the stacks reflect light into the silvers, blues and iridescent sheens.

The fish can change how those cells look within seconds to hours, by spreading pigment out through a cell or pulling it into a tight dot. Hormones and nerves run that change. The hormone alpha-MSH darkens a fish, melanin-concentrating hormone and melatonin generally pale it, and the stress hormones released in a fight-or-flight response act on the same cells. The same fish can look different at breakfast and at dinner without anything being wrong.

## The Colors a Fish Has to Eat

Most animals cannot make carotenoids from scratch; plants and algae can. A wild fish gets them by eating algae, or by eating the small crustaceans that ate the algae, and astaxanthin is the main carotenoid in crustaceans. Fish farms add carotenoids to feed for the same reason. The main commercial sources are synthetic astaxanthin, the green microalga *Haematococcus pluvialis*, red yeast, krill meal and crayfish oil, with lutein from marigold used for yellow and zeaxanthin from spirulina used as a red for goldfish and ornamental carp.

Fish do not all handle those pigments the same way. Carp, the family goldfish and koi belong to, can turn zeaxanthin into astaxanthin inside their own bodies. Perch-like fish, the group the cichlids belong to, cannot make astaxanthin from other carotenoids, so the astaxanthin they carry has to come in with the food already made.

Feeding trials show the effect plainly. Platies fed brine shrimp enriched with beta carotene for eight weeks carried more than four times the skin carotenoid of platies fed plain brine shrimp, and they measured redder. Red swordtails fed marigold petal meal at 15% of the feed held about ten times the carotenoid of fish on plain feed after three to four weeks. Oscars fed astaxanthin for 50 days carried about twice the skin carotenoid of fish on a plain diet.

## What No Food Will Change

A carotenoid food works only on carotenoid color. Black is melanin, made in the fish's own cells, so no diet darkens a black bar or a black molly. Blues, silvers and iridescent greens are reflected light, so no diet brightens a neon's stripe or a danio's sheen. Even the reds are only partly food, since some of the red in fish comes from pteridines the fish makes itself.

Food also deepens a color the fish already carries without adding one. In the platy trial, beta carotene made the fish redder and left their yellow where it was. The pattern, the black markings and the variety a fish was bred as come with the fish.

## Color Foods: What Is in Them and What to Expect

A color-enhancing flake or pellet is a carotenoid food, and the ingredients doing the work are the ones above: astaxanthin, *Haematococcus* algae, krill or shrimp meal, spirulina, marigold, and red pepper or paprika, which supplies the carotenoid capsanthin. Brine shrimp carry pigment into a fish only after they have eaten it, which is how the platy trial delivered its beta carotene.

Expect weeks. The swordtail trial measured a large rise after three to four weeks, the oscar trial ran 50 days and the platy trial eight weeks. Not every fish answers every pigment either: red female bettas fed annatto oil extract, an orange-red plant pigment, for four weeks showed no change in skin color at all.

More is not better. Every pigment-enriched diet in the swordtail trial grew the fish more slowly than plain feed, and doses above the optimum may blunt part of a fish's early immune response to infection. A color food belongs in the rotation as one food among several, and the staple still carries the diet.

The pigments do more than color. In the oscar trial, fish fed astaxanthin or the beta carotene-rich alga *Dunaliella* died less often after a bacterial infection, and the enriched platies had more of the protective enzymes and antibodies in their skin mucus.

## Light, Background and the Time of Day

Much of what looks like a color change is a fish adjusting its pigment cells to its surroundings. Neon and cardinal tetras turn lighter in bright light over a pale substrate in clear water, and duller, with a violet stripe, at night, so a school that looks washed out at the first feed of the morning is usually still in its night pattern. Zebrafish match their background by gathering or spreading their dark pigment, and zebra danios show their best color in a heavily planted tank over a dark substrate. Black skirt tetras look their best among dense plants with floating cover to dim the light, and glowlights under fairly dim lighting.

## When Lost Color Means Something Is Wrong

Stress changes color through the same hormones and nerves, so a fish under pressure from bullies, a move or bad water can go darker or paler. Disease shows in color too. Dulled color with a light gray-white film of mucus is a sign of skin protozoa, fish with larger skin parasites become pale and can have sores, very pale gills are the clearest sign of anemia, and ich pairs its white spots with dark coloration, lethargy and clamped fins. Soft-water tetras add water chemistry to the list: a rummy-nose's red fades considerably when the fish is out of condition and fades in alkaline water, and glowlights are more colorful in acidic water.

A pale fish that is also clamping its fins, gasping or refusing food is [showing a water problem or an infection](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/), and the water gets tested before anything else.

## Tetras and Danios

### Neon and Cardinal Tetras

Both carry the same two-part color. The blue stripe is iridophore color: light falling on a tetra's lateral stripe widens the spacing between its reflecting platelets and shifts the color it reflects, and in the neon the platelets tilt like the slats of a Venetian blind. Food cannot reach that color. The red along the lower body is the carotenoid and pteridine kind, so a varied diet with a carotenoid source keeps it deep.

### Glowlight Tetra

The glowlight shows its best color in acidic water and under fairly dim lighting, and it can look washed out in a sparse, bare tank. Small live and frozen foods are recommended for the best condition and color.

### Rummy-Nose Tetra

The red head works as a gauge of the whole school. It fades considerably when the fish are out of condition or kept in alkaline water, and a varied diet is essential for the best color development, so a nose that fades on every fish at once points to the water before the menu.

### Ember Tetra

The ember's orange-red is the carotenoid kind of color, so a staple that lists krill, algae or astaxanthin is worth having. Adult males are the more intensely colored sex, and they are brightest in spawning condition.

### Black Skirt Tetra

The black skirt's black is melanin, which no food deepens, and the intense black of a juvenile fades as the fish grows. Its best color comes with dense planting and floating plants dimming the light.

### Zebra Danio

A danio's dark stripes are melanin and its silver-blue sheen comes from reflective cells, both made by the fish itself. Background does more for it than food: zebrafish lighten or darken to match the floor beneath them, and danios color best in a heavily planted tank over a dark substrate.

## Livebearers

### Platy

The platy is the clearest case. Its red, orange and yellow are carotenoid color, and in the eight-week trial brine shrimp enriched with beta carotene raised skin carotenoid more than fourfold and made the fish measurably redder. A flake or pellet listing spirulina, krill, astaxanthin or marigold, fed a few times a week, does the same job.

### Swordtail

Red swordtails answer to plant pigment. Marigold meal at 15% of the feed raised their carotenoid about tenfold in three to four weeks, though the same diets slowed growth, which is why the color food stays one part of the rotation.

### Guppy

A male guppy's orange spots are carotenoid and pteridine color, so a carotenoid source in the diet feeds part of them. Females are larger, plumper and generally dull.

### Molly

A black molly's black is melanin and does not respond to diet. Gold and orange mollies carry carotenoid color, so they are the ones a carotenoid-rich food can brighten.

## Cichlids

### Oscar

The oscar's orange markings respond to food. Astaxanthin in the diet roughly doubled skin carotenoid over 50 days, and because a cichlid cannot build astaxanthin from other carotenoids, a food that lists astaxanthin itself, or krill and shrimp that carry it, is the direct route.

### Angelfish

An angelfish's silver and black bars are reflective cells and melanin, which food does not change. Any orange or red on a variety is carotenoid color, and as a cichlid it needs that astaxanthin already made in the food.

### Discus

Discus reds and oranges follow the cichlid rule: astaxanthin has to arrive ready-made, from krill, shrimp or a food that lists it. Blue and green sheens in any fish are reflected light, and diet does not alter them.

## Goldfish and Koi

Goldfish and koi have the most flexible chemistry of any common pet fish. As carp they can convert zeaxanthin into astaxanthin themselves, which is why spirulina, a zeaxanthin source, is used across the industry as a red colorant for goldfish and ornamental carp, alongside the astaxanthin in krill and shrimp meal.

## Bettas

Bettas have the least evidence behind color food of any fish here. In a four-week trial, red female bettas fed annatto oil extract showed no change in skin color, growth or survival. The blue, green and metallic sheens of many varieties are reflected light, which food does not touch.

## Bottom Dwellers: Corydoras and Bristlenose Plecos

The browns, blacks and bronze sheen of corydoras and bristlenose plecos are melanin and reflective cells, so color foods have almost nothing to work on. What changes in these fish is shade, which they adjust within hours through the same hormones as any other fish.

## Dyed and Injected Fish

Some fish reach stores in colors they were never bred to carry, because dye has been injected under the skin. The process is very stressful to the fish and often shortens its life considerably, so a dyed fish is best left in the store.

The red a platy carries home from the store is where its color starts. Eight weeks of a carotenoid food can deepen it, and clean water, a calm tank and the right light let it show.
