# 10 Surprising Fire-Bellied Toad Facts

Small, colorful, and mostly aquatic, fire-bellied toads look like a beginner-friendly novelty pet, and mostly are, but that bright belly is doing real biological work.

1. Their bright belly is a warning sign, not decoration. The vivid red, orange, or yellow and black pattern on their underside is a textbook example of aposematism, bold coloring that tells predators "I'm toxic, leave me alone."

2. They have a signature defensive pose with its own name: the Unkenreflex. When threatened, a fire-bellied toad arches its back and flips its legs up and over, flashing that warning-colored belly at whatever's threatening it.

3. Their skin secretes a real chemical defense, not just a bluff. It is not the heart-acting bufotoxin of true toads. Fire-bellied toads belong to a separate family, and their secretion is rich in peptides, including antimicrobial families called bombinins and bombinin H that were first found in Bombina skin. Researchers now study them as leads for new antibiotics.

4. For a person, casual contact is only mildly irritating. The secretion is not poisonous to intact human skin, but it is harmful rubbed into the eyes, mouth or broken skin, which is why washing your hands after handling one, before touching your face, matters.

5. They're one of the more aquatic toad species people keep as pets. Unlike a lot of toads, fire-bellied toads spend the large majority of their time in water, not just during breeding season.

6. Their skin gave medicine a hormone. Bombesin, a small peptide first isolated from the skin of the European fire-bellied toad, Bombina bombina, turned out to have close relatives in mammals, gastrin-releasing peptide among them. That family of peptides affects secretion, smooth muscle, metabolism and behavior, and the receptors they act on are still named for the toad.

7. They're vocal for an amphibian this small. Males of the Oriental fire-bellied toad give a soft, musical call, a tapering "oop, oop, ooop," both to attract females and to tell other males they have grabbed the wrong partner. Breeding runs through the warm months, and a female lays anywhere from 38 to 257 eggs a season in small batches of 3 to 45, every 7 to 10 days.

8. They can live 10 to 15 years in captivity with good care, and the recorded maximum runs to about 30 years, long for an amphibian this small.

9. Their skin toxin means other pets should never share their water. Even trace amounts can be dangerous to fish, other amphibians, and small mammals that might come into contact with the same tank water. A new toad does best in a tank of its own for the first six to eight weeks anyway, the standard [amphibian quarantine](/blog/amphibian-quarantine-and-water-guide/) window for screening out chytrid and other pet-trade pathogens.

10. Their belly pattern is individually distinct. Some keepers with multiple toads can tell them apart by the unique blotches on each one's underside, similar to a fingerprint.

<FunFact>
The Unkenreflex is genuinely a full-body performance, not a subtle flinch. A startled fire-bellied toad arches its spine backward and flips all four limbs up and outward at once, going from a plain green-brown blob to a flash of bright warning color in under a second.
</FunFact>
