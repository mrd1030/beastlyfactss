# Aquarium Substrate: Sand, Gravel and Planted Floors

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A goldfish spends much of its day sucking up mouthfuls of the tank floor and spitting out whatever is not food, and a corydoras pushes its barbels into the same layer hunting for scraps. For fish like these the substrate is a feeding ground. For every fish it is part of the water chemistry too, because some floors leach calcium into a tank for years, and even a neon tetra swimming in midwater changes color with it. This guide covers what any freshwater floor does: sand against gravel, how deep and how much, what it does to pH and hardness, the three ways to feed a planted bed, when to leave the glass bare, the fish that dig and sift, and how to keep the bed clean.

## Sand or Gravel

Both work, and the main difference is where the dirt goes. Very fine sand lets little dirt sink into it, so waste sits on the surface where the filter can draw it in or a siphon can lift it off. The larger the grain, the more dirt gets trapped between the pieces. Gravel makes up for that by standing up to a vacuum: a siphon pulls waste out of it without pulling up the bed. Coarse gravel is a poor medium for plant roots, while fine sand suits roots well but is more prone to going anaerobic, starved of oxygen, when it is laid deep. Strong filter flow can also shove sand around, leaving craters and drifts.

The two go in at different depths. Gravel is normally laid about 2 inches deep or more, while a freshwater tank generally gets only a thin layer of sand, around 1 inch.

Grain size matters more to some fish than others. Some species sift fine gravel through their gills, while others get gravel stuck in their mouths or swallow it easily, so the right grain depends on what lives in the tank. Substrate of the wrong size and texture can damage barbels and skin through repeated contact, which is why the bottom dwellers and diggers get their own section below. For a first planted tank, start with an inert substrate that carries no nutrients of its own, either aquarium gravel or sand.

**Color.** A floor changes how a fish looks. Dark, sandy substrates make tetras' bright colors stand out, and embers, zebra danios and swordtails are all described as looking their best over a dark substrate in a planted tank. Embers in particular are best kept heavily planted, preferably over a dark floor, and keepers report brighter color in planted tanks with a dark substrate and background, an observation from the hobby that nobody has measured. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0018CLX3C?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Seachem Flourite Black Sand, 7 kg (15.4 lb)">black sand</AffiliateLink> or a dark, small-grained gravel meets it. Neon and cardinal tetras show the effect from the other side: in bright light and clear water, their colors turn lighter over a pale substrate or background. That is a color effect only. In the farmed food fish studied so far, a black tank made fish more sensitive to stress, and no study shows a dark floor calming an aquarium fish.

**What fish choose.** The one controlled substrate preference test on an aquarium species used zebrafish, and they chose gravel. Any setup with gravel beat any setup with sand, and groups preferred substrate or plants of any kind over a barren tank.

## How Deep and How Much

Depth follows purpose. In a community tank without rooted plants, the usual maximums are about 2 inches for coarse gravel and about 1 inch for fine sand, because deeper beds hold more waste and are likelier to turn anaerobic. Vet-written care sheets for goldfish and tetras both land on 1 to 2 inches of sand, gravel or pebbles. A tank planted into the floor needs more, at least 2 to 3 inches, and a thin layer of sand is enough where little is rooted. Deep beds of porous planting soil are the one exception to the shallow limits.

A planted bed usually slopes. One plant grower lays gravel about 1.2 to 1.6 inches deep at the front and 2.4 to 3.1 inches at the back, over a thin nutrient layer, and a slope from front to back also makes the tank look deeper than it is.

**How much to buy.** Rules of thumb run from 1 pound of gravel per gallon of water to 1.5 pounds per gallon. At 1.5 pounds, a 10 gallon tank takes about 15 pounds, which gives a 1 to 2 inch layer. Both rules count gallons, and gallons do not measure the floor. A tall tank holds more water over the same footprint, so a per-gallon rule hands it a deeper bed than a long, low tank of the same volume. Seachem's calculator for its own substrates skips gallons entirely: it asks for the depth you want and the tank's length and width, then returns a number of bags. Measure the floor and work from the depth you chose.

Depth has one more limit. Hydrogen sulfide, which is toxic to fish, forms in deep beds where waste builds up and runs out of oxygen, and it is released into the water when that layer is disturbed.

## What the Floor Does to the Water

A substrate is either inert or it is not. Inert sand, gravel and rock leave the water chemistry alone. Calcareous substrates carry calcium and other minerals that dissolve into the tank and raise hardness and pH. Inert floors matter most for soft-water fish such as tetras and discus: whatever goes on the floor of a soft-water tank has to leave the water soft.

Several common floors are less inert than they look. Even standard pea gravel contains enough calcium to raise pH and hardness. Many colored gravels, especially rough, angular ones with a powdery coating, are made from white dolomite, a mineral rich in calcium and magnesium, and then coated in paint or resin, so they raise both figures as well. Shells, coral and sand collected from a beach can shift pH. Coral sand belongs only in a tank meant to stay hard and alkaline, where it can push pH past 8.0. Seachem says its Flourite and Flourite Black Sand will not alter pH.

**Buffering on purpose.** For hard-water fish on soft tap water, the same chemistry becomes a tool. Crushed coral and aragonite are calcium carbonate. They raise carbonate hardness (KH) strongly and general hardness (GH) somewhat, and they add calcium without the magnesium that dolomite carries. Aquarium Co-Op's directions for its crushed coral are 1 pound per 10 gallons of water, mixed into the gravel. It dissolves faster the lower the pH and slows as pH climbs, so in the substrate it needs replacing every 1 to 2 years, depending on how low the tap water's pH runs. Smaller amounts in a filter bag need replacing after 1 to 3 months. Rinse it until the water runs clear, and test pH and KH to know when to add more.

**Floors that pull the other way.** Aquasoil lowers pH. One well-known brand is described as running pH 5.0 to 6.0, which pulls a tank toward the soft, acidic range that embers and other soft-water tetras are kept in. A mild acid source like this moves pH far more in soft water than in hard water, because carbonate hardness neutralizes acid before pH can shift, the buffer at the center of [how pH, GH and KH work together](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/).

**Rock follows the same rule.** Limestone and other calcareous rocks, and seashells, leach calcium carbonate into the water. To check a stone, put a few drops of vinegar on it: fizzing means calcium carbonate. Dragon stone passes, and rock with visible veins is best left out of a soft-water tank.

## Planted Floors: Root Tabs, Capped Soil, Aquasoil

An inert bed leaves the water alone and feeds nothing, so a planted tank adds nutrients one of three ways. Inert gravel paired with a liquid fertilizer and no planting substrate at all also grows plants.

Planting substrates work by trapping nutrients and releasing them slowly to roots, and some start out carrying a source of ammonia. Digging into one can release its nutrients into the water and set off algae. One aquasoil maker tells keepers to change 25 to 50% of the water at least twice a week for the first four weeks. A long-lasting planting gravel does not wear out the same way: Seachem says Flourite is good for the life of the tank, that root tabs over it help but are not a necessity, and that additives such as laterite are not needed.

**Leaves and wood on top.** Leaves are optional, and they sit on the substrate. Dried leaf litter and driftwood branches both suit embers, and at the one wild ember site described firsthand, near Aruanã on the Araguaia River in Brazil, the bottom was leaf litter over a yellow, clay-like floor mixed with fine gravel and sand. As leaves decay, colonies of microbes establish on them, and the tannins and other compounds they release are thought to be beneficial, which is a belief among keepers more than a tested result. Catappa leaves are the common choice: they grow a biofilm that fry graze on, and they slightly acidify water whose pH runs high. Bogwood and mopani leach tannic acid as well. Leaves and branches bring pH down a little and keep the water moderately soft, usually not enough to turn it into blackwater, and hard water with high KH buffers away most of that change. None of it softens hard water. In hard tap water the hardness has to come down through an RO blend first. Old leaves come out and fresh ones go in every few weeks, before they rot and foul the water. Catappa's reputation against disease traces to a study in which leaf extract was injected into farmed catfish. That says nothing about a leaf lying on the floor of a tank, so a leaf is cover and biofilm, not a treatment.

## Bare Bottoms, and the Koi Pond

A bare glass floor is easier to keep clean, because any solid waste is in plain view, and in a disease outbreak it is easier to clear dormant pathogens off the base. Substrate can hide feces and uneaten food, and it can harbor parasites and pathogens that are hard to remove. The [quarantine and hospital tank](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) is built bare for those reasons: an inexpensive 10 gallon tank with a sponge filter, a small air pump and a heater, broken down and disinfected between uses. Breeding tanks for egg-scattering fish such as zebra danios, black skirt tetras and rummy-nose tetras skip substrate as well and use a mesh base.

Discus are the display fish most often kept bare. Many discus keepers prefer it because it is the easiest floor to clean, and breeding discus calls for a bare tank. A layer of sand is the middle ground, since uneaten food lies on top where it is easy to spot and remove, and small gravel works with regular vacuuming. Plenty of discus keepers go the other way entirely, into a heavily planted tank, and that tank needs an inert floor.

Going bare has costs. Substrate gives beneficial bacteria more surface to grow on, and a study of zebrafish tanks measured more nitrification with gravel in place. The same study found that substrate also held more waste and more bacteria, Pseudomonas among them, so a bed only helps when it is kept clean. The zebrafish themselves preferred substrate to a barren tank, and in a research facility gravel enrichment raised the share of viable eggs. A display tank gets a floor for the fish, the plants and the bacteria, and the price is cleaning it.

A koi pond is the same choice at a larger scale. A bare liner is the easiest pond floor to keep clean and works well with a bottom drain, which directs flow and carries waste out. Gravel and small rocks have to be stirred often or they cement together, a pond vacuum keeps sucking up the small stones, and the layer collects debris that is hard to clean out. Laid deeper than 3 inches, pond gravel risks anaerobic bacteria and pockets of hydrogen sulfide that can kill fish if they mix into the water. Rocks and pebbles also block circulation: sludge falls into the gaps, the water stops moving there, and without flow there is no oxygen. One layer of larger stones collects less debris than several, and a vacuum works a little better on stones too big to fit up the hose, but any rock laid on a liner, sheet plastic or sprayed, wears through it faster. Where a pond does get a floor, a shallow layer of smooth-sided pebbles, rocks or gravel is the suitable choice.

## Fish That Work the Floor

**Goldfish.** Goldfish are grubbers. They evolved to take in mouthfuls of sand, silt and mud, and in a tank they forage by sucking up substrate and spitting out what is not food. A stone usually comes back out on its own within a few minutes, and lodging is rare. When a large piece of gravel does stick in the mouth, it takes sedation and forceps at a fish vet to remove without damaging the lining. Pea gravel is the size that goes wrong: swallowed, it can block the esophagus, and that blockage also needs a vet, because trying to clear it at home is likely to injure the fish. The fix is a floor at either extreme, very fine material or pieces larger than the fish can take in. Fine grit, very small rounded gravel or sand all suit a goldfish, which can also live on a bare bottom, and a goldfish tank can run [bare, on sand, or on gravel too big to fit in the mouth](/blog/goldfish-tank-setup-guide/).

**Oscars.** An oscar has a habit of digging into the substrate, and soft sand is the floor to give it. It spawns on flat rocks, so a few belong on top of the sand.

**Corydoras.** Cories feed by pushing their barbels into the bed, and the consensus floor for them is smooth, fine sand or fine, smooth gravel, kept scrupulously clean. One cory specialist puts the maximum depth at about 0.6 inch, shallow enough for the fish to reach through to the glass. Sharp grain gets the blame for damaged barbels that then become infected, and so does a dirty, poorly kept bed. No study settles which cause matters more, so the bed has to be both smooth and clean. The paper most often cited on substrate and bacteria tested zebrafish, and it found more Pseudomonas with gravel or sand than without. Breeding groups of cories appear less prone to disease over a thin layer of sand than in a bare tank. Undergravel filters, which use the gravel itself as the filter medium, do not belong under cories.

**Bristlenose plecos.** Given plenty of cover, a bristlenose is unfussy about decor, and the caves the males spawn in matter more than the grain. Coarse grains can affect catfish and burrowing fish, so a smooth floor suits it as well. Pleco waste piles up quickly, and the bed needs vacuuming when it does.

## Cleaning

**Before it goes in.** Rinse new substrate thoroughly. Most of it is dusty enough to cloud the tank, and powder-coated colored gravel is the worst. Dust also clogs filters early and can damage water pumps. Rinse sand in a bucket about 2 to 3 inches at a time, stirring hard while flushing it with cold water. A sieve loses the lot.

**Gravel.** Stirring and vacuuming the bed lifts out the solid waste trapped in it. Vacuum at least every two weeks, and weekly is better, since a neglected bed brings nitrate spikes and disease. A careful pass with a gravel cleaner can reach most of the floor while drawing off only about 20 to 30% of the water. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00BDPFX62?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Python Pro-Clean Gravel Washer and Siphon, Large, with Squeeze Bulb">gravel vacuum with a squeeze bulb</AffiliateLink> starts the siphon without mouth suction. Crimping the hose slows the flow, and working the floor in rows, like mowing a lawn, keeps the pass even. White gravel can need siphoning again a day or two after the last pass.

**Sand.** A gravel cleaner pushed into sand just lifts the sand out. Rake your fingers gently through the surface once a week to loosen the deposits, then skim the siphon just above the sand to take them off. Clean a quarter to a third of the floor per water change; the quarter disturbs the bed's filtration least. A little sand leaves with every pass, so top it up now and then. Netting uneaten food before it settles saves part of the job.

**Planted beds.** A planted bed is the exception. A gravel cleaner can suck a planting substrate out, and capped soil clouds the tank when its plants are moved, so the cleaner works the open patches and the rooted areas are left alone. Some aquascapers lift out a section of the substrate every few weeks or months, clean it and put it back. When a plant comes out, dig it out instead of pulling it, and take every root with it: roots left behind decompose and add nitrate.

## The Takeaway

A tank floor is chosen for the fish that touch it and the water it sits in. Grubbers like goldfish need a bed they cannot swallow, cories need one that is smooth, shallow and clean, and most midwater fish care about the floor only through what it does to the water and to their color. A soft-water tank needs an inert floor and inert rock, a hard-water livebearer tank can carry crushed coral on purpose, and a planted bed needs depth and a feeding plan. Keep the bed shallow enough to stay clean, vacuum it on schedule, and leave the glass bare wherever the job is quarantine or breeding.
