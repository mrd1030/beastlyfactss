# Corydoras Catfish Tank Setup: Substrate Is the Detail That Actually Matters

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Corydoras catfish are easy to house well once you get one specific decision right, what's on the bottom of the tank. Everything else about this species' care is fairly standard community-fish practice.

<Figure src="/assets/images/corydoras-catfish-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a corydoras catfish tank showing soft sand substrate, unobstructed surface access, and a school foraging near the bottom" caption="easy to house well once you get one specific decision right, what's on the bottom of the tank." shareable />

## Tank Size

A 20-gallon long tank is the practical minimum for a proper school of six standard-sized cories, dwarf or pygmy species can work in something smaller, around 10 gallons. A 29 to 30-gallon tank or larger is genuinely better, especially if you're keeping a bigger group of 8 to 15. Favor a wide footprint over a tall one, this is a bottom-dwelling species that uses floor space, not vertical space.

## Temperature

72 to 78°F suits most commonly kept species, and the exceptions sit at both ends of it.

- **Warm: Corydoras sterbai.** The sterbai cory lives at 75 to 82°F (24 to 28°C), which makes it the cory for a tank that has to run warm for its other fish.
- **Cool: the panda cory, the peppered cory and the julii cory.** The panda wants 72 to 77°F (22 to 25°C), and a panda kept above that range for long is likely to live a good deal shorter. In the wild, some panda streams are fed by Andean snowmelt and drop to about 66°F (19°C). Peppered and julii cories are the other two cooler-water species in the common trade.

Pick the species to suit the tank's temperature, never the other way round. A tank held at 80°F suits a sterbai school and is too warm for pandas. Since the group should be one species anyway, that choice is made once. ## Water Parameters

pH around 7.0 to 7.8 for captive-bred cories, which make up most of what's available in the trade. Ammonia and nitrite should read zero, and nitrate should stay low through regular water changes, this ties directly into preventing barbel erosion. A reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">water test kit</AffiliateLink> is worth keeping on hand so you catch a rising nitrate reading before it becomes a barbel problem.

## Substrate: The Decision That Matters Most

Soft sand or smooth, fine, rounded gravel is genuinely essential for this species. Avoid sharp or coarse gravel entirely, and be cautious with certain specialty planted-tank sands, some sharp-grained options are specifically flagged by manufacturers as unsuitable for bottom-dwelling fish like cories. About 2 inches of depth works well.

## Lighting

Standard community tank lighting is fine. Cories are most active around dawn and dusk and appreciate some shaded cover and hides where they can retreat during brighter parts of the day.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Gentle Flow With Rest Spots

Provide gentle water movement with calmer areas where cories can rest, rather than uniform strong current throughout the tank. A sponge filter is a common way to get gentle, even flow without the strong current a hang-on-back filter can produce.

## Cleaning Sand Without Clouding the Tank

Fine sand clouds the moment a siphon tube digs into it and keeps pulling. The fix is to let weight do the sorting:

1. **Crimp.** Work the tube through the top layer and pinch the hose every few seconds to stop the pull. Sand is heavier than waste, so it drops back out of the tube while the debris stays suspended and goes to the bucket.
2. **Mow in rows.** Move back and forth across the bed in rows rather than at random, so no patch gets dug twice.
3. **Work in thirds.** There is no need to clean the whole bed in one session. Do about a third of it, and the next third at the next [weekly water change](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/).

Leave the cories in the tank. Netting them out is more stressful than working slowly around them, and a slow tube gives them time to move off the sand being cleaned.

<FunFact>
Avoid over-cleaning or disturbing the substrate too aggressively during maintenance, and make sure your fish have easy access to the surface, they periodically gulp air as part of normal breathing, and that behavior depends on being able to reach the top of the tank without obstruction.
</FunFact>
