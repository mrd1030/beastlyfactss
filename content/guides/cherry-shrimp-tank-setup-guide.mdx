# Cherry Shrimp Tank Setup: Size, Hard Water, and a Gentle Sponge Filter

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cherry shrimp are native to streams, rivers, ponds, and lakes across Taiwan, eastern China, the Korean peninsula, and Vietnam, generally in clear, vegetated water rather than open current, according to [Aquariadise](https://www.aquariadise.com/caresheet-cherry-shrimp/). That background explains most of what this species wants in a tank: less raw space than a comparable fish, but noticeably less tolerance for hard water swings or a strong filter intake.

For what it all costs, see our [Cherry shrimp cost guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-cost-guide/), and everything outside setup is in the full [Cherry shrimp care guide](/guides/cherry-shrimp/).

## Tank Size

5 gallons is a commonly cited practical minimum, according to [Aquariadise](https://www.aquariadise.com/caresheet-cherry-shrimp/). [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/cherry-shrimp-care) notes shrimp can technically survive in a 2-gallon nano tank, but recommends 10 gallons or larger for a healthy, self-sustaining breeding colony, since more water volume buffers the small, constant parameter shifts a growing colony naturally causes. Aquarium Co-Op also suggests keeping at least 10 shrimp to reliably end up with both sexes in a starting group.

## Water Hardness: The Detail Most Fish-Only Keepers Skip

This is the single most shrimp-specific requirement on this page. [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/cherry-shrimp-care) recommends a GH of at least 6 degrees (about 110 ppm) and a KH of at least 2 degrees (about 40 ppm). Shrimp-focused sources like [Tropical Treasures Wyo](https://tropicaltreasureswyo.com/blogs/tank-buddy-blog/shrimp-water-parameters-guide) tighten that further, to a target of roughly 6 to 8 dGH and 2 to 4 dKH, alongside a pH of 6.5 to 7.5. Mineral hardness is what a shrimp actually pulls calcium from to build a new shell after every molt, so a tank that's too soft leads directly to the failed and soft molts covered in our [health issues guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-health-issues-guide/). Test your source water before assuming it's automatically suitable, and adjust gradually with a remineralizing product rather than all at once if it comes up short.

## Temperature

[Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/cherry-shrimp-care) lists a comfortable range of 60 to 82°F, with 72 to 76°F called out as optimal, while [Aquariadise](https://www.aquariadise.com/caresheet-cherry-shrimp/) puts the outer tolerated range at a wide 57 to 86°F. In practice, that means most home rooms sit comfortably inside this species' range without any supplemental heat at all, a real point of contrast with a lot of tropical fish. ## Filtration: Gentle, and Covered

Keep flow gentle, and make sure the intake can't pull shrimp in. [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/cherry-shrimp-care) is direct about this: "use a sponge filter or cover the filter input with a pre-filter sponge to ensure that no baby shrimp accidentally get sucked up." A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> is the simplest way to satisfy both requirements at once, gentle output and an intake a shrimp physically can't be pulled into.

## Substrate and Decor

A simple, inert substrate works fine, cherry shrimp aren't picky about substrate composition the way some plants are. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">Driftwood</AffiliateLink> adds grazing surface as it develops a biofilm coating, and dried Indian almond leaves do the same thing faster, our [feeding guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-feeding-guide/) covers how much of a colony's actual diet comes from that kind of grazing surface.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Let the Tank Mature Before Shrimp Go In

Cherry shrimp should go into an established, fully cycled tank, not a brand-new one. Beyond ammonia and nitrite reading zero, the tank needs real time for a biofilm layer to develop on surfaces and plants, since that biofilm is a meaningful part of what a healthy colony eats day to day. Dense live plants or moss speed that process along and double as cover where newly hatched shrimp can hide from adults, filter flow, and any fish tankmates. [Amano shrimp](/guides/amano-shrimp/) do well in a similar setup and are a common, compatible tankmate; [ghost shrimp](/guides/ghost-shrimp/) tolerate a wider range of conditions but are more likely to prey on smaller or freshly molted cherry shrimp, so give them extra cover if you mix the two.

<FunFact>
Even taxonomists don't fully agree on what to call this shrimp. The species has gone by several names over the past century, including *Neocaridina heteropoda* and *Neocaridina denticulata sinensis*, before most references settled on *Neocaridina davidi*. But a [2024 study in *Current Issues in Molecular Biology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593339/) reopened the question, arguing on genetic grounds that *davidi*, wild *denticulata* populations, and the aquarium-trade name *heteropoda* are all the same species, and proposing that the older name *N. denticulata* should actually take priority. The researchers also note that decades of selective crossbreeding for aquarium color varieties have muddied the genetic picture further, making a tidy answer harder to reach. Whatever the shrimp in your tank ends up officially called, its care needs won't change.
</FunFact>
