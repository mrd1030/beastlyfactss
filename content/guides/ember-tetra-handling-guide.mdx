# Ember Tetras Are Never Handled, So the Trip Home Is the Part to Get Right

An ember tetra is a small South American characin from the Araguaia River basin in central Brazil, and a full-grown one measures about 0.8 inch (2 cm) from snout to the base of the tail. Nobody picks one up. The handling this fish gets comes down to a net, a bag and the hour it takes to move from shop water into tank water, and at this size each of those steps has its own way to go wrong.

## Netting a Fish This Small

A net is the one tool that touches an ember tetra, and the common way to use it is the clumsy one: a helper holds a half-filled bag open in the air while the fish get tipped into it from the net. The better method keeps the bag in the water. Float a partly filled bag on the surface of the tank, open it there, and net the fish straight across into it, so the whole transfer happens at the waterline.

Size is the other problem. Once the fish are moved, look over the net, the bag and the floor around the tank. A fish 0.8 inch long can stay stuck in a net or jump out along the way without anyone seeing it go. Counting the fish before and after the move confirms the school arrived whole.

## Bagging and the Drive Home

Fill the bag one part water to three parts air. It looks underfilled, but the air is what holds the oxygen the fish breathe on the trip. For the journey, set the bags in a dark container, or wrap them in a towel or a few sheets of newspaper.

The water in the bag gets worse the longer the trip runs. Ammonia builds up from the fishes' waste, and pH drops as carbon dioxide accumulates. Both are reasons the bag water never ends up in the tank, and anything longer than a short local drive calls for the oxygen, insulation and container choices that go into [moving fish on a longer trip](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/).

## Acclimation, Step by Step

Nothing in this method is specific to ember tetras. It is the standard routine for small fish, and with a species this small the last step matters as much as the first.

1. **Switch the tank lights off.** New arrivals should be kept fairly dark, never blinded by the tank lighting.
2. **Mix the water slowly.** Add a little tank water to the bag every few minutes, and keep going until the bag holds more aquarium water than shop water. The whole process takes 30 to 60 minutes.
3. **Net the fish out.** Lift them from the bag and release them into the tank. Never pour the bag in: its water carries the waste from the trip and can carry pest snail eggs from the shop.
4. **Check for strays.** Before the bag goes in the trash, look through it, the net and the floor once more for a fish that got stuck or jumped.

Ember tetras are soft-water fish, kept at 1 to 10 dGH and a pH of 5.0 to 7.0. When the shop's water and the home tank sit far apart on either figure, drip acclimation changes the chemistry more gradually, over about two hours. It is aimed at sensitive animals such as Caridina shrimp, and a big chemistry gap is the case where it earns those two hours. Testing the tank's [pH and hardness](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before the trip, and asking the shop for its figures, settles which method the day needs.

The same routine applies when the bag goes into a quarantine tank first, which is advisable wherever the fish were bought. New fish spend at least [30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before they join an established tank.

## How Many to Bring Home

Ten. A minimum of 8 to 10 is the recommended purchase, and 10 is the number to plan around. Embers are relatively outgoing, especially in large groups, and a bigger school is where that shows.

No study has tested group size in this species. The closest evidence is a [shoaling study on neon tetras](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/), in which groups of 10 had the greatest tendency to shoal and larger groups showed less aggression than smaller ones. That finding is about neons, and embers have no equivalent.

A school of 10 needs a 10-gallon tank with an 18 x 12 inch (45 x 30 cm) footprint, and the [ember tetra tank setup guide](/blog/ember-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) explains why that size beats the 5 gallons sometimes suggested. Ten fish arriving in one trip is a sudden jump in waste, so they go into a tank that has finished its [nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) and has been running for a while since.

## Temperament: Peaceful, and Easily Outcompeted

Ember tetras are very peaceful fish. They do not compete well with very boisterous or much larger tankmates, and that one trait decides most of what goes in the tank with them. In a calm community they work well, and they make an ideal dither fish for Apistogramma and other dwarf cichlids.

## Tankmates That Work

The test is a peaceful animal of similar size that won't eat them. That covers:

- Similarly sized, peaceful characids.
- Small catfish.
- Calm fish that keep to the surface.

Bettas and dwarf gouramis are the two to think twice about, since either may turn territorial.

Shrimp are a split answer. Embers leave adult dwarf shrimp alone, so a grown shrimp colony is safe with them. Shrimplets are another matter: almost all fish will opportunistically go after baby shrimp, and embers are no exception. A tank meant to raise shrimp and hold a school of embers will lose some of its young to the fish.

## Telling Males From Females

Adult males carry the stronger color, most of all in spawning condition. Males are slender in profile, while females are rounder through the body, which shows best when the fish are seen from above. A mixed school needs no sexing at all. The difference matters only to a keeper setting up a breeding group.

<FunFact>
The ember tetra goes by several names in the trade. Shops also sell it as the fire tetra, the red tetra, the dwarf red tetra and Amanda's tetra, so any of those labels on a tank can mean the same fish. The scientific name on the label, Hyphessobrycon amandae, settles it.
</FunFact>
