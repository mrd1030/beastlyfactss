# Ember Tetras Are Never Handled, So the Trip Home Is the Part to Get Right

An ember tetra is a small South American characin from the Araguaia River basin in central Brazil, and a full-grown one measures about 0.8 inch (2 cm) from snout to the base of the tail. Nobody picks one up. The handling this fish gets comes down to a net, a bag and the hour it takes to move from shop water into tank water, and at this size each of those steps has its own way to go wrong. The keeper's hands go into that water too, and they carry a small risk of their own.

## Netting a Fish This Small

A net is the one tool that touches an ember tetra, and the common way to use it is the clumsy one: a helper holds a half-filled bag open in the air while the fish get tipped into it from the net. The better method keeps the bag in the water. Float a partly filled bag on the surface of the tank, open it there, and net the fish straight across into it, so the whole transfer happens at the waterline.

Size is the other problem. A fish 0.8 inch long can stay stuck in a net or jump out along the way without anyone seeing it go. The routine that catches it is simple:

1. **Count the school before the net goes in.** Ten fish going into the bag should be ten fish coming out of it.
2. **Bag at the waterline.** Float the open bag and net the fish straight across into it.
3. **Count again, then check.** Look over the net, the bag and the floor for a fish that got stuck or jumped before anything is rinsed or thrown away.

On the way, ammonia builds up from the fishes' waste, and pH drops as carbon dioxide accumulates. Both are reasons the bag water never ends up in the tank, and anything longer than a short local drive calls for the oxygen, insulation and container choices that go into [moving fish on a longer trip](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/).

## Acclimation, Step by Step: Mixing or Dripping

This is the standard routine for small fish, and with a species this small the last step matters as much as the first.

1. **Switch the tank lights off.** New arrivals should be kept fairly dark, never blinded by the tank lighting.
2. **Mix the water slowly.** Add a little tank water to the bag every few minutes, and keep going until the bag holds more aquarium water than shop water. The whole process takes 30 to 60 minutes.
3. **Net the fish out.** Lift them from the bag and release them into the tank. Never pour the bag in: its water carries the waste from the trip and can carry pest snail eggs from the shop.
4. **Count the school.** Ten fish went into the bag, so ten come out. Before the bag goes in the trash, look through it, the net and the floor for a fish that got stuck or jumped.

Ember tetras are soft-water fish, kept at 1 to 10 dGH and a pH of 5.0 to 7.0. When the shop's water and the home tank sit far apart on either figure, drip acclimation changes the chemistry more gradually. It is aimed at sensitive animals such as Caridina shrimp, and a big chemistry gap is the case where it earns those two hours. Testing the tank's pH and hardness before the trip, and asking the shop for its figures, settles which method the day needs.

The same routine applies when the bag goes into a quarantine tank first, which is advisable wherever the fish were bought. New fish spend at least [30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before they join an established tank.

## How Many to Bring Home

Ten. A minimum of 8 to 10 is the recommended purchase, and 10 is the number to plan around. Embers are relatively outgoing, especially in large groups, and a bigger school is where that shows.

In the group-size study on neon tetras, groups of 10 had the greatest tendency to shoal.

A school of 10 needs a 10-gallon tank with an 18 x 12 inch (45 x 30 cm) footprint, and the [ember tetra tank setup guide](/blog/ember-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) explains why that size beats the 5 gallons sometimes suggested. Ten fish arriving in one trip is a sudden jump in waste, so they go into a tank that has finished its nitrogen cycle and has been running for a while since.

## Fish Tank Granuloma: Gloves Over Cuts

Netting, acclimation and every bit of tank work put a keeper's hands in aquarium water, and that water can carry Mycobacterium marinum, the bacterium behind fish tank granuloma. It lives in fresh and salt water, unchlorinated pools and aquarium tanks, and it gets into a person through a break in the skin such as a cut.

The first sign is a reddish to purple bump that slowly becomes a painful nodule, most often on the elbows, the fingers or the backs of the hands. It usually shows about 2 to 3 weeks after exposure, and sometimes takes a month or longer. Treatment often runs several months and may combine antibiotics.

Two case reports of aquarists, published in 2013 by Slany, Jezek and Bodnarova, show how a small wound turns into a long one. One aquarist cleaned an injured knee with a brush normally used on the aquarium; the other was hurt while clearing out a tank. One was first misdiagnosed and treated with steroids for three months, and the other needed eight months of treatment. The authors name delayed diagnosis as the main cause of the patients' serious complications, and they ask labs to consider M. marinum whenever an injury started at an aquarium.

Prevention is three habits:

- **Gloves over cuts.** Wear rubber gloves for tank work, and never put a hand with a cut or scrape into the water bare.
- **Wash afterward.** Wash hands and arms thoroughly when the job is done.
- **Name the tank.** If a slow, painful bump appears on a hand or elbow, tell the doctor about the aquarium at the first visit.

## First-Timer Mistakes

Each of these undoes a step above.

- **Pouring the bag into the tank.** The fish cross by net; the water goes down the drain.
- **Bagging with too much water.** One part water to three parts air.
- **Leaving the lights on.** New arrivals stay fairly dark.
- **Rushing the mix.** 30 to 60 minutes, a little at a time.
- **Throwing the bag away uncounted.** Count the school and check the bag, net and floor first.
- **Buying a handful.** Ten is the number to plan around.
- **Skipping quarantine because the shop seems good.** Quarantine wherever the fish came from.
- **Adding a rowdy or much bigger tankmate.** Embers lose out to boisterous or much larger fish.
- **Working bare-handed over a cut.** Gloves first.

An ember school that arrives whole, counted at both ends and moved by net into water it was eased into, is a school of ten from the first day, which is the size where this outgoing little fish shows it.
