# Budgie Feeding: The Squeak That Means Iodine

<VetDisclaimer />

Put a seed mix in front of a budgie and watch what it does. It works the bowl over for the millet, leaves most of the rest, and by the end of the week the dish looks full while the bird has eaten one thing. I have looked after neighbors' budgies on and off for years, and the tidy-looking, barely touched seed dish is the single most misleading sight in the cage.

## What the Diet Should Be

A formulated pellet does most of the work, at 60 to 80% of daily intake. Vegetables with a little fruit make up 20 to 25% at most.

Seed does not have to disappear entirely, but it stops being the diet. The trouble with a seed mix is not one missing nutrient. It runs high in fat and carbohydrate, low in protein, and short on vitamins and minerals generally, and then selective eating makes the real intake narrower than the label suggests.

## Iodine, and Why This Species in Particular

Here is where budgies differ from the other small parrots.

Many seeds are low in iodine. The thyroid needs iodine to make its hormone at all, so a bird eating mostly seed and nothing else runs short of the raw material for it. Budgies are unusually prone to thyroid trouble compared with other pet birds, which is why this is a budgie problem specifically.

What follows is goiter: the thyroid enlarges and, because the glands sit right where the windpipe branches toward each lung, the swelling presses on the airway and the voicebox.

**That produces a sound.** A click, a wheeze, or persistent squeaking, and the detail that matters is that it comes on both the in-breath and the out-breath. A budgie making that noise should be seen by a vet and evaluated for a thyroid problem. Other signs that can come with it are vomiting and a visibly engorged jugular vein, and in a bad case a bird will hold its head up to keep the airway open.

It builds slowly, which is the cruel part, because the noise gets a little louder over months and a keeper hearing it daily stops noticing. Iodine deficiency can also produce a degree of hypothyroidism on its own: weight gain, fat deposits, lethargy and poor feather quality.

The good news is the shape of the fix. A mild case corrects with supplemental iodine; a severe one needs a vet, hospitalization and injections. Prevention is the same move as the rest of this guide, since formulated diets carry iodine in adequate amounts, and a bird kept on seed needs a proper vitamin and mineral supplement instead.

One caution worth carrying to the vet rather than solving at home: a thyroid tumor can look exactly like goiter. The difference is that goiter resolves quickly once iodine arrives and a tumor does not.

## Skip the Grit

Grit is sold beside budgie seed and it is one of the few products actively worth avoiding for this bird.

The logic behind grit applies to species that swallow seed whole and need help grinding it. A budgie does not do that: it shells the seed and eats the kernel, leaving the husk behind. So the grit has no job to do, and a bird that eats it in quantity can end up with a gastrointestinal obstruction.

## Fresh Food and Water

Vegetables are the part that gets treated as optional and should not be. They carry what the seed does not, and a budgie introduced to them early takes them far more readily than one meeting them at three years old.

Water needs to be there, clean, all the time. Dishes and bottles want washing daily with soap and water and a thorough rinse, because a dish that has held vegetables and droppings for a day is not somewhere you want a bird drinking.

## Moving a Seed Bird Onto Pellets

Do not do it by taking the seed away. A budgie that does not register pellets as food can starve with a full dish in front of it, which is why the [pellet conversion guide](/blog/bird-pellet-conversion-guide/) covers the stepped schedules, the daily weight check, and what to do when a bird stalls partway.

Two things that help specifically with this species: offer pellets first thing in the morning when the bird is hungriest, and scatter them on a flat surface rather than presenting them in a bowl, since foraging across a surface is closer to how a budgie eats anyway. Expect it to take a while. Days, weeks or months are all normal.
