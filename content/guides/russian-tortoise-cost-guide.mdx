# How Much Does a Russian Tortoise Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Russian tortoises are one of the more approachable tortoises to own, both in price and, unlike the sulcata, in the fact that this species can live its entire life indoors.

## The Tortoise: $50 to $400

Hatchlings and young captive-bred animals commonly run $50 to $150, with older captive-bred individuals reaching $225 to $400. Wild-caught adults occasionally show up cheaper, sometimes under $50 at reptile expos, though captive-bred is the better choice for this species, as for most.

One reason the smallest animals are not the ones in shops: a federal rule bars selling any turtle with a shell under four inches commercially, which pushes hatchlings out of retail and into private hands. Legal status varies more than the price tag suggests, Colorado bans the species outright and several other states require a permit; our [legal guide](/blog/russian-tortoise-legal-guide/) has the full state-by-state picture, worth checking before you buy.

## Ongoing Costs: $365 to $715 a Year

Greens, weeds and grass hay run $140 to $280 of that, supplements $20 to $35, substrate replacement $40 to $90, the annually replaced UVB bulb $45 to $90, electricity for heat and light $60 to $120, and an annual wellness check with a fecal test $60 to $100. That works out to roughly $30 to $60 a month. Electricity can spike somewhat in winter if you're maintaining warmth in a colder climate. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4fbLrVy" product="Penn-Plax E-Cuttlebone, Omega 3 & Honey Enriched (2 pack)">cuttlebone</AffiliateLink> as a calcium source is a small, worthwhile addition to that annual total, running $8 to $12 and lasting a good while.

## Vet Costs

A routine exotic-vet checkup runs around $75, meaningfully more reliable than a general vet visit for this species. Illness requiring tests or medication commonly runs $150 to $300 or more.

## Lifespan: The Number That Matters Most for Planning

40 years or more is typical, with some individuals reaching 50-plus. Run the annual figure across that span and it is the number that should actually decide the purchase: at $365 to $715 a year, a Russian tortoise costs roughly **$15,000 to $30,000 over a forty-year life**, before a single illness. That is a used car spread thin over four decades, and it is a larger commitment than the $50 hatchling price implies. This is a multi-decade, potentially generational commitment, the kind of pet you might realistically be planning a succession of care for, not just your own ownership.

## Why This Species Is a Good Fit for Apartment Living

This is the detail that separates the Russian tortoise from the sulcata. Sulcatas grow enormous and need real outdoor space within a few years, see our [Sulcata Tortoise cost guide](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-cost-guide/) for what that scale of commitment costs. For the fuller side-by-side beyond just the price tag, our [Sulcata Tortoise vs. Russian Tortoise guide](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-vs-russian-tortoise-guide/) walks through exactly how the space and lifespan tradeoffs compare. Russian tortoises stay small, 5 to 10 inches, and can be kept entirely indoors for their full, decades-long lifespan without ever needing outdoor housing. If you're drawn to tortoises but don't have, or won't eventually have, serious outdoor space, this is the more realistic choice.
