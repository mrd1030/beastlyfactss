# How Much Does a Russian Tortoise Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Russian tortoises are one of the more approachable tortoises to own, both in price and, unlike the sulcata, in the fact that this species can live its entire life indoors.

## The Tortoise: %%animal:russian-tortoise%%

Hatchlings and young captive-bred animals commonly run $50 to $150, with older captive-bred individuals reaching $225 to $400. Wild-caught adults occasionally show up cheaper, sometimes under $50 at reptile expos, though captive-bred is the better choice for this species, as for most.

One reason the smallest animals are not the ones in shops: a federal rule bars selling any turtle with a shell under four inches commercially, which pushes hatchlings out of retail and into private hands. Legal status varies more than the price tag suggests, Colorado bans the species outright and five other states require a permit; our [legal guide](/blog/russian-tortoise-legal-guide/) has the full state-by-state picture, worth checking before you buy.

## Upfront Setup: %%setup:russian-tortoise%%

<CostTable guide="russian-tortoise" section="necessities" />

Highly recommended, not counted in the total: a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076GXJFJG?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Solarmeter Model 6.5R Reptile UV Index Meter">UV index meter</AffiliateLink>, about $240 to $265. It is the only way to check that the UVB tube sits at the right height and to know when the tube needs replacing; UV cards do not measure UVB.

The wide top end is not padding. Three of those lines, the dimming thermostat, the infrared temperature gun and the gram scale, are the ones most first setups leave out, and they are exactly the equipment that prevents the thermal burns and the slow weight loss. Skipping them is how a setup lands at the low end and stays there until something goes wrong. An open-topped tortoise table, not an aquarium, is worth $125 to $250 of that total on its own. Every row adds up to %%setup:russian-tortoise%%, before the tortoise and its first vet checkup. A T5 tube's wattage is not its length: a 24-watt tube is about 22 inches long, a 39-watt tube about 34 inches and a 54-watt tube about 46 inches, so go by the length in inches when you match it to a third to half of the table length.

## Ongoing Costs: %%annual:russian-tortoise%% a Year

Greens, weeds and grass hay run $140 to $280 of that, supplement top-ups $20 to $35, substrate replacement $40 to $90, replacement UVB tubes $50 to $70 (two a year, one every 6 months), electricity for heat and light $60 to $120, and an annual wellness check with a fecal test $60 to $100. That works out to roughly $30 to $60 a month. Electricity can spike somewhat in winter if you're maintaining warmth in a colder climate. The cuttlebone from the first supply lasts a good while, and the supplement top-ups cover replacing it along with the calcium and multivitamin.

<FunFact>
A well-kept Russian tortoise can live 40 years or more, and with excellent care some reach 50 or beyond, all while staying small enough at 5 to 9 inches to spend that entire life on a tortoise table in your living room.
</FunFact>

## Vet Costs

A routine exotic-vet checkup runs around $75, meaningfully more reliable than a general vet visit for this species. Illness requiring tests or medication commonly runs $150 to $300 or more.

## Lifespan: The Number That Matters Most for Planning

40 years or more is typical, and with excellent care some reach 50 or beyond. Run the annual figure across that span and it is the number that should actually decide the purchase: at %%annual:russian-tortoise%% a year, a Russian tortoise costs **$14,800 to $27,800 over a forty-year life**, before a single illness. That is a used car spread thin over four decades, and it is a larger commitment than the $50 hatchling price implies. This is a multi-decade, potentially generational commitment, the kind of pet you might realistically be planning a succession of care for, not just your own ownership.

## Why This Species Is a Good Fit for Apartment Living

This is the detail that separates the Russian tortoise from the sulcata. Sulcatas grow enormous and need real outdoor space within a few years, and the [sulcata tortoise cost guide](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-cost-guide/) shows what that scale of commitment costs. [Sulcata Tortoise vs. Russian Tortoise](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-vs-russian-tortoise-guide/) walks through the space and lifespan tradeoffs beyond the price tag. Russian tortoises stay small, 5 to 9 inches, and can be kept entirely indoors for their full, decades-long lifespan without ever needing outdoor housing. If you're drawn to tortoises but don't have, or won't eventually have, serious outdoor space, this is the more realistic choice.
