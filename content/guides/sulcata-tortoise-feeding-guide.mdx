# Sulcata Tortoise Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why They Stop Eating

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Sulcata tortoises need a genuinely stricter diet than most tortoises kept as pets, closer to a grazing farm animal's than a typical "salad-eating reptile," and getting this right is one of the most important things you can do for this species' long-term health. For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Sulcata Tortoise care guide](/guides/sulcata-tortoise/).

## Feeding Schedule

Sulcatas are natural grazers, not meal-eaters, so grass and hay should be available essentially all the time, the non-negotiable foundation of the diet. Hatchlings can be offered chopped, softer greens daily since tough grass is hard for them to manage at first. Once your tortoise passes about 6 months, many keepers shift supplemental feedings to every other day, and adults commonly move to 3 to 4 times a week for greens and vegetables, always with hay available in the background.

The goal is slow, lean growth, a healthy juvenile should show a slight visible waistline from above, not a rounded, overfed silhouette. Overfeeding, too many pellets or too much rich food relative to grass, drives excessively fast growth that the shell can't structurally keep up with, which is what shell pyramiding actually is.

## What Sulcata Tortoises Actually Eat

Grass and grass hay should make up the overwhelming majority of a sulcata's diet, commonly cited at 75 to 80% or more. Timothy, orchard, meadow, Bermuda, and barley grass all work well, fresh where possible, hay as the winter and everyday backup. The remaining share should come from edible weeds, leaves, and flowers, dandelion, hibiscus, and prickly pear pads are all good choices. A high-fiber commercial tortoise pellet can round things out in modest amounts, but it's a supplement to grass, not a replacement for it. Fresh water should always be available, and juveniles benefit from regular soaking.

A multivitamin containing vitamin A and D3 is used more sparingly, somewhere between weekly and once every few weeks. Indoor tortoises without regular natural sunlight need D3 supplementation more than tortoises kept outdoors with real UV exposure. Leaving cuttlebone or crushed oyster shell in the enclosure gives your tortoise a free-choice calcium source alongside scheduled dusting, aim for a dietary calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of at least 2:1.

## Foods to Avoid

Skip animal protein entirely, no dog or cat food, no meat. This isn't a minor preference, feeding animal protein causes progressive kidney and liver damage over time and is considered one of the leading causes of premature death in captive sulcatas, arguably the single most consequential feeding mistake with this species. Fruit should be avoided or offered only as a rare, tiny treat, the sugar disrupts the gut bacteria this species relies on to digest fiber, leading to loose stools and digestive imbalance. Also limit high-protein "human" vegetables and legumes, and avoid high-oxalate foods like spinach or alfalfa hay as a dietary staple, both interfere with calcium absorption.

## Why Sulcata Tortoises Stop Eating

1. **An enclosure that isn't warm enough.** By far the most common cause. Sulcatas need real heat, roughly 85 to 95°F during the day with a basking spot, to digest at all, and appetite drops fast when temperatures fall short.
2. **Dehydration.** Suppresses appetite on its own and is worth ruling out alongside temperature before anything else.
3. **Mouth rot or oral lesions.** Infectious stomatitis makes eating physically painful.
4. **Vitamin A deficiency.** Shows up alongside lethargy and swollen, inflamed eyelids in addition to appetite loss.
5. **Bladder stones or reproductive issues.** Urogenital disease and egg-binding (dystocia) in females are both documented causes.
6. **Respiratory infection.** Usually from cold, damp conditions, and needs veterinary treatment.
7. **Parasites.** Can take months to cause visible symptoms, appetite loss is frequently the very first sign.
8. **Stress.** A new environment or a change in housing can cause a temporary, lower-urgency dip in appetite.

## When to Actually Worry

If your tortoise continues refusing food for more than about a week, or shows any other sign of illness, that's the point to see a vet rather than wait it out. Because parasite-driven appetite loss specifically can take months to become obvious, a tortoise that's slowly eating less over time deserves the same attention as one that stops outright.

Low humidity during the growing years is strongly linked to shell pyramiding, the single most common cosmetic and structural issue in this species, alongside overfeeding and too-rapid growth.
