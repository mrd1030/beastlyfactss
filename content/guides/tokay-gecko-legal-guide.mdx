# Is It Legal to Own a Tokay Gecko? State-by-State Guide

The tokay has a reputation problem. It is loud, it bites hard, and it is notoriously unwilling to let go once it has. Keepers assume an animal with that description must attract regulatory attention, and the opposite is true: it clears 49 of 52 jurisdictions with no permit at all, and in one state it is treated better than every other gecko.

## New Jersey Exempts the One Gecko You Would Expect It to Restrict

New Jersey requires a possession permit for exotic and nongame species, and its reptile list handles geckos in a single entry: Geckos - Family Gekkonidae other than Tokay Gecko.

Read that carefully, because the effect is the reverse of what a keeper would guess. A [crested gecko](/blog/crested-gecko-legal-guide/) needs a permit in New Jersey. A leopard gecko needs a permit. The tokay, the largest and by some distance the most defensive of the three, does not.

The rule does not say why, and it would be guessing to supply a reason. What can be said is the effect: a regulation that sounds restrictive contains a carve-out benefiting the species with the worst reputation in the group, and a keeper who reads the entry quickly will take away the opposite of what it says.

## The Two That Bar It Do So for Reasons That Have Nothing to Do With the Tokay

Hawaii names the species on Part A of its Restricted Animal List, which is headed for research and exhibition. That is a route for a university or a licensed exhibitor and not for a household. Hawaii's concern is establishment rather than temperament, and it applies the same treatment to geckos far more docile than this one.

The District of Columbia bars it by omission. Section 8-1808(j)(1) prohibits possession of wildlife with seven exceptions, and the reptile category covers non-venomous snakes, fish and turtles. No lizard appears anywhere in the seven, so the tokay is barred in the same breath as the bearded dragon and the green iguana, with nothing in the rule addressing any of them individually.

Minnesota's conditional status is likewise not about this species. Section 97A.401 subd. 8 makes snakes, lizards and salamanders protected wild animals as whole categories, regardless of where they come from.

## The Listing That Does Matter

The tokay gecko was added to CITES Appendix II at CoP18 in Geneva in August 2019, on a proposal brought jointly by the European Union, India, the Philippines and the United States. The proposal put documented exports above 770,000 animals a year, with actual trade likely past a million once undocumented export is counted. That listing governs international movement rather than domestic keeping, so it does not appear in any of the state answers above.

It is still the most likely paperwork a keeper will meet. Buying a captive-bred animal from a domestic breeder raises no CITES question at all. Importing one does, and the documentation is the seller's to produce.

## What to Check Before Buying

Wild-caught tokays are still common in the trade and arrive with parasite loads and a temperament shaped by capture, which is a welfare question rather than a legal one but worth the same attention.
