# How Much Does Discus Ownership Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Discus are one of the pricier community fish you can keep, and the cost isn't the fish itself, it's the filtration and water-change routine this species demands.

## The Fish: $20 to $450 or More

Young, common-strain discus run $20 to $40. Nicer adult specimens more typically run $50 to $150, and premium or rare strains, blue diamond, pigeon blood, and high-grade red melon among them, regularly climb to $200 to $450 or more per fish. Price is heavily strain-dependent, more so than for most other community fish, and it stacks up fast: discus are a social fish that do best in a group of 5 to 6 or more rather than alone or in pairs, so the starting cost is closer to $150 to $250 for a small group even at the budget end.

<FunFact>
Discus have been nicknamed the "king of the aquarium" for decades, largely for their color and their reputation as one of the harder freshwater fish to keep well. That reputation is also exactly why price varies so much within the species. A plain, common-strain juvenile and a show-quality pigeon blood adult are the same animal biologically, but selective breeding for pattern and color is what actually drives the gap between a $20 fish and a $400 one, not size or rarity in the wild.
</FunFact>

## Upfront Setup: Roughly $600 to $800

A 55-gallon tank is commonly cited as the practical entry point for a small group, though several experienced sources recommend 75 gallons or more, since a 55-gallon tank forces noticeably more frequent water changes to keep up with this species' bioload. Either way, tank height and filtration strength matter more here than the exact gallon number.

That's roughly $630 to $780 before you factor in water treatment. If your tap water is on the hard side, and discus want soft, slightly acidic water, a basic RO or RO/DI unit is a real additional cost, commonly landing somewhere in the $70 to $150-plus range depending on capacity. Between that and the case for a larger tank, an all-in budget of $700 to $900 is realistic for a proper first setup, which lines up with what dedicated discus-keeping sources actually recommend having on hand before you start.

Food and conditioner alone are a modest cost, similar to a lot of community fish. What actually pushes the discus budget up is the water-change habit this species needs to stay healthy long term, more electricity for a heater working to hold a warmer tank, and real water usage from changing a meaningful fraction of the tank on a regular basis rather than the smaller, less frequent changes a hardier community fish tolerates.

## Vet Costs

Aquatic vet care is uncommon and specialized. Most keepers manage illness themselves with water-quality correction and over-the-counter medications like <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01M5LEDRA?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Seachem MetroPlex Metronidazole">metronidazole (sold as Fish Zole or MetroPlex)</AffiliateLink> rather than professional visits. A water test kit on hand to catch a water-quality problem early does more for this species than for most, since discus decline is often not obvious until it's already advanced.

## Lifespan

10 to 15 years with excellent, stable care is the commonly cited range, though some sources put the typical captive lifespan closer to 8 to 10 years. Consistency is genuinely the deciding factor here more than for most community fish: discus kept in clean, stable, properly heated water tend to live meaningfully longer than ones kept in conditions that swing between good and neglected. Compare that to [angelfish](/blog/angelfish-cost-guide/), a related, hardier Amazonian cichlid, which tolerates a wider margin of error and is generally considered the more forgiving of the two.

## The Cost Lesson With This Species

The fish itself is rarely the biggest line item unless you're chasing a premium strain. What actually drives the discus budget is [filtration sized well above the tank's actual volume and a water-change routine](/blog/discus-tank-setup-guide/) more demanding than most freshwater community fish need, both non-negotiable for keeping this species healthy long term rather than just alive.
