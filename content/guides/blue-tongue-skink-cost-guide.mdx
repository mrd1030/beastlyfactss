# How Much Does a Blue-Tongued Skink Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

In a lot of ways, "blue-tongued skink" is two pets wearing one name, and nowhere is that clearer than the price tag. Where your skink comes from, Northern Australia or Indonesia, changes almost every number in this guide. For the full care picture beyond budgeting, see our complete [Blue-Tongued Skink care guide](/guides/blue-tongue-skink/).

## The Skink Itself: $100 to $700+, and the Price Gap Has a Reason

Northern (Australian) blue-tongued skinks typically run $150 for babies up to $250 for adults, with high-color or rare morphs reaching $400 to $700. Indonesian skinks are noticeably cheaper, generally $100 to $250. That price gap isn't random. Australia's export laws make it extremely difficult to bring wild Northern skinks out of the country, so nearly all Northerns in the US pet trade are captive-bred, which costs more to produce but results in tamer, healthier animals. Indonesia has no such restriction, and most Indonesian skinks sold in the US are still wild-caught, which keeps the price down but comes with real downsides covered in our [health issues guide](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-health-issues-guide/).

## Vet Costs

A routine exam runs roughly $100 to $150, with annual checkups recommended for any skink. Here's the detail that matters most for budgeting: wild-caught Indonesian skinks frequently need a fecal exam and deworming treatment right after purchase, an added cost that Northern buyers usually skip entirely. Our [health issues guide](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-health-issues-guide/) covers exactly why that risk exists and what treatment involves.

## Lifespan: Genuinely Long

15 to 20 years is the commonly cited average, with well-documented cases living past 30. This is a multi-decade commitment regardless of which type you choose, worth sitting with before bringing one home.

## The Real Cost Decision

The cheaper Indonesian skink isn't actually the budget option once you factor in likely deworming treatment, a longer taming process, and generally higher parasite-related vet costs early on. For a first-time keeper, a captive-bred Northern usually works out to a comparable total cost with meaningfully lower risk.
