# Common Neon Tetra Health Issues (Including the Disease Named After This Fish)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most neon tetra health problems are treatable and trace back to water quality. One is different: it is incurable, and it carries this fish's name.

## Neon Tetra Disease (NTD)

This condition is named after the species because neon tetras are especially associated with it, caused by a parasite that invades the fish's muscle tissue.

Watch for: restless or erratic swimming, often more noticeable at night, fading or patchy color, particularly along the back, visible lumps under the skin, a curved or deformed spine in advanced cases, and difficulty swimming normally.

This is highly contagious to other fish in the tank, spreading when fish consume infected tissue, including from a fish that's already died. It can also affect other species sharing the tank, cardinal tetras, [guppies](/blog/guppy-health-issues-guide/), and several others among them. It is not transmissible to humans.

There is no effective cure once the disease reaches an advanced stage. The fish will not recover, and aquatic specialists recommend humane euthanasia for a confirmed case, both to end its suffering and to protect the rest of the tank. Prevention is the only tool available: [quarantine new fish](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) for 2 to 4 weeks before adding them to an established tank, buy from sources with healthy stock, and never feed a tank the remains of a fish that died from an unknown cause.

<FunFact>
NTD is likely over-diagnosed in the hobby. True cases are relatively uncommon in a well-established, well-filtered tank, and the signs overlap with treatable bacterial infections, especially in newer or under-filtered setups.
</FunFact>

## The Lookalike That Can Be Treated

A neon that turns pale in patches, grows restless or hangs apart from the school is not always a lost fish. Columnaris, a bacterial infection, produces signs close enough to NTD that the two get mixed up, and columnaris responds to antibacterial treatment. That makes the order of events matter. Move the fish to a hospital tank, treat it as bacterial, and watch. A fish that improves had columnaris. A fish that keeps declining through a full course points back to NTD.

Stock quality sits behind a lot of this. Decades of mass commercial breeding have left morphological defects and ill health widespread in farmed neons, so the fish that arrive in a shop are not all equally sound. Choosing a tank of bright, evenly striped fish that school tightly, and skipping one with pale or lagging fish, prevents more than any medication cures.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt, along with flashing against decor and rapid breathing. From stress or temperature instability. This one is treatable. Raise the temperature a couple of degrees a day, but only to about 81°F, the top of a neon's workable range. The 86°F that kills ich on its own is [past what this fish tolerates](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/), so a standard commercial ich medication, or aquarium salt at about a tablespoon per 5 gallons, does the killing.

## Fin Rot

Ragged or bloody-edged fins, bacterial, from poor water quality or an untreated injury. Treatable with an antibacterial medication alongside correcting water conditions.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems from overfeeding or a poor diet. Manageable through proper feeding.

## When a Neon Has to Be Put Down

A confirmed NTD case ends in euthanasia, and it should not end in the toilet or the freezer. A fish vet is the first choice, found through the professional directories that list [vets who treat fish](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/). Where none can be reached, a clove oil overdose in a container of the fish's own tank water is the humane method, and for a fish the size of a neon that container can be small. Remove the body from the display tank at once either way, since tankmates that eat it are how NTD spreads.
