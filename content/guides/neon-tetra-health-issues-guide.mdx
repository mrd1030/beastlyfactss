# Common Neon Tetra Health Issues (Including the Disease Named After This Fish)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

The neon tetra is a fish of about 1.2 inches from the forest streams of the Amazon basin, and most of the ones sold today were bred commercially. Most of its health problems are treatable and trace back to water quality. One is different: it is incurable, and it carries this fish's name.

## Signs That Need Action Today

A neon school is checked as a group: a count at feeding time, and a look for the one fish that has drifted away from the rest. These signs cannot wait for tomorrow.

**Test ammonia and nitrite first:**

- Fast breathing or gasping at the surface.
- Several fish turning lethargic or refusing food at once, the pattern an immature tank produces.
- A fish found dead. The body comes out before anything else, since tankmates that eat it are how neon tetra disease spreads.

**Start ich treatment today:**

- Small white spots like grains of salt, with flashing against decor and rapid breathing.

**Move the fish to a hospital tank today:**

- Restless or erratic swimming, often more noticeable at night.
- Fading or patchy color, particularly along the back.
- Lumps under the skin, or a curved or deformed spine.
- A fish hanging apart from the school.

**Watch closely and check again tomorrow:**

- Ragged or bloody-edged fins.
- A cotton-like growth on the skin or fins.
- A fish growing thin while it keeps eating.
- A fish floating, sinking or struggling to hold its level.

The hospital-tank signs belong to neon tetra disease, and also to a bacterial infection that can be treated, which is why the fish is isolated and treated before anyone gives up on it.

## What a Healthy Neon Looks Like

| Check | Healthy | Worth a closer look |
|-------|---------|---------------------|
| Skin and scales | Smooth, no blemishes | White patches, lumps, cotton-like tufts |
| Fins | Whole, edges clean | Ragged or bloody-edged |
| Eyes | Bright, not cloudy | Cloudy or bulging |
| Swimming | Steady, inside the school | Restless, erratic, or apart from the group |
| Body | Evenly filled out | Growing thin on a full appetite |
| Color | Bright blue stripe over red by day | Faded through the day, or patchy along the back |

Color is the check most likely to raise a false alarm. A neon turns duller at night, its stripe shifting toward violet, and it lightens over a pale substrate in bright, clear water. A fish that looks washed out first thing in the morning is wearing its night coloring.

## Water Quality Comes First

Neons do best in soft, slightly acidic water held steady: 70 to 77°F, pH 6.0 to 7.0 and 1 to 12 dGH. Steadiness is most of the job. A stable temperature maintained with a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">aquarium heater</AffiliateLink> and regular partial water changes using a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rg5gTT" product="Water conditioner">water conditioner</AffiliateLink> prevent far more than any medication does after the fact.

The two riskiest tanks are the newest and the most neglected. Fish in an immature tank can suddenly turn lethargic, stop eating and die, usually from ammonia or nitrite. In an old tank where water changes are rare and evaporation is only topped up, pH can sink gradually or crash, and a rapid fall can kill the fish outright. Soft neon water carries little carbonate hardness to buffer that drop. Testing pH, alkalinity, ammonia, nitrite and nitrate every two weeks catches both problems while they are still numbers.

Food is the other half. Flakes can be too large for a small-mouthed fish to swallow easily, so they get crushed, and feeding a wide variety is the practical way to meet its nutritional needs.

## Ich: Warm Only to 77°F, and Skip the Copper

Ich, or white spot disease, shows as small white spots resembling grains of salt, along with flashing against decor and rapid breathing. The cause is the parasite Ichthyophthirius multifiliis; stress and temperature swings open the door.

This one is treatable. Raise the temperature a couple of degrees a day, but only to 77°F, the top of a neon's range, since warmth speeds the parasite's life cycle and brings it into reach of treatment sooner. At 75 to 79°F that cycle takes about three to six days. The 86°F that most ich strains cannot survive [sits well outside this fish's range](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/), so one of these does the killing:

- **Aquarium salt** at about a tablespoon per 5 gallons.
- **A commercial ich medication without copper.** Copper products are the ones to pass over in soft neon water, for the reason set out under copper below.

Medication is dosed daily, often for three to seven treatments, and carried on until the deaths stop.

## Fin Rot and Fungus: Damage First, Then Infection

Both tend to follow something else going wrong. Fin rot is bacterial: ragged or bloody-edged fins, from poor water quality or an untreated injury. Water molds such as Saprolegnia, which are fungus-like organisms and not true fungi, move into tissue already damaged by an injury, bad water or a chill, and show as grayish-white, cotton-like growths on the skin, gills, eyes or fins. Columnaris, the bacterial lookalike of neon tetra disease, can also leave slimy or cotton-like patches.

Fin rot is treatable with an antibacterial medication alongside correcting water conditions. In clean water, a single potassium permanganate treatment can often control external water mold. When a bacterial infection does not respond, lab testing picks the next drug. Switching quickly between antibiotics, or giving several at once, breeds resistant bacteria.

## Internal Parasites

A neon that thins while it keeps eating may be carrying worms or protozoa. Camallanus is the one that shows: blood-red worms protruding from the vent. Cyclops and Daphnia are its intermediate hosts, so live crustaceans can bring it in, and tapeworm larvae reach fish the same way, inside the invertebrates they eat. Capillaria causes weight loss and a pot belly, Spironucleus causes weight loss, a poor appetite and deaths among young fish, and heavy tapeworm loads stunt growth and swell the belly, though many infected fish show nothing at all.

Each one takes a different drug, and a wet mount under a microscope is what tells them apart. That puts the choice with a vet or a diagnostic lab, since a dewormer guessed from the signs may be the wrong one.

## Neon Tetra Disease and Its Lookalikes

This condition is named after the species because neon tetras are especially associated with it. The cause is a microsporidian parasite, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, that invades the fish's skeletal muscle. The neon is its type host, but it infects some 20 species of freshwater fish.

Watch for: restless or erratic swimming, often more noticeable at night, fading or patchy color, particularly along the back, visible lumps under the skin, a curved or deformed spine in advanced cases, and difficulty swimming normally.

This is highly contagious to other fish in the tank, spreading when fish consume infected tissue, including from a fish that's already died. It can also affect other species sharing the tank, among them guppies, barbs and angelfish. It is not transmissible to humans.

There is no effective cure once the disease reaches an advanced stage. The spores are extremely resistant, and microsporidia are considered untreatable. The fish will not recover, and humane euthanasia for a confirmed case ends its suffering and protects the rest of the tank. Prevention is the only tool available: quarantine new fish for at least 30 days before adding them to an established tank, buy from sources with healthy stock, and never feed a tank the remains of a fish that died from an unknown cause.

NTD is easy to misidentify. It gets mistaken for columnaris and other whitish-film conditions, because they can all leave pale patches on the fish. Confirming it takes a microscope with good lenses, and the spores are found in skeletal muscle.

### The Lookalike That Can Be Treated

A neon that turns pale in patches, grows restless or hangs apart from the school is not always a lost fish. Columnaris, a bacterial infection, produces signs close enough to NTD that the two get mixed up, and columnaris responds to antibacterial treatment. That makes the order of events matter. Move the fish to a hospital tank, treat it as bacterial, and watch. A fish that improves had columnaris. A fish that keeps declining through a full course has not been proven to have NTD, because a second untreatable disease fits the same picture. Only a wet mount of the affected muscle, read under a microscope, confirms the NTD spores.

### The Lookalike That Cannot: Mycobacteriosis

Tetras belong to one of the fish families more prone to mycobacteriosis, a bacterial disease. Its signs vary from fish to fish: paleness, wasting, a bent spine or other skeletal deformity, bulging eyes, skin ulcers and a swollen belly can all appear, which matches NTD at the pale color and the curved spine. There are no effective treatments that clear mycobacteria from a fish. Low dissolved oxygen, low pH and a heavy organic load favor it, and an infected tank is disinfected before other fish go in. Mycobacteria can infect people as well.

For both diseases, the dead fish is the danger: it comes out the moment it is found, whatever killed it.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems from overfeeding or a poor diet. Manageable through proper feeding.

## Copper, Soft Water and Shrimp

Copper is a standard ich and parasite treatment, and a neon tank holds about the worst water for it. The lower the total alkalinity, the more toxic copper is to fish, and once alkalinity drops under 50 ppm, about 2.8 dKH, the chance of killing the fish is high enough that copper is not recommended. Wild neons live in water with negligible carbonate hardness, and a soft tank kept to match can sit under that line. So the alkalinity test comes before any copper product is even considered.

Copper is also toxic to invertebrates, snails included. No medication label makes it safe to treat around them: every shrimp and snail is moved out first, and treating in the hospital tank keeps the drug away from the display tank and everything living in it.

## Quarantine and Buying Clean

New fish often show active disease one to three weeks after shipping, which is why a new neon spends [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before it meets the school, and longer when anything shows. A neon added later goes through its own 30 days. Buying well prevents the most. Watch the fish in the shop before choosing, and pass on any tank holding fish that are restless, carry white patches on the skin or keep apart from the main school. Where NTD signs show, skip the purchase entirely. The tank itself should be clean, and the other fish in it should look healthy too.

Stock quality sits behind a lot of this. Years of commercial breeding have left the farmed strain somewhat less robust than wild fish, so the fish that arrive in a shop are not all equally sound. Choosing a tank of bright, evenly striped fish that school tightly, and skipping one with pale or lagging fish, prevents more than any medication cures.

## When a Fish Vet Is Worth It, and How to Find One

Nearly every diagnosis above ends at a microscope: the spores in muscle, the parasites in a wet mount, the bacteria behind a course that failed. Fish are often treated with the wrong over-the-counter medicine, and when the first one fails and a second is tried, the cycle usually does more harm than good.

A vet's license covers one state, yet any vet may call in an aquatic vet from any state to consult, which makes the household's own exotic or small-animal vet a workable first call. The American Association of Fish Veterinarians keeps a directory of fish vets by state or country, and one fish vet's own advice to keepers names the World Aquatic Veterinary Medical Association as the place to look beyond it. An online consultation with no exam is not a legal basis for a treatment plan.

Bring:

- About a quart of tank water in a clean container, drawn as soon as the fish start acting wrong and before any water change, and kept cold. The water the fish travels in does not count: its pH drops and its ammonia rises on the way.
- The sickest fish that is still alive. A dead fish is rarely usable, and a frozen one is not.
- The records: tank size and age, the species and numbers in it, recent additions, when the signs began, what food and how much, the last water change, and any treatment with its dose.

## When a Neon Has to Be Put Down

A confirmed NTD case ends in euthanasia, and it should not end in the toilet or the freezer. A fish vet is the first choice. Where none can be reached, [a clove oil overdose](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) in a container of the fish's own tank water is the humane method, and for a fish the size of a neon that container can be small. Remove the body from the display tank at once either way, since tankmates that eat it are how NTD spreads.

## The Pattern Worth Remembering

Nearly everything treatable on this list traces back to water quality and stability, exactly what a properly cycled tank with consistent temperature and regular water changes prevents. The one condition that isn't treatable, NTD, is prevented primarily through quarantine, the single habit that matters most for keeping this species healthy long-term.
