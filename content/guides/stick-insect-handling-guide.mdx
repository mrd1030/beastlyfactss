# How to Handle a Stick Insect (And Why You Can Keep More Than One)

Stick insects are docile, slow, and low-drama to keep, relying entirely on camouflage and stillness rather than any real defense.

## The Right Way to Handle One

Gently grasp the body or thorax if you need to move a stick insect, and never grab a leg. Most species practice autotomy, deliberately dropping a leg to escape a threat, and a leg lost this way doesn't fully regenerate in adults, only partially across molts in younger animals. Some species also have mildly irritating leg spines or can release a faint defensive scent, minor compared to most invertebrate defenses, but worth a gentle approach regardless.

<FunFact>
Many stick insect species can deliberately drop a leg to escape a threat, a defense called autotomy, but that dropped leg only partially regrows across molts in younger animals and doesn't come back at all once the insect reaches adulthood.
</FunFact>

## Signs It Is Stressed by You

A stick insect that is stressed by handling shows it in three ways, and one of them is permanent.

- **A dropped leg.** The Amateur Entomologists' Society caresheet says stick insects can lose limbs for a number of reasons, and rough handling is one of the causes it lists. Some species shed legs far more readily than others: it names the pink winged stick insect as one that loses them very easily. A lost leg regrows only at the next molt, so an adult keeps the gap for life. If a leg comes off, the session was too rough. Next time let the insect step off onto a plant on its own.
- **Playing dead.** A stick insect that goes rigid and drops, or stays stiff in your hand, is not calm. It is in thanatosis. Skelhorn showed in *Current Biology* (2018) that birds which had learned dead Indian stick insects were unpalatable went on to reject live insects displaying thanatosis, which is the point of the act. In a keeper's hand it means the insect has classed the hand as a predator. Put it down on its food plant and leave it.
- **A smell.** Stick insects generally carry a pair of repellent glands in the front of the thorax and release defensive chemicals when disturbed, per a 2024 anatomical survey in *BMC Zoology* by Niekampf and colleagues. A whiff during handling is the same message as the leg and the stiffness.

Refusing to climb or move is not on the caresheet, and no source here describes it. Stick insects spend most of the day motionless by design, so stillness alone means nothing. The society's practical lines are the ones to keep: the Indian and giant spiny stick insects are the sturdy species to hand to a child, a few species such as the giant spiny, the jungle nymph, and the thorn legged stick insect can pinch and bite if they are not used to being handled, and great care in handling at all times.

## A Social Invertebrate, Unlike the Mantis

This is the detail that sets stick insects apart from the [praying mantis](/blog/praying-mantis-handling-guide/). Mantises are solitary and cannibalistic, always housed one per enclosure. Stick insects are the opposite: herbivorous, non-aggressive toward each other, and Indian stick insects specifically can be housed in groups without issue. If you're looking for an invertebrate you can keep as a small colony rather than a single animal, this is genuinely the better fit.

## A Detail Worth Knowing: Reproduction Without Males

Several commonly kept species, Indian and Vietnamese stick insects among them, reproduce parthenogenetically, meaning females produce viable eggs without a male present at all. This means a single female, or a small group of females, can genuinely establish an ongoing colony on their own, worth knowing if you're not looking to breed but might end up with more stick insects than you started with regardless.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Grabbing by a leg instead of the body
- Providing an enclosure too short for a proper molt
- Leaving an open water dish, which can drown nymphs, mist for hydration instead
- Feeding the wrong host plant for your specific species
