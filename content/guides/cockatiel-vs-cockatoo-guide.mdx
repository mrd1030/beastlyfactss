# Cockatiel vs. Cockatoo: Which Parrot Actually Fits Your Life

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Both are popular, intelligent parrots with real personality, but they sit at almost opposite ends of what "keeping a parrot" actually demands. The real differences come down to size, noise, and just how much of your daily life a cockatoo expects to be part of. ## Size and Noise Set the Tone Immediately

A cockatiel tops out around 12 to 13 inches and 90 grams, small enough to fit comfortably in most homes without a dedicated bird room. A cockatoo ranges enormously by species, from a smaller Goffin's cockatoo up to a Moluccan or umbrella cockatoo pushing over a kilogram with a wingspan to match. Noise scales right alongside that size difference: a cockatiel mostly whistles and chatters at a volume most households barely register, while cockatoos are consistently ranked among the loudest birds anyone keeps as a pet, capable of screams that genuinely carry through walls.

<KeyTakeaway>
Choose a Cockatiel if you want a genuinely manageable first parrot that fits a normal household and schedule. You get real personality and an expressive, easy-to-read crest, without the noise or space a cockatoo demands.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The "Velcro Bird" Reputation Is Not an Exaggeration

Cockatoos are famous for bonding so intensely to a person that owners describe them as wanting physical contact for hours a day, which is where the "velcro bird" nickname comes from. That's not a small quirk, it's a defining feature of the species that shapes an owner's entire schedule, and it cuts both ways: a cockatoo that gets that level of attention consistently can be an extraordinary companion, while one that doesn't frequently develops screaming and feather-destructive behavior as a stress response. Cockatiels bond just as genuinely but in a much less demanding way, content with real but reasonable interaction rather than near-constant contact.

<FunFact>
A cockatiel's crest works almost like a built-in mood indicator: straight up usually signals excitement or alarm, held back and relaxed signals contentment, and flattened tight against the head signals fear or aggression. Cockatoos and a handful of other crested parrots share the same basic signal, but a cockatoo's far more powerful beak means misreading that warning sign carries a lot more risk.
</FunFact>

## Beak Strength and Destructive Potential

A cockatoo's beak is built to crack tough nuts and dense wood in the wild, and that same power shows up at home as serious destructive chewing when the bird is bored or under-stimulated, wood trim, furniture, and cage bars are all fair game. <KeyTakeaway>
A Cockatoo is the better fit only if you can realistically offer it hours of daily contact and enrichment for decades. Falling short of that isn't a minor shortfall, it's the single biggest driver of the screaming and feather-plucking that lands so many cockatoos in parrot rescues.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Lifespan Gap Is Enormous

A cockatiel's 15 to 25 year lifespan is already a real commitment. A cockatoo's is on another scale entirely: commonly 40 to 60 years, and the largest species have documented individuals living into their 70s, with at least one well-documented Moluccan cockatoo reaching 92 in captivity. A cockatoo bought today can genuinely outlive the person who bought it, which is worth taking as seriously as the daily attention it needs.

## Bottom Line

Pick a cockatiel if you want a genuinely rewarding, expressive parrot that fits a normal household without demanding your whole day. Pick a cockatoo only if you can realistically offer the hours of daily contact, mental engagement, and multi-decade commitment the species needs, and have a plan for who cares for it if it outlives you. Neither is the "easy" bird, they're just demanding in very different ways.
