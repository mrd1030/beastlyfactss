# Common Angelfish Health Issues (And What Actually Prevents Them)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Angelfish are hardy in a properly sized, well-maintained tank, and most health problems trace back to water quality and stress.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt, along with flashing against decor, clamped fins, and rapid gill movement. Triggered by stress and sudden temperature changes or declining water quality. Angelfish can take the 86°F that most ich strains cannot survive, so [heat is a real treatment for this species](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/): raise the temperature a couple of degrees a day to 86°F, run an air stone since warm water holds less oxygen, and hold it for two weeks, alongside an anti-ich or copper-based medication. Move affected fish into a bare 10 to 20 gallon [hospital tank](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) where possible, and give any new angelfish 30 to 60 days there before it joins the display.

## Hexamita ("Hole-in-the-Head" Disease)

A protozoan parasite that turns opportunistic when a fish's immune system is already compromised. Watch for pitting lesions on the head and along the lateral line, white, stringy feces, appetite loss, and fading color. This is genuinely linked to poor water quality, overcrowding, and vitamin deficiency, not just bad luck. Treat with <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01M5LEDRA?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Seachem MetroPlex Metronidazole">metronidazole (sold as Fish Zole or MetroPlex)</AffiliateLink> alongside correcting the underlying water conditions, treatment alone without fixing the environment tends not to hold.

<FunFact>
Hexamita isn't some rare invader, it's often already present in a tank and kept in check by a healthy immune system. It only turns into visible "hole-in-the-head" disease once poor water quality, overcrowding, or vitamin deficiency give it an opening, which is exactly why treating the fish with medication alone, without fixing the tank conditions behind it, tends not to hold.
</FunFact>

## Fin Rot

Ragged, receding fins with discolored edges, bacterial, usually from poor water quality or an untreated fin injury, sometimes from fin-nipping by a tankmate. Correcting water quality plus an antibacterial treatment resolves most cases caught early, though damaged tissue may not fully regrow. Territorial disputes between angelfish, or with tankmates, are a real source of the fin-nipping injuries that can start this process.

## Cotton Wool Disease (Fungal Infection)

Fluffy white growths, typically secondary to an existing injury or a period of stress. Isolate the affected fish and treat with an antifungal medication alongside water quality correction.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Nearly everything on this list connects back to water quality and stress. A properly cycled tank, a stable temperature maintained with a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">aquarium heater</AffiliateLink>, and regular partial water changes prevent far more illness than any medication does after symptoms appear. The routine is [25 to 30% a week with nitrate tested first](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/): under 20 ppm it is working, and past 40 ppm the next change is 50%. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">water test kit</AffiliateLink> lets you catch declining water quality before it becomes a visible problem, rather than finding out after the fact. Given how sensitive Hexamita specifically is to overcrowding and poor water, adequate tank size and filtration aren't just about comfort, they're preventive medicine for this species.

## What a Vet Has to Confirm

Several angelfish problems look alike from outside the glass and are told apart only under a microscope, which is where a fish vet earns the visit.

**Thin fish that keep eating.** Cichlids, angelfish included, are highly susceptible to the intestinal flagellate Spironucleus, which causes weight loss and kills fry and juveniles. The worm Capillaria is common in freshwater angelfish, and heavy infections in juveniles stunt growth and leave fish unable to survive shipping and handling. Cryptobia produces the most confusing picture: extreme weight loss in a fish that may be eating greedily yet keeps a sunken belly. Each is diagnosed from a wet mount of gut contents or tissue, and the treatments differ. Spironucleus is treated with metronidazole, Capillaria with a wormer such as fenbendazole dosed by the fish's weight in its food, and Cryptobia has no treatment at all.

**A grey sheen of mucus.** A herpesvirus of angelfish makes affected fish produce so much skin mucus that they look grey, and they often carry several parasite and bacterial infections at once. There is no treatment, and survivors are suspected to stay carriers, which matters for anyone selling or trading fish from that tank.

Most small-animal clinics do not see fish, so finding one that does is its own step, and the two vet directories and the fallback routes are in [the guide to spotting a sick fish](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/). The same page covers humane euthanasia, which is the recommendation for a fish with confirmed Cryptobia: affected fish should be culled, since they do not recover and the tank's management is what protects the rest.

[Overfeeding](/blog/angelfish-feeding-guide/) is one of the quieter causes of the water-quality problems behind most of this list.
