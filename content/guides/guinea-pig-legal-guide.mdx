# Is It Legal to Own a Guinea Pig? The Short Answer

The guinea pig is the least restricted animal on the exotic pet law map. Fifty-one jurisdictions place no condition on it whatsoever, and the fifty-second, Hawaii, allows it by name. No state requires a permit, none bans it, and none leaves the answer in doubt. That is unusual enough to be worth explaining, and it leaves room for the question people arrive with instead.

## Being on the List Is the Point

Hawaii's conditional approval sounds like a restriction and functions as the opposite. Hawaii bars the introduction of any non-domestic animal that appears on none of its lists, so the thing that matters is whether a species was written down at all. The guinea pig was, under both its current name and an older synonym.

That is the whole of Hawaii's answer. The contrast with animals Hawaii does restrict is instructive: the [degu](/blog/degu-legal-guide/) appears on no Hawaii list under any name and is therefore barred, while the guinea pig and the chinchilla are both conditionally approved. Hawaii works species by species rather than by group, and the guinea pig made the cut.

## Domestication Is Doing the Work

The guinea pig's clean record across the mainland comes down to two things happening at once. It is domesticated, with thousands of years of selective breeding behind it and no wild population of Cavia porcellus anywhere, and it was already an ordinary pet when most state rodent rules were written.

That combination puts it on the short cleared list in state after state. Alaska clears the guinea pig and the cavy by name. Wyoming's domesticated list clears it. Oklahoma, Arkansas and Connecticut all name it among the rodents excepted from otherwise sweeping restrictions. California restricts the entire order Rodentia and then names five exceptions, and the guinea pig is one of them.

Read enough of those lists and the pattern behind the whole rodent section of this map becomes clear: the animals that clear are the ones that were already in living rooms when someone sat down to write the rule.

## The Pairs Question

A large share of the legal questions people ask about guinea pigs are not about state law at all. They are some version of whether buying just one is illegal.

In the United States, it is not, anywhere. No state, no territory on this map, and no federal rule requires guinea pigs to be kept in pairs or groups.

The rule people are thinking of is real but foreign. Article 13 of Switzerland's Animal Protection Ordinance, in force since 2008, requires social species to be kept with at least one animal of the same species, and guinea pigs are on that list alongside mice, rats, gerbils, degus, chinchillas and ferrets. It circulates online often enough, usually with the country stripped off, that it reads as though it were general law. It is not US law and never has been.

None of that makes solitary keeping a good idea. Guinea pigs are highly social and the welfare case for pairs is strong and separate from the legal one. The honest position is that the law leaves this to the keeper, and the animal does better with company regardless.

## What to Check Before Buying

With state law effectively silent, the constraints that bite are tenancy terms, HOA rules and the occasional municipal cap on animal numbers.
