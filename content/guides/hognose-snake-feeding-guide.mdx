# Hognose Snake Feeding: Prey Size, Schedules, and the Picky Hatchling

<VetDisclaimer />

A western hognose hatchling can weigh three grams. At that size the question is not which mouse to buy but which part of one, and Reptiles Magazine's sizing chart starts exactly there: "Prey size for micro hogs 3 grams and under, small pinky head."

## Prey Size

The chart runs by weight, which is more useful than by age for an animal that arrives at wildly different sizes.

- 3 grams and under: a small pinky head
- 4 to 6 grams: extra small pinks
- 7 to 9 grams: small to medium pinks
- Larger juveniles: large pinks to peach fuzzy, then peach fuzzy to fuzzy

Past the hatchling stage the rule stops being a chart and becomes a proportion. Reptiles Magazine: "A good rule of thumb is a prey item slightly thicker than their center mass." The Reptile Centre describes the same progression in words, hatchlings on pinky mice with sizes increasing "until the snake is taking large mice or even jumbos."

Mice are the staple. The Reptile Centre calls them "the best diet for a western hognose snake", with larger individuals sometimes taking smaller rats, and treats gerbils, hamsters, multimammate mice or chicks as fallbacks for a snake that will not take anything else.

## How Often, and Why Adults Eat Less

Both sources agree that feeding slows as the snake matures. They put the adult interval in different places, and the gap is worth seeing.

Reptiles Magazine feeds young snakes every 5 to 7 days, keeps adult males at 70 grams and up on a hopper or a couple of fuzzies with the frequency dropping as metabolism slows, and moves mature females to every 10 to 14 days, with some large females going 14 to 21.

The Reptile Centre is simpler: hatchlings weekly, adults "once every two weeks as they can become overweight if fed weekly."

Every two weeks sits inside both. The reason both slow down is the same one the Reptile Centre states outright, and it is the most common husbandry mistake with this species: an adult kept on a hatchling's schedule gets fat. The MSD Veterinary Manual makes the general version of the point for reptiles, noting that diets high in protein may predispose them to uric acid accumulation, which can cause gout.

## Frozen Thawed

Both sources feed thawed prey. Reptiles Magazine describes approaching a snake "with a frozen-thawed pinky mouse", and the Reptile Centre specifies frozen thawed foods over live.

The reason is straightforward: a live rodent can injure a snake that does not immediately subdue it, and a hognose working a three-gram meal has no margin for that. Thawed prey also lets the keeper control temperature and size precisely.

## The Refusing Hatchling

This species has a reputation for it, and a hatchling that turns down a meal is not automatically in trouble.

Reptiles Magazine's threshold is practical: start scenting "if they refuse their first three meal offerings." The scents it lists are fish (sardine, tuna, salmon), chicken bone broth, and frog, toad, lizard or gecko, with commercial scent strips as a packaged option. The amphibian scents map onto what the species evolved eating, which is why they work on an animal ignoring a plain pinky.

Two other causes are seasonal. A male ready to breed may refuse, and seasonal change heading toward brumation can put a snake off food on its own. Neither is fixed by offering more food.

A refusal that continues past that, or comes with weight loss, is a vet question, no longer a feeding one.

## Feed Off the Substrate

The hognose set is consistent on this and it belongs here too: feed in a separate container away from the loose, dry, diggable substrate this species needs. A snake that takes a mouse with a mouthful of substrate attached is the impaction case, and the [tank setup guide](/blog/hognose-snake-tank-setup-guide/) explains why the substrate has to be deep and loose in the first place.

That is also the one place where the display enclosure and the feeding setup should look nothing alike. Plain, lined, boring, easy to clean.
