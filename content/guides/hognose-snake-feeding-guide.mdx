# Hognose Snake Feeding: Prey Size, Schedules, and the Picky Hatchling

<VetDisclaimer />

A western hognose hatchling can weigh three grams. At that size the question is not which mouse to buy but which part of one, and the sizing charts start exactly there: for a hog of 3 grams and under, a small pinky head.

## Prey Size

The chart runs by weight, which is more useful than by age for an animal that arrives at wildly different sizes.

- 3 grams and under: a small pinky head
- 4 to 6 grams: extra small pinks
- 7 to 9 grams: small to medium pinks
- Larger juveniles: large pinks to peach fuzzy, then peach fuzzy to fuzzy

Past the hatchling stage, sizing goes by proportion instead: prey no wider than the snake's head. The progression runs from pinky mice at hatching up through fuzzies and hoppers. Where it ends depends on the animal: a 70 gram male may settle on a hopper or a couple of fuzzies, while a large adult female works up to considerably bigger prey.

Mice are the staple, rotated with other prey rather than fed alone, since a diet of nothing but mice is calorie-dense. Larger individuals sometimes take smaller rats. Gerbils, hamsters, multimammate mice and chicks are fallbacks for a snake that will not take anything else.

## How Often, and Why Adults Eat Less

Hatchlings and juveniles eat every 4 to 5 days. Once grown, a hognose of either sex eats about once a week.

The reason to stretch the interval is the most common husbandry mistake with this species: an adult kept on a hatchling's schedule gets fat. Across reptiles generally, diets high in protein may also predispose them to uric acid accumulation, which can cause gout.

## Frozen Thawed

Thawed prey over live, without exception.

The reason is straightforward: a live rodent can injure a snake that does not immediately subdue it, and a three gram hatchling taking a pinky head has no margin for that. Thawed prey also lets the keeper control temperature and size precisely.

## The Refusing Hatchling

This species has a reputation for it, and a hatchling that turns down a meal is not automatically in trouble.

One practical threshold: start scenting if a snake refuses its first three meal offerings. The scents that work are fish (sardine, tuna, salmon), chicken bone broth, and frog, toad, lizard or gecko, with commercial scent strips as a packaged option. The amphibian scents map onto what the species evolved eating, which is why they work on an animal ignoring a plain pinky.

Two other causes are seasonal. A male ready to breed may refuse, and seasonal change heading toward brumation can put a snake off food on its own. Neither is fixed by offering more food. A pet hognose kept at steady temperatures [needs no deliberate brumation](/blog/snake-brumation-guide/), which is a breeding tool, but one that slows down on its own is fine while it holds its weight. Weight loss, wheezing or open-mouth breathing, or lethargy well past the season's usual quiet end the wait and start a vet visit.

## Feed Off the Substrate

Feed in a separate container, away from the loose, dry, diggable substrate this species needs. A snake that takes a mouse with a mouthful of substrate attached is the impaction case, and the [enclosure setup guide](/blog/hognose-snake-tank-setup-guide/) explains why the substrate has to be deep and loose in the first place.

That is also the one place where the display enclosure and the feeding setup should look nothing alike. Plain, lined, boring, easy to clean.

## Feed the Adult, Not the Hatchling

The schedule that grows a three gram hatchling is the one that makes an adult fat, so the gap between meals has to stretch as the snake matures. Keep the prey thawed, no wider than the snake's head and served in that plain container, and give a picky spell scent and patience for as long as the snake holds its weight.
