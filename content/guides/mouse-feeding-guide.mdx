# What Do Pet Mice Eat? Diet, Portions, and Foods to Avoid

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Mice are opportunistic omnivores that need noticeably more protein than a hamster or gerbil, and because a full-grown mouse weighs about as much as two or three paperclips, portion control matters here in a way it simply doesn't for a bigger rodent. For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Mouse care guide](/guides/mouse/).

## The Staple Diet

A nutritionally complete pelleted diet, often sold as "nuggets" or a lab block, should make up roughly 75 to 80% of a mouse's diet according to [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/exotic/mouse-care-sheet). The [RSPCA](https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/rodents/mice/diet) is blunt about why pellets beat a loose seed mix: mice pick out their favorite pieces the same way gerbils and hamsters do, so a bag that looks balanced on the label doesn't actually get eaten that way.

Protein is where mice genuinely diverge from the hamster and gerbil guides elsewhere on this site. The [American Fancy Rat and Mouse Association](https://www.afrma.org/bc_mineralproteinmice.htm) puts general maintenance crude protein for mice at 20 to 25%, with growing or breeding animals needing even more depending on strain, and notes plainly that "the mouse has similar nutrient requirements as the rat." That's meaningfully higher than the 14 to 16% that's adequate for a [gerbil](/blog/gerbil-feeding-guide/) or hamster, which is exactly why we're not pointing you toward the same hamster-and-gerbil pellet linked elsewhere on this site. It's formulated for a lower protein target than a mouse genuinely needs. Look for a product labeled specifically for mice or rats instead.

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Feed a measured pelleted ration once daily, and lean toward scattering some of that ration around the cage rather than dumping it all in one dish. The RSPCA specifically recommends this to encourage natural foraging behavior. Fresh vegetables like leafy greens, carrot, bell pepper, and broccoli can be added daily in genuinely small amounts, cut small. "Small" means something different for a 1 to 1.6 ounce animal than it does for a hamster, closer to a pea-sized piece than a chunk of carrot. Most fruit is fine occasionally, but skip fruit seeds and pits, and treat sugary items as an occasional extra rather than a daily habit. ## Foods to Avoid

According to [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/exotic/mouse-care-sheet) and the [RSPCA](https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/rodents/mice/diet), avoid grapes and raisins, rhubarb, citrus fruit, chocolate, caffeine, and alcohol (genuinely toxic, not just unsuitable), plus garlic, onion, raw beans, and raw potato. Lettuce is a specific one worth knowing: it isn't toxic, but the RSPCA notes it causes diarrhea in mice, so it's better left out entirely rather than offered "in moderation."

## Water and Supplementation

Fresh water needs to be available constantly. Mice are small enough that going without it for even a short stretch is genuinely dangerous, per the RSPCA. Use a bottle with a metal sipper tube and check it daily, a clogged spout is easy to miss on an animal that doesn't drink much at any one sitting. No vitamin or mineral supplement is needed on top of a complete pelleted diet.

## Coprophagy Is Normal, Not a Problem

Mice eat some of their own droppings directly from the cage floor, and it's a genuine nutritional strategy rather than a sign anything's wrong. Research from a series of studies on laboratory mice found the behavior peaks around 13 pellets a day at 5 to 6 weeks old, and gradually declines with age to roughly 2 pellets a day by 18 months. It works because feces are astonishingly concentrated in nutrients a single pass through the gut misses, especially B vitamins.

<FunFact>
Coprophagy in mice isn't just a fixed habit, it responds to actual nutrient need. Researchers found mice fed a standard diet ate their own droppings about 9.6 times a day on average, but once the feed was fortified with vitamin B12, that dropped to roughly 4.7 times a day, direct evidence the behavior tracks real nutritional demand rather than instinct alone. The same research found mouse feces run 124 to 197 times higher in vitamin B12 than the diet that produced them, which is part of why the habit never fully disappeared even on the fortified diet.
</FunFact>

## The Mistakes That Actually Matter

A seed-heavy mix as the staple is the single biggest dietary risk, for the same selective-eating reason it's a problem in [gerbils](/blog/gerbil-feeding-guide/). Overfeeding fresh produce is the second, mice are small enough that what looks like a modest vegetable portion by human standards is actually oversized for the animal. And reaching for a hamster-and-gerbil formulated pellet because it's already in the house is an easy, understandable mismatch that quietly underfeeds protein over time.
