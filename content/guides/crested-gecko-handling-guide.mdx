# How to Handle a Crested Gecko (And Why Tail Loss Is Different Here)

Crested geckos are gentle and generally easy to handle once settled, but there's one fact about this species every owner needs to know before they ever pick one up: unlike leopard geckos, a crested gecko's tail does not grow back.

## Give It Time to Settle First

Don't handle a new arrival for about two weeks. Let it acclimate to its enclosure and start eating normally before any hands-on interaction. Wait until your gecko is a sub-adult or older juvenile, roughly 10 to 15 grams, which is about 4 to 5 inches at around 6 months old, before regular handling begins. Geckos under about 10 grams are too fragile for routine handling.

## How Often

Start with sessions of about 5 minutes every other day. Build up gradually to around 15 minutes once your gecko is tame and comfortable, and keep total daily handling under about 20 minutes even then. Evening tends to work best, since crested geckos are most active at dusk and after dark.

## The Correct Way to Handle One

Let the gecko walk onto your hand rather than grabbing it. A technique called "treadmilling" works well: cup your free hand in front of the gecko as it reaches the edge of the one it's on, and it will hop hand to hand on its own. Stay low, over a bed, couch, or soft surface: crested geckos jump readily, and a fall onto a hard floor can cause real injury. Never grab or restrain the tail. Wash your hands before and after each session: crested geckos carry Salmonella like any reptile, and the [reptile hygiene guide](/blog/reptile-salmonella-hygiene-guide/) sets the rules that keep it off your hands.

## Signs It Wants to Be Put Back

- Tail-wagging or whipping
- Squeaking or vocalizing
- Erratic jumping
- Gaping or open-mouth behavior
- Eyes closed
- Actively trying to flee

Any of these mean the session is over. Skittish, rushed handling is also exactly what costs a gecko its tail.

A color change on its own is not on that list. Crested geckos can "fire up": the dark parts of the body turn darker and the light parts lighter. It seems to happen when the gecko is excited, and the cause is not fully understood, but it is normal and not a sign of illness.

## Tail Loss Is Permanent

Like many geckos, crested geckos can voluntarily drop their tail as a defense mechanism, called autotomy. It detaches along built-in fracture points and keeps wiggling on its own to distract a predator while the gecko escapes.

Here's the critical difference from a species like the [leopard gecko](/blog/leopard-gecko-handling-guide/): **a crested gecko's tail does not grow back.** Once it's gone, it's gone for good.

<FunFact source="Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital">
Nearly all adult crested geckos in the wild have lost their tails.
</FunFact>

The good news is that this isn't the crisis it sounds like. A tailless crested gecko, often called a "frog butt" by keepers, lives a completely normal, healthy life. It doesn't affect breeding, climbing, or overall health, and the drop itself is thought to be painless or close to it.

The stump needs little from a keeper. Losing the tail is not a medical emergency, and the wound closes on its own if it stays clean. Swap any loose substrate for paper towel until the stump has sealed, so soil or bark cannot pack into the open wound, and change the towel whenever it is soiled. Watch the stump for a soft or hard swelling, the sign of an abscess, and take the gecko to a vet if it looks infected rather than drying and closing. A tailless gecko needs a closer eye on its weight and hydration from then on.

Tail loss happens from fright, rough handling, being bullied by a cagemate, a fall, or a tail getting trapped in a lid or door. Since the loss is permanent, prevention is the only real strategy: handle gently, keep sessions calm and unhurried, never grab the tail under any circumstances, and keep crested geckos singly, since cagemates are one of the common causes.

## Telling Males From Females

Handling sessions are also when a keeper can check the sex, because the gecko has to sit on a flat hand or the inside of a clear container to be seen from below. The only dependable sign is the hemipenal bulge: a male develops a prominent swelling at the base of the tail, just behind the vent, and a female stays flat there. That bulge usually appears between 18 and 25 grams, so a gecko under that weight with no bulge is simply unsexed.

Some breeders sex earlier by examining the femoral pores with a magnifying glass or a jeweler's loupe. That method is not always accurate, so an early call stays a guess until the bulge settles it. It is one more reason juveniles are not housed together: two unsexed youngsters can turn out to be two males, and males housed together fight, badly enough to injure or kill, costing tails as well.

## Treadmilling Is the Whole Method

A crested gecko that walks onto your hand on its own is a gecko that never had to be caught, and that is where calm sessions come from. Keep them short, keep them over something soft, and stop at the first tail whip or squeak.
