Axolotls breathe four ways at once: through their skin, the lining of the mouth, the feathery external gills, and a pair of simple lungs they fill by gulping air at the surface. Three of those four take oxygen straight from the water, and the skin and gills also take in whatever else is dissolved in it. That is why the water, more than the tank, the decor or the food, decides whether an axolotl stays healthy. An axolotl gulping air at the surface more than usual is often a sign that the water is not delivering enough oxygen through the gills.

## Why cold matters

Axolotls do best at 60 to 64°F (15.6 to 17.8°C). The laboratory colony at the University of Kentucky holds its animals at 60 to 65°F and never above about 72°F. Above 75°F (24°C), axolotls go off their food, develop fluid swelling (ascites), float and cannot stay down, and often pick up secondary bacterial infections.

Two pieces of water chemistry explain why the ceiling sits where it does, and they stack:

- **Warm water holds less oxygen.** Oxygen solubility falls as temperature rises, so a tank at 75°F carries less oxygen than the same tank at 62°F, at the moment a warmer animal is burning through more of it.
- **Warm water makes ammonia more toxic.** Ammonia in water exists in two forms, and only the un-ionized form (NH3) is highly toxic, roughly 100 times more so than the ionized form. The share of un-ionized ammonia rises with temperature and with pH.

So a small ammonia reading that a cold tank at pH 7.4 shrugs off becomes a real problem in a warm tank at pH 8. Heat does not just stress the axolotl directly. It turns every other small water problem into a bigger one. In a warm room, [cooling a tank without a chiller](/blog/cooling-an-aquarium-without-a-chiller-guide/) with fans and evaporation is the first step, and a chiller is the answer when the room itself runs above the mid-60s for weeks.

## Ammonia and nitrite: the cycle comes first

Axolotls excrete ammonia. In a cycled tank, one group of bacteria (*Nitrosomonas* and *Nitrosospira*) turns it into nitrite, and a second (*Nitrospira* and *Nitrobacter*) turns the nitrite into nitrate, which is far less toxic and leaves with water changes. A new filter needs weeks to grow enough of those bacteria, which is why the tank goes through a full [fishless cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) before the axolotl goes in.

Ammonia and nitrite should both read 0 ppm. Nitrite is toxic to fish at levels as low as 0.1 mg/L (0.1 ppm), and there is no reason to assume a gilled amphibian tolerates more. If either shows up:

1. Change 25 to 30 percent of the water with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water.
2. Skip or cut the next feeding and remove any uneaten food.
3. Check the filter is running and that nobody rinsed the media in tap water.
4. Test again the next day, and repeat until both read zero.

## Nitrate, pH and hardness

Nitrate is the slow problem. Keep it under 10 ppm: prolonged higher levels are linked to eye problems in axolotls. About 30 percent of the water each week is the standard maintenance change, with more if nitrate climbs. The [water change guide](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/) covers how to match new water to the tank.

For pH, 7.4 to 7.6 is preferred and 6.5 to 8.0 is acceptable. Stability matters more than the exact number, and the high end is where ammonia bites hardest, so a tank running near 8 needs closer ammonia testing.

Axolotls need mineralized water, and straight distilled or reverse-osmosis water harms them. The University of Kentucky colony adds a modified Holtfreter's salt mix to its treated water to restore hardness and to discourage parasites and fungus. No specific GH target for axolotls traces back to a published study, which is why the [amphibian water guide](/blog/amphibian-quarantine-and-water-guide/) sets a floor (never unremineralized RO or distilled water) instead of a number. Aquarium salt is sodium chloride and does not raise general hardness, so it is not a hardness fix.

## What the lab does differently

The Kentucky colony keeps adults in very small volumes of water, about half a gallon per animal, and changes that water at least every other day, before feeding. That works because the water is replaced before waste can build up. A home aquarium does the opposite: a large volume and a biological filter buy time between changes. Neither approach is wrong. What fails is the middle ground, a small tank with no cycled filter and weekly changes.

## Warning signs

An axolotl often shows a water problem before a test does:

- **Gills curled forward** toward the face, a common stress sign linked to poor water or heat.
- **Appetite loss and lethargy**, often the first response to ammonia, nitrite or temperature.
- **Floating that it cannot correct**, one of the named heat-stress signs above 75°F.
- **White, cottony patches** on the skin or gills, a fungal infection that usually follows stress.
- **More air gulping than usual**, a sign the gills are not getting enough oxygen from the water.
