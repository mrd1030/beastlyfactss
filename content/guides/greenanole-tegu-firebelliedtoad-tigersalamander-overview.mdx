# Four Pets Whose Size Tells You the Wrong Thing

Small usually reads as easy. Large usually reads as intimidating. Cute usually reads as harmless. None of that holds up for this group of four, and each one proves it in its own way.

## Green Anole: Small Doesn't Mean Simple

At 6 to 8 inches and often under $15, a [green anole](/blog/green-anole-cost-guide/) reads as a starter reptile. It's actually more fragile than every larger lizard already covered on this site, including the bearded dragon, blue-tongued skink, ackie and savannah monitors, and uromastyx. It drops its tail easily under fairly gentle handling, and it needs real UVB and consistent humidity to reach its actual potential of up to 10 years instead of the 3 to 4 years casual, under-invested care usually produces. The cheap price tag is exactly what drives the under-housing, people assume a low-cost animal doesn't need much, when the opposite is closer to true.

## Argentine Tegu: Large Doesn't Mean Hard to Love

A 4 foot lizard that can run $200 to $1,200 and needs an 8 by 4 by 4 foot adult enclosure sounds intimidating on paper. In practice, the [Argentine tegu](/blog/argentine-tegu-cost-guide/) is one of the most consistently tameable reptiles on this entire site, genuinely enjoying handling once it's been properly socialized, a real comparison exotic vets and breeders make to a dog, not typical marketing exaggeration. That tameness has to be earned through daily handling, it won't develop on its own, and even indoor pets brumate seasonally, slowing down, eating less, and burrowing for roughly 2 to 4 months, the kind of deliberate seasonal behavior you wouldn't expect from something people assume is "just a big lizard."

## Fire-Bellied Toad: Cute Doesn't Mean Harmless

A small, brightly colored amphibian that costs as little as $10 to $25 looks like an easy beginner pet, and in most ways it is. But the [fire-bellied toad's](/blog/fire-bellied-toad-cost-guide/) skin genuinely secretes toxins, specific defensive peptides that have been identified and studied in scientific research, not folklore. Unless those secretions reach your eyes, mouth, or an open wound, they aren't dangerous to people under normal contact, but that's exactly why minimal, glove-only handling is the right default here rather than an overcautious rule. It's also worth knowing this species is actually a frog, not a true toad, and semi-aquatic, which is why its enclosure needs real water filtration rather than just humidity control, a useful contrast with the fully aquatic [axolotl](/blog/axolotl-tank-setup-guide/), which never leaves the water at all.

## Tiger Salamander: Unassuming Doesn't Mean Short-Lived

Rarely visible, mostly living underground, the [tiger salamander](/blog/tiger-salamander-cost-guide/) is easy to mistake for a low-commitment pet, especially at a purchase price of just $30 to $125. It can live 12 to 15 years typically, with a recognized longevity database recording a captive maximum of 25 years, genuinely rivaling many reptiles already covered here despite the animal's modest cost and low monthly upkeep. It also starts life looking and living almost exactly like the [axolotl](/blog/axolotl-cost-guide/) already covered on this site, fully aquatic and gilled, before metamorphosing entirely into the terrestrial, burrowing adult that spends most of its life out of sight.

## The Common Thread

Size and first appearance are genuinely unreliable guides across this entire group. The small green anole needs more care than it looks like it should, and its cheap price tag hides a real setup cost. The large Argentine tegu is gentler than it looks, once that gentleness is actually earned through consistent handling. The cute fire-bellied toad is riskier to touch than it looks, thanks to a documented skin toxin rather than any folklore. And the quiet, rarely-seen tiger salamander outlives what its unassuming appearance suggests by a decade or more. Read the actual guide for whichever one you're considering, not the first impression it gives you.
