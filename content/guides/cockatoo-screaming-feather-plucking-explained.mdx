# Why Cockatoos Scream and Pluck: The Behavior Behind the Headlines

Cockatoos have a reputation problem, and it's not entirely undeserved: rescue organizations report that cockatoos are surrendered more often than almost any other parrot, frequently citing screaming or feather plucking as the reason. What gets left out of most warnings is that both behaviors have identifiable roots, and understanding them changes how a keeper actually prevents them rather than just reacting after the fact.

## Screaming Isn't Random

In the wild, cockatoos use loud, far-carrying contact calls to locate flock members across dense forest canopy - it's a functional communication tool, not noise for its own sake. In captivity, that same instinct doesn't disappear just because the bird lives indoors. A cockatoo separated from its "flock" (the household) in another room, or one whose social needs aren't being met throughout the day, will often escalate contact calling because, biologically, that's exactly what it evolved to do when isolated. That same flock wiring drives problems well beyond noise, and our guide to [why parrots need social interaction](/blog/why-parrots-need-social-interaction-and-what-loneliness-does-to-them/) covers what sustained isolation does to a bird psychologically.

<FunFact>
Screaming that gets a strong reaction - even a negative one like yelling back - can accidentally become reinforced. To a cockatoo, any reliable response to a scream (attention, even annoyed attention) can register as the call working as intended.
</FunFact>

The pattern that works across most reported cases is proactive engagement rather than reactive discipline - giving a cockatoo predictable, generous attention on a schedule reduces the pressure that builds into screaming in the first place.

## Feather-Destructive Behavior Is Usually Not "Just a Habit"

Feather plucking and self-mutilation are frequently described as a bad habit the bird picked up, but that framing skips the underlying driver. In the vast majority of cases, feather-destructive behavior traces back to one or more of: chronic insufficient social interaction, boredom from an under-stimulating environment, anxiety (including separation-related anxiety), or an unaddressed medical or nutritional issue that should always be ruled out by an avian vet first.

Once established, plucking can become genuinely difficult to reverse, since the behavior itself can become self-soothing and habitual even after the original cause is addressed. That self-soothing loop is not unique to parrots, and our guide to [what anxious pet behavior is really telling you](/blog/when-your-pet-wont-stop-pacing-or-hiding-what-anxious-pet-behavior-is-really-telling-you/) covers the same pattern in dogs and cats. This is why prevention is disproportionately more effective than treatment after the fact.

## Over-Bonding and Building Independence

Cockatoos hyper-bond harder than almost any other commonly kept parrot, and that intensity is a direct driver of both screaming and feather-destructive behavior, not a side issue. Over-bonding looks specific once you know what to watch for: the bird follows one person from room to room and refuses to settle anywhere else, it screams or paces the moment that person leaves its sight, it bites or lunges at other household members who try to handle it, and it stops eating or interacting normally when its preferred person is out of the house. A cockatoo that only tolerates one household member is not showing affection in a healthy sense. It is showing a bond that has narrowed to the point where the rest of the household, and often the bird's own independence, has been crowded out.

Hand-raised cockatoos and birds that are held or carried constantly during their first year are the most likely to end up this way. A chick raised without exposure to multiple calm caregivers tends to imprint on whoever fed and handled it most, and if that one person remains the bird's near-exclusive source of contact into adulthood, the bird never builds the skill of being comfortable with anyone else, or of being alone. Constant carrying reinforces the same pattern from the other direction: a cockatoo that is rarely put down and given its own space never practices self-entertainment, so it has no fallback behavior when its person eventually isn't available.

<KeyTakeaway>
Over-bonding and separation anxiety are the same mechanism from two angles. A cockatoo that has never learned to be alone experiences a favorite person leaving the room as a genuine loss, and screaming and plucking are the two most common ways that anxiety shows up.
</KeyTakeaway>

The link to screaming is the contact-calling instinct described above, dialed up: a cockatoo that is over-bonded doesn't just call out when the flock scatters, it calls out because its entire sense of security is tied to one specific bird-shaped human. The link to plucking runs through the same anxiety. Feather-destructive behavior is frequently a self-soothing response to stress, and separation from the one person the bird has been allowed to depend on is one of the more common stressors behind it. Sleep deprivation compounds both problems and is easy to overlook, since a cockatoo kept up late in a shared living space, or one whose light exposure runs long into the evening, doesn't get the rest it needs to regulate its own stress response. Our [bird photoperiod and sleep guide](/blog/bird-photoperiod-sleep-guide/) covers how to set a consistent light and dark schedule that supports this.

Building independence works best as a set of ongoing habits rather than a one-time fix.

- Rotate handlers deliberately. Have every adult (and responsible older child) in the household feed, offer step-ups, and spend one-on-one time with the bird, not just the person it prefers.
- Build in structured alone time. Give the cockatoo periods each day in its cage or play area with the preferred person in the house but out of the room, so the bird practices being calm without direct attention on demand.
- Prioritize foraging and independent play over constant handling. Food-dispensing toys and rotating foraging setups give the bird something engaging to do that doesn't require a person, which is the opposite of what constant carrying teaches.
- Don't reinforce contact calls. If the bird screams when a person leaves the room, don't rush back in response. Reward quiet moments with a return visit or a treat instead, so calm behavior is what reliably gets attention.
- Avoid cuddle and petting patterns that mimic a mate bond. Limiting physical contact to head and neck scratches rather than full-body petting or lap cuddling reduces the odds of triggering hormonal, mate-directed attachment that intensifies over-bonding rather than easing it.

None of this means withholding affection. It means spreading it across the household and pairing it with real independence, so the bird's wellbeing doesn't hinge entirely on one person's schedule never changing.

## Reading the Warning Signs Early

- Increased time spent preening a specific area, beyond normal grooming.
- Small bald patches, especially on the chest or under the wings, developing gradually.
- A noticeable increase in vocalization or agitation that coincides with a change in routine - a new work schedule, a move, or reduced attention.

Catching these early and increasing interaction, foraging enrichment, and environmental stimulation immediately is far more effective than waiting until a clear bald patch or full-blown screaming pattern has set in.

## When to Call In an Avian Behaviorist

Home adjustments (more interaction, more foraging enrichment, a consistent schedule) resolve a meaningful share of early cases. If screaming or plucking continues to escalate after several weeks of consistent effort, or if plucking has drawn blood or reached the skin, it's time for two things in parallel: an avian vet visit to rule out a medical cause, and a referral to a certified avian behaviorist if the vet clears the bird medically. Behaviorists work with entrenched cases far more effectively than generic advice can, and waiting too long to involve one tends to make the behavior more deeply ingrained.

## The Honest Takeaway

Neither screaming nor plucking is an inevitable cockatoo trait that "just happens." Both are strongly linked to how well a cockatoo's exceptionally high social and enrichment needs are being met day to day. That's genuinely good news for prevention - but it also means a cockatoo is not a bird that tolerates being an occasional companion. The behaviors that make cockatoos difficult are, in large part, a direct readout of unmet needs rather than a fixed personality trait.

A cockatoo isn't broken because it screams or plucks - it's telling you something about how its day is going.
