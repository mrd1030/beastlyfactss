# Flying Squirrel Cage Setup: Tall, Not Wide

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Flying squirrels are arboreal climbers and gliders, and their housing needs reflect that directly, height matters far more than floor space, the same priority as their unrelated look-alike, the sugar glider.

## Cage Size

The floor is roughly 24x24x36 inches, or 30 wide by 18 deep by 36 tall, which is the same volume in a different shape. Both put height first, and that is the dimension that matters for a climbing, gliding animal. Go bigger than the minimum wherever you can, especially for a pair or small group. ## Bar Spacing

Keep it to no more than half an inch, matching the standard used for sugar gliders. A young or small-bodied flying squirrel is small enough to squeeze through wider gaps, so escape-proofing matters here as much as the cage's overall size does.

## Temperature

Here's a genuine difference from the sugar glider. Sugar gliders are native to Australia and New Guinea and need a warmer 75 to 88°F range. Flying squirrels are native to temperate North American forests, where they deal with real seasonal cold every winter, huddling together in tree cavities for warmth, so they're comfortable in a noticeably cooler 65 to 75°F range indoors. They tolerate a cool room reasonably well but struggle with heat, so avoid letting the room climb much past 80°F, and still keep the cage away from cold drafts and direct sun.

## Humidity and Lighting

No special humidity control is needed for this species. Lighting is where it actually differs from a sugar glider's setup, though. There's no UVB requirement the way a [bearded dragon](/blog/bearded-dragon-tank-setup-guide/) has, but flying squirrels can synthesize some vitamin D through skin exposure to light, unlike a sugar glider, whose care sheets generally treat lighting as a non-issue. Regular access to natural daylight or full-spectrum lighting, at least 20 minutes a day, alongside dietary calcium and D3, is generally recommended here rather than optional.

## Substrate

A solid cage bottom with a washable liner underneath the main living area works better than loose bedding, which accumulates waste and gets harder to keep clean over time. Water goes in a bottle or a shallow dish, changed daily. Inside the nest box or sleeping pouch itself, a soft lining, shredded paper or a scrap of fleece, gives them something to burrow into the way they would with leaves and shredded bark in the wild.

## The Non-Obvious Requirements

Position sleeping pouches or a nest box high in the cage, matching this species' natural preference for denning up off the ground. A standard hamster or rat wheel is genuinely dangerous for a flying squirrel, not just the wrong size. Many of those wheels have a center axle bar running through the middle, and it can seriously injure a small, bouncy animal's spine or tail mid-run. Look specifically for a solid, axle-free wheel at least 12 inches across, the same design sold for sugar gliders and hedgehogs, like an <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01B15GZ1U?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Exotic Nutrition Silent Runner 12in Wide + Cage Attachment">axle-free 12 inch wheel</AffiliateLink>, rather than assuming any small-pet wheel will do.

Add plenty of <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3QQNV3L" product="Climbing branch (Mopani wood)">branches and climbing structure</AffiliateLink> at varying angles and heights. This isn't just enrichment, it's the closest a caged animal gets to the constant vertical movement it would do in the wild every night, and a squirrel with room to move and retreat settles faster than one in a cramped, understimulating setup.

Once the cage is dialed in, the other big piece of the puzzle is [diet](/blog/flying-squirrel-feeding-guide/). A properly balanced diet and calcium supplementation matter more for this species than almost anything else in the setup.
