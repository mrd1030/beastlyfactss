# How Much Does Neon Tetra Ownership Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Neon tetras are one of the most affordable fish you can keep, and unlike a solitary species like the betta, cost planning here starts with the fact that you need to buy several at once, not just one.

## The Fish: $1 to $5 Each

Most neon tetras run $1 to $3, occasionally reaching $5 depending on the source. Since this is a schooling species, you're buying a group, not a single fish. A school of [6 to 12](/blog/neon-tetra-enrichment-guide/) runs $10 to $40 total, still one of the cheapest ways to stock a community tank.

<FunFact>
Buying six or more neon tetras at once can feel like a bigger upfront expense than buying a single fish, but the math actually favors the tetra. A full school of 10 to 12 fish, the number that keeps them behaviorally healthy, often costs less than a single pair of fancy guppies, which our [Guppy cost guide](/blog/guppy-cost-guide/) covers in full. It's one of the rare cases in the hobby where doing right by the fish is also the cheaper option.
</FunFact>

## Vet Costs

Not applicable in the traditional sense. Fish health management is entirely about water quality and, when needed, over-the-counter treatments, not veterinary visits.

## Lifespan

Around 5 years is achievable with good, stable care, though average conditions often see neon tetras live closer to 2 to 3 years. Water stability genuinely matters more for lifespan in this species than in some hardier fish, worth keeping in mind when budgeting for the equipment that maintains it. A [guppy](/blog/guppy-cost-guide/) averages 2 to 3 years and up to 4 or 5 with excellent care, a similar range, though neon tetras lean more heavily on stable water quality to reach their upper end.

## Why This Costs Less Than It Looks Like It Should

Neon tetras are inexpensive individually, but the schooling requirement means you're never really buying just one or two. Budgeting for a proper group from the start, rather than starting small and adding more later, is both the more humane approach and the more cost-effective one, since a stressed, understocked school is more prone to health issues, which cost more to correct than simply stocking correctly from day one.
