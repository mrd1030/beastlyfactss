# How Much Does Neon Tetra Ownership Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Neon tetras are one of the most affordable fish you can keep, and unlike a solitary species such as the betta, they are never bought one at a time. Each fish is a farmed characin about 1.2 inches (3 cm) long whose wild relatives live in forest streams of the Amazon basin, and it lives in a school. The price of one fish is small. The bill is the tank around the school, the spare tank beside it and, for fish ordered online, the shipping.

## The Fish: %%animal:neon-tetra%% Each

Nearly every neon tetra sold is farm-bred, and most run $1 to $3, occasionally reaching $5 depending on the source. Since this is a schooling species, the purchase is a group: a mixed-sex school of at least 8 to 10. A school of 8 to 12 runs $15 to $40 total, still one of the cheapest ways to stock a community tank.

<FunFact source="Seriously Fish">
A neon tetra's colors shift with the hour. At night the fish turns duller and its blue stripe takes on a violet tone, and under bright light over a pale floor in clear water it runs lighter. The species does best under fairly dim lighting.
</FunFact>

## Upfront Setup: %%setup:neon-tetra%% for a 20 Gallon Long

<CostTable guide="neon-tetra" section="necessities" />

The table is built around a 20 gallon long, which gives a school of 10 the 24 inches of tank length it needs. The recommended base for neons is 24 x 12 inches or larger, and a standard 10 gallon tank is only 20 inches long. Complete starter kits at this size commonly run $50 to $200, with the tank itself, if bought separately, running $20 to $150 depending on size and material.

The sand in the table is black because of what a pale floor does to the fish. Neons lighten over pale substrate in bright, clear water, so a dark bed keeps the stripe at its strongest. The table prices two bags for the floor of a 20 long.

## The Extras: A Spare Tank for Quarantine

<CostTable guide="neon-tetra" section="extras" />

Every new neon spends at least 30 days in a separate tank before it meets the school. At the warm temperatures tropical fish are kept at, disease often becomes visible 1 to 3 weeks after shipping, so a 30-day hold lets it surface in the bare tank first. Setting that tank up and treating in it is laid out step by step in the [fish quarantine guide](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/).

The tank's job does not end with the first school. Every new batch, whether two replacements or a second group of ten, starts its own 30 days, and the display school waits until the clock runs out. Buying the whole school in one trip means one wait instead of several.

## Buying Checklist: Healthy Neons, Healthy Tank

Time spent watching the shop tank before anything goes into a bag is the cheapest step in the whole budget. Check each fish, then the tank around it.

| Check | Buy | Leave it |
|---|---|---|
| Skin and scales | Smooth and unblemished | White patches, spots or sores |
| Fins | Intact | Bloody or ragged |
| Eyes | Bright | Cloudy |
| Swimming | Steady, moving normally with the rest | Circling, drifting, listless or restless |
| Place in the school | Inside the group | Holding itself apart from the main school |
| The tank | Clean, with other fish that look healthy | Dirty, or holding fish that look sick |

Three of those red flags, restlessness, white patches on the skin and a fish setting itself apart from the school, are the signs of neon tetra disease. When any fish in the tank shows them, the advice is to skip the purchase entirely, not to pick the healthiest-looking fish out of it.

## Shipping Fish Online

A school ordered online makes the trip in a sealed bag of oxygenated water inside a foam box. The fish keep breathing and excreting the whole way, so oxygen falls while carbon dioxide and ammonia build up, and the pH of the bag water drops as the ammonia rises. Temperature is the other risk: shippers add ice gel packs or heat packs in summer and winter, or on long trips, to hold the water at a temperature the fish can take.

## Ongoing Costs: Food, Conditioner, Test Kit Refills and Power

Food and water conditioner are the fixed lines. A micro-pellet or micro-crisp food runs about $15 to $30 a year, and water conditioner $10 to $15 a year. The liquid test kit in the setup table is bought once and then refilled as its reagents run out. The heater, filter and light add to the power bill every month, by an amount that depends on the equipment and the room. Together that is a modest monthly total for a planted community tank of this size.

## Vet Costs

Rarely a line item. A single neon's health comes down to water quality and, when needed, over-the-counter treatment. When something is taking out the school, professional directories list the [vets who treat fish](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/), and one visit can settle whether the whole group needs treating. Guesswork has its own price: fish are often given one over-the-counter product after another when the first one fails, which wastes time and money and usually does more harm than good.

## Lifespan

Around 5 years is achievable with good, stable care, though average conditions often see neon tetras live closer to 2 to 3 years. Water stability matters more for lifespan in this species than in some hardier fish, worth keeping in mind when budgeting for the equipment that maintains it. A guppy is shorter-lived still, with females averaging about 13 months in one lab study, and neon tetras lean more heavily on stable water quality to reach their upper end.

## Why This Costs Less Than It Looks Like It Should

Neon tetras are inexpensive individually, but the schooling requirement means you never buy just one or two. Eight or more at once can feel like a bigger upfront expense than a single fish, yet a full school of 10, the number that suits them best, often costs less than a single pair of [fancy guppies](/blog/guppy-cost-guide/) bred for show fins and color. Budgeting for a proper group from the start is both the more humane approach and the more cost-effective one, since a stressed, understocked school is more prone to health issues, which cost more to correct than simply stocking correctly from day one. It is one of the rare cases in the hobby where doing right by the fish is also the cheaper option.

## Where a Cheap Fish Gets Expensive

The fish cost little, so the money gets lost in replacing them. Three habits cause most of it.

Buying from the wrong tank. Years of commercial breeding have left the farmed strain somewhat less robust than wild fish, and a weak batch can die off over the following weeks whatever the keeper does. A shop tank of bright, evenly striped fish schooling tightly is worth a longer drive than a tank with pale or lagging fish at a lower price.

Buying too soon. A tank that finished cycling days ago is still settling. Neons react to shifts in water chemistry more than most community fish. Fish added then are the ones that get replaced first.

Skipping the second tank. Neon tetra disease has no cure once it takes hold, and it spreads through a school when fish eat a dead tankmate. The bare hospital tank in the extras table costs less than restocking a school of twelve.
