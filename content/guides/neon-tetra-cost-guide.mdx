# How Much Does Neon Tetra Ownership Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Neon tetras are one of the most affordable fish you can keep, and unlike a solitary species like the betta, cost planning here starts with the fact that you need to buy several at once, not just one.

## The Fish: %%animal:neon-tetra%% Each

Nearly every neon tetra sold is farm-bred, and most run $1 to $3, occasionally reaching $5 depending on the source. Since this is a schooling species, the purchase is a group. A school of 8 to 12 runs $15 to $40 total, still one of the cheapest ways to stock a community tank.

<FunFact>
Buying eight or more neon tetras at once can feel like a bigger upfront expense than buying a single fish, but the math favors the tetra. A full school of 10 fish, the number that suits them best, often costs less than a single pair of [fancy guppies](/blog/guppy-cost-guide/) bred for show fins and color. It's one of the rare cases in the hobby where doing right by the fish is also the cheaper option.
</FunFact>

Food, water conditioner, and a small amount of electricity for the heater and filter make up the monthly total, modest for a planted community tank of this size.

## Vet Costs

Rarely a line item. A single neon's health comes down to water quality and, when needed, over-the-counter treatment. When something is taking out the school, professional directories list the [vets who treat fish](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/), and one visit can settle whether the whole group needs treating.

## Lifespan

Around 5 years is achievable with good, stable care, though average conditions often see neon tetras live closer to 2 to 3 years. Water stability matters more for lifespan in this species than in some hardier fish, worth keeping in mind when budgeting for the equipment that maintains it. A guppy averages 2 to 3 years and up to 4 or 5 with excellent care, a similar range, though neon tetras lean more heavily on stable water quality to reach their upper end.

## Why This Costs Less Than It Looks Like It Should

Neon tetras are inexpensive individually, but the schooling requirement means you never buy just one or two. Budgeting for a proper group from the start is both the more humane approach and the more cost-effective one, since a stressed, understocked school is more prone to health issues, which cost more to correct than simply stocking correctly from day one.

## Where a Cheap Fish Gets Expensive

The fish cost little, so the money gets lost in replacing them. Three habits cause most of it.

Buying from the wrong tank. Years of commercial breeding have left the farmed strain somewhat less robust than wild fish, and a weak batch can die off over the following weeks whatever the keeper does. A shop tank of bright, evenly striped fish schooling tightly is worth a longer drive than a tank with pale or lagging fish at a lower price.

Buying too soon. A tank that finished cycling days ago is still settling. Neons react to shifts in water chemistry more than most community fish. Fish added then are the ones that get replaced first.

Skipping the second tank. Neon tetra disease has no cure once it takes hold, and it spreads through a school when fish eat a dead tankmate. The small hospital tank that holds new arrivals apart for at least 30 days costs less than restocking a school of twelve.

The tetra's schooling minimum is another example of getting the number right, covered alongside three other pets in [Four Pets, Four Different Right Numbers](/blog/boa-glider-mantis-tetra-overview/).
