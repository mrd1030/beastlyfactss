# Common Quaker Parakeet Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Quaker parakeets are hardy birds, and the health issues that do come up trace back overwhelmingly to diet and social wellbeing, both within an owner's control.

## Fatty Liver Disease (Hepatic Lipidosis)

One of the most common problems in this species, and almost always tied to a seed-heavy, high-fat diet. **Watch for:** anorexia, lethargy, an overgrown beak and nails, and green-tinted droppings, among the signs of hepatic lipidosis the [MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/pet-birds/geriatric-diseases-of-pet-birds) lists, plus the labored breathing and swollen abdomen [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/liver-disorders-in-birds) lists for liver disease in birds generally. Quaker parakeets are also among the parrot species the [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/pet-birds/nutritional-diseases-of-pet-birds) flags as prone to obesity, which raises the risk. Caught early, correcting the diet toward a pellet-based foundation alongside veterinary support leads to real recovery. **See a vet.**

## Feather Plucking

Can be behavioral, driven by boredom, insufficient socialization, poor sleep, or stress, or medical, tied to skin irritation or underlying illness. Given how social and intelligent this species is, daily interaction and genuine enrichment matter more here than for a less demanding bird, covered in the handling guide. In Quakers specifically, this can go past ordinary plucking into self-inflicted skin damage, usually on the chest and shoulders, a pattern some keepers call Quaker mutilation syndrome, though it's more formally described simply as feather-destructive behavior. **Always see a vet first** to rule out a medical cause before assuming it's purely behavioral.

<FunFact>
Quakers are among the loudest small parrots for their size, with a piercing contact call used to locate the flock, which in a home means you. It isn't macaw or cockatoo territory, but a bored, understimulated Quaker reliably escalates that call, which is exactly why enrichment and daily interaction do double duty here, they prevent both the noise and the feather-destructive behavior on this list.
</FunFact>

## Psittacine Beak and Feather Disease (PBFD)

A viral disease affecting feathers, beak, and the immune system. There's no cure, which makes early diagnosis and preventing spread to other birds genuinely important. **See a vet.**

## Psittacosis (Chlamydiosis)

A bacterial infection (*Chlamydia psittaci*) spread through droppings and respiratory secretions, and notably zoonotic, meaning it can pass to people in the household, per the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/psittacosis/about/index.html). **See a vet**, this needs proper diagnosis and treatment for everyone's sake, not just the bird's.

## Obesity and Vitamin A Deficiency

Both stem from an all-seed diet lacking real nutritional variety. **Manageable at home** through genuine diet correction, pellets should make up roughly 60 to 70% of intake, with fresh vegetables and limited fruit filling out the rest.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

An all-seed diet is directly responsible for both the fatty liver disease and the vitamin deficiencies on this list, arguably the single most impactful thing you can get right with this species. Insufficient social interaction and enrichment drives feather plucking in a bird this intelligent and social. And basic hygiene and quarantine practices prevent the spread of PBFD and psittacosis between birds. Diet and genuine daily engagement do most of the preventive work here.
