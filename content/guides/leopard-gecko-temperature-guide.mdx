# The Complete Leopard Gecko Temperature Guide

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A leopard gecko cannot make its own heat. Everything it does, from digesting a cricket to fighting off an infection, runs on the warmth it borrows from the enclosure floor, and a gecko kept a few degrees too cool goes off food long before it looks sick. Getting the numbers right is the whole job.

For the rest of their housing and diet needs, see our full [Leopard Gecko care guide](/guides/leopard-gecko/).

## The Thermal Gradient, Zone by Zone

Leopard geckos need a range of temperatures so they can thermoregulate by moving between warm and cool zones. The warm hide floor is the number to build everything else around.

The sources bracket these numbers rather than repeat them: LafeberVet gives a 70 to 80°F gradient with a warm area reaching 90°F, and Zen Habitats' heating guide puts the warm hide at 90 to 92°F over a 70 to 77°F cool zone. The Merck Veterinary Manual's general rule for reptiles is that basking surfaces run about 5°C above the air gradient and nights fall about 5°C below it, which is why the floor number sits above the ambient one here.

## Under-Tank or Overhead

<AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/44hAxJ1" product="Under-tank heat mat + thermostat kit">Under-tank heaters (UTHs) connected to a **thermostat**</AffiliateLink> are the gold standard for belly heat. Never run a UTH without a thermostat. They can reach dangerous temperatures and cause burns. Overhead heat panels work too, but belly heat is preferred for this species.

The two methods trade different things. A mat makes a small, steady warm patch under one hide, uses little power, and can run all night without light. Its weaknesses are coverage, and the fact that a mat or heat tape can fail into a hot spot away from the thermostat probe, which a thermostat will not catch. That is why the mat goes under an inch of substrate and gets checked with an IR gun rather than trusted. Overhead heat, a halogen or a deep heat projector on a dimming thermostat, warms a wider area and reads more like sun to a lizard that in the wild burrows down to cool off rather than up to warm up. It also dries the air and can burn a gecko that climbs too close to the fixture. Many keepers run both: overhead by day for the gradient, a thermostat-controlled mat under the warm hide for the floor. Glass is a poor insulator, so the same mat runs a warmer floor inside a PVC or wood enclosure; verify with the IR gun whenever the enclosure changes.

## The Thermostat Rule

Merck's husbandry guidance is one sentence: heaters should be thermostat controlled, screened from the animal, and placed at one end of the enclosure to make a gradient. In practice that means a mat on an on/off probe thermostat, and a halogen or deep heat projector on a proportional or dimming thermostat, which is the split Zen Habitats' heating guide draws. Put the probe inside the warm hide, on the floor where the gecko lies, so the thermostat regulates the surface that matters rather than the air above it.

## What NOT to Do

- **Hot rocks**: uneven heat distribution causes burns
- **Coil CHE (ceramic heat emitter) without thermostat**: fire risk
- **No cool side**: prevents thermoregulation and causes chronic stress

## Measuring Temperatures

Use an <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4flGgUg" product="Infrared temp gun">infrared (IR) temperature gun</AffiliateLink> to check surface temps, especially on the warm hide floor. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vp7leJ" product="Zoo Med Digital Thermometer (3 Pack)">Digital thermometers with probes</AffiliateLink> work for ambient air temps. Check temps at different times of day. Leopard geckos are nocturnal, so night temps matter too.

Measure the surface the gecko lies on, not the glass or the air at the top of the tank: the IR gun for the warm hide floor, a probe for each zone's air. A thermostat set to 90 does not mean the floor is 90; the IR gun is how you find out.

## Night Temperatures

Temperatures can safely drop to 65°F at night, mimicking natural desert conditions. If your home drops below this, use a ceramic heat emitter (no light, just heat) on a thermostat.

The sources do not agree on the floor. LafeberVet says to drop nights into the 70s, Zen Habitats' heating guide allows 60°F, and Merck's rule of thumb, 5°C below the daytime air gradient, lands around 68°F. This guide keeps 65°F as the floor: inside every one of those ranges but the strictest, and a number a room thermostat can hold.

## Signs the Temperatures Are Wrong

Too cold shows up slowly, as appetite and movement rather than anything you can see on the animal. Unfavorable environmental temperatures are one of the causes the Merck Veterinary Manual lists for respiratory infection, so open-mouth breathing, discharge from the nose, or difficulty breathing in a cool enclosure is a vet visit, not a heating tweak.

Too hot is louder. Glass surfing or pacing the walls away from the warm side, gaping or panting, refusing the warm hide, flattening into the coolest substrate, and a gecko that is either sluggish or oddly restless all point to heat the animal cannot escape. Darkened skin, wobbliness, or limpness is an emergency: move the gecko to a cooler spot, shut the heater off, and call a reptile vet.

Either way, the first step is the same. Read the floor with the IR gun, read the cool side with a probe, and fix the number before you change anything else.
