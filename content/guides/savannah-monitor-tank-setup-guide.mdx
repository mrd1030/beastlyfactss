# Savannah Monitor Enclosure Setup: The 8x4x4 Foot Reality

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A savannah monitor enclosure needs to support this species' size, digging instincts, and thermoregulation needs, cutting corners on any of these is what leads to the health and behavioral problems this species is known for.

<Figure src="/assets/images/savannah-monitor-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a savannah monitor enclosure showing the halogen basking cluster, humid burrow, and full-submersion water basin" caption="An 8x4x4ft enclosure is the current welfare standard for this species, and it reflects genuine need, not excess." shareable />

## Enclosure Size

A hatchling can start around 36x24x20 inches, but a single adult needs an 8-foot by 4-foot by 4-foot enclosure at minimum, this is the current welfare standard and reflects genuine need, not excess. Floor space matters more than height for this ground-dwelling species. House one monitor per enclosure.

True adult-sized housing for this species is typically a DIY build or ordered direct from a manufacturer.

## Temperature

Basking surface temperature should reach 140 to 150F, measured directly with an <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4dEII7J" product="Etekcity Infrared Thermometer Laser Temperature Gun">infrared thermometer</AffiliateLink> rather than an air temperature reading. Cool side should run 75 to 85F, with nighttime dropping to around 70 to 75F. Use a cluster of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DJRLWX1H?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Arcadia GoldenSun Basking Halogen Lamp 100W (PAR38 Heat Flood)">halogen flood bulbs</AffiliateLink> on a thermostat for basking heat, this species needs multiple bulbs working together to create adequate heat across a large enclosure. Avoid ceramic heat emitters, red or blue bulbs, or heat mats as the primary basking heat source, they don't provide the intensity this species needs. The halogens go off with the lights, and a room that holds 70 to 75F overnight needs nothing more. In a cooler room, a ceramic heat emitter or deep heat projector on its own thermostat holds the night range, because [night heat](/blog/reptile-heating-thermostats-guide/) has to come from a source that gives off no light, and that is the one job a ceramic emitter does well here.

## Humidity

Around 50% ambient, with access to a genuinely humid burrow where conditions run considerably wetter. Achieve this by pouring water directly into the deep substrate in one area and providing a humid hide, rather than trying to raise ambient humidity across the whole enclosure.

## Substrate

Deep is the operative word here, 12 to 24 inches or more of a diggable soil, sand, and clay mix that genuinely holds a burrow shape. A topsoil and play sand blend, or a burrowing clay mix, both work. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07Z5CJ573?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="The Bio Dude Terra Sahara Bioactive Reptile Substrate, 36 qt">bioactive-style substrate</AffiliateLink> is a functional option here too, though multiple bags will be needed to reach adequate depth. Avoid shallow substrate or loose, particulate-only setups that collapse rather than holding tunnels.

### Keeping the Soil Bed Working

A deep soil bed only holds tunnels while it stays firm and damp. Pack it down hard as it goes in, layer by layer, so the monitor digs through it instead of sinking into it. Keep it moist with periodic spraying or by pouring water into one area, and stop short of puddles. Soil that dries out stops holding a tunnel, which defeats the point of the depth.

Cleaning works around the tunnels. Pull feces, urates, and uneaten food off the surface as soon as they appear, dig out any wet or soiled patch with the soil around it, and replace it with fresh mix packed to the same firmness. Hatchlings and young juveniles can live on a thinner layer of soil, which makes them easier to watch and feed; the full depth goes in as the animal grows. A bed that is not bioactive still needs replacing in full on the loose soil interval in the [reptile enclosure cleaning schedule](/blog/reptile-salmonella-hygiene-guide/), every one to two months, with the whole enclosure scrubbed and dried at the same time.

## Lighting

Strong UVB is best practice for this species: a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TipZXE" product="UVB - Arcadia Desert 12% (ProT5 kit, 22in/24W)">T5 HO bulb in the 10 to 12% range</AffiliateLink>, spanning roughly half the enclosure, positioned 14 to 16 inches from the basking area. Pair with a bright 6500K daylight bulb. Adjust photoperiod seasonally, around 11 hours in winter, 13 in summer.

## Diet Basics

The staple is gut-loaded insects: dubia roaches, crickets, locusts, superworms, silkworms, grasshoppers, crayfish and other low-fat foods. Lean mice, chicks or freshwater fish belong in the occasional column and stay there, because rodents and fatty foods fed too often lead to severe obesity and organ disease. Skip processed meats and dog or cat food entirely.

Juveniles eat daily or every other day. Adults eat two to three times a week. Dust insects with calcium, without D3 where UVB is running and with D3 where it is not, and add a multivitamin weekly. The calcium supplement should be low in or free of phosphorus, at a minimum calcium to phosphorus ratio of 2:1.

No published figure puts a number on the portion, so frequency and food choice do most of the work. [Obesity and the fatty liver disease behind it](/blog/savannah-monitor-health-issues-guide/) kill more pet savannah monitors young than anything else, so an animal that is putting on a rounded, heavy body shape is being fed too richly whatever the portion size says.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: A Real Water Basin

Provide a water basin large enough for your monitor to fully submerge its entire body, not just a shallow dish. Combined with the humid burrow, this genuinely matters for hydration and shedding, a big, dry enclosure with just a small water bowl isn't an adequate setup for this species regardless of how large the dry footprint is.

Twice a week, a soak in something bigger still, a kiddie pool or a large storage tub, adds exercise on top of the water.
