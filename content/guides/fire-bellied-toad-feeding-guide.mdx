# Fire-Bellied Toad Feeding: Live Prey It Has to Leap For, Two to Six Items Every Few Days, and Nothing Hard-Shelled

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A fire-bellied toad is a small, bold, semi-aquatic hunter with one anatomical quirk that shapes its whole diet: it cannot flick its tongue. Here is what that means at feeding time, the portion and the schedule, the supplement on every feeding, the feeders that stay out, and how to read a toad that has stopped eating.

## Feeding Schedule by Age

The published range for an adult runs from every two to three days, where the amphibian specialist and the zoo's three-times-a-week both land, to once or twice a week on the veterinary sheet, which is the lean end of the same schedule. Feed every two to three days and let the toad's shape adjust it: a fire-bellied toad is built to be a compact, slightly plump animal, and one that is going round and heavy across the back gets the lean end.

Two rules hold at any age. Prey is no larger than the width of the toad's head, and the feeding ends when the toad stops, with whatever is left removed. A cricket that lives on in the enclosure chews on a resting toad and fouls the water when it dies.

<KeyTakeaway>
Alive, and moving. This family of toads cannot extend its tongue like other frogs, so it leaps forward and catches prey in its mouth, and it hunts by watching for movement. Few toads recognize non-living food at all. Everything offered is live, and a toad that seems uninterested in a bowl of stationary worms is not being difficult.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What Fire-Bellied Toads Eat

Wild adults eat terrestrial invertebrates, worms, mollusks and insects, and the captive diet follows. Live crickets make up the majority, with waxworms, earthworms, blackworms and small silkworms substituted in every few feedings, and dubia roach nymphs, hornworms and black soldier fly larvae rounding out the rotation. Variety is the diet: a toad on crickets alone is on a poor one, and the veterinary sheet's instruction is to feed a range of insects and worms rather than the same one every day.

**Gut-load first.** Feeders are gut-loaded for at least 24 hours before they are dusted and offered, so what the cricket ate is what the toad gets. The [gut-loading guide](/blog/gut-loading-feeder-insects-guide/) covers the feeder diet.

**Dust every feeding.** Insects are dusted with a calcium supplement that carries vitamin D before each feeding session, and with a multivitamin once or twice a week. A juvenile gets supplement at every feeding; an adult can drop the multivitamin to every two to four feedings while the calcium stays on. The [White's tree frog feeding guide](/blog/whites-tree-frog-feeding-guide/) carries the same schedule for a frog that gets fat on it faster.

**Feed on the land.** Offer prey on the land side or at the water's edge, from tongs or released and watched. A cricket dropped in the water drowns, and a toad striking at a worm on fine substrate takes a mouthful of the substrate with it.

## Foods to Avoid

Mealworms and other hard-shelled feeders. One veterinary sheet lists mealworms among the options, but their chitin shell impacts a small toad's gut the way a mouthful of gravel does, and impaction is one of the conditions the health guide is built around, so this guide leaves them out. Superworms for the same reason. Anything wider than the head. Wild-caught insects from anywhere sprayed, and fireflies, which are toxic. Dead or freeze-dried prey, which the toad will not recognize. And any feeder left in the enclosure after the feeding.

## Why Fire-Bellied Toads Stop Eating

**It is cold.** A fire-bellied toad below about 65°F slows down and stops feeding, and a cool room in winter is the commonest cause. Most rooms need no heat; a cold one needs a little.

**The water is bad.** A toad in water fouled with ammonia stops eating before the reddening of the legs and belly that gives red leg its name, and water quality is the defining requirement for this species. Test it, change it, and the appetite returns. The [amphibian quarantine and water guide](/blog/amphibian-quarantine-and-water-guide/) covers what the water needs to be.

**It is winter.** A toad kept with a seasonal cool-down eats less or nothing through it while holding its weight, and comes back with the warmth.

**It is in shed.** A day off food, and a toad that eats its own shed.

**It is new, or it has company it does not like.** A new arrival takes a week to settle. And a fire-bellied toad housed with any other species is stressed by it and stressing it, since its skin toxins make it a bad neighbor, which is why it lives only with its own kind.

## When to Worry

A few days off food in a cool spell is nothing. A week of refusal in warm, clean water, or any refusal with reddened skin, a bloated belly, cloudy eyes, or a toad that floats or sits listless in the water, is the point to see an exotic vet, and the [fire-bellied toad health guide](/blog/fire-bellied-toad-health-issues-guide/) sorts those signs. Red leg moves fast, and the toad that stopped eating on Monday can be beyond help by the weekend.

Two to six live crickets or worms every few days, no wider than the head, dusted, offered on the land, and the leftovers out. A fire-bellied toad fed that way in clean water is a hungry, active, long-lived amphibian, and the whole of the diet is in keeping it moving.
