# Lovebird Enrichment: Foraging, Toys, and What Cuts Boredom

A lovebird in the wild spends most of its waking day foraging, not eating from a bowl. Recreating that search-and-work behavior in a cage is the single biggest lever for preventing the boredom behaviors this species is known for. For everything else about keeping one, see our complete [Lovebird care guide](/guides/lovebird/).

<KeyTakeaway>
Foraging isn't a bonus activity, it's the behavior a food bowl accidentally deletes. Parrots will often choose to work for food over eating from a dish for free, a documented effect called contra-freeloading, which is why hiding food matters more than the food itself.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Foraging Comes First

Wild parrots spend an estimated 60 to 80% of their waking hours foraging, searching, problem-solving, and manipulating objects to get at food. A cage with a single bowl removes nearly all of that. Start by hiding a portion of the daily diet inside a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4fFoJqn" product="Kyouki Natural Foraging Box Toy">foraging box or puzzle toy</AffiliateLink> instead of putting everything in the dish. Paper cups with pellets wrapped inside, or a foraging box the bird has to open, both work. Rotate the method every few days so it stays a puzzle rather than a memorized routine.

## Toy Rotation and Novelty

Offering 3 to 5 toys at a time, rotated weekly, keeps novelty without cramming the cage. Mix categories rather than duplicates: something to forage in, something to shred, and something to chew. Reintroducing a toy that's been out of rotation for a few weeks often gets treated as new again, which stretches a small toy collection further than buying constantly.

<FunFact>
Boredom in captive parrots doesn't just look like inactivity, research links it to feather plucking, repetitive pacing, and screaming that goes beyond normal vocalizing. Some studies report enrichment reducing these behaviors by a wide margin when it's provided consistently rather than occasionally.
</FunFact>

## Shred and Chew Items

Lovebirds are enthusiastic shredders. Untreated paper, cardboard tubes, and bird-safe wood pieces give an outlet for that behavior that isn't the cage bars or nearby furniture. Balsa and other soft woods disappear fast for this species, so keep a rotating supply on hand rather than a single expensive toy that's gone in a day.

## Social and Cognitive Stimulation

A bonded pair reduces loneliness-driven stress, but pairing alone doesn't cover foraging or problem-solving needs, both birds still need their own enrichment. For a single lovebird, daily supervised out-of-cage time and direct interaction substitute for some of what a cage mate would otherwise provide. Talking to the bird, offering a hand or shoulder perch, and short training sessions using positive reinforcement all count as real cognitive engagement, not just entertainment.

## Reading the Warning Signs

Feather plucking, pacing the same stretch of perch repeatedly, and screaming that's new or louder than usual are the clearest signals that current enrichment isn't enough. Since these same signs can also point to a medical problem, a vet check alongside any enrichment changes is worth doing rather than assuming it's behavioral by default.
