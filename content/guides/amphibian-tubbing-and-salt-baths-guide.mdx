# Tubbing, Cooling, and Salt Baths for Sick Axolotls

<VetDisclaimer />

Ask in any axolotl forum what to do about a stressed, sick, or injured animal, and the first answer is almost always the same: tub it. Tubbing shows up before medication, before a vet visit, sometimes before anyone has actually worked out what's wrong. Some of that instinct is sound, a bare container with clean, cool, fully-changed water genuinely helps. Some of it is guesswork dressed up as a protocol, especially once cooling and salt baths get involved, since the specific numbers repeated online rarely come with a source attached. This guide covers what tubbing actually is, how to set one up, what cooling and salt baths do and don't do, and where the line sits between something you can manage yourself and something that needs an exotics vet. It's written primarily for axolotls, since they're the amphibian this method is used for most and the species the veterinary literature behind it actually covers, with a note at the end on where it does and doesn't extend to other amphibians.

## What Tubbing Actually Is, and Why It Works

A tub, in this context, is nothing more than a clean plastic container of dechlorinated, temperature-matched water with no filter, no substrate, and nothing decorative beyond maybe a simple hide. That plainness is the entire point. The University of Kentucky's Ambystoma Genetic Stock Center, which maintains research colonies of axolotls, houses many of its adult animals this exact way as standard practice, describing them as kept "either individually in one gallon bowls" in systems that "don't need continuously circulating water if the water is replaced regularly," since frequent changes prevent the buildup of ammonia and other metabolic waste. That's a research-husbandry statement about routine housing, not a treatment claim, but it explains why the setup works: a small, unfiltered volume of water is trivial to fully replace, and a fully replaced volume can't accumulate the waste, bacteria, or leftover medication that a filtered tank slowly processes instead.

For a keeper, that translates into a few concrete advantages over just watching a sick animal in its main tank. A tub is easy to observe against a plain background, with nowhere for a growth, a wound, or a change in gill color to hide behind decor. It's easy to isolate a stressed or injured animal from tankmates without disturbing an established, cycled system. And because there's no bacterial filter to protect, water quality in a tub can be reset completely, on a schedule as often as once or even twice a day, in a way that would strip a real filter of the colony it depends on if you tried the same thing in a display tank.

## Setting Up a Tub and the Water Change Routine

The container itself doesn't need to be elaborate: a clean plastic tote, bin, or bucket sized so the axolotl can turn around without repeatedly bumping the walls, with no gravel, sharp decor, or anything an unwell animal could get stuck against. Fill it with dechlorinated water, treated the same way you'd treat water going into the main tank, and match the temperature to what the axolotl is already used to rather than shocking it with a sudden shift in either direction. Quarantine and hospital setups for amphibians generally work the same way: enclosures that are easy to clean, built around a simple substrate or none at all, with owners encouraged to keep a log of appetite and behavior during the period an animal is being watched closely.

The water change is the actual maintenance routine, not an occasional top-up. Because there's no filter cycling the water, waste has nowhere to go except into the water column, so a full change, done once daily as a baseline and potentially twice for a genuinely sick animal, is what keeps the tub from becoming worse than the tank it replaced. Every change means fresh, dechlorinated, temperature-matched water again, checked by hand or thermometer before the axolotl goes back in, not assumed. Skipping a day, or changing only part of the water because a full swap feels excessive, defeats the reason a tub is used over a filtered tank in the first place: a partially refreshed small volume with no biological filtration can spike faster than a large, established, cycled tank would.

Moving the animal itself deserves the same care as the water. Use a soft aquarium net or, better, cup the axolotl in your wet hands and support its body rather than dragging it across a net's edge, since a stressed or injured animal is exactly the one most likely to be handled roughly by accident. A single simple hide, a length of PVC pipe or a piece of smooth decor, gives it somewhere to settle rather than sitting exposed in a bare bowl, without adding enough surface area to complicate cleaning. If you're tubbing to isolate an animal from tankmates for more than a day or two, keep checking the main tank's own water quality in parallel, our [aquarium cycling guide](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) covers what a properly cycled system should read, since a tub solves one animal's exposure but doesn't fix whatever made the main tank a problem in the first place.

## Cooling as a Treatment, Not Just a Comfort Setting

Axolotls are a cold-water species, and that baseline matters here specifically because cooling shows up in the veterinary literature as an actual part of treatment, not just a comfort adjustment. R. Loh, in a paper on common disease conditions in axolotls presented at the 2015 WSAVA World Congress, describes bacterial septicemia in axolotls, caused by *Aeromonas* and *Pseudomonas* species and treated with systemic antibiotics such as enrofloxacin or trimethoprim-sulfonamides, and states plainly that "lowered water temperature appears to aid sick axolotls and so they should be maintained between 5-15°C for several weeks during treatment." Converted, that's roughly 41 to 59°F, held for an extended stretch rather than a quick dip, and documented specifically alongside a prescribed antibiotic course, not as something to try on its own.

That same source describes a separate, more acute scenario: an axolotl in outright hyperthermic crisis, from prolonged exposure above 24°C (75°F), showing signs like loss of appetite and uncontrollable floating. For that emergency, Loh describes "placing the axolotl in a dish in the refrigerator" as helpful, a short-term measure to pull the animal's temperature down fast when it's already in a dangerous state, distinct from the sustained 5-15°C recovery range used during an actual antibiotic course. Both are documented for axolotls specifically. Neither is a general instruction to routinely chill a healthy animal, and the sustained cooling range in particular is described as part of a treatment a vet is already managing, which is worth sitting with before assuming a cold tub alone is doing the job of an antibiotic.

## Salt Baths: What They're For and the Numbers Behind Them

Salt baths are a genuine veterinary intervention for axolotls, and they're also the single most repeated, least sourced number in the entire hobby. The one veterinary figure that traces back to an actual named source is the same Loh WSAVA paper, and it's worth reading carefully because of how it's actually framed. In a section on toxicosis, the paper states that "excessive treatment with salt can also cause damage," and gives the boundary as "10 minute salt baths at a dose of 10-15 g/L, once to twice daily." That's presented as the ceiling before salt itself starts harming an axolotl's skin and gills, a caution against overdoing it, rather than a prescription telling you to run a bath at that exact strength for a specific illness. The paper doesn't say what condition calls for a salt bath in the first place, and it doesn't specify iodized versus non-iodized salt.

That gap matters more than it looks. A number that reads as authoritative because it appeared in a WSAVA proceedings paper is still only documented as an upper limit in one context, for one species, and it circulates far more widely on axolotl forums and care sheets than the actual source does, which is exactly how a rough approximation gets treated as settled fact. If a vet examines your axolotl and prescribes a salt bath for a specific fungal or bacterial issue, expect them to specify the exact strength, duration, and salt type for that animal and that condition, and to weigh it against Loh's caution about salt's own capacity to cause harm at higher doses. That's a different, more careful thing than measuring out a concentration you found online and running it at home on your own judgment.

## What Tubbing Does Not Fix

None of this, the tub, the cooling, the idea of a salt bath, is a treatment for an underlying illness. It's a controlled environment: clean water, a stable temperature, and an easy view of the animal, and controlled environments help a sick axolotl cope while something else does the actual curing, or while you figure out what's wrong. Tubbing does not fix bacterial infection, which per Loh's proceedings needs systemic antibiotics in its early stages and carries a poor prognosis once advanced. It does not fix fungal growth that's spreading rather than resolving. It does not fix impaction from swallowed substrate, which our [axolotl health issues guide](/blog/axolotl-health-issues-guide/) covers in more detail, or a wound that's gone past simple trauma into infection. A tub buys you clean water and a clear look at the animal. It does not diagnose anything, and it does not replace medication a vet has actually prescribed.

It's also worth being honest about what a tub can make harder to notice. An animal moved into a bare container loses its normal points of comparison, the substrate it usually rests on, the decor it usually swims past, which can mask subtle behavior changes a keeper would otherwise catch in the main tank. Treat a tub as a tool for a specific purpose, isolation, observation, a cooler or cleaner short-term environment, rather than a default place to keep an axolotl "just in case" once the original reason for moving it has passed.

## Where Home Care Stops

Tubbing, matching temperature, and doing full water changes are things any keeper can manage alone, and they're worth doing well before anything looks wrong, since our guide on [why axolotls need cold, clean water](/blog/why-axolotls-need-cold-clean-water/) covers the baseline water quality that prevents most of this in the first place. Cooling a tub down as general support for a stressed animal, within its normal range, is also reasonable on your own; our [guide to cooling an aquarium without a chiller](/blog/cooling-an-aquarium-without-a-chiller-guide/) covers how to do that without shocking the animal.

Past that, the line is clear. A specific symptom, not just general stress, is the signal to call an exotics or aquatic vet rather than keep managing it at home: open sores or red streaking, a cotton-like fungal growth that isn't improving within a couple of days, uncontrollable floating, bloating consistent with impaction, or appetite loss running several days. Any deliberate cooling protocol meant to run for weeks, and any salt bath at a real therapeutic strength, belongs to a vet who has actually examined the animal and can weigh the dose against what's actually wrong, not a number pulled from a forum thread or, for that matter, from this article. An axolotl that's already declined to the point of advanced bacterial septicemia has, per Loh's own proceedings, a poor prognosis, which is the entire argument for treating early symptoms as a reason to call ahead rather than wait to see what a tub does on its own.

<FunFact>
Axolotls lack the thyroid trigger most salamanders use to metamorphose out of water, which is why they stay fully aquatic their entire lives and why every intervention here, tubbing, cooling, salt baths, happens in water rather than on land.
</FunFact>

<KeyTakeaway>
Tubbing is a controlled environment, not a cure: a bare container, dechlorinated water, and a full change on a real schedule. Cooling and salt baths are documented veterinary tools for axolotls specifically, with figures that exist in the literature but were never meant as a DIY substitute for a vet who has actually examined the animal.
</KeyTakeaway>
