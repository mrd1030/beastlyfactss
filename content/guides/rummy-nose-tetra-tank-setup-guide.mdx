# Rummy-Nose Tetra Tank Setup: A 36-Inch Run, Soft Acidic Water, and Dim Light

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The [rummy-nose tetra](/encyclopedia/animal/rummy-nose-tetra/) (Petitella bleheri, formerly Hemigrammus bleheri) is a South American tetra from tannin-stained blackwater rivers, where the water runs brown and very acidic. Adults reach 1.5 to 2 inches (4 to 5 cm), and they move in a way that shapes the whole tank: a sudden burst of speed, a glide, then another burst, with the rest of the school matching each move.

## Tank Length Comes First

A school of 10 needs a tank at least 36 inches (90 cm) long. In volume that is a 30 to 40 gallon tank, and an <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0002AS23U?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Aqueon 40 gallon breeder tank">Aqueon 40-gallon breeder</AffiliateLink> sits right on the minimum at 36 x 18 inches. Buy by the measurement along the front glass. A 20-gallon long is only 30 inches, and a tall 30-gallon on a short base fails the same test.

Length matters because this is one of the tighter schooling tetras and an active one. The group swims laps from end to end, and each burst covers ground, so a short tank turns the school back on itself before a run has started. Ten is the smallest school to plan for, and [why the group needs that many](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-enrichment-guide/) comes down to how the fish behave in numbers. The tank should be a calm one, since rummy-noses lose out to boisterous or much larger fish, and [which species share a tank safely](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) is the next decision after the size.

## Temperature

Hold the water at 73 to 79°F (23 to 26°C) with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">submersible heater</AffiliateLink>, set near 76°F so a warm room has somewhere to go. 79°F is the ceiling.

That band sits wholly inside the 73 to 84°F a [cardinal tetra tank](/blog/cardinal-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) can run at, and it shares 73 to 78°F with a [neon tetra tank](/blog/neon-tetra-tank-setup-guide/). A tank holding all three settles in the mid 70s.

## Soft, Acidic Water

The target is pH 5.5 to 7.0 with a general hardness of 2 to 12 dGH, and the soft, acidic end suits the species best. That pH range falls inside the 5.0 to 7.0 that cardinal and [ember tetra](/blog/ember-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) tanks are kept at, though rummy-noses take a little more hardness than an ember's 10 dGH limit. Water that drifts alkaline washes out their color.

Test the tap before the tank is filled. Hard source water is fixed at the source, and [how GH and KH actually come down](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) decides how the tank is first mixed and how every change after it is made up.

The red head is the quickest visual check in the tank. It fades when the fish is stressed or kept in poor water, so a school that has gone pale is the signal to run the test kit. It does not tell you which reading is off.

## Filtration and Flow

Size the filter to the tank's volume, using [the turnover figures for a real tank](/blog/aquarium-filtration-guide/) rather than the number on the box. Adults ask nothing special of the current. Flow becomes the deciding factor only in a breeding tank, where a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> moves water gently enough that the intake cannot swallow the fry.

For a blackwater setup, a small net bag of aquarium-safe peat can sit in the filter.

## Planting and Layout

Two layouts both work. A planted tank with plenty of green shows off the red heads well. The other route copies the wild rivers, where aquatic plants are scarce: a river-sand bottom, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">driftwood</AffiliateLink> and roots, and a few handfuls of dried beech or oak leaves, swapped for fresh ones every few weeks. Whichever is chosen, keep a long open lane through the middle of the tank for the school to run.

## Lighting

Keep the light fairly dim. Dim for an aquarium is still far above the level a school needs to work. In lab trials, groups of five held together only above 1.5 lux, and in schools of about 50, very low light broke the group into fish that could not move as one. As the light rose, the same fish lined up and then settled into a steady, circling mill. Floating plants or a dimmed fixture give the shade this fish prefers without taking the tank anywhere near that threshold.

<FunFact>
Rummy-nose tetras are a big wild fishery. Out of the 20 to 30 million ornamental fish shipped from Brazil's Amazonas state each year, the rummy-nose is the second most exported, about 6% of the total, behind only the cardinal tetra.
</FunFact>

## Cycle the Tank, Then Change Water Weekly

A rummy-nose tank is [fully cycled before the school goes in](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/), with ammonia and nitrite both at zero and nitrate starting to rise. This species reacts badly to stress, which is why it is usually not suggested for a first tank, and an unfinished cycle is the worst stress a new tank can hand it. New fish sometimes arrive carrying ich, so they spend their first weeks in [a separate quarantine tank](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before they meet the display.

Partial water changes every week keep the water soft and clean, and they matter most in a planted layout. [The size of each change follows the nitrate reading](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/) taken before it, and the new water goes in mixed to the same soft chemistry and temperature as the tank, so the school never meets a jump.
