# Amano Shrimp Tank Setup: Minimal Bioload, Real Current, and a Lid That Actually Seals

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Amano shrimp are native to fast-flowing freshwater streams in southwestern Japan and Taiwan, and that origin shapes their tank setup more than most keepers expect.

## Tank Size and Stocking Density

A 10-gallon tank is a practical minimum. Stock it at one shrimp per 2 gallons, so five in a 10-gallon.

Their bioload is low enough that they can live in most nano tanks or larger as long as the lid is secure, and they do not need to be kept in groups. Low bioload is a real point, and it means a tank already fighting an algae outbreak can carry more than five without a water quality problem. It is not a license to pack one, though: a small group technically surviving in 5 gallons is not the same as thriving with room to forage, and more shrimp in the same algae only means the supply runs out sooner.

## Temperature and Water Chemistry

64 to 82°F covers the tolerated range, but shrimp kept toward the cooler two-thirds of that band, roughly the upper 60s to mid-70s, tend to live longer, since warmer water speeds up metabolism and shortens lifespan. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">heater</AffiliateLink> set conservatively is usually the right call rather than one pushed toward the top of the range. Aim for pH 6.0 to 7.5.

General hardness (GH) deserves real attention here, not just clean water. Amano shrimp take a wide working range of [roughly 5 to 15 dGH](https://www.shrimpscience.com/species/amano-shrimp/), and that hardness is what supplies the calcium and other minerals this species needs to build a new shell every molt. Too little GH and a shrimp can't successfully harden its new exoskeleton, too much and the old one can become too rigid to break out of cleanly. Carbonate hardness (KH) in the 2 to 8 dKH range is a reasonable target alongside it.

<FunFact>
A pair of studies on wild Caridina larvae help explain why this species tolerates real current so well as an adult. Research in Crustacean Research found Caridina multidentata larvae can adapt across a genuinely wide salinity band, roughly 17 to 34 parts per thousand, wider than several lowland relatives in the same genus need. A related study in Hydrobiologia found a likely reason why: C. multidentata is one of the Caridina species that lives and breeds in the middle and upper reaches of rivers, real headwater country, so its larvae evolved to survive a longer, rougher journey downstream to the sea than a shrimp that already lives near the coast. A species built for that kind of current doesn't want still water as an adult either.
</FunFact>

## Filtration: Safe Intake, Not Necessarily Calm Water

This is worth correcting directly, since it's easy to assume "gentle filter" means "gentle current." A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> matters for amano shrimp because it blocks a standard filter intake from pulling in or injuring a shrimp, particularly one that's just molted and is a weak swimmer for a short while afterward. It's an intake-safety choice, not a statement about how much flow the species can handle. A hang-on-back filter is a good fit for the extra flow it provides, which simulates the shrimp's natural home in a river. One filter does the job, and either kind works. A hang-on-back with a sponge pre-filter over its intake gives both the current this species evolved for and an intake it cannot be pulled into; a plain sponge filter, the [gentlest filter type](/blog/aquarium-filtration-guide/), gives the safety with less current. A bare hang-on-back intake is the one setup to avoid.

## Substrate, Décor, and a Lid With No Gaps

Driftwood, rocks, and <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4grZYPq" product="Live terrarium plants">live plants</AffiliateLink> all do double duty here: they're grazing surface for biofilm and algae, and cover for a molting shrimp to retreat into. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">Driftwood</AffiliateLink> especially gives them a stable, textured surface to work over.

The lid is not optional. Amano shrimp are well known escape artists, especially in their first few days in a new tank, and a gap of even a fraction of an inch around a cord or filter intake is enough for one to climb out and turn up dried out the next morning. A tight-fitting glass or acrylic lid, with any gaps sealed, is genuinely one of the most important pieces of equipment in this entire setup.

## Algae Load: How Much Do They Need?

Not an endless supply, but more than a spotless, newly cycled tank usually has on hand. Algae functions as a starting food reserve the shrimp work through steadily rather than a self-renewing buffet, and when that reserve runs low it's time to [add <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00025JZAY?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Sinking Wafers for Catfish, Loaches and Bottom Feeders, 3.88 oz">sinking wafers</AffiliateLink> or blanched vegetables](/blog/amano-shrimp-feeding-guide/), lightly and every couple of days rather than heavily.

This is also, honestly, the most common real-world reason people buy amano shrimp at all. Far more often than being planned into a brand-new tank from day one, a group gets added specifically because a hair, thread, or beard algae outbreak has already shown up and the keeper wants a genuine cleanup crew rather than another chemical treatment. That's a legitimate strategy, amano shrimp are the strongest true algae eaters commonly sold in the hobby, but it's worth going in with a plan for the day the outbreak is actually under control and natural algae growth drops off, since that's exactly when supplemental feeding becomes necessary rather than optional.

Always run tap water through a water conditioner before it goes anywhere near the tank, dechlorinating it and neutralizing chloramine and heavy metals that are otherwise harmful to invertebrates.
