# Cat Enrichment: Five Pillars, and Most Homes Miss the Second One

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cats have something dogs mostly do not: an agreed veterinary framework for what their environment has to provide.

<KeyTakeaway>
The AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines set out **five pillars** of a healthy feline environment. The one most homes fail is the second: **multiple and separated key resources**. Food, water, litter, scratching and resting places all bunched in one corner is one resource, not five, and it forces the cat into trade-offs it should not have to make.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Guidelines Set Out

Ellis, Rodan, Carney, Heath, Rochlitz, Shearburn, Sundahl and Westropp published the **AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines** in the *Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery* in 2013. It is one of the most-cited documents in feline medicine and it organizes the whole question into five pillars.

1. **A safe place.** Somewhere the cat can retreat to and feel secure, ideally elevated and enclosed.
2. **Multiple and separated key environmental resources.** Food, water, toileting areas, scratching areas, play areas and resting places, provided in multiples and in separate locations.
3. **Opportunity for play and predatory behavior.**
4. **Positive, consistent and predictable human to cat social interaction.**
5. **An environment that respects the cat's sense of smell.**

The guidelines' own framing is that a cat's comfort with its environment is **intrinsically linked to its physical health**, and that getting this right reduces stress-related disorders as well as unwanted behavior. That link is the part worth taking seriously: this is not a comfort checklist, it is preventive medicine.

<FunFact>
The reason this exists as a veterinary document rather than a lifestyle one is that feline stress presents as illness. Idiopathic cystitis, over-grooming and inappetence are environmental problems that arrive at the vet looking like medical ones, which is why the profession wrote the environment guidelines itself.
</FunFact>

## Separate the Resources

The pillar most homes fail, and it is free to fix.

Food and water in **different places**, not side by side, since cats generally prefer not to drink next to where they eat. Litter trays **away from both**, in separate locations, never in a row. Scratching posts near sleeping and entry points, where cats actually want to scratch. Resting places at several heights around the home.

In a multi-cat home this is the whole ballgame. Two trays beside each other are one resource that one cat can guard. Two trays at opposite ends of the house are two. The standard recommendation is **one tray per cat plus one**, separately sited.

## Vertical Space and Safe Places

Pillars one and two at the same time. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vlaH1R" product="Cat tree / scratching post">cat tree</AffiliateLink> gives height to observe from, somewhere enclosed to retreat into, and a scratching surface.

Height multiplies territory without needing more floor, which matters most in small homes and multi-cat households. Shelves, window perches and the tops of furniture all count.

A safe place has to be somewhere the cat can be **left alone in**. A retreat that gets reached into is not a retreat.

## Predatory Play, Finished Properly

The third pillar, and the detail that gets missed is the ending.

A wand toy moved like prey, in short bursts with pauses, ending in a **catch** the cat is allowed to have, runs the full sequence: stalk, chase, pounce, grab. A laser pointer runs the sequence and never lets it resolve, which is why some cats get visibly frustrated with them. If you use one, finish by landing the dot on a physical toy the cat can catch.

Several short sessions a day beat one long one. Cats hunt in bursts. Rotate toys so they stay novel, and store the ones not in use.

## Food That Takes Work

Cats in the wild make many small kills a day. A bowl filled twice removes all of it.

A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vg6egE" product="Puzzle feeder">puzzle feeder</AffiliateLink> puts part of the ration behind a problem. Scatter feeding, food hidden around the house, and treat balls do the same thing more cheaply. Start easy, since a cat that fails at a puzzle simply stops trying.

Take it out of the daily ration. None of it goes on top. Our guide to [pet obesity and body condition](/blog/pet-obesity-body-condition-guide/) covers why that matters, and indoor cats are the population it hits hardest.

## Predictable Human Interaction

The fourth pillar is about **consistency and the cat's choice**, not quantity. Let the cat initiate contact and end it. Handle in ways an individual cat likes rather than in ways cats are supposed to like.

Predictable routines around feeding, play and quiet time do more for a nervous cat than any object. Our guide on [pacing and hiding](/blog/when-your-pet-wont-stop-pacing-or-hiding-what-anxious-pet-behavior-is-really-telling-you/) covers what it looks like when this pillar is missing.

## Respect the Nose

The fifth pillar, and the one that sounds soft until you understand what scent does for a cat. Facial marking on furniture and corners is how a cat makes a space its own.

Practically: do not scrub every surface a cat has marked, do not deep-clean everything at once, keep strong-smelling cleaners away from resources, and introduce new furniture gradually. Litter changes count here too, since a cat that dislikes a new litter's smell stops using the tray.

## Scratching, Provided Deliberately

A normal behavior that is going to happen. Posts tall enough for a full stretch, stable enough not to wobble, in the places cats want to scratch, which is near sleeping spots and entrances. The corner you would prefer is not one of them.

Offer more than one surface type, since cats differ on sisal, cardboard and carpet, and some want a horizontal surface where others want a vertical one.

## Priority Order

1. Key resources separated across the home, not stacked in one corner
2. One litter tray per cat plus one, in separate locations
3. Safe elevated retreats the cat can be left alone in
4. Short daily predatory play sessions that end in a catch
5. Part of the ration delivered through puzzles and scatter feeding
6. Scratching surfaces where the cat wants them
7. Consistent, cat-led human interaction and a stable scent environment

## What Not to Do

Do not stack food, water and litter in one corner. Do not line litter trays up together and count them as separate. Do not rely on a laser pointer that never lets the cat catch anything. Do not reach into a cat's retreat. Do not deep-clean every marked surface at once. Do not add enrichment food on top of a full bowl. And do not read a cat that hides all day as simply shy, since that is often the first pillar failing.
