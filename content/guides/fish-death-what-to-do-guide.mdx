# When a Fish Dies: What to Do Next

<VetDisclaimer />

A dead fish in a home aquarium is two problems at once: a body to deal with, and a tank that may still hold whatever killed it. The next hour has an order. Confirm the death, get the body out, and test the water before changing any of it. Then work out why it happened. Disposal comes last, and the quickest options, the toilet and the nearest pond, are the two that do harm.

## Make Sure It Is Dead

A fish lying still is not proof. Loss of movement, no response to touch and a limp body all point to death. Two checks are more reliable for most fish. The gill covers stop their rhythmic movement and stay still for at least 30 minutes, and the eyes no longer roll when the fish is gently rocked from side to side. That eye roll is a reflex, and losing it is one of the clearer markers. A heartbeat proves less than it seems, because a fish's heart can keep contracting after the brain has died.

Death has to be confirmed before anything is disposed of. A fish that is still breathing is not ready for any of what follows. If it is failing and cannot recover, [ending its life humanely](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) is a separate decision, and it comes first.

## Take the Body Out Now

Once death is confirmed, the body comes out of the tank. Three reasons sit behind that. Some infections, Mycobacterium among them, pass to fish that eat an infected dead fish. Decaying matter is a source of fungal infection, and clearing it out is part of preventing one. And decay adds ammonia to the water, which at high enough levels kills fish.

## Test the Water Before You Change It

Raised ammonia or nitrite, swings in pH and low oxygen all cause disease in fish, and none of them can be seen. Test ammonia, nitrite, pH and temperature, and write the readings down. Frequent tests and a written record are how a change in water quality gets noticed.

Any ammonia reading matters, because even a low level stresses fish and damages their gills. At 2 mg/L or higher, change at least half the water. The exception is old tank syndrome: very high ammonia, often above 10 mg/L, little or no alkalinity, and a low pH. There a simple water change is the wrong move. The new water raises the pH, and the rise can turn the ammonia already in the tank toxic and kill the fish. A tank like that comes back on [small changes, daily at first, over about a month](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/), never one big one.

If a vet or lab may get involved, take a water sample first, before any water is changed. Keep it in its own container, separate from the fish, refrigerated and in the dark. The water a fish travels in does not show what is happening in the tank, so it does not count as the sample.

<KeyTakeaway>
Sample, then test, then change. A sample taken after the change describes the new water. One taken before it describes the water the fish died in.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Look for a Cause

The cause usually sits in the tank's recent history. New arrivals first, and whether they went through quarantine before joining the tank. Then the timeline: when the first odd behavior showed, and how many fish have been sick or died each day since. Then the routine: when the water was last changed and how much went out, and whether feeding or maintenance changed recently.

The body carries clues while it is fresh. Note gills that look discolored, bloody or frayed, and any sores or growths on the skin.

## When a Vet or Lab Is Worth Calling

Contact a vet who treats fish, a fish health professional or a fish disease lab as soon as disease is a suspicion. Without a proper diagnostic workup, fish diseases are often misdiagnosed, and treating for the wrong one can leave the fish worse off.

A dead fish is rarely good material for tests. One that died recently and is in good condition, with clear eyes and gills still red, may still be worth sending. One that has started to decompose or smells is not.

To keep a fresh body for a vet or lab, wrap it in wet paper towels so it stays moist and seal it in a heavy plastic bag. Keep it cold with ice packs in a foam cooler. Do not freeze it: freezing damages the tissue a lab examines under the microscope. Wrap the bag in bubble wrap or a few extra layers of paper towel so it never touches the ice directly.

Call before anything goes out. Most labs need notice that a sample is coming, and an overnight shipment should be booked for morning arrival, since a fish that lands late in the afternoon may not be examined that day.

## Disposing of the Body

Do not flush it. A toilet can drain to a waterway, and a dead fish can carry disease: remains go where they spread nothing and reach no open water. Never put a fish into a pond, stream or lake either, alive or dead. A released aquarium fish can become an invasive species, and the damage lands on the waterway, on the people who fish and boat it, and on whoever pays to clean it up. In Minnesota, for one, releasing a non-native species into public waters is illegal. The tank's water and plants follow the same rule: none of it goes into a lake, river or wetland.

Beyond that, the remains fall under federal, state and local rules, and those rules decide whether the household trash or a spot in the yard is acceptable where you live. Whichever route they allow, the body should end up where pets, pests and wildlife cannot reach it.
