# Fish Color and Diet: What Brings It Out, and What No Food Can Change

A fish wears three kinds of color, and only one of them comes from its food. The black is a pigment the fish makes for itself. The blues, silvers and iridescent greens are light bouncing off crystals in its skin. The reds, oranges and yellows are built partly from carotenoids, pigments no fish can make and every fish has to eat.

## Where a Fish's Color Comes From

Each of those colors sits in its own kind of skin cell. The black cells make their melanin, which also gives the browns. The red, orange and yellow cells mix the carotenoids the fish eats with a second pigment they make for themselves. The reflective cells hold no pigment at all, only stacks of tiny colorless crystals.

The fish can change how those cells look within seconds to hours, by spreading the pigment out or pulling it into a tight dot. Hormones and nerves run that change, including the stress response, which is why a frightened or unwell fish can look darker or paler than usual.

## The Colors a Fish Has to Eat

Most animals cannot make carotenoids from scratch; plants and algae can. A wild fish gets them by eating algae, or by eating the small crustaceans that ate the algae, and astaxanthin is the main carotenoid in crustaceans. Fish farms add carotenoids to feed for the same reason, from sources such as krill and algae, with marigold used for yellow.

Fish do not all handle those pigments the same way. Carp, the family goldfish and koi belong to, can turn zeaxanthin into astaxanthin inside their own bodies. Perch-like fish, cichlids among them, cannot make astaxanthin from other carotenoids, so the astaxanthin they carry has to come in with the food already made.

Feed a fish carotenoids and more of them end up in its skin, from about double to about ten times as much, depending on the fish and the pigment. Not every pigment works on every fish.

## What No Food Will Change

A carotenoid food works only on carotenoid color. Black is melanin, made in the fish's own cells, so no diet darkens it. Blues, silvers and iridescent greens are reflected light, so no diet brightens them either. Even a red is only partly food, because part of it is the pigment the fish makes for itself.

Food also deepens a color the fish already carries without adding one. Beta carotene made platies redder and left their yellow where it was. The pattern, the black markings and the variety a fish was bred as come with the fish.

## Color Foods: What Is in Them and What to Expect

A color-enhancing flake or pellet is a carotenoid food, and the ingredients doing the work are the ones above plus spirulina and red pepper or paprika, which supplies the carotenoid capsanthin.

Expect a month at least, and two to be sure, before judging a color food.

More is not better. Overdoing it does not help, and too much may work against the immune response. A feed that is heavy in pigment can cost growth, which is why the color food stays one part of the rotation and the staple still carries the diet.

The pigments do more than color. Oscars fed astaxanthin or a beta carotene alga died less often after a bacterial infection, and platies on enriched brine shrimp carried more protective enzymes and antibodies in their skin mucus.

## Light, Background and the Time of Day

Much of what looks like a color change is a fish adjusting its pigment cells to its surroundings. Zebrafish match their background by gathering or spreading their dark pigment. Neon and cardinal tetras turn lighter in bright light over a pale substrate in clear water, and duller, with a violet stripe, at night. The same fish can look different at breakfast and at dinner without anything being wrong.

## When Lost Color Means Something Is Wrong

Stress changes color through the same hormones and nerves, so a fish under pressure from bullies, a move or bad water can go darker or paler. Disease shows in color too. Dulled color under a gray-white film of mucus points to skin protozoa, paleness with sores to larger skin parasites, and very pale gills to anemia. Ich goes the other way: white spots with dark coloration, lethargy and clamped fins.

A pale fish that is also clamping its fins, gasping or refusing food is [showing a water problem or an infection](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/), and the water gets tested before anything else.

## Tetras and Danios

### Neon and Cardinal Tetras

Both carry the same two-part color. The blue stripe is iridophore color: light falling on a tetra's lateral stripe widens the spacing between its reflecting platelets and shifts the color it reflects, and in the neon the platelets tilt like the slats of a Venetian blind. Food cannot reach that color. The red along the lower body is partly carotenoid color, so a varied diet with a carotenoid source keeps it deep.

### Glowlight Tetra

Glowlights color best in acidic water under dim light, on small live and frozen foods, and wash out in a sparse, bare tank.

### Rummy-Nose Tetra

The red head works as a gauge of the whole school. It pales badly when the fish are out of condition or kept in alkaline water, and the red stays deepest on a varied diet, so a nose that fades on every fish at once points to the water before the menu.

### Ember Tetra

The ember's orange-red is the carotenoid kind of color, so a staple that lists krill, algae or astaxanthin is worth having. Adult males are the more intensely colored sex, and they are brightest in spawning condition.

### Black Skirt Tetra

The black skirt's black is melanin, which no food deepens, and the intense black of a juvenile fades as the fish grows. Its best color comes with dense planting and floating plants dimming the light.

### Zebra Danio

A danio's dark stripes are melanin and its silver-blue sheen comes from reflective cells, both made by the fish itself. Background does more for it than food: danios color best in a heavily planted tank over a dark substrate.

## Livebearers

### Platy

The platy is the clearest case. Its red, orange and yellow are carotenoid color: platies fed brine shrimp enriched with beta carotene for eight weeks carried more than four times the skin carotenoid of platies on plain brine shrimp. A flake or pellet listing spirulina, krill, astaxanthin or marigold, [fed a few times a week alongside the platy's usual staple](/blog/platy-feeding-guide/), carries the same pigments.

### Swordtail

Red swordtails answer to plant pigment. Fed marigold petal meal, a lutein source, at 15% of the feed, they held about ten times the carotenoid of fish on plain feed within three to four weeks. Marigold did more for them than hibiscus (China rose) or carrot, so it is the ingredient worth finding on the label.

### Guppy

A male guppy's orange spots are partly carotenoid color, so a carotenoid source in the diet feeds part of them. Females are plain by comparison.

### Molly

Gold and orange mollies carry carotenoid color that a carotenoid-rich food can brighten, while a black molly has none for it to work on.

## Cichlids

An angelfish's silver and black bars are reflective cells and melanin, so only the orange or red of some varieties answers to food.

In discus and oscars the reds and oranges respond to food, and the astaxanthin has to come already made, from krill, shrimp or a food that lists astaxanthin itself. Oscars fed astaxanthin for 50 days carried about twice the skin carotenoid of fish on a plain diet.

## Goldfish and Koi

Goldfish and koi have the most flexible chemistry of any common pet fish. Spirulina, a zeaxanthin source they turn into astaxanthin, goes into their feed as a red source, alongside the astaxanthin in krill and shrimp meal.

## Bettas

A red betta's red is carotenoid color, and food moves it. When male bettas ranging from blue to red were given extra carotenoids, the redder fish turned redder than unsupplemented fish, while the bluer fish did not redden at all and put the pigment into immune response instead. Not every pigment source works: red females fed annatto oil extract for four weeks showed no change in skin color. The blue, green and metallic sheens of many varieties are reflected light, and a black betta's black is melanin the fish makes for itself, so food does not touch either. A female ready to spawn turns paler and shows dark bars along her flanks, which is breeding dress, not illness.

## Bottom Dwellers: Corydoras and Bristlenose Plecos

The browns, blacks and bronze sheen of corydoras are melanin and reflective cells, so a color food has almost nothing to work on. Bristlenose plecos are built the same way, and what changes in them is shade, not hue.

## Dyed and Injected Fish

Some fish reach stores in colors they were never bred to carry, because dye has been injected under the skin; [skirt tetras are among the fish it is done to](/blog/black-skirt-tetra-health-issues-guide/). The injection stresses the fish badly and such fish often die young, so a dyed fish is best left in the store.

## Bringing Out a Fish's Best Color

Start with what the fish can show. Its reds, oranges and yellows answer to food: a flake or pellet with astaxanthin, krill, spirulina or marigold on the label, fed for a month or two alongside the usual diet, deepens them, and more than that does not help. Its blacks, blues, silvers and sheens come from the fish itself, and no food moves them. For every fish, clean water, a calm tank and the right light and background let the color show, and a fish that loses color while acting unwell needs a water test before it needs a new food.
