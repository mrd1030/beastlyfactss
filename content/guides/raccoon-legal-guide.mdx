A raccoon is the one animal on this map that almost no state regulates as a pet, because almost no state thinks of it as one. The rules that decide it were written for the wild animal: furbearer chapters that govern trapping seasons and pelt tags, and rabies statutes that name the raccoon in the same breath as the skunk, the fox and the bat. Maryland bans it in a four-word list, Kentucky files it under "Rabies Vector Species", Montana's prohibition sits in the public health title rather than the wildlife code, and Utah hands it to an agricultural damage board rather than its wildlife agency. Read across all 52 jurisdictions, that produces 26 bans, 18 permits, three states that allow it on a sourcing condition, one that restricts the whole order Carnivora, three whose text will not decide, and exactly one with no rule at all.

The published lists disagree about this animal more than about almost any other. Depending on the page, somewhere between thirteen and nineteen states are said to allow a pet raccoon, and Texas appears in both columns. The disagreement comes from reading the wrong chapter: Texas's dangerous wild animal list does not name the raccoon, so it looks free, and its fur-bearer chapter requires a license to possess one alive for any purpose, so it is a permit state. Counted on the statutes, 18 states issue a permit, and only 16 issue it to a private keeper.

## The Rabies List

The reason the map looks the way it does fits in one clause of Maryland law. Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1) says a person may not import, sell, trade, barter, possess, breed or exchange a live:

> (i) fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear

That list is not an exotic pet list, even though it sits at the top of one. Fox, skunk and raccoon are the three terrestrial rabies reservoirs of the eastern United States, and the clause was written so that nobody keeps a wild-caught local animal that might be incubating the virus. Maryland's exemptions run to zoos, sanctuaries, research and licensed exhibitors, there is no private permit, and subsection (e) lets a county or city go further. The grandfather window for animals held on 31 May 2006 closed that August.

Kentucky makes the reasoning explicit. 301 KAR 2:081 § 6(1) says a person shall not import, transport into Kentucky, or possess:

> (j) Rabies Vector Species: 1. Coyote (Canis latrans); 2. Gray fox (Urocyon cinereoargenteus); 3. Raccoon (Procyon lotor); 4. Red fox (Vulpes vulpes); 5. Spotted skunk (Spilogale putorius); 6. Striped skunk (Mephitis mephitis); or 7. Any hybrid of rabies vector species.

The same regulation defines native wildlife "regardless of ... being captive-bred or taken from the wild", so a raccoon bought from a breeder in Indiana is in exactly the position of one trapped in a Lexington attic. Montana reaches the same place from its public health title: MCA § 50-23-101(4) defines a "wild animal" for the rabies part as "a skunk, fox, raccoon, or bat", and § 50-23-102 says a person may not possess one unless it is used in a fur-bearing enterprise, kept in a zoo exhibit with no public contact, held for research, or was owned for six months before 1 January 1982. Because the raccoon is a Montana native, the exotic wildlife white list that decides most Montana questions never applies; this older ban does the work. Washington's answer comes from its Department of Health rather than Fish and Wildlife: WAC 246-100-197(5)(a) prohibits all persons from "acquiring, selling, bartering, exchanging, giving, purchasing, distributing, or trapping to retain any bat, skunk, fox, raccoon, or coyote", except a zoo, exhibitor, research facility or rehabilitator. Idaho Code § 25-236, an agriculture statute, bars possessing "any fox, skunk or raccoon" outside fur farms, parks, zoos, museums and educational institutions.

Two more states ban it by name in plainer words. North Dakota Century Code § 36-01-08.4 reads "A person may not keep a skunk or raccoon in captivity", with a USDA-licensed zoo as the only exception. Utah is the odd one, because its wildlife table does not list the raccoon at all: R657-3b-1(2) hands raccoons and coyotes to the Agricultural and Wildlife Damage Prevention Board, whose rule R58-14-3(2):

> declares it unlawful to import, distribute, relocate, or possess a live raccoon (Procyon lotor) or coyote (Canis latrans).

The one variance in R58-14-3(3) is per animal and available only to "an organization or corporation" holding raccoons for research or exhibition. A reader who checks only the Division of Wildlife Resources table comes away thinking Utah has no rule, which is how it ends up on the longer lists.

<FunFact>
Delaware has a raccoon permit written into its statute and has never meant it for keepers. Del. Code tit. 7 § 795 says "No live skunks or raccoons shall be sold or possessed in this State or transported into this State for any purpose without a permit from the Division." The Division is DNREC, which issues that permit to wildlife rehabilitators and says state regulations forbid owning native wildlife. A permit sentence in the code, and no permit for a pet.

## Where the Family Is the Rule

Five jurisdictions never needed the rabies argument, because they restricted the whole family Procyonidae and the raccoon is the animal the family is named for. California's 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(K)(4) lists "Family Procyonidae-All species-(D)", the D marking the family as detrimental, and the four exceptions beneath it (two pandas, the ringtail and the cacomistle) are only moved to the welfare category. § 671 reaches any live animal of a listed family whatever its origin, and the only route for a native procyonid is the Native Species Exhibiting permit for unreleasable rehabilitation animals. Hawaii names "Procyon lotor racoon" on Part A of its restricted list, the research and exhibition tier. Pennsylvania's Game Commission list at 58 Pa. Code § 137.1 reads "In the family Procyonidae. All species of raccoons", and because the animal is native, § 137.31(a) bars one taken from the state's wild and § 137.1(a)(11) bars one captive bred elsewhere. New York City's Health Code § 161.01(b)(5) prohibits "All Procyonidae: All raccoon (eastern, desert, ring-tailed cat), kinkajou, cacomistle, cat-bear, panda and coatimundi", and Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(3)(B)(xxi) makes "a member within the family Procyonidae (including, but not limited to, common raccoon)" a Category Two animal, open only to institutions.

## The Native-Wildlife Bars

Ten more jurisdictions ban it without naming the family, by treating the raccoon as the wild animal it is. Georgia's Department of Natural Resources lists the raccoon among native species that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology", and O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues wild animal licenses only to the animal trade and public exhibitors. Rhode Island names "Raccoon (Procyon lotor)" on its list of native mammals whose possession as pets is prohibited, and applies that "regardless of where the animal originated". Virginia's § 29.1-521(A)(10) bars possessing any wild animal except as specifically permitted, and the only permits are for exhibits and scientific collection, the same reasoning that bars the [flying squirrel](/blog/flying-squirrel-legal-guide/) there. New Hampshire puts the raccoon in its controlled table with permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitor category. New York lists "Raccoons in the Genus Procyon" as dangerous animals "including those which are captive bred" at 6 NYCRR § 180.1, with licenses for scientific, educational, exhibition, zoological or propagation purposes only. Maine never categorized the raccoon, and § 7.06(4) makes an uncategorized species ineligible for any permit. Alabama bars "species of raccoon" brought from outside the state and treats an Alabama raccoon as a protected wild animal. Mississippi's Rule 8.4 names it in a closed list of game and furbearing animals whose live possession "is prohibited". Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars the department from issuing any permit for a game animal "for use as a pet", and the District of Columbia's closed list of seven pet categories has nothing a raccoon fits.

## The Permit States, Sorted by What the Permit Is For

Eighteen states issue a permit, and the word hides three different answers.

The first group has a license a keeper can hold, and it is a furbearer license. Texas is the clearest, and the most misread. Parks and Wildlife Code § 71.001(1) defines a fur-bearing animal as "wild beaver, otter, mink, ring-tailed cat, badger, skunk, raccoon, muskrat, opossum, fox, or nutria", and § 71.005(c) says:

> No person may capture or possess a live fur-bearing animal for any purpose, except as otherwise authorized by this code, unless he has acquired and possesses a fur-bearing animal propagation license.

"For any purpose" makes it a keeper's license as well as a breeder's, at $50.75 under § 71.010. The word "wild" at the head of the definition is the argument that a captive-bred animal falls outside it, but the word reads as describing the species rather than the animal's history. Ohio names the raccoon as a fur-bearing animal in R.C. 1531.01(W), and § 1533.71(A)(2) offers a "noncommercial propagating license" to hold fur-bearing animals in captivity "for the licensee's own use", twenty-five dollars a year, no sales. Illinois protects "Procyon lotor" by name in 520 ILCS 5/2.2, and § 3.25 requires a fur-bearing mammal breeder permit before any individual may "hold, possess or engage in the breeding or raising of live fur-bearing mammals", $25 a year, breeding or not. Wisconsin's § 169.15(1)(b) says the department "shall issue" a Class B captive wild animal farm license to any applicant who files properly and pays, and NR 16.15(1) applies it to hobbyists. Tennessee moves native furbearers into Class II, a $10-an-animal permit. Indiana lists "Raccoon (Procyon lotor)" at 312 IAC 9-11-7(a)(8) as a Class II animal: apply within five days of possession, ten dollars, a cage inspection and a vet's statement within 45 days. West Virginia's § 20-2-51 is the cheapest permit on the map, two dollars, for a wild animal "acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season".

The second group issues a pet permit that reaches only a captive-bred animal. Michigan's Permit to Hold Game in Captivity names the raccoon on its closed list and covers bred animals only. New Jersey's N.J.A.C. 7:25-10.7(a)1 offers an Individual Hobby permit "to persons holding game animals for hobby purposes or as pets", 10.8(a)1 requires the animal not to be of wild origin, and the Division's rabies vector policy bars importing a raccoon from any state carrying a rabies variant other than the raccoon variant, even from a breeder. Nebraska lists "Raccoon - (Procyon lotor)" among the wild mammals that may be kept under a Captive Wildlife Permit, bars issuing one for an animal taken from the wild, and requires proof it came from a licensed breeder. Oklahoma exempts a pet that is "legally obtained wildlife from a source other than the wild", with the import permit and noncommercial breeder's license on top. Florida's answer is a free Class III personal use permit under 68A-6.003(8), closed to the wild animal by 68A-6.003(13): "Fox, skunks, bats, raccoons, or whitetail deer taken from the wild shall not be possessed as personal use wildlife". Minnesota's § 97A.401 subd. 3 lets the commissioner issue special permits without a fee "to take, possess, and transport wild animals as pets", on conditions the commissioner sets. Missouri's Wildlife Hobby Permit allows one game mammal "purchased from a Missouri wildlife breeder permit holder", never propagated or sold, and 3 CSR 10-9.223(2) bars importing a live raccoon, so the animal has to be Missouri bred. South Carolina names the raccoon as a furbearer and requires a Department permit to bring one into the state.

The third group is a permit in name only. Massachusetts and North Carolina each have a license in the statute and an agency that will not issue it for a pet. MassWildlife says permits for non-exempt wildlife "are issued only for certain scientific, educational, commercial, or other specific reasons" and that "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) tells the Executive Director not to issue a captivity license unless the animal "was not acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet". Vermont's license sits outside the group: Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue one to a keeper on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard.

## Three Conditions, Pointing Two Ways

Iowa, Arkansas and Wyoming allow a raccoon without a permit, each on a condition, and the conditions contradict each other. Iowa Code § 481A.61(1) reads:

> Except as otherwise provided by law, a licensed game breeder whose original stock is obtained from a lawful source may possess any game bird, game animal, or fur-bearing animal, or any of their parts. Possession and use of the game birds, game animals, or fur-bearing animals obtained from a licensed game breeder are lawful.

The raccoon is one of Iowa's named fur-bearing animals, § 481A.38 bars possessing one except as the chapter provides, and that sentence is what the chapter provides: possession is lawful when the animal came from an Iowa-licensed game breeder, and § 481A.61(4) bars that breeder from stocking with animals taken from the Iowa wild. So in Iowa the only lawful pet raccoon is a bred one.

Arkansas and Wyoming turn that around. Arkansas Code 09.14(A)(1)(i) lets a household keep "no more than 6 per household of any combination" of hand-captured opossum, rabbit, raccoon and squirrel, with no sale and no removal from the state, while Addendum R1.03 says the Commission will not issue breeding, sale or importation permits for the raccoon, which closes the captive-born route completely. Wyoming's statute names the raccoon a "predatory animal", and Chapter 10 § 14(b) says such animals "may be possessed without a permit if captured in the state, but no importation is allowed". In both states the breeder's raccoon is the illegal one and the one from the woods is lawful, which is the reverse of Iowa, Florida and Nebraska and the reason no single sentence about captive breeding is true across the map.

## Where the Text Will Not Say

Three states are gray on the map rather than guessed at. Oregon's OAR 635-044-0460(1) says raccoons in captivity "require a Wildlife Holding or Wildlife Exhibitor/Animal Entertainment Industry or Wildlife Sanctuary Permit", 0460(6)(a) caps a Wildlife Holding Permit at two raccoons, and 0460(4) requires the animal to come from an out-of-state USDA breeder, all of which reads as a route for an individual. Against that, 0450(3) says an exhibitor or sanctuary permit "is required to hold species listed in 635-044-0460", 0470 is headed as the grandfathering of raccoons "legally held as pets" on 20 January 2017, and 0460(4)(d) bars offering a raccoon for sale as a household pet in Oregon. A pet raccoon acquired since 2017 sits between those sentences. Kansas bars possessing any wildlife unless a regulation permits it, and the only regulation that permits a live furbearer, K.A.R. 115-5-2(b), lets a Kansas-caught animal be held "only through the last day of the season in which taken"; whether the statutory exception for animals "legally taken outside this state" reaches one bred in captivity is a question no Kansas text answers. Louisiana classes the raccoon as a nongame quadruped, and the only rule that speaks to keeping one is the wildlife rescuer rule at LAC 76:V.131.D.3, under which a sick, injured or orphaned raccoon may be held for ninety days on notice to the department and kept for life only on a special purpose permit after a vet finds it non-releasable. Nothing read either bars or allows a captive-bred raccoon bought as a pet. In all three, ask the agency in writing before buying, and keep the answer.

## The Vaccine Problem Underneath All of It

Every rabies-vector rule on this page rests on one fact that no state can legislate around: there is no USDA-licensed rabies vaccine for the raccoon. A veterinarian can give one a dog or cat vaccine off label, but no health department can treat that animal as vaccinated, so if a pet raccoon bites someone the protocol for an unvaccinated wild carnivore applies, and it ends with euthanasia and testing of the brain, in the permit states as much as the ban states. It is the same gap that decides the [fennec fox](/blog/fennec-fox-legal-guide/), a Saharan animal caught by the word "fox" in most of these clauses, and it is why Rhode Island restricts every carnivore without a licensed vaccine to researchers, exhibitors and AZA facilities.

## The Takeaway

In 26 jurisdictions there is no lawful pet raccoon, and in most of them the reason is rabies rather than anything about the animal's temperament. In 16 states a keeper can hold a permit, nearly always a furbearer or game license that costs between two and fifty dollars, and in eight of those it covers only a captive-bred animal. Iowa allows a bred one from a licensed breeder; Arkansas and Wyoming allow only one caught in state. South Dakota alone has no rule. Wherever the answer is yes, the county or city ordinance is the next thing to read, because Texas, Ohio, Illinois, Michigan, Nevada and Kentucky all say in terms that local government may prohibit what the state allows, and raccoons appear on municipal exotic animal lists far more often than on state ones.
