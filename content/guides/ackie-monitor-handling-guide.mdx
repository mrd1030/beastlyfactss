# How to Handle an Ackie Monitor

Ackie monitors are a different kind of lizard to interact with, compared to the sit-and-bask species already covered on this site. If you've kept a bearded dragon or a blue-tongued skink, expect this species to feel like a completely different level of energy.

## What Makes This Species Different

Ackies are active, bold, intelligent, and strongly food-motivated, keepers frequently describe them doing something close to backflips while chasing crickets. Where a [bearded dragon](/blog/bearded-dragon-handling-guide/) tolerates passive handling and a [blue-tongued skink](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-handling-guide/) stays calm and ground-bound, an ackie is busy, curious, and constantly investigating its environment. This is a genuinely more engaging lizard to keep, but it's also a more demanding one.

For everything that energy demands of a keeper, see our complete [Ackie Monitor care guide](/guides/ackie-monitor/).

## The Right Way to Build Trust

Never grab an ackie from above or chase it out of a hide, this triggers a real prey-flight response rather than building trust. Let the monitor voluntarily climb onto you instead, and use tong-feeding as your main bonding tool, offering a feeder insect as a reward for approaching or climbing on calmly. Tame ackies become interactive over time, but they're also fast and can scratch or nip with sharp teeth and claws if startled or handled carelessly.

<FunFact>
Keepers frequently describe ackie monitors doing something close to backflips while chasing down a cricket. It's a level of food-driven energy that's hard to picture until you've actually watched a lizard do it, and it's a big part of why this species reads as so much more interactive than a typical pet lizard.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Undersizing the enclosure, which leaves an animal this active with nowhere to burn energy.
- Providing shallow substrate that can't hold a proper burrow, undermining a core part of this species' natural behavior.
- Chasing or grabbing to force handling instead of letting the monitor approach on its own terms.
- Overfeeding into obesity, easy to do with such a food-motivated animal.

## Reading an Ackie Before It Bolts

An ackie shows fear with its whole body. ReptiFiles' body-language notes for the species cover the ones a keeper sees at the enclosure:

- **Bolting.** The lizard runs for the nearest hide. That is fear. Leave it in the hide and wait.
- **Tail whipping.** A quick lash at your hand, the same move it would use on a predator. Pull the hand back and try later.
- **Closed eyes.** An ackie that shuts its eyes with you in the enclosure is hoping you lose interest. ReptiFiles reads it as stress and a signal to back off.
- **Tongue flicks.** Slow flicks usually mean ordinary interest. Rapid flicks mean heightened interest, usually food or a mate. Dr. Eric Los Kamp of Winter Park Veterinary Hospital notes that bites often come from a finger mistaken for food, and he watches for long, unhurried flicks as the sign of a curious ackie.
- **Biting.** ReptiFiles calls it a last resort that ackies rarely use on a human, and reads it as usually fear that has escalated. Los Kamp adds that the bite is still powerful for the size and the lizard often will not let go willingly.

Climbing you is the opposite signal. ReptiFiles' picture of a trusting ackie is one that runs up an outstretched arm on its own. Keep the arm out in front and stop the lizard at the elbow, because an ackie will jump from a high point like your head. Expect fine scratches from the nails, which the site calls normal, and keep a gentle hold unless you are certain it will not bolt: an ackie that decides to run is fast enough that a loose grip means a lost lizard.

ReptiFiles passes on a recommendation of at least 30 minutes of presence in the enclosure per visit: a juvenile will try to wait you out, and leaving early teaches it that hiding makes you go away. Force handling stays a last resort.

## How This Compares to More Sedentary Lizards

This is the central fact to understand before choosing an ackie. Bearded dragons and blue-tongued skinks are calm, largely stationary lizards well suited to relatively passive interaction. An ackie is neither calm nor stationary, it's an active, intelligent predator that needs genuine enrichment and space to express that energy. That's exactly why ackies are classed as an intermediate reptile rather than a true beginner one, the temperament itself isn't difficult, but the husbandry demands that come with supporting it are considerably higher.
