# How to Handle an Ackie Monitor

Ackie monitors are a different kind of lizard to interact with than the sit-and-bask species most keepers meet first. If you've kept a bearded dragon or a blue-tongued skink, expect this species to feel like a completely different level of energy.

## What Makes This Species Different

Ackies are active, bold, intelligent, and strongly food-motivated. Where a [bearded dragon](/blog/bearded-dragon-handling-guide/) tolerates passive handling and a [blue-tongued skink](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-handling-guide/) stays calm and ground-bound, an ackie is busy, curious, and constantly investigating its environment. This is a genuinely more engaging lizard to keep, but it's also a more demanding one.

## The Right Way to Build Trust

Never grab an ackie from above or chase it out of a hide, this triggers a real prey-flight response rather than building trust. Let the monitor voluntarily climb onto you instead, and use tong-feeding as your main bonding tool, offering a feeder insect as a reward for approaching or climbing on calmly. Tame ackies become interactive over time, but they're also fast and can scratch or nip with sharp teeth and claws if startled or handled carelessly.

<FunFact>
A wild ackie digs burrows as deep as about 1.3 feet. When it retreats into one, it inflates its lungs and plugs the entrance behind it with its spiny tail as a defense.
</FunFact>

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Undersizing the enclosure, which leaves an animal this active with nowhere to burn energy.
- Providing shallow substrate that can't hold a proper burrow, undermining a core part of this species' natural behavior.
- Chasing or grabbing to force handling instead of letting the monitor approach on its own terms.
- Overfeeding into obesity, easy to do with such a food-motivated animal.

## Reading an Ackie Before It Bolts

An ackie shows fear with its whole body, and these are the signs a keeper sees at the enclosure:

- **Bolting.** The lizard runs for the nearest hide. That is fear. Leave it in the hide and wait.
- **Tail whipping.** A quick lash at your hand, the same move it would use on a predator. Pull the hand back and try later.
- **Closed eyes.** An ackie that shuts its eyes with you in the enclosure is hoping you lose interest. It is stress and a signal to back off.
- **Tongue flicks.** Slow flicks usually mean ordinary interest. Rapid flicks mean heightened interest, usually food or a mate. Bites often come from a finger mistaken for food, and long, unhurried flicks are the sign of a curious ackie.
- **Biting.** A last resort that ackies rarely use on a human, and usually fear that has escalated. The bite is still powerful for the size and the lizard often will not let go willingly.

Climbing you is the opposite signal. A trusting ackie runs up an outstretched arm on its own. Keep the arm out in front and stop the lizard at the elbow, because an ackie will jump from a high point like your head. Expect fine scratches from the nails, which are normal, and keep a gentle hold unless you are certain it will not bolt: an ackie that decides to run is fast enough that a loose grip means a lost lizard.

Plan on at least 30 minutes of presence in the enclosure per visit: a juvenile will try to wait you out, and leaving early teaches it that hiding makes you go away. Force handling stays a last resort.

## Telling Males From Females

Ackies are hard to sex by eye. In several monitor species males tend to grow larger, with bigger heads and more obvious femoral pores, but those are tendencies, not a test. A reptile vet has firmer methods: contrast dye radiography, an X-ray for the small bones some male monitors carry in their hemipenes (an animal with none showing is not proven female), and probing. Popping the hemipenes by pressing on the tail base can injure the lizard, and probing done wrong can too, so neither is a home job.

## How This Compares to More Sedentary Lizards

This is the central fact to understand before choosing an ackie. Bearded dragons and blue-tongued skinks are calm, largely stationary lizards well suited to relatively passive interaction. An ackie is neither calm nor stationary, it's an active, intelligent predator that needs genuine enrichment and space to express that energy. That's exactly why ackies are classed as an intermediate reptile rather than a true beginner one, the temperament itself isn't difficult, but the husbandry demands that come with supporting it are considerably higher.

If you've handled either of those calmer species and are considering the jump, the contrast is the point: a bearded dragon or a blue-tongued skink will sit still for a session, and an ackie spends one investigating.
