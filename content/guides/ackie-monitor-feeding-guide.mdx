# Ackie Monitor Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why They Stop Eating

Ackie monitors are lean, active insectivores, and getting their diet right is really about resisting the urge to feed them like they're a bigger, meatier lizard than they actually are. For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Ackie Monitor care guide](/guides/ackie-monitor/).

## Feeding Schedule by Age

- **Hatchlings and juveniles (up to about 6 months):** Fed daily, allowed to eat as much as they want, they need the calories to fuel rapid growth.
- **Subadults and adults (6+ months):** Every other day, offering roughly what your ackie can finish within a 5 to 10 minute window rather than leaving food available indefinitely.
- **Reproductive females** may be allowed to eat more freely during breeding cycles.

Small, frequent meals are genuinely healthier for this species than large, occasional ones.

## What Ackie Monitors Actually Eat

This species is primarily insectivorous, with a smaller amount of lizard prey rounding out its natural diet. Build the captive diet around well-gutloaded insects: black soldier fly larvae, crickets, discoid roaches, dubia roaches, grasshoppers, and silkworms all work well as regular staples. Mealworms and superworms are fine as occasional additions but shouldn't anchor the diet, they're higher in fat and have harder exoskeletons. Isopods and hornworms are useful too, and notably don't need calcium dusting since they're naturally high in it already. Gutload every feeder insect at least 24 hours before offering it, and size prey to roughly the width of your ackie's head, not bigger.

Lightly dust most insect feeders with a reptile calcium powder at each feeding, skipping isopods and hornworms since they're already calcium-rich. Use a reptile multivitamin containing true vitamin A, not just beta-carotene, occasionally rather than on a fixed weekly schedule, over-supplementing vitamins is a real risk with this species, so when in doubt, use less rather than more. If your UVB setup is solid, covered in the [tank setup guide](/blog/ackie-monitor-tank-setup-guide/), you generally don't need a separate D3 supplement. Inconsistent calcium dusting is one of the more common feeding mistakes with this species, and it opens the door to metabolic bone disease even when UVB is otherwise adequate.

## Foods to Avoid

The biggest one: don't make rodents a dietary staple. Mice and rats are calorie-dense, and a rodent-heavy diet is directly linked to the fatty liver disease that shortens more captive monitor lifespans than almost anything else, arguably the single biggest feeding mistake with this species. Eggs, quail chicks, and young mice or rats are fine as occasional treats, genuinely sparingly, not a weekly habit. Skip dog or cat food entirely, and don't offer fruits or vegetables, ackies are carnivores and get no real benefit from plant matter.

The second most common mistake is simply overfeeding adults. Ackies have voracious appetites even once they've stopped growing, and free-feeding an adult the way you'd feed a juvenile leads to obesity.

## Why Ackie Monitors Stop Eating

1. **Dehydration.** A leading cause of health problems in hatchlings specifically, showing up alongside sunken eyes and wrinkled, inelastic skin.
2. **Shedding.** A common, temporary appetite dip in younger ackies from the stress and physiological changes of a shed.
3. **Stress or boredom.** An under-enriched enclosure or a recent change in environment can suppress appetite even in an otherwise healthy animal.
4. **An incorrect basking temperature.** Like any ectotherm, an ackie that isn't warm enough can't digest properly, and appetite drops accordingly.
5. **Parasites.** Internal or external parasites commonly show up as decreased appetite alongside lethargy, weight loss, and irregular stool.
6. **Illness.** Metabolic bone disease, mouth rot, respiratory infection, and gastrointestinal impaction from substrate ingestion are all genuine medical causes of appetite loss.

## When to Actually Worry

We couldn't find a single, consistently sourced number for how many days an ackie monitor can safely go without eating, and would rather say that honestly than invent one. What's well documented instead is the troubleshooting order every reptile source points to: check temperature and hydration before assuming illness, since an incorrect basking temperature or a dehydrated hatchling can suppress appetite entirely on its own.

See a vet if appetite loss comes with lethargy, sunken eyes, wrinkled or tented skin, irregular or absent stool, or any visible weight loss, and don't wait out a persistent refusal in a hatchling given how little margin young, fast-growing animals have.
