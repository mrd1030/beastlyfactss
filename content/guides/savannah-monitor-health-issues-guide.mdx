# Common Savannah Monitor Health Issues (Obesity Is the Defining One)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Nearly everything on this list traces back to one root cause more than in almost any other pet reptile: a mismatch between how much this species gets fed in captivity and how much it would eat and burn off in the wild.

## Obesity and Hepatic Lipidosis (Fatty Liver Disease)

In the wild, savannah monitors feast and fast with the seasons: they eat heavily through a wet season of about eight months, taking up to a tenth of their body weight in a day, and live off stored fat through the dry one. Captivity removes the fast. The animal gets rich food year-round with a fraction of the activity it would have in the wild, and the fat that was meant to be burned in the dry months builds up in the liver instead.

## Body Condition: What the Numbers Look Like

Weight alone does not tell you whether a monitor is fat, because adults range so widely in size, from about 3 to 4 feet on average, and some well past that. Body condition does. Vets score reptiles on a 1 to 9 scale, where 1 is emaciated, 5 is normal, and 9 is obese, and the target is a 5.

- **Score 5, normal:** a tail about as thick as the body where it joins, with neither bones showing through the skin nor fat hiding the spine and ribs.
- **Too thin, 1 to 3:** the pelvis, hips, ribs, and spine stand out through the skin, the tail thins, and the eyes look sunken.
- **Too heavy, 7 to 9:** a thick layer of fat over the back and sides so the spine and ribs cannot be felt, fat jowls under the neck, a pear-shaped torso, and a tail base wider than the body.

Score the monitor the same way every month and weigh it on a scale at the same time. The number that matters is the trend: a monitor gaining weight while its score climbs past 5 is eating more than it burns, and the fix is fewer meals, leaner food, and more room to move. Rodents belong in the diet only occasionally for exactly this reason. Advanced fatty liver disease needs a vet, and by then the damage may not be fully reversible.

<FunFact>
Wild savannah monitors are built for famine. They gorge through the rainy season and fast through the dry one on stored fat, which is why a captive animal fed the same way every week of the year ends up in trouble.
</FunFact>

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Caused by too little calcium or vitamin D3, too much phosphorus, or no <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TipZXE" product="UVB - Arcadia Desert 12% (ProT5 kit, 22in/24W)">UVB</AffiliateLink>. The body pulls calcium out of the skeleton to keep blood levels up, and the signs follow from that: bowed legs, a shortened snout, stunted growth, and fragile bones that fracture. As it progresses, the monitor becomes weak, lethargic, and stops eating. Improved husbandry and long-term calcium under a vet's supervision reverses most cases; severe ones need hospitalization.

## Parasites

Common in this species, particularly wild-caught or farmed animals, and feeder insects can carry them in too. Pinworms, coccidia, and flagellated protozoa are the usual finds. A small load may cause no trouble, but an overgrowth brings poor nutrient absorption, appetite loss, lethargy, and abnormal stool. A new monitor gets a fecal exam before any symptom shows, and every monitor gets one yearly after that.

Antibiotics from a vet clear the infection; correcting the enclosure keeps it from coming back.

## Thermal Burns

A risk that comes with the high basking temperatures this species needs. Burns come from hot rocks and other in-enclosure heaters, and from heat pads or bulbs running without a thermostat. They heal slowly and need long-term treatment to keep them from becoming infected, so every heat source goes on a thermostat and out of the lizard's reach.

## Finding a Vet Before You Need One

The name of a good local herp vet is part of the equipment list for this species, and it should be settled before the monitor comes home. A savannah monitor is a large, strong lizard, and a routine nail trim can take two or three people trained in reptile restraint, so ask whether the practice sees large monitors, not just reptiles in general. The [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) covers how to find and vet a reptile practice, and the first visit should include the fecal test. Plan on regular checkups after that, where the vet can track weight and adjust the feeding schedule with you.

## The Husbandry Pattern

Overfeeding relative to this species' activity level drives the obesity and fatty liver disease that shorten more savannah monitor lives than any other single factor. Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD. An unregulated heat source causes burns. And an unscreened, farmed, or wild-caught animal brings parasites home with it. The one habit that prevents the most here is resisting the urge to feed generously just because the monitor is always eager to eat.
