# Common Savannah Monitor Health Issues (Obesity Is the Defining One)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Nearly everything on this list traces back to one root cause more than any other pet reptile covered on this site: a genuine mismatch between how much this species gets fed in captivity and how much it would eat and burn off in the wild.

## Obesity and Hepatic Lipidosis (Fatty Liver Disease)

This is the signature savannah monitor health problem, and it's worth understanding why it happens so often. In the wild, savannah monitors eat a lean, largely insect-based diet on a seasonal cycle, active and hunting for much of the year, then far less active during dry periods. In captivity, that natural rhythm disappears, animals get fed rich food year-round with a fraction of the activity level they'd have in the wild.

Watch for a rounded, heavy body shape, reduced activity, and general lethargy. Manageable at home through genuine diet correction and encouraging exercise via a properly large enclosure, but advanced liver or organ disease needs a vet, and by that point the damage may not be fully reversible.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Soft or deformed bones and fractures, from inadequate UVB or calcium supplementation. ## Parasites

Genuinely common in this species, particularly wild-caught or farmed animals. See a vet for a fecal exam and appropriate treatment, this is worth doing proactively for any newly acquired savannah monitor rather than waiting for symptoms.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Overfeeding relative to this species' actual activity level is directly responsible for the obesity and fatty liver disease that shortens more savannah monitor lives than any other single factor. Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD. And an unscreened source animal brings parasite risk home with it. If there's one thing to get right with this species above all else, it's resisting the urge to feed generously just because your monitor is always eager to eat.
