# How to Handle a Fire Skink (This Isn't the Blue-Tongued Skink)

Fire skinks and blue-tongued skinks share a family name and not much else temperamentally. If you've kept the docile, handleable blue-tongued skink, expect something genuinely different here.

## Shy, fast, and quick to hide, not aggressive

Fire skinks aren't defensive or bitey in any meaningful sense, but they are notably skittish, fast-moving, and prone to burrowing out of sight the moment they feel exposed. Wild-caught animals especially stay flighty, while captive-bred individuals tame more readily with patient, gradual handling. A skink that takes insects from tongs has settled at feeding time, which is a separate thing from being held. Even settled fire skinks tend to stay squirmy and quick rather than becoming the sit-still, seemingly-enjoys-it handling experience a [blue-tongued skink](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-handling-guide/) can offer.

## The handling risks are minor but real

An occasional hard bite if cornered, and like many lizards, tail autotomy under grasping pressure. Never grab or restrain by the tail. Given how fast and squirmy this species is, handle low over a soft surface as a sensible precaution against an accidental drop or escape.

<FunFact>
Give a fire skink a shallow scattering of substrate and it vanishes into a corner under a hide. Give it the 4 to 6 inches of a soil-and-fiber mix it can actually tunnel through, and the same animal builds a burrow system, comes out to bask, and behaves like a completely different lizard, substrate depth changes this species' visible behavior more than almost any other single factor.
</FunFact>

## First-timer mistakes

- Expecting a lap lizard. This is a display animal that can learn to tolerate a hand, and individuals vary widely in how far they get.
- Under-furnishing the enclosure. A bare tank leaves this naturally shy species perpetually hidden and stressed, exactly the wrong setup for a burrowing animal that needs to feel secure before it'll ever be visible or calm.
- Keeping substrate too dry, which compounds the skittishness and creates real health risk.
- Grabbing the tail.

## Telling Males From Females

Fire skinks are among the hardest lizards in the hobby to sex, and there is no single clear marker. Males are usually a little bulkier, with a flatter, wider head and heavier jaws. Females are usually slimmer through the body but wider at the hips, with a shorter, rounder snout, and within one population they tend to be duller in color. Those differences are subtle enough that only a practiced eye reads them with confidence. Probing, the method used on snakes, is never done on a fire skink: the animals are highly strung, bolt under restraint, and the chance of injuring one is high.

That difficulty explains most failed breeding attempts. Fire skinks that are a true pair tend to breed readily, and a "pair" that never produces eggs is usually two animals of the same sex. A male and female can share an enclosure if there is room, but both need watching through the breeding season to make sure each is eating and neither is fighting. Two males never share.

## If a Pair Breeds

Fire skinks mature at about three years. Mating follows a cooler spell, around 64°F, and a female lays roughly 6 to 10 eggs into the substrate or a laying box, and may guard them. Incubated on damp medium at about 84°F, they hatch in around 55 days. Hatchlings are prone to metabolic bone disease, so they need generous feeding with calcium supplementation, humid housing, and ideally separate enclosures of their own. A gravid female eats more than usual and benefits from a varied diet while she carries eggs.
