# How to Handle a Fire Skink (This Isn't the Blue-Tongued Skink)

Fire skinks and blue-tongued skinks share a family name and not much else temperamentally. If you've kept the docile, handleable blue-tongued skink already covered on this site, expect something genuinely different here.

## Shy, fast, and quick to hide, not aggressive

Fire skinks aren't defensive or bitey in any meaningful sense, but they are notably skittish, fast-moving, and prone to burrowing out of sight the moment they feel exposed. Wild-caught animals especially stay flighty, while captive-bred individuals tame more readily with patient, gradual handling, more on sourcing in the [cost guide](/blog/fire-skink-cost-guide/). Even settled fire skinks tend to stay squirmy and quick rather than becoming the sit-still, seemingly-enjoys-it handling experience a blue-tongued skink can offer.

The full care picture is in our complete [Fire Skink care guide](/guides/fire-skink/), and our [blue-tongued skink handling guide](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-handling-guide/) is worth reading beside it for the contrast.

## The handling risks are minor but real

An occasional hard bite if cornered, and like many lizards, tail autotomy under grasping pressure. Never grab or restrain by the tail. Given how fast and squirmy this species is, handle low over a soft surface as a sensible precaution against an accidental drop or escape.

<FunFact>
Give a fire skink four inches of substrate and it vanishes into a corner under a hide. Give it eight to ten inches of a soil-and-fiber mix it can actually tunnel through, and the same animal builds a burrow system, comes out to bask, and behaves like a completely different lizard, substrate depth changes this species' visible behavior more than almost any other single factor.
</FunFact>

## First-timer mistakes

- Buying a fire skink expecting the same docile, cuddly relationship the blue-tongued skink offers, this is genuinely a different kind of pet.
- Under-furnishing the enclosure. A bare tank leaves this naturally shy species perpetually hidden and stressed, exactly the wrong setup for a burrowing animal that needs to feel secure before it'll ever be visible or calm.
- Keeping substrate too dry, which compounds the skittishness and creates real health risk, covered in the [health issues guide](/blog/fire-skink-health-issues-guide/).
- Grabbing the tail.

## The honest comparison

Both are skinks, and that's about where the similarity ends. Blue-tongued skinks are larger, drier-climate, omnivorous, and personable, tolerant of and often seeming to enjoy interaction. Fire skinks are smaller, humidity-loving, insectivorous, and fossorial, closer to a beautiful display animal you occasionally get to interact with than a lizard built for regular handling. Go in expecting the latter, and this is a genuinely rewarding species to keep.
