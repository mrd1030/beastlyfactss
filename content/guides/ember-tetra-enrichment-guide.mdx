# Ember Tetra Enrichment: Bright Orange, Built to Vanish

<AffiliateDisclosure />

An ember tetra is a bright orange fish about 0.8 inch long, and seen from above, in its own river, it is hard to spot at all.

<KeyTakeaway>
Ember tetras live as a school in clear, well-lit water beside dense plants, where predators hunt close by. Enrichment means giving them that setup: **a school of 10 or more**, **heavy planting with floating plants and leaf litter**, calm tankmates, and **daily small live or frozen food**.
</KeyTakeaway>

## How They Live in the Wild

The ember tetra comes from the Araguaia River basin in central Brazil, and the type specimens came from the Rio das Mortes. The species is presumed to live in minor tributaries, backwaters and oxbow lakes, with soft, weakly acidic water, rather than in the main channels. Very little has been written about its habitat.

The one published field account comes from near Aruanã, on the Araguaia in Goiás. The water there was clear, and with little overhead cover, a lot of light reached it. Stands of Eichhornia, Cabomba, Ludwigia and flooded grasses lined the site, and the middle of the channel was mostly open, with leaf litter over clay, sand and fine gravel.

From the bank, the orange fish were difficult to see. When cloud covered the sun, their dark backs blended into the dim water. The school shifted its position every few minutes, and the reason turned up soon after: pike cichlids were creeping through the tall Eichhornia.

That picture is the brief for the tank. A group that keeps moving, close to dense cover, with open water to move through.

<FunFact>
Brazilians near the Araguaia call ember tetras foguinhos, which means "little fires." The scientific name, Hyphessobrycon amandae, honors Amanda Bleher (1910 to 1991), a German limnologist whose son, Heiko Bleher, collected the fish that the species was described from in 1987.
</FunFact>

## The School: Ten or More

No one has studied group size in ember tetras. The recommended purchase in the care references is a minimum of 8 to 10, so the target is 10 or more of this one species.

The closest research is on a different fish. A home aquarium study on [neon tetras and three other species](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) found that the neons were less aggressive and darted less in larger groups, and that groups of 10 shoaled the most. That result belongs to neons, not embers. It agrees with the references, though, and it fits the field account, where the school's whole defense was moving together.

## Dense Plants, Floating Plants, Leaf Litter

Ember tetras should be kept in a heavily planted tank, ideally over a dark substrate. Plant thickly along the back and sides, the way the stands lined the field site, and leave the middle open for the school to cross.

Floating plants are a useful addition. No source gives a lighting figure for this species, and the wild fish lived in bright, clear water, so a well-lit tank is not wrong. What the wild school relied on was cover close at hand.

Leaf litter is optional, and it does more than decorate: as the leaves decompose, they feed the growth of microbe colonies in the tank.

The water behind all of it is soft and acidic: pH 5.0 to 7.0, 1 to 10 dGH, and 75 to 82°F with gentle filtration. [GH and pH are separate measurements](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/), so test the tap water for both before buying fish. The 10-gallon, 18 x 12 inch build for a school of 10 is in the [ember tetra tank setup guide](/blog/ember-tetra-tank-setup-guide/).

## Calm Tankmates

Ember tetras are very peaceful, and they do not compete well with boisterous or much larger fish. Good companions are similar-sized, peaceful fish. Embers are also an ideal dither fish for Apistogramma and other dwarf cichlids.

With dwarf shrimp, embers leave the adults alone but will eat shrimplets.

## Feeding as Enrichment

In the wild, ember tetras are probably micropredators of tiny invertebrates and zooplankton. Small live and frozen foods are the closest a tank comes to that diet, and they give the school something to chase.

Offer small live or frozen food daily, such as baby brine shrimp and moina, alongside a dried staple sized for the fish: <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004PBB5TK?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Semi-Floating Micro Pellets, 0.77 oz">micro-pellets</AffiliateLink> or flake crushed fine. Their mouths are small, so the food has to be tiny and slow-sinking. How often to feed, and how to size a pinch the school can clear, is the job of the [ember tetra feeding guide](/blog/ember-tetra-feeding-guide/).

## Priority Order

1. A school of 10 or more ember tetras, one species
2. Heavy planting at the back and sides over a dark substrate, open water in the middle
3. Floating plants
4. Soft, acidic water at 75 to 82°F with gentle filtration
5. Peaceful, similar-sized tankmates, or dwarf cichlids
6. Daily small live or frozen food alongside a tiny dried staple
7. Leaf litter

## What Not to Do

Do not buy five because the tank looks small. Do not keep them with boisterous or much larger fish. Do not leave the tank bare and open. Do not run the heater above 82°F, the top of every main reference's range. Do not skip a [30-day quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) for new fish.

In the Araguaia, an ember's safety was the school around it and the plants beside it. A tank that supplies both covers most of what this fish needs.
