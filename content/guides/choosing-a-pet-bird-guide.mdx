# Choosing a Pet Bird: What You're Actually Signing Up For

<VetDisclaimer />

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most of the decisions that make or break a bird-owning household happen before the bird does. Which species barely matters next to three other questions: how long the bird will live, whether it was hand-raised or parent-raised, and where it's actually coming from. Get those three right and almost any species works out. Get them wrong and even an easy species turns into a rehoming case a year in. This guide walks through all three, plus what to actually look for in the cage in front of you, whether that cage belongs to a breeder, a shop, or a rescue.

## What You're Actually Committing To

### Lifespan Is the Commitment People Underestimate

Ask someone how long a pet bird lives and the guess is usually a few years, something in dog-or-cat territory. It's off by a lot, and it's off in a direction that matters, because a bird bought for a child or bought on impulse can easily outlive the household it was bought into.

VCA Animal Hospitals lists a cockatiel's life span at 10 to 14 years, with a documented maximum of 24. That's already longer than most people plan for a "starter" bird. Move up to a cockatoo and VCA puts the number at 25 to 45 years, with the larger cockatoo species living 70 to 80 years or more. A cockatoo bought by a 30-year-old can plausibly still be alive when that person is in their 90s, and somebody has to plan for what happens to the bird after that. This isn't a pet you casually rehome partway through, and it isn't one you buy assuming a shorter run than the species is actually capable of.

The practical takeaway: match the lifespan to your own life stage and your household's long-term stability, not just to how cute or affordable the bird is right now.

### Noise: What's Actually Documented, and What Isn't

This is the question apartment dwellers and light sleepers most need answered honestly, especially before a large parrot, and it deserves a straight answer instead of a made-up number.

A specific decibel figure circulates constantly online for cockatoos and other large parrots. This article looked for a measured sound-pressure figure from an acoustic, veterinary, or academic source and did not find one worth citing. The numbers that get repeated do not trace back to any study, so none appear here. Naming that gap honestly is more useful than repeating a figure nobody can actually back up.

What is documented is the behavior itself. VCA Animal Hospitals states plainly that cockatoos may scream often and are not recommended for noise-sensitive individuals, and that they scream for attention when left alone even in another part of the house. Lafeber's bird behavior resources describe a broader pattern across pet birds generally: most are most vocal at dawn and dusk, a pattern rooted in wild contact calling, where a bird calls loudly expecting a flock member to call back, confirming that both are safe. In captivity, a bird separated from its "flock" (you, in another room) doesn't stop running that instinct just because it lives indoors.

The other honest point, also from Lafeber: you cannot train this away entirely. Their guidance on screaming states you can't stop a parrot from screaming entirely, since it's their nature, though you can reduce nuisance screaming by ignoring the noise and rewarding quiet. Reduction is realistic. Silence is not. If a completely quiet home is the goal, a large parrot, and especially a cockatoo, is the wrong species regardless of how the individual bird in front of you happens to be behaving on the day you meet it. Our deeper look at [why cockatoos scream and pluck feathers](/blog/cockatoo-screaming-feather-plucking-explained/) covers what makes the behavior worse or better once you already own one.

### Mess and Daily Time

Beyond noise and lifespan, budget for genuine daily time and genuine daily mess before you buy. Parrots throw food, shed feather dust, and need real out-of-cage interaction, not just a full food dish, to stay behaviorally healthy. A bird that gets an hour a day of company from a bored, distracted owner is not getting the same thing as a bird that gets an hour of deliberate attention. If your realistic daily bandwidth is closer to fifteen minutes than an hour, that's worth admitting now, not after the bird has already bonded to a household that can't keep up.

<KeyTakeaway>
Lifespan and noise are the two facts buyers most consistently get wrong before purchase. Confirm both for the actual species you're considering, not the species you assumed it would resemble, before you commit to anything else on this list.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Hand-Raised Versus Parent-Raised

This is the single decision our own [lovebird cost guide](/blog/lovebird-cost-guide/) names as the one that sets how tame a bird becomes, and it deserves its own full explanation rather than a one-line mention.

### What Each Term Means

A hand-raised bird is pulled from the nest partway through development and fed by a human, usually with a syringe or spoon, rather than left with its parents to fledge naturally. A parent-raised bird stays in the nest with its parents through weaning and only meets people afterward, sometimes with regular handling worked in during that period, sometimes not.

### The Tameness Tradeoff

VCA's cockatiel guidance is direct about the upside: hand-raised babies often make better pets, since they've been completely socialized with humans from an early age. That's the entire appeal, and it's real. A hand-raised bird typically steps up more readily, tolerates handling better, and bonds to people faster than a parent-raised one does.

But tameness isn't free, and treating it as a simple upgrade skips over what the bird actually goes through to get there.

### The Welfare Argument

A 2006 study by Rachel Schmid, Marcus Doherr and Andreas Steiger at the University of Bern, comparing the adult behavior of hand-reared and parent-reared African grey parrots, found, in the abstract published in AFA Watchbird, that hand-reared birds become imprinted on a specific human and are socially more dependent on people than parent-reared birds are. The same research links hand-rearing to a higher rate of what the authors describe as frustration-related or attention-seeking behavioral disorders, including aggression, feather picking, stereotyped movements, and abnormal sexual behavior, compared to parent-reared birds. It also cites separate research finding that hand-reared macaws, African greys, and cockatoos have significantly more trouble bonding and breeding with other birds of their own species than birds that were not hand-reared.

None of this means a hand-raised bird is doomed to a bad life. Most live out perfectly fine lives as companion animals. It means the tameness people are paying extra for is bought with a documented shift toward human-dependence and a higher baseline risk of the exact behavior problems, feather plucking and aggression among them, that later drive birds into rescues. That tradeoff is worth knowing going in rather than discovering after the fact. A parent-raised bird takes longer to tame and may never step up as readily, but it starts from a more typical developmental baseline for the species.

Where the hobby's opinion outruns the documented research: how much daily handling a parent-raised chick needs, at what age, to end up reasonably tame is genuinely debated among breeders and isn't something the cited study settles. Treat specific handling-schedule advice from an individual breeder as their practiced opinion, not settled science.

## Where to Get a Bird

Once you know roughly what you want, three sources cover almost every purchase: a breeder, a pet shop, and a rescue. VCA lists all three as standard, legitimate ways to acquire a bird, and each comes with its own questions worth asking before you commit.

### Breeders

A reputable breeder should let you see the parent birds, tell you plainly whether a chick was hand-raised or parent-raised and at what age it left the nest, and answer basic questions about diet and any health issues in their line without getting defensive. Ask how old the chick is, whether it's already weaned onto solid food, and whether you can visit in person rather than buying sight unseen and having a bird shipped.

### Pet Shops

A shop is often the easiest first contact point, but the questions matter more here, not less, because a shop's birds usually come from multiple breeders and mixing sources means mixing health histories. Ask how long the individual bird has been in the store, whether it's been examined by a vet already, and whether the store quarantines new arrivals separately from birds already on the floor. If new stock goes straight onto the sales floor next to established birds, that's a real disease-transmission risk worth asking about directly. Our [bird quarantine guide](/blog/bird-quarantine-guide/) covers what proper quarantine actually looks like once the bird is home with you, which matters regardless of where it came from.

### Rescue

Rescue is worth taking seriously as a first option rather than a fallback, and the case for it is strongest with larger parrots specifically. VCA notes that thousands of cockatoos are given up to bird rescue facilities every year, largely over the destructive or loud behavior described above. That means a rescue is often holding adult birds with a known temperament, a known health history, and, not infrequently, an already-established tameness, at a fraction of what a breeder charges for a young bird. The tradeoff is that a rescued adult may carry the anxiety or over-bonding habits of a previous bad placement, so ask the rescue directly about the bird's history and any known behavioral issues before taking it home. For a side-by-side on two commonly compared starter species, the [budgie versus cockatiel guide](/blog/budgie-vs-cockatiel-guide/) is a useful gut check on scale before you commit to a breeder or rescue search for either.

## Assessing the Bird in Front of You

Once you've picked a source, the individual bird still needs its own evaluation. This is the part most first-time buyers skip, because it's tempting to fall for the first bird that leans toward the cage bars.

### What a Healthy Bird Looks Like

VCA's description of a healthy cockatiel is a useful template across species generally: lively, alert, interactive, and attentive. Watch the whole cage before you focus on one bird. A healthy bird tracks movement around the room, responds to sound, and interacts with cage mates or its environment rather than sitting motionless in a corner. Feathers should lie smooth and the bird should be actively moving around the cage, not just perched in one spot for the entire time you're watching.

### Red Flags

The mirror image of that VCA description is the warning sign. A bird that stays fluffed up and still for an extended stretch, sits with eyes half or fully closed during normal waking hours, ignores activity around the cage, or doesn't react at all when you approach and tap gently on the bars is not the alert, interactive bird VCA describes, and it's not the one to take home that day, regardless of price or how limited the selection looks. If something about a particular bird's behavior looks off compared to every other bird in the same cage or room, ask the seller directly what they've observed rather than assuming it's just personality. None of this is subtle once you know to look for it, but it's easy to miss if you're focused on which bird seems friendliest rather than which one looks healthiest.

<FunFact>
The instinct behind a lot of unwanted noise and clingy behavior in pet parrots traces straight back to the wild: contact calling exists so flock members separated in dense cover can confirm to each other that both are still safe. A pet bird calling for you from another room is running the exact same program, just aimed at a household instead of a rainforest canopy.
</FunFact>

### The Vet Visit Isn't Optional

Treat a post-purchase veterinary exam as a standard part of the purchase, not an add-on for problem birds. VCA recommends cockatiels receive annual veterinary checkups, and scheduling the first one soon after you bring a new bird home, rather than waiting for a visible problem, is the norm avian vets actually recommend. A bird can look fine in a cage on a single visit and still be carrying something a shop, breeder, or your own eye missed entirely. A clean bill of health from an avian vet in the first week or two also gives you a real baseline to compare against later if something changes.

Choosing a pet bird well is mostly a matter of being honest with yourself before the bird comes home: about how long you're actually signing up for, about what hand-raised tameness costs the bird psychologically, about which source fits the species you want, and about what you're actually looking at in the cage. Get those four things right and the species-specific care from here, diet, enrichment, housing, is the easy part by comparison.
