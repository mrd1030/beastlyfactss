# Common Swordtail Health Issues (Including the One That Isn't a Pathogen)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ich, fin rot, dropsy, and swim bladder disorder cover most of what shows up in a swordtail tank, and water quality sits underneath nearly all of them. But this species carries one more genuine health risk that has nothing to do with a parasite or bacteria at all.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt across the body and fins, caused by the protozoan parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. The parasite burrows into skin and gills before dropping off to reproduce, and a single organism can produce hundreds of new ones, which is what makes an outbreak escalate quickly. The Merck Veterinary Manual lists treatment as copper sulfate or formalin, both available through a vet or pet store, alongside correcting whatever water quality issue let it take hold in the first place.

## Fin and Tail Rot

Ragged, frayed, or discolored fin edges, bacterial in origin and usually tied to poor water quality, ammonia buildup, or an untreated injury. Improving water quality alongside an antibacterial treatment resolves most cases caught early. With swordtails specifically, an untreated injury is worth taking seriously as a possible cause, not just poor water, since fin damage from male chasing is a real, documented trigger covered further down this page.

## Dropsy

Fluid buildup in the abdomen that makes the scales stand out in a raised, pinecone-like pattern. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes it as fluid accumulation in the abdomen tied to a systemic *Aeromonas* bacterial infection, and it's the hardest condition on this list to treat successfully, often carrying a poor outlook by the time it's obvious.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems, floating, sinking, or swimming at odd angles, usually from overfeeding or constipation. Often manageable at home with a short fast and a return to normal, appropriately portioned feeding afterward.

## The Swordtail-Specific Risk: An Unbalanced Sex Ratio

This is the condition that doesn't show up on a typical fish disease list, and it's worth treating as a genuine health topic rather than just a behavioral footnote. Male swordtails compete for females and can be persistent to the point of harassment, and a female or subordinate male fielding that attention in an unbalanced tank takes real, visible fin damage from chasing and nipping.

<FunFact>
The fin damage itself is only part of the picture. A fish under sustained social stress runs a weaker immune response, the same physiological pathway that makes a fish in poor water more likely to come down with ich or fin rot in the first place. Put plainly, an unbalanced male-to-female ratio doesn't just look unpleasant to watch, it measurably raises the odds of the pathogen-driven conditions above actually taking hold.
</FunFact>

The fix is a ratio of one male to three or four females spreads mating attempts out enough to meaningfully reduce chasing, and a tank with real planted cover gives a harassed fish somewhere to break line of sight. If you already have a fish showing chewed fins or clamped, listless behavior in a male-heavy tank, separating that individual into a recovery tank alongside correcting the ratio addresses both the injury and its underlying cause at once.
