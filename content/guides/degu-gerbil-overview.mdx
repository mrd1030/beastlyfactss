# Two Pets Where You're Really Buying a Pair

The hamster is a lesson in solitude, one per cage, always. These two small mammals teach the exact opposite lesson, and it's worth understanding before you bring just one home.

<Figure src="/assets/images/degu-gerbil-overview-1.jpg" alt="Comparison table of degu versus gerbil housing, temperature, social structure, activity pattern, defining health risk, and lifespan" caption="Same lesson, different animal: both degus and gerbils are obligately social and need a same-sex pair or group, never one alone." shareable />

## Degu: Social, Day-Active, and Prone to a Very Specific Problem

A degu left alone doesn't just get lonely, it gets genuinely stressed and starts showing it through anxious or aggressive behavior. Pair that with this species' unusual, well-documented predisposition toward diabetes, one bite of fruit isn't harmless the way it would be for most small pets, and the degu turns out to be a far more specific animal to keep well than its cute, chirpy reputation suggests.

The diabetes risk sets the menu. Degus are highly prone to the disease, so the core diet is unlimited grass hay plus a measured portion of plain degu or [chinchilla](/blog/chinchilla-feeding-guide/) pellets with no molasses, sugar or dried fruit, topped with a little leafy green or safe herb. Fruit is off the list entirely, treats included. Housing is the other surprise: a tall, multi-level cage at least 90 cm (about 3 feet) high with solid floors instead of wire, a deep digging area, wood to gnaw, and a sand or dust bath. Degus tolerate heat poorly, so the cage belongs out of direct sun in the coolest room of the house. The commitment runs 5 to 9 years, long for a rodent this size.

## Gerbil: The Other Social, Daytime Rodent

Gentle, social, and active on a schedule that matches yours. Like the degu, a solitary gerbil is a stressed gerbil, and like the degu, the setup this species needs surprises people, deep digging substrate in a real glass tank, not the shallow-bedded wire cage often marketed for small rodents.

Digging is the whole reason. Wild Mongolian gerbils build burrows 2 to 3 feet long in soft soil and spend most of their time in them, and given the chance, pet gerbils build extensive tunnel systems of their own. A family group shares one burrow in the wild, a dominant breeding pair with its offspring helping to raise the next litter, which is why a lone gerbil does so poorly. The species comes from the deserts and arid steppes of Mongolia and northern China, and it shows: gerbils resist heat stress and dehydration well and pass very little urine, so a gerbil tank needs cleaning less often than a [hamster](/blog/hamster-tank-setup-guide/) cage. The flip side of all that is a short life, 2 to 3 years.

## The Common Thread

Both of these animals get shopped for with a hamster-shaped mental model, buy one, house it alone, done. Neither one works that way. Both are obligately social, both are active during the day rather than at night, and both need a same-sex pair or small group from the start to thrive. If you're set on a solitary small pet, a hamster is the better fit. If you want two animals that interact with each other and with you, either of these is a real option, just plan for two from day one.

<FunFact>
Neither of these is a "starter pet" in the low-effort sense they're often marketed as. Both are genuinely rewarding animals once housed properly, the gap between the common version of their care and the correct version just happens to be wider than most new owners expect going in.
</FunFact>
