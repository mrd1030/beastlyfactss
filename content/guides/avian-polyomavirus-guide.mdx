# Avian Polyomavirus: Budgerigar Fledgling Disease and French Molt

<VetDisclaimer />

Two birds can carry the same virus and end up with completely different stories. One is an unweaned chick that seems fine at breakfast and is dead by dinner. The other is a budgie that pulls through, keeps eating and flying around for years, and simply never grows a normal set of feathers again. Both are avian polyomavirus. The gap between those two outcomes is mostly a matter of how old the bird was when it got infected, and understanding that gap is what actually helps an owner make sense of a sick nest of chicks or a grown bird with permanently ragged plumage.

## What Avian Polyomavirus Is, and Why Budgies Get Their Own Name

Avian polyomavirus (APV) is a virus in the Polyomaviridae family, according to VCA Animal Hospitals, and it causes disease specifically in young parrots. The Merck Veterinary Manual splits the disease into two forms based on which species is infected: budgerigar fledgling disease and non-budgerigar polyoma infection. That split exists because the virus does not behave the same way in every host. VCA notes that APV can cause outcomes ranging from benign feather lesions in budgies to slow crop emptying in newly weaned parrots, skin hemorrhages, or death, depending on the bird and its age at exposure.

Budgerigar breeders have used the term "French molt" for generations to describe the feather damage left behind in surviving chicks, long before the virus behind it was identified. That older name has stuck around even after the cause was pinned down, which is part of why the same disease shows up under two different labels depending on which corner of the bird world you're reading.

## The Acute Form: What Happens to Unweaned Chicks

This is the form that makes avian polyomavirus genuinely dangerous, and it hits birds too young to have left the nest box. The Merck Veterinary Manual is specific about the timeline: clinical signs occur 7 to 10 days after exposure and include lethargy and crop stasis, the crop stops emptying normally, and death follows within 24 to 48 hours of those signs starting. That is a window measured in days, not weeks, from a healthy-looking chick to a dead one.

VCA Animal Hospitals adds a detail that makes this form even harder to catch in practice: mortality can reach 100% in birds less than 15 days old, and many juvenile and newly weaned parrots infected with APV die quickly with no clinical signs at all. In other words, the chick that looks completely normal at one nest check can simply be gone by the next one, without the lethargy or crop stasis Merck describes ever being obvious enough for a breeder or owner to catch in time. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital's clinical rundown of the acute form lists the signs that can appear before death as depression, loss of appetite, weight loss, delayed crop emptying, regurgitation, dehydration, diarrhea, excess urination, breathing difficulty, and bleeding under the skin, though not every chick shows all of them, or shows them long enough to be noticed.

The practical upshot for anyone hand-raising or monitoring budgie chicks, or any unweaned parrot chick, is that "looked fine yesterday" is not reassuring on its own with this virus in the picture. A nest with one sudden, unexplained death in a chick under about two weeks old is a reason to get the rest of the clutch, and the breeding pair, checked rather than assuming it was an isolated fluke.

## French Molt: The Feather Form in Survivors

Chicks that make it past the acute window don't necessarily walk away clean. The Merck Veterinary Manual documents that surviving budgerigars older than three weeks often exhibit feather dystrophy, which it names as French molt or feather dusters. VCA Animal Hospitals describes the same outcome in its own terms: budgerigars that recover from the infection are often left with abnormal feathers, the condition it also calls budgerigar French molt.

What that damage actually looks like, according to Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital, is reduced formation of down feathers around the head and neck, along with abnormal contour feathers and flight feathers. In practice that can mean a bird with patchy, stubby, or missing flight feathers that never lets it fly normally, sometimes described informally as a "runner" for a budgie that stays grounded because its wing feathers never came in properly. This is not the same picture as an ordinary molt working itself out over a few weeks. A bird recovering from the acute infection and going on to develop French molt typically keeps that abnormal feathering, rather than replacing it with a normal coat the way a healthy bird replaces feathers every year. The [bird feather loss and molt guide](/blog/bird-feather-loss-and-molt-guide/) shows what a completely normal molt looks like by comparison, whole feathers shed gradually with new pin feathers coming in underneath, and covers the PBFD differential referenced below in more depth.

## Which Species Are Affected, Beyond Budgerigars

Budgerigars are the species this disease is named after, but they are not the only one it touches, and the picture looks different once you move past them. VCA Animal Hospitals names budgies, eclectus parrots, caiques, and hawkhead parrots as species particularly susceptible to APV infection. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital's list of commonly affected species is broader still, including young budgerigars, macaws, conures, eclectus parrots, lovebirds, ring-necked parakeets, and caiques, and separately documents canaries and finches among susceptible Passeriformes.

The Merck Veterinary Manual's two-form framing matters here. Non-budgerigar psittacines that survive infection past the youngest, most vulnerable age may have subclinical disease, meaning no obvious symptoms, or present instead with hemorrhages and coagulopathy, bleeding and clotting problems, a different picture from the crop stasis and death seen in unweaned budgie chicks. Merck also notes that adult birds across species are typically resistant to the disease itself: they seroconvert, meaning their immune system responds and produces antibodies, and can shed the virus for up to 90 days before clearing the infection, without necessarily getting sick from it themselves. That resistant-adult-carrier pattern is exactly what makes an outwardly healthy adult bird risky to introduce to a nursery or a mixed collection of birds. ## How It Spreads

VCA Animal Hospitals names direct exposure, a non-vaccinated bird coming into contact with an infected bird or a carrier showing no symptoms, as the most common route of transmission, with feather dander and bodily fluids from infected birds as a frequent additional source. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital's rundown adds more specific routes: virus-contaminated feather dust, feces, urine, urates, and respiratory and crop secretions, spread either by inhalation or ingestion. Budgerigars have one transmission route the other affected species don't, according to that same source: vertical transmission from an infected parent to the embryo inside the egg, a route documented specifically in budgies rather than across psittacines generally.

That combination, an environment easily contaminated by feather dust and droppings plus a route that can pass straight from parent to chick, is why the Merck Veterinary Manual's control guidance for aviaries centers on separation and hygiene: avoid housing budgerigars or lovebirds on premises where other bird species are also being bred, keep nursery traffic limited, and quarantine and test new birds before mixing them with an existing collection. For anyone keeping budgies specifically, our [budgie health issues guide](/blog/budgie-health-issues-guide/) covers the other conditions worth watching for alongside this one.

## The Vaccine

A vaccine for avian polyomavirus does exist. VCA Animal Hospitals confirms one is available for selected psittacine birds to help prevent APV infection. What that means for a specific bird is genuinely a question for an avian vet rather than a one-size-fits-all answer, because the documented schedules vary by product, species, and age.

A 2021 USDA product summary for a licensed killed-virus avian polyomavirus vaccine, PSITTIMUNE APV, made by Huvepharma, lays out the administration used in its own efficacy studies. In a study run in cockatiels and Quaker parakeets, the product summary states plainly: "First dose given by subcutaneous injection at 35 days of age; second vaccination 2-3 weeks following the first vaccination." A separate duration-of-immunity study in that same USDA summary used adult budgerigars, with the first dose given by subcutaneous injection in adult birds and the second vaccination again 2 to 3 weeks after the first, without a specific starting age attached since the study birds were already adults.

The Merck Veterinary Manual independently reports the same manufacturer schedule, that the first dose goes in "when the chick is >35 days old, with a booster vaccination in 2-3 weeks," which is worth noting because two separate references landing on the same numbers is a good deal more reassuring than one. Merck also describes a different regimen for a different group: for breeding birds, "two doses of the vaccine are administered at a 2-week interval; this should be done off-season." Those are not competing versions of one schedule, they are chicks and breeding adults being handled differently, and which one applies to a given bird is precisely the kind of judgment an avian vet makes rather than something to read off a page.

Those two studies alone show the schedule isn't identical across every bird: the 35-day starting age was documented in cockatiels and Quaker parakeets, not in budgerigar chicks specifically, and the budgerigar study in the same summary started with adult birds instead. That's exactly the kind of detail an avian vet sorts out for your actual bird, its species, its age, and its risk of exposure to other birds, rather than something to standardize from a single number read online.

## Avian Polyomavirus vs. PBFD: The Comparison Owners Actually Need

An owner looking at a bird with damaged or abnormal feathers is usually trying to answer one question: is this French molt, or is it psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD)? The two conditions get confused constantly because both can leave a bird with feathers that look wrong, but they're caused by different viruses with different typical ages of onset and different host ranges.

The host range row is the one worth sitting with. A macaw, Amazon, or conure owner reading about PBFD is working from a low-risk baseline for that specific disease, per VCA's own description of it as rare in neotropical parrots, but that same owner isn't automatically low-risk for avian polyomavirus, since APV's own documented host list includes macaws and conures alongside budgies. <KeyTakeaway>
Avian polyomavirus is really two different diseases wearing one name. In unweaned chicks it's an acute illness that can kill within days of the first signs. In birds that survive past three weeks, it can instead show up later as permanent feather dystrophy, French molt, with no acute illness required in between. Both are the same virus, documented by the Merck Veterinary Manual, just caught at different ages.
</KeyTakeaway>

## When to Call an Avian Vet

Any unexplained death in a young, unweaned chick is worth a call, especially if more than one chick in the same clutch is affected, since the acute form can move through a nest quickly. A grown budgie or other parrot with feathers that look stunted, patchy, or abnormal rather than simply shed and regrown is worth a vet visit too, both to get an actual diagnosis rather than a guess and, given the PBFD differential above, because the two conditions call for different handling and carry very different prognoses. An avian vet can test for avian polyomavirus directly rather than leaving an owner to compare photos online, and can advise on whether vaccination makes sense for your specific bird, species, and household.
