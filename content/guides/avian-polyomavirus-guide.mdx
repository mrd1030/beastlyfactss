# Avian Polyomavirus: Budgerigar Fledgling Disease and French Molt

<VetDisclaimer />

Two birds can carry the same virus and end up with completely different stories. One is an unweaned chick that seems fine at breakfast and is dead by dinner. The other is a budgie that pulls through, keeps eating and flying around for years, and simply never grows a normal set of feathers again. Both are avian polyomavirus. The gap between those two outcomes is mostly a matter of how old the bird was when it got infected, and understanding that gap is what helps an owner make sense of a sick nest of chicks or a grown bird with permanently ragged plumage.

## What Avian Polyomavirus Is, and Why Budgies Get Their Own Name

Avian polyomavirus (APV) is a virus in the Polyomaviridae family, and it causes disease specifically in young parrots. It splits into two forms by host: budgerigar fledgling disease and non-budgerigar polyoma infection. That split exists because the virus does not behave the same way in every host. Depending on the bird and its age at exposure, APV can cause anything from benign feather lesions in budgies to slow crop emptying in newly weaned parrots, bleeding under the skin, or death.

## The Acute Form: What Happens to Unweaned Chicks

This is the form that makes avian polyomavirus dangerous, and it hits birds too young to have left the nest box. The timeline is short: clinical signs appear 7 to 10 days after exposure and include lethargy and crop stasis, the crop stops emptying normally, and death follows within 24 to 48 hours of those signs starting. That is a window measured in days, not weeks, from a healthy-looking chick to a dead one.

It is even harder to catch than that suggests. Mortality can reach 100% in birds less than 15 days old, and many juvenile and newly weaned parrots infected with APV die quickly with no clinical signs at all. The chick that looks completely normal at one nest check can simply be gone by the next one, without the lethargy or crop stasis ever being obvious enough for a breeder or owner to catch in time. When signs do appear before death, they can include depression, loss of appetite, weight loss, delayed crop emptying, regurgitation, dehydration, diarrhea, excess urination, breathing difficulty, and bleeding under the skin, though not every chick shows all of them, or shows them long enough to be noticed.

## French Molt: What Survivors Are Left With

Chicks that make it past the acute window don't necessarily walk away clean. Budgerigars older than three weeks that survive often develop feather dystrophy, known as French molt or feather dusters.

The damage shows up as poor growth of the down feathers around the head and neck, along with abnormal contour and flight feathers. In practice that can mean a bird with patchy, stubby, or missing flight feathers that never lets it fly normally, sometimes described informally as a "runner" for a budgie that stays grounded because its wing feathers never came in properly. This is not the same picture as an ordinary molt working itself out over a few weeks. A bird that develops French molt typically keeps that abnormal feathering, rather than replacing it with a normal coat the way a healthy bird replaces feathers every year. A completely normal [molt](/blog/bird-feather-loss-and-molt-guide/) looks different: whole feathers shed gradually with new pin feathers coming in underneath.

## Beyond Budgies: Other Species and How the Picture Changes

Budgerigars are the species this disease is named after, but they are not the only one it touches, and the picture looks different once you move past them. Budgies, eclectus parrots, caiques, and hawkhead parrots are particularly susceptible, and macaws, conures, lovebirds, and ring-necked parakeets are commonly affected when young. Among songbirds, canaries and finches are susceptible too.

Non-budgerigar parrots that survive infection past the youngest, most vulnerable age may have subclinical disease, meaning no obvious symptoms, or show hemorrhages and clotting problems instead, a different picture from the crop stasis and death seen in unweaned budgie chicks. Adult birds across species are typically resistant to the disease itself: they seroconvert, meaning their immune system responds and produces antibodies, and can shed the virus for up to 90 days before clearing the infection, without necessarily getting sick from it themselves. That resistant-adult-carrier pattern is exactly what makes an outwardly healthy adult bird risky to introduce to a nursery or a mixed collection, and why a newly acquired bird goes through the full [bird quarantine](/blog/bird-quarantine-guide/) period before it meets an existing flock.

## How It Spreads

Direct exposure, a non-vaccinated bird coming into contact with an infected bird or a carrier showing no symptoms, is the most common route of transmission, with feather dander and bodily fluids from infected birds a frequent additional source. The virus travels in contaminated feather dust, feces, urine, urates, and respiratory and crop secretions, and spreads by inhalation or ingestion. Budgerigars have one route the other affected species don't: vertical transmission from an infected parent to the embryo inside the egg.

That combination, an environment easily contaminated by feather dust and droppings plus a route that can pass straight from parent to chick, is why control in aviaries centers on separation and hygiene: avoid housing budgerigars or lovebirds on premises where other bird species are also being bred, keep nursery traffic limited, and quarantine and test new birds before mixing them with an existing collection. Polyomavirus and psittacosis are the two infections on the list of [budgie health problems](/blog/budgie-health-issues-guide/) that a new bird can carry in looking perfectly healthy.

## The Vaccine

A vaccine for avian polyomavirus does exist for selected psittacine birds. What that means for a specific bird is a question for an avian vet rather than a one-size-fits-all answer, because the documented schedules vary by product, species, and age.

A 2021 USDA product summary for a licensed killed-virus avian polyomavirus vaccine, PSITTIMUNE APV, made by Huvepharma, lays out the administration used in its own efficacy studies. In a study run in cockatiels and Quaker parakeets, the first dose went in by subcutaneous injection at 35 days of age, with the second 2 to 3 weeks later. A separate duration-of-immunity study in the same summary used adult budgerigars, with the second dose again 2 to 3 weeks after the first and no starting age attached, since the study birds were already adults.

Chicks and breeding adults are handled differently. Chicks start after 35 days of age with a booster 2 to 3 weeks later; breeding birds get two doses two weeks apart, given in the off-season. Which one applies to a given bird is the kind of judgment an avian vet makes. The 35-day starting age was documented in cockatiels and Quaker parakeets, not in budgerigar chicks specifically, which is one more reason the vet, not a number read online, sets the schedule for your bird, its species, its age, and its risk of exposure to other birds.

## Avian Polyomavirus vs. PBFD: The Comparison Owners Need

An owner looking at a bird with damaged or abnormal feathers is usually trying to answer one question: is this French molt, or is it psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD)? The two conditions get confused constantly because both can leave a bird with feathers that look wrong, but they're caused by different viruses with different typical ages of onset and different host ranges.

The host range row is the one worth sitting with. A macaw, Amazon, or conure owner reading about PBFD is working from a low-risk baseline for that specific disease, since it is rare in neotropical parrots, but that same owner isn't automatically low-risk for avian polyomavirus, since APV's host list includes macaws and conures alongside budgies. The two risks don't move together, which is exactly why guessing between them from a photo online is a bad plan. PBFD's own signs, and how it is told apart from behavioral plucking, belong to the wider picture of [feather loss in pet birds](/blog/bird-feather-loss-and-molt-guide/).

<FunFact>
"Feather duster" isn't a nickname invented by frustrated bird owners. It sits alongside "French molt" as the clinical name for the feather dystrophy seen in budgerigars that survive avian polyomavirus, a rare case of a slightly whimsical name making it into veterinary reference texts.
</FunFact>

<KeyTakeaway>
Avian polyomavirus is two different diseases wearing one name. In unweaned chicks it's an acute illness that can kill within days of the first signs. In birds that survive past three weeks, it can instead show up later as permanent feather dystrophy, French molt, with no acute illness required in between. Both are the same virus, just caught at different ages.
</KeyTakeaway>

## When to Call an Avian Vet

Any unexplained death in a young, unweaned chick is worth a call, especially if more than one chick in the same clutch is affected, since the acute form can move through a nest quickly. A grown budgie or other parrot with feathers that look stunted, patchy, or abnormal rather than simply shed and regrown is worth a vet visit too, both to get an actual diagnosis rather than a guess and, given the PBFD differential above, because the two conditions call for different handling and carry very different prognoses. An avian vet can test for avian polyomavirus directly and can advise on whether vaccination makes sense for your specific bird, species, and household.
