# Shrimp Molting: What a Normal Molt Looks Like, and the White Ring That Means It Failed

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The invertebrate molting guide covers tarantulas, hermit crabs, jumping spiders and the land animals that have since joined them, and its one rule, leave the animal alone, holds underwater too. But a shrimp molts in a different world, where the shell is built from the minerals dissolved in the water and a water change can undo it in an afternoon. Molting is the most load-bearing topic on every shrimp page here, and each health guide carried its own version. This is the shared one: the cycle, the signs that are not illness, the numbers, and the failure.

## The Cycle, by Age

The baseline: in healthy conditions an adult shrimp molts every 3 to 4 weeks, and younger shrimp molt more often because of how fast they grow, perhaps every 1 or 2 weeks. The [ghost shrimp health guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-health-issues-guide/) has the same curve for that species, weekly when young and roughly monthly when older, which is why a young colony shows molts constantly. A tank full of empty shells is a tank full of growing shrimp. The [invertebrate molting guide](/blog/invertebrate-molting-guide/) has the same age curve for a tarantula, from weeks between molts as a spiderling to a year or more as an adult.

Before the molt, a shrimp goes still and stops eating for an hour or more. After it, the shrimp is very soft and vulnerable, so it hides for a few days until the outer shell has hardened again. Neither is a symptom. The [cherry shrimp health guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-health-issues-guide/) makes the same point from the other direction: a shrimp that hides and stays still for a few days right after a normal molt is not showing a problem.

<KeyTakeaway>
Leave the shed shell in the tank. Shrimp eat their old shell as a rich source of calcium, and it is the mineral they need most for the next one. A hollow shell with no shrimp in it is good news.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Numbers That Make a Molt Work

A shrimp builds its new shell from the calcium and magnesium in the water, which is what general hardness measures. GH and KH are the two most important parameters to check when molts go wrong. The ranges: GH 6 to 12 for Neocaridina, with 7 to 8 optimal, and GH 4 to 6 for Caridina. Below GH 6 for a cherry shrimp, or 4 for a Caridina, the advice is to begin raising it. The amano is the exception inside Caridina: a hard-water species that takes GH 5 to 15, so the 4 to 6 band is for the soft-water Caridina, not the amano. The [pH, GH and KH guide](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) covers what each number measures and how to move it without chemicals.

KH matters in its relationship to GH. Very high KH with low GH can interfere with mineral absorption, KH 10 against GH 3 being the shape of the problem. And the rate of change matters as much as the number: never raise GH by more than 2 points a day, because sudden increases cause their own problems.

## The Water Change That Kills

The white ring of death is a molt that split in the wrong place. The shell breaks all around the body instead of just at the top, exposing the clear white body parts, which leaves the shrimp with two disconnected exoskeletons it cannot bend out of. It follows most often from a sudden, large water change or a swing in parameters, particularly a rapid jump in general hardness. Underneath that, the primary cause is too little calcium and magnesium in the water, and a large change with softer water can trigger a premature molt the same way.

That is why the shrimp rows on the [water changes guide](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/) are so much smaller than the fish baseline. A cherry shrimp tank gets about 10 percent a week, and an amano or ghost tank small and steady changes, because the risk is not dirty water but a shell that was half built to one hardness and finished at another. The other causes fill in the list: a calcium- and protein-poor diet that weakens the new exoskeleton, cold water that stalls the cycle, and ammonia, nitrite, pH swings or copper on top.

<KeyTakeaway>
Change shrimp water by the test, not by the calendar, and match the new water's hardness to the tank. The molt that fails is the one that started in one water and ended in another.
</KeyTakeaway>

## When It Has Already Gone Wrong

Once the white ring is visible the shell has already failed, and the outlook is blunt: the shrimp will die within hours to days, and the effort belongs in preventing the next one. Some keepers have held part of the old shell very carefully with tweezers to give the shrimp leverage, as a last resort. It is. A shrimp is too small to help with any confidence, and the intervention usually finishes what the molt started.

What a failed molt is telling you is about the tank, and the response is the test kit: GH and KH first, then the size of the last water change, then the food. One failed molt in a colony that is otherwise molting cleanly is a shrimp. Two in a week is the water.

## Feeding for the Shell

A shrimp that eats its shed shell is telling you what it wants. The rotation is algae wafers, blanched vegetables and protein-rich foods, and a diet without calcium-rich foods contributes to molting problems especially when GH is borderline. The [amano shrimp health guide](/blog/amano-shrimp-health-issues-guide/) covers the rest of the diet for the one aquarium shrimp that is more often underfed than overfed.

## The Takeaway

Adults molt every 3 to 4 weeks and juveniles every 1 or 2. An hour of stillness before and a few days of hiding after are the molt, not an illness, and the empty shell stays in the tank. Hold GH at 6 to 12 for cherry shrimp, 4 to 6 for soft-water Caridina and 5 to 15 for amano, move it by no more than 2 a day, and keep water changes small enough that the hardness never jumps. The white ring is the water's fault, and the fix is the next molt, not this one.
