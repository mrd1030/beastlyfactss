# Is It Legal to Own a California Kingsnake? State-by-State Guide

California kingsnakes are one of the most widely kept pet snakes in the US, prized for a calm temperament and the huge range of colors and patterns decades of captive breeding have produced. Legally, they're about as uncomplicated as a pet snake gets across most of the country, tripped up mainly by two states that write their rules around how the snake looks rather than what species it is. For everything else about keeping one, see our full [California Kingsnake care guide](/guides/kingsnake/), and the [Kingsnake encyclopedia profile](/encyclopedia/animal/kingsnake/) for more on the species itself.

### Mostly a Non-Issue Across the Country

Across the great majority of states, a California kingsnake clears whatever test that state applies to pet reptiles. Some, like Alaska and Idaho, draw the line at venom, not species, so a nonvenomous colubrid is automatically fine. Others, including Massachusetts and Rhode Island, name the genus Lampropeltis directly on their license-exemption lists, and Arkansas and Colorado clear the same genus with narrow carve-outs that don't reach the California kingsnake specifically. In a long list of other states, from Alabama and Maryland to Missouri, New York, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, and Wisconsin, the deciding factor is simply that the California kingsnake isn't a native species, so it falls outside the permit systems built around each state's own native reptiles.

### Oregon and Nevada: Why Your Kingsnake's Pattern Can Matter

<FunFact>
Oregon's noncontrolled-species rule for kingsnakes, OAR 635-056-0060(D), was written under an older taxonomy that classified the California kingsnake as a subspecies, Lampropeltis getula californiae. The rule only clears individuals "morphologically distinct from native species," which in practice means a recognizable color or pattern morph, not an ordinary wild-type-looking kingsnake or its offspring. The more natural your kingsnake looks, the shakier its footing under this rule. Nevada runs a similar pattern-based test from the opposite direction: its unprotected-reptile category, NAC 503.090, specifically singles out California kingsnakes that lack a continuous banded or ringed pattern from head to vent as the unprotected form. Between the two states, a kingsnake's color pattern, not its species identity, is what the law actually checks.
</FunFact>

## The Real Takeaway

California kingsnakes are legal to own across nearly the entire US, and in most states the analysis barely takes a sentence: not venomous, not native, not on any restricted list. Hawaii is the one flat, no-exceptions ban, covering every snake species with nothing an owner can do about it. Delaware requires a permit before the snake can even enter the state, the same as any other reptile exotic to Delaware. New Mexico and Vermont are the two places this research couldn't pin down a clear answer either way. The most interesting wrinkle by far is Oregon and Nevada, where legality doesn't just depend on being a California kingsnake, it depends on what your specific snake looks like.
