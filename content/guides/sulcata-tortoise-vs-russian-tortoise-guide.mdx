# Sulcata Tortoise vs. Russian Tortoise: Which Tortoise Actually Fits Your Space

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Both start out as palm-sized hatchlings that look nearly identical in size at the pet store, which is exactly how so many sulcata owners end up surprised a few years later. The real difference between these two isn't temperament or diet, it's how much space and how many decades you're actually signing up for. ## Size Is the Whole Story Here

A Russian tortoise tops out at 6 to 10 inches and 2 to 3 pounds, a size it holds for its entire adult life. A sulcata starts at that same hatchling size and grows into a 70 to 150+ pound animal with a shell over two feet long, making it the third-largest tortoise species in the world after the Galapagos and Aldabra giant tortoises. That growth happens fast, often reaching most of its adult size within 5 to 10 years, which is exactly the window where so many sulcata owners realize their indoor setup has become hopelessly inadequate.

<FunFact>
A sulcata tortoise's shell can grow past two feet in length even though the hatchling that produced it emerged from an egg smaller than a golf ball, one of the largest adult-to-hatchling size ratios of any commonly kept reptile.
</FunFact>

## Housing: A Table vs. Acreage

A Russian tortoise can live its whole life on a well-set-up <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rkp3S6" product="Tortoise table">open-top tortoise table</AffiliateLink>, indoors, with no yard required. A sulcata can use that same kind of table only while it's a juvenile. As an adult, it needs genuine outdoor space, ideally several hundred square feet, plus a heated shelter for cold nights. That outdoor requirement isn't a nice-to-have, it's the difference between a healthy adult sulcata and a stressed, cramped one.

<KeyTakeaway>
Choose a Sulcata only if you have, or will soon have, real outdoor acreage and a heated shelter for a full-grown adult. This is one of the largest tortoise species alive, and underestimating that growth is the single biggest reason sulcatas end up surrendered to reptile rescues.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Escape-Proofing Looks Different at Each Scale

Both species dig, but the stakes are completely different. A Russian tortoise's digging is manageable within an indoor table's walls. A sulcata is a powerful, dedicated digger capable of undermining ordinary fencing, which is why any outdoor sulcata enclosure needs <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4aCGvb1" product="Escape-proof outdoor fencing">fencing buried at least 12 inches deep</AffiliateLink> from day one, not added after the first escape.

## The Lifespan Commitment Is Generational

A Russian tortoise's 40 to 50 year lifespan is already longer than most pets people keep. A sulcata's 70 to 100+ years is on another scale entirely, genuinely long enough to outlive the person who buys it as a hatchling. That's not a hypothetical, it's the single biggest reason sulcatas end up surrendered to reptile rescues: the animal is fine, the space and multi-generational plan around it never existed.

<KeyTakeaway>
A Russian Tortoise is the better fit if you want a tortoise that stays manageable indoors for its entire life. It's genuinely cold-hardy and can brumate safely with proper preparation, something a sulcata, built for the edge of the Sahara, simply isn't equipped for.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Bottom Line

Pick a Russian tortoise if you want a tortoise that stays manageable indoors for its entire life and don't have serious outdoor space to offer. Pick a sulcata only if you genuinely have, or will soon have, the outdoor acreage and heated shelter a full-grown adult needs, and you're comfortable planning for an animal that may outlive you. This is one comparison where the "cooler-looking" hatchling is very often the wrong choice for the home it's going into.
