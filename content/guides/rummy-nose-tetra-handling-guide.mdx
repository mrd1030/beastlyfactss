# Rummy-Nose Tetra Handling Comes Down to One Stressful Trip Home

The rummy-nose tetra (Petitella bleheri, formerly Hemigrammus bleheri) is a silver South American schooling fish with a blood-red head, native to blackwater rivers of the Rio Negro and Rio Meta basins and grown to 1.5 to 2 inches (4 to 5 cm). Nobody holds one. Its handling is a net, a bag and the hour it takes to move from shop water into tank water, and this is a fish that takes stress hard: its sensitivity is the reason many keepers steer beginners away from it.

## Choosing Fish From the Shop Tank

The red head is the first thing to read. A rummy-nose in poor condition loses much of that color, so a tank where most of the noses look washed out is a tank to walk past. Healthy rummy-noses also hold together closely, so a group drifting apart around the tank deserves a second look.

Look over every fish for white spots before agreeing to a bag. New rummy-noses sometimes come home carrying ich. Ask the shop for its pH and hardness while there, since those two figures decide how the acclimation goes.

## Netting and Bagging

A net is the only thing that touches a rummy-nose. Skip the shop habit of having a second person hold a half-full bag open in the air while fish get tipped in from the net. A rummy-nose takes stress hard, and a transfer that goes wrong in midair is the kind it can do without.

In the bag, one part water to three parts air is the ratio. The air is where the oxygen for the journey sits, so a bag that looks underfilled is filled correctly. For the ride, put the bag in a dark box, or wrap it in a towel or newspaper.

A rummy-nose may lie motionless or on its side in the bag. It is playing dead. Set the bag down on a solid surface and the fish rights itself.

Bag water gets worse by the hour. Waste raises the ammonia, and carbon dioxide building up pulls the pH down. Both are reasons that water never reaches the tank, and a long drive or a shipped order calls for the oxygen and insulation planning in [moving fish over a longer trip](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/).

## Acclimation, Step by Step

1. **Lights off.** Fresh arrivals should sit in fairly dim conditions, so the tank lights stay off for the whole process.
2. **Mix water in slowly.** Every few minutes, pour a little tank water into the bag. Stop once the bag holds more aquarium water than shop water, which takes 30 to 60 minutes.
3. **Net, do not pour.** Lift the fish out with the net and let them go in the tank. The bag water carries the waste from the trip and stays out.
4. **Count the strays.** Before the bag is thrown away, check it, the net and the floor for a fish that stuck or jumped.

Rummy-noses want soft, acidic water: a pH of 5.5 to 7.0 and 2 to 12 dGH. When the shop's figures and the home tank's sit far apart, drip acclimation is the slower method experienced keepers often use instead. Testing the tank's [pH and hardness](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before the trip shows whether the gap is there.

The same routine applies when the bag goes into a [quarantine tank](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) first, which is worth doing wherever the fish came from and doubly so with a species that tends to arrive with [ich](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/).

## Rummies Go in First

Order matters with this fish. Rummy-noses react strongly to their surroundings, and a territorial fish already settled in the tank, an angelfish being the usual case, can send a new school into hiding and keep it there. Add the rummy-noses before the bigger fish. Let them settle, preferably with the lights off, and put the angelfish in about half an hour later.

## How Many to Bring Home

Ten or more. Rummy-noses are among the more tightly schooling small tetras, and [how a rummy-nose school moves and turns](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-enrichment-guide/) changes with the number of fish in it.

A school of 10 needs a tank at least 36 inches (90 cm) long, and the [rummy-nose tetra tank setup guide](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) explains why the length matters more than the gallon figure. The school goes into a tank that has finished its [nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/).

## Temperament: Very Peaceful, Easily Outcompeted

Rummy-nose tetras are very peaceful, and they do poorly against tankmates that are boisterous or much larger than they are. That one trait sets most of the [stocking rules](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/). A bullied school often shows it in the noses, which lose their red under stress, so a pale school in a community tank is a reason to test the water and then watch who is chasing whom.

## Tankmates That Work

Peaceful fish of similar size are the rule, and the list is long:

- Other small tetras, including Hemigrammus and Hyphessobrycon species.
- Pencilfish.
- Corydoras and small loricariid catfish.
- Apistogramma and West African dwarf cichlids such as Pelvicachromis.
- In a general community, smaller rasboras, barbs and anabantoids.

Angelfish work when the rummy-noses are stocked first, as above. Discus are a classic pairing, and many discus keepers run a rummy-nose school in their show tanks. The catch is temperature: rummy-noses top out at 79°F, below the [82 to 86°F discus are usually kept at](/blog/discus-tank-setup-guide/).

## Telling Males From Females

Sexing rummy-noses is hard. Mature females are noticeably rounder in the body, and males run slightly slimmer. In a mixed school none of this matters; it only comes into play for a keeper setting up a breeding group.

<FunFact>
Rummy-nose parents eat their own eggs given the chance, so breeders spawn a group of about six of each sex and lift the adults out once eggs or fry appear.
</FunFact>
