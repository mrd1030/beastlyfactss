# Common Veiled Chameleon Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Veiled chameleons are hardy by chameleon standards, but a handful of conditions come up often enough, and one is specific to females and urgent, that every owner should recognize the signs.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

The most common and most preventable serious issue. Watch for a soft or bendable "rubber jaw," curved or broken limbs, tremors, and an inability to grip branches or properly shoot the tongue to catch food. This comes from inadequate <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TiqeC4" product="UVB - Arcadia Forest 6% (ProT5 kit, 36in/39W)">UVB</AffiliateLink> and/or a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D82LT16W?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Repashy Calcium Plus LoD 3 Oz JAR">calcium and vitamin D3 imbalance</AffiliateLink>.

<FunFact>
Research on juvenile veiled chameleons found that proper calcium and vitamin A supplementation prevented MBD even without added UVB or D3, underscoring how much diet matters alongside lighting. It's not an either-or, but it's a reminder that lighting alone can't cover for a poor supplement routine.
</FunFact>

Damage caught early is only partly reversible and advanced cases are permanent, so a chameleon with any of these signs needs a vet promptly.

## Egg-Binding (Dystocia)

This is the health issue every owner of a female veiled chameleon needs to know cold. Females produce egg clutches whether or not a male is present, and a female that can't pass her eggs is in genuine danger.

**Watch for:** restlessness, digging behavior without producing eggs, straining, lethargy, and swelling. Sky-blue spots appearing on the flanks are a normal sign after a female has successfully laid, worth knowing so you don't mistake healthy post-laying color for illness.

This is an emergency. Egg-binding can kill within 24 hours, and severe cases often require surgery. A permanent, always-available egg-laying bin and proper calcium supplementation are the real prevention here, set up long before a female shows signs of trouble.

## After She Lays: Infertile Clutches

Female veiled chameleons can start producing eggs very young. Vets see many at 6 to 7 months old, often weighing under 100 grams, already carrying active follicles. They lay whether or not they have ever met a male, and they lay big: in one three-year study of captive females, clutches averaged about 50 eggs and each female laid around six clutches over the three years.

When a female has been digging and comes out of the bin noticeably slimmer, she has probably finished. What to do next:

1. **Dig up the clutch.** Eggs from a female who has never been housed with a male are infertile and will not hatch, so there is nothing to incubate. Remove them and discard them. A female that has mated may be carrying fertile eggs, which go into an incubator instead.
2. **Count and compare.** If she still looks swollen after laying, or keeps digging without producing more, treat it as possible egg-binding and call the vet.
3. **Refill the bin.** Put the substrate back damp and firm enough to hold a tunnel, so the bin is ready for the next clutch.
4. **Let her recover.** Laying takes a lot out of her. Give her food, water, and privacy for the first days, then taper back to her normal maintenance diet within a week or two, since she can start building another clutch soon after.

Her diet and temperatures decide how often this happens. Keeping a female's basking spot in the low 80s°F with ambient in the 70s, letting nights drop into the 60s, and feeding an adult female about three feeders every other day slow egg production, which is easier on her body. Hormone implants have not proved reliable here: in the same study, deslorelin implants did not stop egg production in any of the seven treated females. Surgical removal of the ovaries is the permanent option for a female that keeps running into trouble.


## Eye Problems

The second most commonly reported issue in this species, ranging from minor discharge to more serious turret abscesses and infections, often linked to vitamin A deficiency. Vitamin A was also half of what prevented bone disease in the juvenile feeding trial above, so a supplement plan that covers it protects the eyes and the skeleton at once. Swelling or discharge around the turret needs a vet.

## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, gaping, and visible mucus point here, usually from temperatures running too cool or humidity that's off. ## Dehydration and Kidney Disease

A frequently overlooked but serious risk. Inadequate misting leads directly to chronic dehydration, which shows as lethargy, sunken eyes, and a poor appetite, and over time it becomes kidney disease, a leading cause of death in this species. ## Vet Visits

A newly acquired chameleon should see a vet experienced with reptiles within its first few weeks home, and every chameleon benefits from a yearly wellness exam after that. For a female, that yearly visit is the time to ask about her egg-laying history and whether her diet is pushing her to lay more often than she needs to.
