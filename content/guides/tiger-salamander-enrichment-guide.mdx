# Tiger Salamander Enrichment: Underground Is Where the Animal Lives

<AffiliateDisclosure />

An adult tiger salamander is a burrowing animal, and almost every enrichment decision for one comes down to how much substrate you are willing to give it.

<KeyTakeaway>
This is a fossorial amphibian that spends most of its adult life in a burrow it dug itself. Shallow substrate does not make the animal more visible in any meaningful sense, it removes the behavior and leaves a salamander sitting on a floor with nowhere to go.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Says

Nothing on *Ambystoma tigrinum*, and very little on amphibians in general. A 2014 review found only **fourteen relevant primary research papers** on amphibian enrichment across the entire class.

The two usable findings both come from frogs. **Plant cover** improved body size, growth rate and skin bacterial communities in tree frogs, covered in full in our [White's Tree Frog enrichment guide](/blog/whites-tree-frog-enrichment-guide/). And in a preference test, aquatic frogs **chose planting over PVC pipes and over open space**.

A frog is a long way from a burrowing salamander, and the transferable part is narrow but real: cover matters to amphibians physiologically, not only behaviorally, and given a choice they take the more complex option. For a fossorial species, cover means substrate.

Their closest relative in the hobby is the axolotl, which is the same genus in permanently aquatic form. Our [tiger salamander versus axolotl guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-vs-axolotl-guide/) covers how differently the two ended up, and our [Axolotl enrichment guide](/blog/axolotl-enrichment-guide/) covers the aquatic side.

<FunFact>
Axolotls are essentially tiger salamanders that never metamorphosed. Given the right hormonal push an axolotl can be forced through metamorphosis into something that looks much like an adult tiger salamander, which is a strange thing to know about two pets kept in completely different setups.
</FunFact>

## Substrate Depth, Which Is the Whole Guide

Several inches at absolute minimum and more if the enclosure allows. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">20 gallon long tank</AffiliateLink> gives useful floor area for one adult, and the depth of what goes in it is what decides whether the animal can behave normally.

A deep bed of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CY23K32S?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="VCEPJH Loose Coconut Fiber Substrate, 2 Pack Natural Coco Coir Reptile Bedding">coconut fiber substrate</AffiliateLink>, damp enough to hold a tunnel, is the standard. Other sizes and volumes are available, and a deep setup takes more than one pack. Add leaf litter on top so there is cover at the surface as well as under it.

Floor area over height. This animal does not climb.

## Cool, and Cooler Than You Think

Temperate North American amphibians. They do badly at the temperatures a warm house sits at, and a heat source is almost always the wrong answer for this species.

Overheating is the more common failure here, not cold. A cool room, a cool corner, and no basking lamp.

## Water Quality

Permeable skin, so water is a welfare input rather than a drink. A shallow dish large enough to sit in, with <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4tXcbib" product="API Tap Water Conditioner (16 oz)">dechlorinated water</AffiliateLink> changed regularly, and damp substrate that is not waterlogged.

Never untreated tap water on an animal that breathes through its skin.

## Feeding, With Restraint

Enthusiastic feeders that strike at more or less anything and have no useful sense of when to stop. Obesity is the standard captive problem in long-kept tiger salamanders.

Release worms and insects into the leaf litter so finding them is the activity, vary where they go, and feed on a schedule rather than in response to interest. Keep prey off loose substrate where you can, since impaction is a real risk in an animal that swallows whatever it strikes.

## Surface Cover as Well as Depth

Cork flats laid down, leaf litter, and low planting give somewhere to be **at** the surface without being exposed. That matters at night, when the animal is actually up and moving, and it is the layer the burrow cannot provide.

## Priority Order

1. Substrate deep enough to dig a real burrow
2. Cool temperatures, and no heat source
3. Floor area over height
4. Dechlorinated water in a sittable dish, changed often
5. Surface cover, leaf litter and cork
6. Prey released into the litter, on a schedule
7. Nothing much else, and that is fine

## What Not to Do

Do not give shallow substrate to a fossorial animal in order to see it more, which trades the animal's behavior for your convenience. Do not add a basking lamp. Do not use untreated tap water. Do not feed to appetite, because there is no natural stopping point. Do not house two together. And do not treat handling as enrichment, since handling an amphibian damages the mucus layer and is a health event, never a stimulation one.
