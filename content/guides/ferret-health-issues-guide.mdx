# Common Ferret Health Issues (And Why Middle Age Is the Real Turning Point)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ferrets are genuinely healthy and low-maintenance as young adults. The real story with this species is what happens starting around age 2 to 3, when a set of chronic endocrine diseases becomes common enough that budgeting for them isn't pessimism, it's realism. For everything else about day-to-day care, see our full [Ferret care guide](/guides/ferret/).

## Adrenal Gland Disease (Hyperadrenocorticism)

Extremely common in US pet ferrets. Estimates of how many ferrets are affected vary widely across studies, ranging from under 1% to as high as a quarter of the population, with one frequently cited figure suggesting around 70% of US pet ferrets were affected as of 2003. The wide range reflects real differences between study populations, but the consistent message is the same: this is not a rare condition in this species.

**Watch for:** symmetrical hair loss, often starting at the tail and working forward, itchy skin, a swollen vulva in females, and in males, an enlarged prostate that can cause a life-threatening urinary blockage.

**What causes it, and why it's genuinely debated:** the mainstream explanation is that neutering removes the normal hormonal feedback loop to the pituitary gland, leading to unchecked stimulation of the adrenal glands, and that early spay and neuter practices common in the US pet trade contribute to this. That link is real and supported by veterinary literature, but it's not the whole story.

<FunFact>
A study of Dutch ferrets neutered much later than the US norm, between 12 and 18 months instead of a few weeks old, still found the disease showing up. The gap between neutering and diagnosis was nearly identical to what's seen in US ferrets neutered as kits. That's a genuinely surprising result, and it's why the more accurate picture is that neutering itself, combined with prolonged indoor light exposure and genetic factors, appears to drive the disease more than the exact age at which a ferret is neutered.
</FunFact>

Urinary blockage in males is an emergency. The underlying disease itself is a manage-for-life condition, treated medically or surgically. ## Insulinoma

A tumor of the insulin-producing cells in the pancreas, and per veterinary sources, the single most common tumor type diagnosed in ferrets, making up roughly a quarter of all ferret tumors. Common starting around age 2 to 3.

**Watch for:** lethargy, a glassy or unfocused stare ("stargazing"), weakness in the hind legs, drooling or pawing at the mouth, and in severe cases, seizures or collapse. Symptoms often ease temporarily after eating, since the underlying issue is low blood sugar.

This requires lifelong management, medication, dietary changes, and sometimes surgery. A sudden collapse or seizure is an emergency requiring immediate care.

## GI Blockage

Ferrets chew on and swallow rubber and foam, and a blockage is a genuine surgical emergency. Watch for vomiting, appetite loss, and lethargy. This is almost entirely a ferret-proofing issue, covered in our [Ferret cage setup guide](/blog/ferret-tank-setup-guide/), rather than something to treat after the fact. ## Canine Distemper

Nearly always fatal in ferrets, and entirely preventable with vaccination. There's no good reason to skip this one.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Prolonged exposure to artificial light is implicated in adrenal disease risk, which is why a normal day and night cycle matters more for this species than it might seem. Inadequate ferret-proofing is the direct cause of GI blockages. And skipping vaccination is the only reason a ferret would face canine distemper at all. The endocrine diseases are harder to fully prevent, but early recognition, especially of insulinoma's subtle early signs, makes a real difference in outcomes.
