# How to Handle an Argentine Tegu (The Reptile That Acts Like a Dog)

Argentine tegus have a genuine reputation as the most intelligent, most tameable lizard commonly kept as a pet, and unlike most reptile "friendly" claims, this one holds up consistently across experienced keepers and exotic vets alike.

## A Real Dog Comparison, Not Just Marketing

Exotic vet sources describe tegus as potentially the most intelligent reptile species, noting they demonstrate this by working out clever escape routes and, when handled regularly, becoming genuinely docile and even seeming to enjoy physical interaction. Specialist breeders go further, describing them as behaving something like a dog, just with reptilian housing needs. This is a real, earned reputation, not typical pet store exaggeration.

## The Catch: It Requires Real, Consistent Effort

This tameness doesn't happen automatically. Tegus need regular, supervised handling and free roam time to develop and maintain this dog-like temperament. Skip that consistent socialization, and you get a considerably more defensive animal instead, tail whipping, scratching, and biting are all real possibilities from an under-socialized tegu, and a bite from an animal this size is powerful.

<FunFact>
Specialist breeders describe a well socialized Argentine tegu as behaving something like a dog, just with reptilian housing needs. It's a genuine, earned comparison rather than typical pet store hype, but it only holds up when the daily handling and free roam time behind it actually happens.
</FunFact>

## Tegus Can Drop Their Tails, Unlike Monitors

This is a detail worth knowing specifically because it surprises people familiar with monitor lizards, such as the [savannah monitor](/blog/savannah-monitor-handling-guide/), another large lizard that cannot do this. Unlike monitors, tegus are capable of tail autotomy, dropping the tail as a defense mechanism. This matters especially with flighty subadults or any newly acquired wild caught individual, worth factoring in if you're handling a tegu that hasn't fully settled yet.

## First Timer Mistakes

- Underestimating the space and daily time commitment this species needs to stay genuinely tame.
- Skipping consistent handling during the taming window and expecting docility to develop on its own.
- Housing two males together, they will fight.
- Not washing hands before and after handling, Salmonella risk applies here as with most reptiles.

## Reading a Tegu by Its Size

A hatchling and an adult show stress differently. ReptiFiles' handling page calls hatchlings, 7 to 10 inches at that age, flighty: anything swooping down from above reads as a predator, and they are more likely to run than bite or tail whip. An untamed adult is bold and defensive. Georgia's Department of Natural Resources: not aggressive toward people, but they will defend themselves, react fast, and lash with the tail.

ReptiFiles' signs for an untamed one:

- **Fast, frequent tongue flicking.** Your hand smells like food. Wash it first.
- **Arched back, head down, heavy breathing.** The aggressive posture. Back away slowly. Closer likely gets a tail whip or a bite.
- **Tail twitching erratically or waving like a snake.** ReptiFiles calls it "snake tail" and reads it as a charge about to happen. Get away.
- **Flailing in your hands.** It does not trust you or feel secure. Adjust the grip, or go back to taming.

Approach from the side, never from above, and never disturb one in its hide. On frequency, LafeberVet says daily and ReptiFiles says short and frequent.

### When one person is not enough

LafeberVet's care sheet puts an adult Argentine at up to 5.2 feet and 11 pounds, ReptiFiles at up to 5 feet and 15 pounds. No source here names a cutoff length. The nearest rule comes from [boa constrictors](/blog/boa-constrictor-handling-guide/), one handler per 3 to 4 feet, and a full-grown Argentine is past it: two people for any job that needs it held still.

### If it bites

ReptiFiles' protocol: wash with mild soap and water, and if the wound is deep or severe, go to the hospital, because stitches and antibiotics are likely. Haddad and colleagues' 2008 case report is a bite that fractured a fingertip and needed prophylactic antibiotics and a skin graft. LafeberVet's prevention: feed from a bowl or with tongs.

## Brumation: A Genuine Seasonal Behavior, Even in Captivity

Argentine tegus brumate, slowing down, eating less, and burrowing for roughly 2 to 4 months during cooler periods, even when kept as pets indoors. This is triggered by shortening day length and changes in humidity and atmospheric pressure, not just temperature alone. Brumation isn't required for a non-breeding pet tegu, and skipping it isn't harmful, but many keepers find allowing it improves their tegu's feeding response and overall health afterward. Notably, Colombian tegus generally don't brumate the way Argentine tegus do, worth knowing if you're comparing the two species.

For handling, that season is closed. LafeberVet's care sheet has many captive tegus retiring to their burrows from about mid-September, once temperatures dip into the 70s F, unless enclosure heating is raised. A burrow counts as a hide, so leave it. When the tegu is up and eating again, start over with short, frequent sessions.

If you're weighing a tegu against another large lizard with a very different temperament, the [green iguana](/blog/green-iguana-handling-guide/) is a strictly herbivorous, arboreal species that doesn't tame anywhere near as reliably, closer to the savannah monitor's demanding end of the spectrum than the tegu's.
