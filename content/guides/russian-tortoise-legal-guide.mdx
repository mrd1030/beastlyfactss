Of the twenty-eight species we have checked state by state, the Russian tortoise is the one with the fewest restrictions in the country. No state bans it. One state requires a permit. Hawaii, which bans almost everything, expressly allows it.

That makes the legal question easy and the practical question harder, because the rule that actually shapes how Russian tortoises are bought and sold is federal, it says nothing about the species, and it is misquoted more often than any other rule in the pet trade.

## The Four-Inch Rule Is Not What You Think

Almost everyone who has kept a turtle has heard some version of it: turtles under four inches are illegal. That is not what the regulation says, and the gap between the two matters.

The rule is 21 CFR 1240.62, an FDA regulation from 1975 written to reduce Salmonella transmission to small children. Its operative sentence is short:

> viable turtle eggs and live turtles with a carapace length of less than 4 inches shall not be sold, held for sale, or offered for any other type of commercial or public distribution

Sold. Held for sale. Offered for distribution. Nothing about owning one, and nothing about the species. It applies to a hatchling Russian tortoise for the same reason it applies to a hatchling red-eared slider: the shell is under four inches.

Then comes the part almost nobody quotes. Subsection (c) lists the exceptions, and the second one reads:

> The sale, holding for sale, and distribution of live turtles and viable turtle eggs not in connection with a business.

A private keeper rehoming a hatchling is not a business. A breeder selling them is. That single clause is why hatchlings move hand to hand at reptile shows and through private breeders while pet stores stock only animals past the four-inch line, and it is the real reason a Russian tortoise you buy is usually already a few years old.

## Hawaii Says Yes, Which Is the Surprise

Hawaii runs an inverted system. Under HAR § 4-71-6, "the introduction into Hawaii of live animals or live non-domestic animals as defined in this chapter at any stage of development is prohibited except for those animals on the lists" of approved species. Nothing has to be banned by name. Not being listed is enough.

That default is why hedgehogs, ferrets, gerbils and every snake are out of the question there. So the reasonable assumption is that a tortoise is too.

It is not. Hawaii's List of Conditionally Approved Animals runs through Family Testudinidae species by species, excluding the Galapagos tortoise, the radiated tortoise and three species of *Gopherus* among others, and then finishes with a single line:

> Testudo (all species in genus)

*Testudo horsfieldii* is in that genus. Conditional approval is not the same as unrestricted, it means importation and possession are allowed subject to the conditions the Board of Agriculture attaches, but it is a real route where most exotics have none.

<FunFact>
Montana writes the federal size rule into its own statute rather than leaving it to the FDA. MCA § 87-5-706 clears "all nonnative tropical and subtropical species of turtles with a carapace or shell length of more than 4 inches and not on the controlled or prohibited lists," then lists Testudinidae among the families included. A state that bans the tarantula by omission from its white list is perfectly happy with your tortoise, as long as it has grown past four inches.
</FunFact>

## New Jersey Is the One Permit

New Jersey works from a closed list too, though it is less absolute than Hawaii's. N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.4 sets out the exotic species exempted from permitting, and 7:25-4.5 then requires a permit for any other exotic species that is not on it.

The Russian tortoise is not exempted, so the permit applies. This is a personal possession permit rather than a commercial one, which puts New Jersey in the same bracket it occupies for hedgehogs and sugar gliders: not a ban, but paperwork before the animal arrives rather than after.

## Rhode Island Names the Exceptions Instead

Rhode Island's rule is worth reading because it shows how a state can regulate tortoises tightly without touching this one. Its exotic animal regulations say:

> All exotic species of turtles may be kept without an exotic animal possession permit, except endangered species.

Three tortoises are then pulled back out and do need a permit: the Chaco tortoise, everything in the genus *Gopherus*, and the pancake tortoise. The Russian tortoise is none of them. Meanwhile Rhode Island's native turtles, the snapping turtle among them, cannot be kept as pets at all.

That is the shape of tortoise law across most of the country. States care intensely about their own native turtles and about a handful of species with conservation or invasive-risk problems, and the Russian tortoise sits outside both categories.

## What Actually Constrains You

Since the law barely does, the constraints worth planning around are the ones the species imposes. ## The Real Takeaway

You can keep a Russian tortoise in every state in the country. New Jersey wants a permit first. Hawaii allows it on conditions, which for Hawaii is generous. Everywhere else the state has nothing to say, so your city ordinance is the only thing left to check.

The four-inch rule will still shape what you can buy and from whom, and it is worth understanding correctly before you assume a seller is doing something shady. A private keeper handing over a two-inch hatchling is inside the regulation. A shop doing the same thing is not.
