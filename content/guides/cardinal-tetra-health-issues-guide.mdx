# Common Cardinal Tetra Health Issues (Including a Disease Named After a Different Fish)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

The cardinal tetra is a small characin from the soft, acidic forest streams of the Rio Negro and Orinoco drainages, and a significant share of the fish in the trade are still caught wild. Most cardinal tetra health problems are treatable and trace back to water quality and stability. One isn't, and the odd part is that it's named after a different species entirely.

## Signs That Need Action Today

Healthy cardinals eat eagerly at a regular feeding time, and sick fish usually stop feeding before they start dying. A fish that holds back at feeding time is the first thing to notice, and the time it stopped is worth writing down, since a fish lab will ask.

**Test ammonia and nitrite first:**

- Fast breathing or gasping at the surface.
- Several fish eating less or turning lethargic at once.
- Floating at the surface unable to swim down, or struggling to rise from the bottom: swim bladder disorder.
- A dead fish. It comes out of the tank before anything else, since the body is what spreads neon tetra disease.

**Start ich treatment today:**

- Small white spots like grains of salt: ich.

**Move the fish to a hospital tank today:**

- Restless or erratic swimming, often more noticeable at night, or difficulty swimming normally.
- Fading or patchy color, visible lumps under the skin, or a curved or deformed spine in advanced cases.

**Watch closely and check again tomorrow:**

- Fin edges discoloring, then fraying: fin rot.
- Cottony growth or brown matted patches on the skin or fins: fungus.
- Clamped fins, or a fish growing thin while it eats.

When fish look sick, the water is the first thing to check, and ammonia gets tested any time behavior or feeding changes. Only once the readings are clean does the search move on to a parasite or an infection.

Color needs a second look before it counts as a sign. Cardinals turn lighter in bright conditions over a pale substrate or background in clear water, so a pale school under a strong light is more likely the light than a disease. The pallor that matters comes with restless, abnormal swimming, which is how the muscle damage of neon tetra disease shows.

## Water Quality Comes First

Cardinals are kept warm and soft, 73 to 84°F and 1 to 12 dGH. In a properly cycled tank, a stable temperature maintained with a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">aquarium heater</AffiliateLink> and regular partial water changes using a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rg5gTT" product="Water conditioner">water conditioner</AffiliateLink> prevent far more than any medication does after the fact.

Cardinal tetras have a reputation for being delicate about water chemistry, and a 2008 laboratory study on this species put numbers on it. Every fish survived 96 hours anywhere from pH 4.0 to 8.5. Half of them died at 23.7 mg/L of total ammonia and at just 1.1 mg/L of nitrite, so the fish is sensitive to nitrite. Cold was the other weak point: deaths rose below about 19.6°C (67°F) and reached the whole group at 15°C (59°F). Those were 96-hour tests on tiny wild juveniles, so they mark where deaths begin and not what is safe: ammonia must read zero, and nitrite is the number to watch closely during the first weeks of a new tank.

Where cardinal tetras were sampled along about 280 miles of the middle Rio Negro, the water was strongly acidic, with a pH between 3.35 and 5.82 and only one of fourteen sites reading above 5.0. It was also very low in dissolved minerals.
</FunFact>

## Ich: Medicate Without Copper, Heat as the Backup

Small white spots resembling grains of salt, caused by the parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*, which burrows into skin and gill tissue before forming cysts that release new infective stages. Alongside the spots, watch for flashing against decor, rapid breathing, and lethargy.

This one is treatable. A commercial ich medication redosed every 24 hours alongside a partial water change each day, carried on until the deaths stop and a day past the last visible spot, clears it. Choose one without copper: in soft cardinal water it is the riskiest option, for the reason given under copper below.

Heat is the other option. Cardinal tetra tolerates water reaching 86°F, the temperature most ich strains cannot survive, so the [ich heat method](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/) is possible: raise the tank slowly, a couple of degrees a day, with strong aeration running, and watch the fish closely. That is the very top of the cardinal's range, so medication is the safer route and heat the backup.

## Fin Rot and Fungus: Damage First, Then Infection

Fin rot is a bacterial infection that frays the fin edges as infected tissue dies off. It progresses in stages: discoloration on the fin edges first, then fraying, and in advanced untreated cases the infection spreading to the body itself. It stems from poor water quality, fin-nipping tankmates, or general stress. Treatment means correcting the water conditions that caused it alongside an antibacterial medication. Most fish recover within one to two weeks with consistent care.

Fungus follows the same route in. Fungal spores are everywhere in aquarium water and do not usually cause disease in a healthy fish. After a parasite, a bacterial infection or a handling injury, the water mold colonizes the damaged tissue, appearing as a cottony growth or as brown matted areas. These water molds, Saprolegnia among them, are fungus-like organisms and not true fungi, and poor water quality and low temperatures are the usual triggers. Cold is also this species' documented weak point. Formalin or potassium permanganate can clear most fungal infections that are not too severe, but the fungus is a secondary problem, and the original one has to be found and corrected too.

## Internal Parasites

Spironucleus shows as weight loss and a poor appetite, with deaths among young fish. Metronidazole is the only treatment for it, and it can be given as a bath to a fish that has stopped eating. Camallanus shows as worms protruding from the vent and is treated with fenbendazole or levamisole. Heavy tapeworm loads can stunt growth, swell the belly and block the gut, and praziquantel is the drug of choice.

One class of drug stays out of a cardinal tank: organophosphates, which should be avoided in systems holding some characins, and the cardinal is a characin. Which parasite is present shows only under a microscope, so the drug is a vet's call made after that look.

## Neon Tetra Disease and Its Lookalikes

This condition takes its name from neon tetra, and is caused by a parasite, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, that invades the fish's muscle tissue. It affects tetras broadly, along with angelfish, rasboras, barbs, and zebrafish, cardinal tetra's own family among them, rather than being confined to its namesake species.

This is highly contagious, spreading when fish consume infected tissue, including from a fish that's already died. The disease runs the same course in its namesake. It is not transmissible to humans.

There is no cure. Once symptoms appear, the fish will not recover, and humane euthanasia for a confirmed case ends suffering and protects the rest of the tank. Prevention is the only tool available, and it rests mainly on quarantine, the single most important step for every fish added: new fish spend at least 30 days in it before joining an established tank. Beyond that, buy from sources with healthy stock, and never let tankmates scavenge a fish that died from an unknown cause.

Two lookalikes complicate the call. Columnaris, a bacterial infection that responds to treatment, mimics the disease closely enough in the [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-health-issues-guide/) and its relatives that a suspect fish goes to a hospital tank on an antibacterial course before anyone gives up on it. Mycobacteriosis is the harder one. Tetras are among the fish families more prone to it, it causes chronic wasting, scale loss and skin ulcers, and like NTD it can leave a fish pale with a deformed spine. There is no effective treatment for infected fish, so prevention through quarantine and disinfection carries all the weight. The bacteria do well in warm, acidic water that is low in oxygen and high in humic and fulvic acids, conditions a warm blackwater tank can drift toward.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems, floating at the surface unable to swim down, or struggling to rise from the bottom. Poor water quality is the most common and most overlooked cause, though overfeeding, stress, and physical injury can also play a role. Correcting water quality and adjusting feeding usually resolves it; fish can often live long, healthy lives with proper care even where the disorder doesn't fully clear.

## Copper, Soft Water and Shrimp

Copper's danger to fish depends on total alkalinity, which is the reading to take before using it, not hardness or pH. As alkalinity falls, copper turns more toxic, and under 50 ppm, about 2.8 dKH, copper treatment is not recommended because it is likely to kill the fish. A soft, acidic cardinal tank with little KH can run below that.

Invertebrates are at risk at any alkalinity, since copper is toxic to them too. Before any medication goes into a tank with shrimp or snails, they are moved out, and no product is treated as an exception to that. The hospital tank is the place to medicate in any case.

## Wild-Caught Stock and the First Weeks

A significant proportion of the cardinals in the trade are still wild caught, or come from community-led fisheries such as Project Piaba. Much of the mortality on the journey from stream to shop is probably stress induced. On the Rio Negro, monitoring of shipments found good water at capture and at the fishing camps, but poor water at the holding facilities in Barcelos and on the boat to Manaus, with large swings in oxygen and ammonia. Hundreds of cardinals ride together, 600 to 1,000 to a tub of about 2 gallons, on the first leg, and the pH they live in climbs from about 4.6 at capture to about 6.2 at the exporters. Before export, fish are often bathed in antibiotics, dyes or salt.

Estimates of loss vary widely. Exporters have claimed up to 70% in capture and transport but under 10% in their own quarantine, and the general agreement is that the worst losses come in capture and transport. In one measured 24-hour run from Barcelos to Manaus, about 95% of the control fish survived, but dissolved oxygen in the water fell close to or below 2 mg/L within three hours.

The fish that reach a shop carry that trip with them, and wild fish cope worse with deteriorating water than commercially bred ones. Transport stress leaves a progressive decline in general health and perhaps a lowered resistance to disease, and apparently healthy wild fish often carry heavy parasite burdens. Buying from a shop that has held its cardinals a while puts the worst weeks behind them. At home, wild stock goes through a rigorous quarantine before it meets the regular population, because acquiring wild fish is risky.

## Quarantine

A new fish stays in it [at least 30 days](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/), however healthy it looks, because active disease in shipped fish often shows one to three weeks after arrival. Thirty to 60 days suits a fish worth being sure of.

Adding a fish to the quarantine tank restarts the clock for everything in it. A school that has shown mycobacteriosis is best kept closed, with no new fish added at all.

## When a Fish Vet Is Worth It, and How to Find One

Fish are often treated with the wrong over-the-counter medication, and when the first treatment fails and another is tried, the cycle usually does more harm than good. The diseases above are told apart under a microscope or in a lab, which is the work a fish vet does.

Licenses do not carry a vet across state lines, but consulting does: any vet can work a case with an aquatic vet in any state, so a local exotic or small-animal practice is a sound place to start. For a fish vet directly, the American Association of Fish Veterinarians lists its vets by state or country, and a practicing fish vet's guide names the World Aquatic Veterinary Medical Association for keepers outside North America. Advice online, without an exam, cannot be the legal basis for a treatment plan.

For the visit:

- **Water.** About a quart in a clean container, taken as soon as the fish begin acting abnormally and before any water change starts, and kept cold. Transport water does not reflect the tank, since its pH falls and ammonia rises on the way.
- **The fish.** The best sample is one near death or in distress. Dead fish are rarely acceptable, and the sample is never frozen.
- **Records.** Tank size and age, the species and how many, recent additions, when the signs began, the food and how much of it, the last water change, and the last treatment with its dose.

## When a Fish Has to Be Put Down

Neon tetra disease is a condition where euthanasia is the right call once confirmed, and a fish vet is the first choice for carrying it out. Where none can be reached, the humane home method for an aquarium fish is a [clove oil overdose](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) in a container of the fish's own tank water, and flushing and freezing a live fish are among the methods to rule out. Remove the fish from the display tank before anything else, since the body is what spreads the parasite to the rest of the school.
