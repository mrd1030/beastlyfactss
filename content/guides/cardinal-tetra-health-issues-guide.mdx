# Common Cardinal Tetra Health Issues (Including a Disease Named After a Different Fish)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most cardinal tetra health problems are treatable and trace back to water quality. One isn't, and the odd part is that it's named after a different species entirely.

## Neon Tetra Disease

This condition takes its name from neon tetra, and is caused by a parasite, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, that invades the fish's muscle tissue. It affects tetras broadly, along with angelfish, rasboras, barbs, and zebrafish, cardinal tetra's own family among them, rather than being confined to its namesake species.

Watch for: restless or erratic swimming, often more noticeable at night, fading or patchy color, visible lumps under the skin, a curved or deformed spine in advanced cases, and difficulty swimming normally.

This is highly contagious, spreading when fish consume infected tissue, including from a fish that's already died. The disease runs the same course in its namesake, and the [neon tetra's version of it](/blog/neon-tetra-health-issues-guide/) is where the outbreak pattern in a mixed school is laid out. It is not transmissible to humans.

There is no cure. Once symptoms appear, the fish will not recover, and humane euthanasia for a confirmed case ends suffering and protects the rest of the tank. Prevention is the only tool available: quarantine new fish for at least 30 days before adding them to an established tank, buy from sources with healthy stock, and never let tankmates scavenge a fish that died from an unknown cause.

## When a Fish Has to Be Put Down

Neon tetra disease is the condition on this list where euthanasia is the right call once confirmed, and a fish vet is the first choice for carrying it out. Where none can be reached, the humane home method for an aquarium fish is a clove oil overdose in a container of the fish's own tank water, and the dose, timing and the methods to rule out, flushing and freezing a live fish among them, are laid out in [the fish euthanasia section](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/). Remove the fish from the display tank before anything else, since the body is what spreads the parasite to the rest of the school.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt, caused by the parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*, which burrows into skin and gill tissue before forming cysts that release new infective stages. Alongside the spots, watch for flashing against decor, rapid breathing, and lethargy.

This one is treatable. A commercial ich medication redosed every 24 hours alongside a partial water change each day, carried on until the deaths stop and a day past the last visible spot, clears it. Heat is the other option. Cardinal tetra tolerates water reaching 86°F, the temperature most ich strains cannot survive, so the [ich heat method](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/) is possible: raise the tank slowly, a couple of degrees a day, with strong aeration running, and watch the fish closely. That is the very top of the cardinal's range, so medication is the safer route and heat the backup.

## Fin Rot

A bacterial infection that frays the fin edges as infected tissue dies off. It progresses in stages: discoloration on the fin edges first, then fraying, and in advanced untreated cases the infection spreading to the body itself. It stems from poor water quality, fin-nipping tankmates, or general stress. Treatment means correcting the water conditions that caused it alongside an antibacterial medication. Most fish recover within one to two weeks with consistent care.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems, floating at the surface unable to swim down, or struggling to rise from the bottom. Poor water quality is the most common and most overlooked cause, though overfeeding, stress, and physical injury can also play a role. Correcting water quality and adjusting feeding usually resolves it; fish can often live long, healthy lives with proper care even where the disorder doesn't fully clear.

## What the Lab Numbers Say About Water

Cardinal tetras have a reputation for being delicate about water chemistry, and laboratory tolerance testing puts numbers on it. Every fish survived 96 hours anywhere from pH 4.0 to 8.5. Half of them died at 23.7 mg/L of total ammonia and at just 1.1 mg/L of nitrite, so the fish is sensitive to nitrite. Cold was the other weak point: deaths rose below about 19.6°C (67°F) and reached the whole group at 15°C (59°F). Those were 96-hour tests on tiny wild juveniles, so they mark where deaths begin and not what is safe: ammonia must read zero, and nitrite is the number to watch closely during the first weeks of a new tank.
