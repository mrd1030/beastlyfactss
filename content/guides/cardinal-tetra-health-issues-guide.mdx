# Common Cardinal Tetra Health Issues (Including a Disease Named After a Different Fish)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most cardinal tetra health problems are treatable and trace back to water quality. One isn't, and the odd part is that it's named after a different species entirely.

## Neon Tetra Disease

This condition takes its name from neon tetra, and is caused by a parasite, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, that invades the fish's muscle tissue. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/aquarium-fish/mycotic-diseases-of-fish) documents it affecting tetras broadly, along with angelfish, rasboras, barbs, and zebrafish, cardinal tetra's own family among them, rather than being confined to its namesake species.

Watch for: restless or erratic swimming, often more noticeable at night, fading or patchy color, visible lumps under the skin, a curved or deformed spine in advanced cases, and difficulty swimming normally.

This is highly contagious, spreading when fish consume infected tissue, including from a fish that's already died. Our [Neon tetra health issues guide](/blog/neon-tetra-health-issues-guide/) covers the disease from that species' side in more detail, worth reading since it crosses over between the two. It is not transmissible to humans.

There is no cure. Once symptoms appear, the fish will not recover, and the honest, difficult recommendation from aquatic specialists is humane euthanasia for a confirmed case, both to end suffering and to protect the rest of the tank. Prevention is genuinely the only tool available: quarantine new fish before adding them to an established tank, buy from sources with healthy stock, and never let tankmates scavenge a fish that died from an unknown cause.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt, caused by the parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*, which burrows into skin and gill tissue before forming cysts that release new infective stages, according to [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/fish-white-spot-disease-ich). Alongside the spots, watch for flashing against decor, rapid breathing, and lethargy.

This one is treatable. [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/how-to-treat-ich-on-freshwater-fish) recommends a commercial ich medication alongside daily partial water changes, and notes that quarantining any new fish for a full 30 days before introduction is the most effective way to keep it out of an established tank in the first place.

## Fin Rot

A bacterial infection that [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/fin-rot) describes progressing in stages: discoloration on the fin edges first, then fraying as infected tissue dies off, and in advanced untreated cases the infection spreading to the body itself. It stems from poor water quality, fin-nipping tankmates, or general stress. Treatment means correcting the water conditions that caused it alongside an antibacterial medication, and most fish recover within one to two weeks with consistent care.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems, floating at the surface unable to swim down, or struggling to rise from the bottom. Poor water quality is the most common and most overlooked cause, though overfeeding, stress, and physical injury can also play a role. Correcting water quality and adjusting feeding usually resolves it; fish can often live long, healthy lives with proper care even where the disorder doesn't fully clear.

<FunFact>
The received wisdom in the hobby is that cardinal tetra is extremely delicate about water chemistry, full stop. A 2008 laboratory study in [Acta Amazonica](https://acta.inpa.gov.br/fasciculos/38-4/BODY/v38n4a23.html) tested that directly, and the real picture is more specific than the reputation. The fish tolerated surprisingly high ammonia concentrations in testing, likely part of why it survives rough wild-capture and transport conditions as well as it does. Nitrite was a different story: it was the parameter the fish handled worst of the four tested, with meaningful mortality at concentrations that wouldn't trouble a lot of other community fish. None of that is a reason to skip cycling a tank properly, sublethal stress from either compound still matters day to day, but it does explain why nitrite specifically, more than ammonia, is the number worth watching closest during the first few weeks of a new tank.
</FunFact>
