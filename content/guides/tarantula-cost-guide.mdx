## Paying for a Female: How Sexing Works

A female Chilean rose hair outlives a male by a decade or more, so the sex of the spider is the biggest single factor in what the purchase buys. Young males and females look almost identical, and the difference only becomes obvious when a male makes his final molt and grows bulbs on the tips of his palps and, in many species, hooks under the first pair of legs. A spider already showing those is a mature male with a short time left.

Before that, sexing works from the shed skin. A female sheds the lining of her spermathecae, the sperm-storage organs, along with the rest of the exoskeleton. Opened out and laid flat, the abdomen of her molt shows them as a flap between the front pair of book lungs, while a male's molt shows only a plain line there. From around a 2 inch leg span an experienced keeper can read a molt under a stereo microscope, and on large adult molts the structures are visible to the naked eye. Checking the live spider's underside is the backup method: males carry a small patch of extra spinnerets just in front of the furrow between the front book lungs. It is less reliable, and hard to read at all on young spiders.

What that means at the point of sale:

- **Unsexed slings** are the cheapest route, and any one of them may turn out male.
- **A "sexed female"** should come with a photo of the molt she was sexed from. A guess from the underside, or from body shape, is not the same product.
- **Sexing your own later** is free: keep each molt, soften it, and have it checked.

## Choosing a Seller

Buy captive-bred. Captive-bred spiders are less likely to carry oral nematodes, the parasite that turns up mostly in wild-caught imports and can spread through a whole collection, and buying them takes pressure off wild populations. The Chilean rose hair is one of the species most often raised as a collection concern. *Brachypelma*, *Tliltocatl* and *Poecilotheria* are listed on CITES Appendix II, but most tarantulas are not, and uncovered species move through the trade with far less scrutiny.

Four questions sort sellers quickly:

1. Is the spider captive-bred or wild-caught, and will the seller say so plainly?
2. Is the species one that is commonly bred in captivity?
3. Is it a newly described or vulnerable species that should raise questions about where it came from?
4. Does the supply chain look straightforward?

On arrival, or at an expo table, look for a plump abdomen. A shrunken, wrinkled one means dehydration, common in spiders kept badly in pet shops, and a white mass around the mouth is the nematode sign to walk away from. New spiders go through the same [invertebrate quarantine](/blog/invertebrate-quarantine-cleaning-and-escapes-guide/) as any other arrival before sharing a room's tools with an existing collection.

## Vet Care and Insurance

There's no pet insurance for tarantulas, and veterinary care is rare, many areas simply don't have a vet equipped to treat one. This isn't a species where you're budgeting for regular vet visits the way you might with a mammal or bird. A small emergency fund is still worth having, but it's a much smaller consideration here than with most pets.

<FunFact>
Female tarantulas keep molting throughout their entire adult lives, unlike most invertebrates that stop once they reach maturity. Males stop molting for good once they mature, which is a big part of why they live so much shorter than females, about five years total for a male Chilean rose hair against 15 to 20 or more for a female.
</FunFact>

## The Number to Sit With: Longevity

Female Chilean rose hairs commonly live 15 to 20 years or more, males considerably less: about 5 years, with 10 the outside figure. If you're getting a female, you're looking at a long-term commitment on a very small ongoing budget. This is arguably the cheapest long-lived exotic pet you can keep, low setup cost, low monthly cost, and no vet bills in the typical case, but the time commitment is real and worth thinking through before you buy.
