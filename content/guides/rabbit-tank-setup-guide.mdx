# Rabbit Housing: Why the Old Pet-Store Cage Isn't Enough

<AffiliateDisclosure />

If you've seen a small wire hutch marketed as a "rabbit cage," it's worth knowing upfront: current welfare standards consider that size inadequate. Rabbit housing guidance has shifted substantially, and the space rabbits need is much larger than what's traditionally been sold for them.

<Figure src="/assets/images/rabbit-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a rabbit exercise pen showing the mesh walls, litter box with hay, safe flooring, and hay rack" caption="The old wire hutch isn't enough. Rabbits need real exercise space, and indoor housing more than doubles their lifespan, 8-12 years versus about 3-5 outdoors." shareable />

## How Much Space, Exactly

The standard is at least 8 square feet of enclosure space combined with at least 24 square feet of exercise space for one to two rabbits, space where they can run and play for a minimum of 5 hours a day. In practice, this means a large foldable metal exercise pen, 4 feet by 4 feet or bigger, is the realistic standard, not a small cage rabbits are let out of occasionally.

## Scaling Up for Large and Giant Breeds

Those figures describe an average rabbit, and pet rabbits run from about 2.5 pounds to more than 20. Between the two extremes, medium breeds weigh about 7 to 12 pounds. The rule that scales is proportional: a rabbit's sleeping and resting area should be about four times its own size, and any cage used as a bedroom should run 3 to 4 times its stretched-out length. A 4 by 4 foot pen suits most rabbits. It does not suit a giant.

A Flemish Giant weighs 15 to 20 pounds and stretches to 2.5 to 4 feet long. Its base enclosure needs to be at least 36 inches wide, 36 inches tall and 120 inches long, about 30 square feet on its own, nearly four times the 8 square foot figure above, and that size doubles for each additional rabbit. Floor time outside it matters just as much: giants need plenty of supervised exercise, and the breed is prone to obesity.

Weight also changes the flooring question. A heavy rabbit puts more pressure on its hocks, so a solid floor with cushioning, such as fleece over a yoga mat, is the standard for giants. Carpet is abrasive and best avoided, and the floor has to be kept clean so urine and droppings do not cake onto the feet.

## Breed Shape Changes the Care

Coat and face shape change the routine more than size does. Long-haired rabbits need brushing more often to prevent matting, and they are at greater risk of flystrike. Rex rabbits have less fur protecting their feet, and giants carry more weight on theirs, so both are more prone to sore hocks and need soft, dry flooring and a close eye on weight. Lops and the flat-faced dwarf and mini breeds are more likely to have dental problems, which is why it is worth lifting the lips once a week to check that the front teeth meet cleanly. Only a vet should correct overgrown or misaligned teeth, and the [rabbit health guide](/blog/rabbit-health-issues-guide/) covers what that involves.

## Indoors Is Strongly the Better Choice

Indoor rabbits typically live 8 to 12 years, and many reach 14 or even 16. Outdoor rabbits average closer to 3 to 5, due to predators, extreme weather, and health problems that go unnoticed without daily close contact. If you're deciding where a rabbit will live, indoor housing is the option that actually protects it.

Avoid aquariums or any solid-walled enclosure. Poor ventilation lets ammonia from urine build up, which irritates a rabbit's respiratory tract over time, the same reason [mice do badly in a glass enclosure](/blog/mouse-tank-setup-guide/).

## Flooring

Skip wire flooring entirely, it contributes to sore hocks. Vinyl or linoleum over plywood, washable rugs, or fleece all give better traction and are easier on a rabbit's feet. A waterproof tarp underneath the whole setup protects your actual floor from the inevitable accidents.

## Cleaning

Clean the toilet area every day and the whole enclosure about once a week, more often if the rabbit soils it. Use non-toxic cleaners, and make sure everything is dry before the rabbit goes back in. A clean-out disrupts a rabbit's routine, so put a little of the used bedding back to leave a familiar smell. Keep the toilet area apart from where the rabbit sleeps.

Frequent cleaning is health care as well as housekeeping. Ammonia from built-up urine and manure irritates the airways and sets a rabbit up for respiratory disease indoors, and in a warm spot the same mess draws flies, which is how flystrike starts. In warm weather, look at the rabbit's rear end, fur, and skin twice a day. Fly eggs or any maggot in the fur mean an emergency vet appointment at once. Do not try to pick the maggots off or treat the rabbit at home, and do not dunk it in water to drown them: both cause pain and can send the rabbit into shock.

## Litter Training

Rabbits naturally tend to eliminate while they eat, so the trick is putting hay directly in or right over the litter box, this alone does most of the training work. Use a large, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3SPggIn" product="Litter box">cat-litter-box-style container</AffiliateLink>, not the small corner boxes often marketed specifically for rabbits, and fill it with <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4voH9Ap" product="Small-pet paper bedding">paper-based</AffiliateLink> or compressed wood-pellet litter.

**Avoid clumping cat litter and pine or cedar shavings entirely.** Clumping litter can cause serious problems if ingested, and pine and cedar release aromatic oils that irritate a rabbit's respiratory system. Spaying or neutering also meaningfully improves litter habits, so if litter training isn't going well with an intact rabbit, that's often the underlying reason.

<FunFact>
Rabbits eliminate almost reflexively while eating, which is exactly why placing hay directly over the litter box works so well as a training shortcut. It's less about teaching a new behavior and more about routing an existing one to the right spot.
</FunFact>

## When You Are Away

Appetite, drinking, droppings, and weight are checked every day, and a rabbit's rear end twice a day in warm weather, and that job passes to someone else while you are gone. Having a person look after the rabbit in its own home is usually best, because it keeps things familiar. A sitter should know what normal droppings look like and have the vet's number. A rabbit that has to stay somewhere else takes some used, unsoiled nesting material from home.

## Rabbit-Proofing the Room

A rabbit loose in the house will bite through anything that looks like a cord or a string, and the shock from a live electric cord is the most immediate danger it faces. Encase every cord the rabbit can reach in split plastic tubing, or block cords and outlets with furniture. Cords that run across its path or under the couch are the most tempting. This is the single most important step in preparing a room, so do it before the rabbit comes home, and keep the pen ready while you check the rest.

Rabbits cannot tell a poisonous plant by taste or smell, so identify every houseplant and move the poisonous ones out of reach. Carpet fiber swallowed in quantity can block the gut, which is an emergency, so give hay, plain wood, cardboard or untreated straw mats to chew and keep the rabbit on an uncarpeted floor where you can. Chewing slows down after neutering, but it never stops, because a rabbit's teeth never stop growing. Rabbits under a year old also jump up, over and into furniture, so a young rabbit needs watching until it settles.

## If a Rabbit Does Live Outside

A backyard hutch does not protect a rabbit from predators. Dogs, feral cats, raccoons and coyotes bend wire and open latches, and the presence of a predator alone can send a rabbit into a panic that ends in injury. Most predators hunt at night, so an outdoor rabbit comes inside each evening, and a sturdy covered pen is enough by day when people are close by. Dirt in a yard pen holds harmful bacteria and invites tunnels the rabbit can vanish into, so give the digging instinct a box of clean straw instead.

Shade and airflow come first in warm weather, because rabbits overheat. Wet towels hung over the side of the enclosure, or ice frozen in cardboard cartons set inside, cool a rabbit that has to sit out the heat. The quiet danger is inattention: a rabbit that is fed and watered but rarely handled or checked develops problems that nobody notices until it is too late. Check the yard for poisonous plants and for lawn chemicals, and look at the rabbit's rear end once or twice a day for flystrike.
