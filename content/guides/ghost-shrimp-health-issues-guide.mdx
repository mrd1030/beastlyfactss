# Common Ghost Shrimp Health Issues (Most Trace Back to Where They Came From)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ghost shrimp are hardy, and that reputation is earned. But "hardy" hides an important caveat: a real share of what goes wrong with a newly bought ghost shrimp traces back to the crowded feeder tank it sat in before you brought it home, not to any inherent fragility in the species itself.

## Feeder-Tank Stress, the Leading Cause of Early Losses

Ghost shrimp move through the trade as live feed as well as cleanup crew, so many of them sit in feeder tanks rather than tanks run to pet-quality standards. A feeder tank is built for volume and turnover: new stock goes in without quarantine, and whatever is sick shares the same crowded water with everything else.

**Watch for:** lethargy, staying hidden far more than the first day or two of normal settling-in, refusing food, and rapid, unexplained losses within a small group shortly after purchase.

**Prevention:** quarantine new shrimp for 2 to 4 weeks in a separate <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4pjiyeT" product="10 gallon tank">simple tank</AffiliateLink> with its own gentle <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> before adding them to an established display, and where possible, buy from a source that's clearly selling shrimp as pets, over one pulling straight from a bargain feeder tank.

## Copper Sensitivity: The One Rule That's Non-Negotiable

This applies to every freshwater shrimp, not just ghost shrimp, but it's worth restating plainly: copper is lethal to shrimp at concentrations that barely trouble a fish. Jen-Lee Yang's 2014 toxicity study on *Macrobrachium nipponense*, a freshwater prawn in the same family as the ghost shrimp, found that 0.0313 mg/L of copper killed half the test animals within 96 hours, and proposed 0.003 mg/L as a safe level. Copper-based fish medications, some plant fertilizers, and old copper household plumbing can all put more than that in the water.

<FunFact>
Shrimp are more vulnerable to copper than fish for a real physiological reason, not just a general "invertebrates are delicate" rule of thumb. Their gills and thin, permeable exoskeleton absorb dissolved metals efficiently as part of normal osmoregulation, and shrimp blood uses a copper-based respiratory pigment called hemocyanin instead of the iron-based hemoglobin fish and mammals rely on. That means the same metal a shrimp's body already depends on in trace amounts becomes directly toxic at only slightly elevated concentrations, there's very little buffer between "essential" and "lethal."
</FunFact>

Never dose a shrimp tank with a copper-based medication, and check that any fertilizer or algae treatment is explicitly labeled invertebrate-safe before using it near shrimp.

## After a Positive Copper Test

**Move the shrimp first.** Every hour in the tank is exposure, so the shrimp go into a bucket or spare tank of water that tests zero before anything else happens. The tap is a common culprit. Most copper in household water comes from corroding pipes, fittings and brass faucets. It builds up while water sits in the plumbing overnight, and hot water dissolves more of it than cold. Running the cold tap for about a minute clears what accumulated. The drinking-water action level is 1.3 mg per liter, more than 40 times the concentration that killed half the prawns in that study, so water that is safe to drink can still be unsafe for shrimp. Test the refill water as well as the tank.

**Pull the copper out of the tank.** A dedicated copper adsorbent removes all forms of copper, chelated copper included, and works faster than activated carbon. Seachem's CupriSorb is dosed at 250 mL per 150 US gallons. Run it alongside partial water changes, and test with a copper kit as you go.

**Keep going after the test reads zero.** Copper that settles into the gravel does not show on a water test, and it leaches back out over time. Seachem's directions keep its adsorbent running for at least two weeks after tests show no copper, specifically to catch what leaches from the substrate.

**Then return the shrimp.** Only once the tank has read zero across that two-week tail, with the adsorbent still in, do the shrimp go back.

## Water Quality Sensitivity

Ghost shrimp genuinely tolerate a wider range of temperature, pH, and hardness than most other aquarium shrimp, part of why they're cheap enough to sell in bulk, but tolerant doesn't mean thriving in anything. Ammonia and nitrite both need to read zero before stocking, and stable conditions matter more than hitting one exact number.

## Molting Problems

[Shrimp Science](https://www.shrimpscience.com/articles/molting-and-common-problems/) traces most molting failures back to two causes: "water parameters and diet." Specifically, [inadequate calcium and general hardness](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) leave a new shell unable to harden correctly, while a shrimp about to molt typically goes still and stops eating for an hour or more beforehand, normal behavior worth recognizing rather than mistaking for illness.

The most serious failure is what's commonly called the white ring of death, a solid band that forms around the shrimp's body where the shell should split cleanly at the head instead, trapping the animal between old and new exoskeleton. Once the ring is visible the shell has already failed and the shrimp usually dies within hours to days; there is no reliable intervention, and holding the old shell with tweezers to give the shrimp leverage is a last resort that usually finishes the job. Prevention is the whole of it, and the [shrimp molting guide](/blog/shrimp-molting-guide/) has the GH numbers and the water change habit that prevent the next one. Molting frequency also changes with age, every 1 to 2 weeks in a fast-growing young shrimp against every 3 to 4 weeks in an adult, so a very young colony will actually show this vulnerable stage more often, not less.

**Prevention:** stable temperature and hardness, and a diet that includes some calcium content, along with enough hiding cover that a molting shrimp isn't an easy target.

## Lifespan Realities

Plan on about a year. [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Palaemonetes_paludosus/) puts this species' life cycle at 6 to 13 months in the wild, and a well-kept tank can stretch that toward a couple of years. Poor water quality, an unresolved molting problem, or a rough start from crowded sourcing are the realistic things that cut a shrimp's life shorter than that baseline, more than any inherent fragility in the species.

## The Pattern Worth Remembering

Nearly everything on this list traces back to four habits: quarantine new arrivals, confirm the tank is cycled before stocking, keep copper at zero, and maintain stable water chemistry for reliable molting.
