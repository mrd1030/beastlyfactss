# Zebra Finch Enrichment: Wild Ones Travel in Twos, Not Flocks

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Zebra finches are sold as flock birds, and the field data on wild ones is more specific than that.

<KeyTakeaway>
Wild zebra finches most often forage, drink and travel in **groups of two**, 94 percent of them mixed-sex and probably pair bonds, or in **groups of three to ten**. Larger groups were rarely seen. The researchers' own recommendation was that captive birds be kept in pairs or small groups, which is a narrower target than either a single bird or a full aviary.
</KeyTakeaway>

For everything else about them, see the [Zebra Finch care guide](/guides/zebra-finch/).

## What the Research Says

**Wild social structure is small.** Observation of wild zebra finches found the commonest unit was a pair, **94.2 percent mixed-sex**, with groups of three to ten also frequent and anything larger uncommon. The paper explicitly recommends pairs or small social groups in captivity.

**Bigger groups changed female stress physiology.** Group housing during adolescence had **long-term effects on the adult stress response in females but not males**. Females raised in larger groups, **independent of social density**, secreted higher glucocorticoid concentrations 45 minutes into restraint, and had higher peak concentrations as adults. Group size was the variable, not crowding.

**And solitary housing is not the alternative.** Individual cages are described in the welfare literature as an impoverished physical and social environment for a gregarious species. Zebra finches also form dominance hierarchies, and introducing males to a new cage typically produces aggression, with serious attacks coming from the heavier dominant bird.

<FunFact>
The density result is the interesting part. It was not that crowded birds were more stressed, which is what you would expect. It was that the number of birds mattered on its own, with density held constant, and only in females.
</FunFact>

## Pairs or Small Groups

The target the evidence points at. Two, or a handful. Not one, and not a crowd.

Mixed-sex pairs match the wild pattern most closely and will breed given a nest site, which is a decision rather than an accident. Same-sex groups avoid that and need enough space, since males in particular sort out a hierarchy on introduction.

Introduce in a large cage instead of a small one, and watch for a lighter bird being singled out. Our [handling guide](/blog/zebra-finch-handling-guide/) covers introductions.

## Nesting Material, With or Without the Nest

Zebra finches carry, shred and arrange material constantly, and it is one of the more obviously enriching things you can provide.

Soft grass, coconut fiber and plain paper strips get worked with all day. Note the consequence: a nest site plus material readily triggers breeding, so if you do not want chicks, provide the material and no nest. Never use loose fibrous nesting fluff, which wraps around legs and toes.

They bathe often, and a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000I6ORHS?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Lixit Quick Lock Bird Cage Bath for Lovebirds, Canaries, Finches, Parakeets, and Cockatiels">shallow bath</AffiliateLink> offered in the morning gets used enthusiastically.

For food, scatter seed in a shallow tray rather than filling a deep cup, hang millet sprays so they have to be worked at, and clip greens to the bars. Small changes, and they turn a thirty second meal into an activity. Our [feeding guide](/blog/zebra-finch-feeding-guide/) covers the diet.

## Priority Order

1. A pair or a small group, not one and not a crowd
2. A long flight cage with a clear flight path
3. Perches of varied diameter at the ends
4. Nesting material to carry and shred
5. Daily bathing water
6. Scattered and hung food, never a full cup
7. Novel objects, last

## What Not to Do

Do not keep a zebra finch alone. Do not assume a bigger flock is automatically better, given the female stress finding. Do not buy a tall cage for a horizontal flier. Do not use loose fibrous nesting fluff, which is a leg entanglement risk. Do not provide a nest site unless you want chicks. Do not introduce males in a small cage.
