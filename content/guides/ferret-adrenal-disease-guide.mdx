# Ferret Adrenal Disease: The Hair Loss Pattern Owners Often Miss

<VetDisclaimer />

Ferrets shed seasonally, so a certain amount of thinning fur is expected and not concerning. Adrenal disease hair loss looks different once you know what to watch for, and it is the most common endocrine tumor in pet ferrets, so recognizing the pattern early makes a real difference in how manageable it is.

## What Adrenal Disease Is

One or both adrenal glands, small glands beside the kidneys, enlarge through hyperplasia, a benign adenoma, or a malignant adenocarcinoma, and begin overproducing sex hormones. Those hormones drive nearly every visible sign. The leading explanation is neutering: without the ovaries or testes, the brain keeps signaling the adrenal glands until they overgrow. The age at which a ferret was neutered is no longer thought to matter much, and diet, genetics and the light cycle have also been proposed as contributors.

## Other Signs

A swollen vulva in a spayed female is a strong and fairly specific sign, sometimes with a mucus discharge. Neutered males may start behaving sexually again. In males, a hormone-enlarged prostate can press on the urethra until the ferret strains or cannot urinate at all, and that is an emergency. Left untreated, advanced disease can also suppress the bone marrow and cause anemia, and a malignant tumor can spread to nearby organs.

## How a Vet Confirms It

No single test settles it. The history and physical exam are often strongly suggestive, and the vet may be able to feel an enlarged left adrenal gland. Ultrasound is the most useful tool, since it shows whether one gland or both are involved. A hormone panel measures the adrenal sex hormones. In a female, the key alternative is an ovarian remnant, a piece of ovary left behind at spaying, which raises the same hormones, so imaging does much of the work of telling the two apart. Bloodwork also checks for insulinoma, another common ferret disease that can be present at the same time.

On those numbers the implant has become the preferred treatment for many ferrets. An older injectable, leuprolide, works the same way but lasts a median of only 3.8 months per dose. Both drugs turn up in avian medicine too, where they are used to switch off [chronic egg laying](/blog/bird-chronic-egg-laying-guide/) by damping the same kind of hormonal signal from the brain.

## What Happens After an Implant

- **First 2 weeks:** signs may get worse before they improve. Some soreness and swelling where the implant went in is normal and settles within 1 to 2 weeks.
- **10 to 14 days:** vulvar swelling and itching start to ease.
- **4 to 6 weeks:** fur starts growing back, and the vulva looks normal by about 6 weeks.
- **8 weeks:** most ferrets have a full coat again, though regrowth is incomplete in some.

Weight gain and lethargy are reported side effects, and some ferrets do not respond. After surgery the timeline runs faster for the prostate, which shrinks within 2 to 3 days, while the coat can take a few months to return.

## The Cost of Managing It

The implant is sold as a once-a-year treatment, and many ferrets go longer before signs return. Expect roughly $170 to $350 for the implant and its placement, plus an exam of about $75 to $80 if the vet needs one first. A ferret diagnosed at 4 may need several implants over the rest of its life, which is a large part of why lifetime vet costs run so high in this species.

<FunFact>
The same implant is being looked at as a replacement for surgical neutering. Preliminary data suggest that ferrets given the implant instead of being neutered develop adrenal disease less often, which fits the idea that the missing hormonal feedback after neutering is what sets the disease off.
</FunFact>
