# Three Small Mammals That Get Dismissed as Just Rodents

Rat, mouse, and flying squirrel all get filed under the same mental category, small, fast, vaguely interchangeable. In practice these three ask for very different things from an owner. Two are genuinely easy, sociable pets that mostly just carry an image problem, and one is a much bigger undertaking than its size suggests.

## Fancy Rat: Unfairly Stigmatized, Sociable

Fancy rats are the domesticated form of the wild brown rat, bred for temperament and color since Victorian England, most famously by Jack Black, Queen Victoria's official rat catcher, who bred unusually colored rats and sold them to the wealthy as pets in the 1800s. They are widely considered among the most intelligent small pets kept. They learn tricks, respond to their own names, and navigate mazes readily, which is also why wild-type rats have been the standard research animal for spatial learning for more than a century. Deeply social and quick to bond with a consistent handler, their real downside is a short pet lifespan, typically 2 to 3 years, occasionally 4, on top of a reputation that still suffers by association with the wild rats they were bred from.

## Fancy Mouse: Smaller, Shorter Lived, and Still Underrated

The fancy mouse is a domesticated form of the house mouse, bred for coat color and pattern since Victorian England, where Walter Maxey founded the National Mouse Club in 1895 and designed the standard show cage still used today. Mice are noticeably smaller than rats, a couple of inches of body length against a rat's 7 to 10, and considerably more skittish. Most keepers find them harder to hand-tame to the same degree a rat reaches. Their pet lifespan is shorter too, typically 1.5 to 2 years against a rat's 2 to 3. The most practical downside is odor: male mouse urine contains trimethylamine, a compound largely absent from rat urine, so a mouse cage needs more frequent cleaning than a same-sized rat setup.

## Flying Squirrel: A Much Bigger Commitment Than It Looks

The Southern flying squirrel is a small nocturnal rodent native to the forests of eastern North America, gliding between trees on a furred membrane called a patagium. Despite the resemblance, it is not related to the [sugar glider](/guides/sugar-glider/), a marsupial that evolved the same gliding trick independently, a genuine case of two unrelated animals converging on the same solution. Where a rat or mouse asks for a year or two of daily attention, a flying squirrel is a decade-plus commitment: captive individuals commonly live around 10 years, with some documented past 19. Add a nocturnal schedule, a large flight-capable enclosure, and ownership rules that are among the most heavily regulated of any small pet in the US and vary considerably by state, and this is a pet worth researching thoroughly, including your own state's laws, before bringing one home.

<FunFact>
All three of these are, technically, rodents, the flying squirrel included, despite looking like it belongs in its own category entirely. But just a rodent hides how different they actually are to keep. A fancy rat gives you two to three years with one of the most trainable small pets available. A fancy mouse gives you a smaller, shorter version of that same deal, with a stricter cleaning schedule. A flying squirrel asks for a decade or more, a nocturnal schedule, and a check of your own state's laws before you even start. Same taxonomic order, three completely different levels of commitment.
</FunFact>
