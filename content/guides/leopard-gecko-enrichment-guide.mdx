# Leopard Gecko Enrichment: What the Research Actually Supports

Most enrichment advice for reptiles is extrapolated from general principles. Leopard geckos are unusual in actually having real studies behind some of it, and the findings are specific enough to change what's worth prioritizing. For everything else about keeping this species, see our complete [Leopard Gecko care guide](/guides/leopard-gecko/).

<KeyTakeaway>
The three-hide system, warm dry, cool dry, and moist, along with correct belly heat, is the foundation everything else sits on. Toys do not compensate for a missing hide or the wrong temperature, no matter how enriched the rest of the setup looks.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Actually Says

Bashaw et al. (2016), published in *Applied Animal Behaviour Science*, tested leopard gecko engagement with five types of environmental enrichment. Thermal and feeding enrichment produced the strongest responses. Mirror-based visual enrichment was the weakest, geckos largely didn't engage with it in a meaningful way.

A more recent study, Rickman et al. (2025), found that geckos in enriched housing showed improved welfare indicators compared to standard sparse setups, and when given a choice in preference tests, they consistently chose naturalistic, enriched conditions over barren ones.

<FunFact>
Additional work on feeding presentation specifically has found that offering small insects in a more naturalistic way, rather than a single static bowl, is associated with sustained activity increases, another point in favor of prioritizing feeding enrichment over novelty items.
</FunFact>

The practical translation is straightforward: prioritize functional enrichment, thermal choice, a maintained moist hide, and hunting opportunities, well ahead of decorative novelty objects.

## Hide Enrichment Is the Baseline, Not an Extra

Three hides, minimum: a warm dry hide over the belly-heat zone, a cool dry hide, and a moist hide with damp media. This isn't just husbandry checkbox material, it's the mechanism that lets a gecko actually exercise choice, moving between microclimates while staying hidden, which is itself a real behavioral option rather than a forced compromise between safety and temperature.

Keep hides snug and cave-like rather than oversized and open. The moist hide runs meaningfully more humid than the rest of the tank, often cited around 70 to 80% inside the hide itself, while overall enclosure humidity stays much lower, commonly around 30 to 40%. Raising the whole enclosure to that higher number instead of using a dedicated moist hide is the wrong approach, and it raises respiratory infection risk. Maintain and replace the damp media so it stays actually damp, not swampy and not bone dry. Our [tank setup guide](/blog/leopard-gecko-tank-setup-guide/) covers the full temperature and humidity breakdown this connects to.

## Feeding and Hunting Enrichment

This is the highest-value enrichment after thermal setup, according to the research above. Tong-feeding, short supervised insect hunts inside the enclosure, and simple timed-release prey containers all give a gecko something closer to natural hunting behavior than a bowl of static food. Evening feeding matches this crepuscular species' natural activity window.

Keep prey no larger than the space between the gecko's eyes, and don't leave feeder insects unsupervised in the enclosure long-term. Never use wild-caught insects or fireflies. Our [feeding guide](/blog/leopard-gecko-feeding-guide/) covers portions and schedule by age in full.

## Climbing, Cover, and Dig Options

Low sturdy branches, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3SKmfOx" product="Cork bark round hide">cork flats or tubes</AffiliateLink>, and stacked slate give a gecko somewhere to climb and rest off the ground, along with visual cover that still allows movement through the space. Height matters less here than it does for arboreal species, floor space and a properly working hide setup do more for this species than a tall build.

A dig box can satisfy digging behavior, which shows up especially in some females, without converting the whole floor to loose substrate. Paper towel or tile stays the safest primary substrate for beginners; sand and soil-style mixes are an advanced option for healthy adults only, never calcium sand.

Occasional novelty, a new piece of cork, a mild rearrangement, has some value but is genuinely secondary to everything above. It's a nice-to-have, not where the research points as a priority.

## Priority Order

1. Correct belly heat plus all three hides, including a genuinely maintained moist hide
2. Feeding and hunting presentation
3. Low climbing structure and visual cover
4. A dig opportunity
5. Light novelty rotation, last on the list for a reason

## What Not to Do

A one-hide setup isn't enrichment with a gap, it's a missing foundation. Skipping the moist hide, or raising overall humidity instead of using one, both work against this species rather than for it. Calcium sand marketed as an enrichment substrate carries a real impaction risk. Lifting a gecko by the tail during "play," or treating a shed cycle as handling time, both cause real harm rather than providing enrichment, see our [handling guide](/blog/leopard-gecko-handling-guide/) for the safe version of both. And don't assume a wheel or a toy substitutes for hides and hunting opportunities, the research above is fairly direct about which of those actually moves the needle.
