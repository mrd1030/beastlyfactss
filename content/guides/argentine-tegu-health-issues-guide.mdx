# Common Argentine Tegu Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Argentine tegus are hardy animals when housed properly, and the issues that do show up trace back to a fairly small set of preventable husbandry gaps, several of them tied directly to this species' large size.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Watch for weakness, decreased appetite, swollen joints or legs, tremors, and soft or fractured bones. Caused by inadequate UVB or insufficient calcium and vitamin D3. Always see a vet, and treat this as preventable: the [enclosure setup guide](/blog/argentine-tegu-tank-setup-guide/) carries the bulb, the percentage and the basking UVI target that make the difference, which matters more here than with a smaller lizard given how fast this one grows.

## Respiratory Infection

Look for lethargy, appetite loss, nasal discharge, and open mouth breathing. Early cases sometimes respond once temperature and humidity are corrected, but persistent symptoms need a vet for antibiotic treatment. Caused by an enclosure that's too cold, too damp, or poorly ventilated, harder to get right in a large enclosure than a small one, worth monitoring closely.

<FunFact>
Metabolic bone disease and respiratory infection are both driven by the same underlying problem, an enclosure that hasn't kept pace with how large and fast growing this species actually is. A setup sized correctly from the start prevents both rather than needing separate fixes later.
</FunFact>

## Obesity

Genuinely common in this species. Tegus are enthusiastic eaters, and continuing to feed them the fatty, rodent heavy diet appropriate for a fast growing juvenile well into adulthood is a common mistake. The schedule that prevents it is set by age: five times a week for a hatchling, four as a juvenile, three from one to two years, and twice a week past two, with a calcium supplement at every meal and a multivitamin weekly. Exercise is the other half, so a tegu that gets room and time to move is doing part of the work. Worth having a vet assess body condition periodically to confirm you're on track. This mirrors a pattern seen in other large lizards, including the [savannah monitor](/blog/savannah-monitor-health-issues-guide/), where the same combination of husbandry gaps and overfeeding drives most of the health problems that show up.

## Parasites

Watch for weight loss, diarrhea, and skin issues, more common in imported or wild caught animals. See a vet for a fecal exam and treatment.

## Dehydration

A tegu kept at 70 to 80% humidity can still dry out. The usual causes are too little water, dirty water, distilled water, low humidity, and temperatures running too hot. Signs are dull, wrinkled skin, often deeply creased around the neck and along the sides, sunken eyes, a sticky tongue, dry mucous membranes, stuck shed, weight loss, lethargy and appetite loss. For a quick check, pinch a fold of skin lightly: healthy skin falls straight back, dehydrated skin stays raised. A moderate case can be soaked in a 50/50 mix of water and unflavored Pedialyte, with clean, fresh water offered every day after. A severe case needs a vet the same day. The earliest warning often shows in the droppings first, where [yellow, orange or gritty urates](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) replace the normal white paste.

## Prolapse

Tissue, either a hemipenis or intestine, pushed out of the vent that does not go back after defecating, mating or laying eggs. Dehydration, impaction and a heavy parasite load are the common drivers. First aid is a cool bath with plenty of sugar dissolved in it, which draws fluid out of the swollen tissue and helps it shrink back. If an hour of soaking changes nothing, coat the exposed tissue in petroleum jelly to keep it from drying and go straight to an emergency reptile vet. An untreated prolapse can kill a tegu, and one that keeps recurring with no clear cause may need surgery.

## Scale Rot

Brownish, flaky scales that can progress to blisters and open sores. It is a bacterial infection, usually of a wound or of skin sitting on wet bedding, which is the trap in a tegu enclosure: the deep substrate should be damp enough to hold a burrow, never soggy. A vet treats it. At home the tegu moves to a quarantine tub on paper towels, and the main enclosure is disinfected with chlorhexidine or F-10 before it goes back. Recovery takes several weeks.

## Weigh It and Write It Down

Tegus hide illness well, and sudden weight loss is often the first sign anything is wrong. A weekly record of weight, feeding, behavior and shedding makes the change visible before the animal looks sick.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD, especially consequential given how fast and large this species grows. Poor temperature or humidity control, harder to maintain consistently in a large enclosure, causes respiratory infection. And continuing a juvenile feeding schedule into adulthood causes the obesity that's common in this species; the feeding guide has the schedule that steps an adult down to two meals a week.
