# Common Argentine Tegu Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Argentine tegus are hardy animals when housed properly, and the issues that do show up trace back to a fairly small set of preventable husbandry gaps, several of them tied directly to this species' large size.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Watch for weakness, decreased appetite, swollen joints or legs, tremors, and soft or fractured bones. Caused by inadequate UVB or insufficient calcium and vitamin D3. Always see a vet, and treat this as preventable: the [enclosure setup guide](/blog/argentine-tegu-tank-setup-guide/) carries the bulb, the percentage and the basking UVI target that make the difference, which matters more here than with a smaller lizard given how fast this one grows.

## Respiratory Infection

Look for lethargy, appetite loss, nasal discharge, and open mouth breathing. Early cases sometimes respond once temperature and humidity are corrected, but persistent symptoms need a vet for antibiotic treatment. Caused by an enclosure that's too cold, too damp, or poorly ventilated, harder to get right in a large enclosure than a small one, worth monitoring closely.

<FunFact>
Metabolic bone disease and respiratory infection are both driven by the same underlying problem, an enclosure that hasn't kept pace with how large and fast growing this species actually is. A setup sized correctly from the start prevents both rather than needing separate fixes later.
</FunFact>

## Obesity

Genuinely common in this species. Tegus are enthusiastic eaters, and continuing to feed them the fatty, rodent heavy diet appropriate for a fast growing juvenile well into adulthood is a common mistake. The schedule that prevents it is set by age: daily for a young tegu, every other day from one to three years, every third day past three, with a calcium supplement at every meal and a multivitamin weekly. Exercise is the other half, so a tegu that gets room and time to move is doing part of the work. Worth having a vet assess body condition periodically to confirm you're on track. This mirrors a pattern seen in other large lizards, including the [savannah monitor](/blog/savannah-monitor-health-issues-guide/), where the same combination of husbandry gaps and overfeeding drives most of the health problems that show up.

## Parasites

Watch for weight loss, diarrhea, and skin issues, more common in imported or wild caught animals. See a vet for a fecal exam and treatment.

## Prolapse, Scale Rot, and Dehydration

Secondary issues that generally trace back to husbandry gaps elsewhere. Prolapse specifically is a genuine emergency requiring immediate veterinary attention, not something to wait out.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD, especially consequential given how fast and large this species grows. Poor temperature or humidity control, harder to maintain consistently in a large enclosure, causes respiratory infection. And continuing a juvenile feeding schedule into adulthood causes the obesity that's common in this species.
