# How to Handle Amano Shrimp (Minimal Contact, Careful Acclimation)

Amano shrimp aren't a hands-on pet, and the handling skill that matters with this species has almost nothing to do with touch. It's acclimation, and getting that step right matters more here than with most freshwater livestock.

## Netting and Transfer, Not Hands

A soft, fine-mesh net is the right tool, guiding the shrimp in gently instead of chasing it around the tank, which only adds stress on top of whatever move it's already going through. Keep it in water as much as possible during any transfer. Be extra careful around a shrimp that looks like it molted recently, right after shedding its shell is soft and its swimming is noticeably weaker than usual, which is exactly the wrong moment for a rough net chase or a long trip out of water.

## Drip Acclimation Over One to Two Hours

Amano shrimp are genuinely sensitive to sudden shifts in water chemistry, more so than a lot of community fish, so drip acclimating new arrivals over roughly one to two hours is worth the time it takes. Run a slow siphon line from the destination tank into the shrimp's transport container so the water mixes gradually rather than all at once, aiming for something in the range of one to two drops per second. A bigger gap between your tap water and the shrimp's transport water calls for a longer, more gradual drip, sometimes stretched to three or four hours.

The reason this matters specifically for amano shrimp, beyond the generic acclimation advice, is the connection to molting. A shrimp thrown straight into different water can go into osmotic shock, and that kind of sudden internal stress is a known trigger for failed, often fatal molts.

## Quarantine

Two to four weeks in a separate, already-cycled tank is a reasonable standard before a new amano shrimp joins an established display. This isn't just generic caution: since virtually every amano shrimp sold in the trade is wild-caught rather than farm-raised, quarantine gives any hitchhiking parasites, pests, or simply a stressed, weakened animal time to show itself before it ever reaches your main tank and its existing residents.

<FunFact>
There's a genuine irony built into this species' biology. Its larvae cannot survive without reaching brackish or marine water within their first stages of life, yet the adults are the opposite: even a small, accidental exposure to salt can kill a mature amano shrimp outright. The same animal that's biologically obligated to spend part of its life cycle in salt water would die if you tried to acclimate it into a saltwater tank as an adult. That mismatch is a big part of why breeding them start to finish requires two completely separate setups, one of the reasons it's so rarely done at home.
</FunFact>

## Why You're (Almost Certainly) Buying a Wild-Caught Adult

This connects directly to the acclimation and quarantine advice above. Amano shrimp larvae need brackish to full marine water to develop before metamorphosing and returning to freshwater as juveniles, a stage no standard home aquarium supports. Because the adults themselves can't tolerate that same salt water, breeding the species end to end takes a dedicated, separate saltwater rearing setup, something the overwhelming majority of hobbyists never build. Reproduction in captivity has been done, but it's a specialist project, not something that happens by accident the way it does with [cherry shrimp](/guides/cherry-shrimp/), which multiply into a self-sustaining colony in plain freshwater with zero intervention.

The practical result is that nearly every amano shrimp you'll ever buy is a wild-collected adult that's already made its own journey from a stream in Japan or Taiwan through a wholesaler and into a retailer's tank by the time it reaches you. That's worth knowing going in: you're buying a fixed number of already-mature animals, not seeding a colony that will grow on its own.
