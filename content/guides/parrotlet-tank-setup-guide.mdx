# Parrotlet Cage Setup: Small Bird, Real Space and Enrichment Needs

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Parrotlet housing gets underestimated for the same reason the bird itself does, the size fools people into assuming a small cage and a quiet life will do. A few specific details, bar spacing tighter than you'd use for a lovebird among them, matter more than the small footprint suggests. This covers the setup itself, for costs and ongoing supplies, see our [Parrotlet cost guide](/blog/parrotlet-cost-guide/), or the full [Parrotlet care guide](/guides/parrotlet/) for everything else.

## Cage Size

Minimum 18x18x24 inches for a single bird. Bigger is always the better call here: parrotlets are surprisingly active and playful for their size, often described as a large parrot's energy packed into a tiny frame, so a wider <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4wuT808" product="Large bird flight cage">flight-style cage</AffiliateLink> gets genuinely used rather than sitting empty. If you're housing a pair, budget for meaningfully more space than the single-bird minimum, not just a modest bump.

## Bar Spacing: Tighter Than You'd Use for a Lovebird

Keep bar spacing no wider than 1/2 inch, with some general bird-safety sources putting the outer limit at 5/8 inch. Lafeber, an avian-specialist source writing specifically about this species, recommends going tighter still, around 1/4 inch. That's meaningfully narrower than the 3/8 to 5/8 inch range that's fine for a lovebird, and it comes down to size: a parrotlet's head is smaller, so spacing that's safe for its bigger relatives can let a parrotlet get a head or foot caught, which is a real, sometimes fatal risk. Skip round cages entirely, and make sure every opening section locks securely, this is a genuinely clever bird capable of working out a simple latch.

## Materials

Powder-coated or stainless steel only. Avoid painted finishes, galvanized wire, or anything using lead or zinc in its construction, all carry a real risk of toxic exposure if chewed, and parrotlets are persistent, strong chewers for their size. Our [Parrotlet cost guide](/blog/parrotlet-cost-guide/) covers why the cheap end of the cage market is a false economy for exactly this reason.

## Temperature

Normal household range, roughly 65 to 80°F, works well. No special heating or cooling is needed, though drafts and direct sun on the cage should be avoided the same as with any pet bird.

## Substrate and Lining

A paper cage liner is the simplest, most hygienic option, changed regularly. Avoid substrates prone to mold in a humid environment.

## Lighting

Indoor birds benefit from supplemental UV exposure to properly synthesize vitamin D3 for calcium absorption, since window glass filters out the UV wavelengths that matter, this is worth taking seriously given how directly calcium status ties into the egg-binding risk covered in our [Parrotlet health issues guide](/blog/parrotlet-health-issues-guide/). A consistent day and night cycle also matters: roughly 10 to 12 hours of genuine, covered darkness supports both healthy sleep and a more stable hormonal cycle in females.

<FunFact>
In the wild, Pacific parrotlets live in flocks of 40 to 50 birds. In captivity, that instinct doesn't translate the way you'd expect, unfamiliar parrotlets housed together can be genuinely aggressive toward each other, which is exactly why setting up for a pair looks so different from setting up for a single bird.
</FunFact>

## Single Bird or Pair: A Setup Decision, Not Just a Relationship One

This deserves its own line item when you're planning the cage, not just the temperament conversation covered in our [Parrotlet handling guide](/blog/parrotlet-handling-guide/). A single bird needs less floor space but real, consistent daily interaction from you to stay well-adjusted. A pair needs a noticeably larger cage from day one, and the introduction itself has to be gradual and supervised: separate cages in the same room first, then supervised time together out of the cage, and only once the birds are visibly comfortable should they share a cage, ideally one neither bird has already claimed as its own territory. Skipping that process and hoping two parrotlets simply get along is a common first-timer mistake, and this species can fight seriously enough to injure a cage mate when it goes wrong.
