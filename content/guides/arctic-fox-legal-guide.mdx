The arctic fox is the animal that finds out what a state meant when it wrote the word "fox". Most fox laws in this country were written about the red fox and the gray fox, the two native animals that carry rabies through the eastern states and get trapped for fur across the rest. Some of those laws name the species. Some say "Canidae" and take every dog alive. And a good number say "fox" and stop, which leaves a Saharan fennec or an arctic fox from a fur ranch inside or outside the word depending on how the agency reads it. The red fox never faces that question because it is the fox every statute meant, and the fennec rarely does because so many states have now named it. The arctic fox, farmed for fur as the blue fox and now sold as a pet, is on almost no list by name, and its map is where the bare word does its work.

Read across all 52 jurisdictions, the arctic fox has 24 bans, nine permits, nine states with no rule, two with a restricted-list status, and eight whose text points both ways. Eight unclear cells is the most of any fox on the map.

## Four States Where "Fox" Means Any Fox

Idaho wrote the shortest version. Idaho Code § 25-236(1):

> No person shall possess, offer for sale, trade, barter, exchange or importation into the state of Idaho any fox, skunk or raccoon, except as provided in subsection (2) or (3) of this section.

"Any fox" carries no species and no nativity, so it reaches the arctic fox as plainly as Idaho's own, and the Department of Agriculture's deleterious exotic animal list separately takes all non-native canids. The exceptions are fur farms holding domestic fur-bearing animals, and parks, zoos, museums and educational institutions with an Agriculture permit. Washington's rabies rule, WAC 246-100-197(5)(a), prohibits all persons from "acquiring, selling, bartering, exchanging, giving, purchasing, distributing, or trapping to retain any bat, skunk, fox, raccoon, or coyote", with zoos, exhibitors, research facilities and rehabilitators as the exceptions; "fox" is unqualified. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1) bars a live "fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear" in clause (i) and a "member of the dog family other than the domestic dog" in clause (v), so the arctic fox is caught twice. Montana's MCA § 50-23-101(4) defines a "wild animal" for its rabies part as "a skunk, fox, raccoon, or bat", and § 50-23-102 bars possessing one outside a fur-bearing enterprise, a no-contact zoo exhibit or research; the arctic fox is also non-native exotic wildlife on none of Montana's white-list classifications, so it is banned twice there as well.

Louisiana and Alabama get to the same place by barring the import. LAC 76:V.113 states its purpose as prohibiting "the importation and exportation of any species of foxes", and D.6 says "No person shall transport, possess, purchase or sell any live foxes or coyotes taken outside the state of Louisiana". Alabama's rule bars "species of fox" brought "from any area outside the state of Alabama". Every arctic fox in the pet trade comes from outside both states, which is the trigger.

## Where the Genus Is Named, Twice

Nevada's prohibited list is the one that saw the taxonomy coming. NAC 503.110(1)(d)(10) prohibits:

> Foxes ... All species in the genera Vulpes, Fennecus, Urocyon, Alopex, Lycalopex and Pseudalopex

The arctic fox was filed as *Alopex lagopus* for most of the twentieth century and is *Vulpes lagopus* on current taxonomy. Nevada names both genera, so the animal is barred whichever name the seller uses. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:082 lists "Fox (Genus Cerdocyon, Genus Lycalopex, Genus Otocyon, Genus Urocyon, and Genus Vulpes)", which reaches the arctic fox on its current name. New Mexico's importation list puts "Vulpes (sp. variety) | Fox (any type)" in Group IV, and an animal unlisted under the old genus falls into Group IV by the list's default clause anyway. Hawaii's Part A takes "Vulpes (all species in genus)", and an animal missing from every Hawaii list is barred by default, so the old name does not help there either.

New York drew the line between the foxes in one parenthesis. ECL § 11-0103(6)(e) puts the family on the wild animal list with that carve-out, and 6 NYCRR § 180.1(b)(1) repeats it among the dangerous animals:

> Canines in the Family Canidae (with the exception of domesticated dogs and captive bred fennec foxes (vulpes zerda))

The carve-out names the fennec and nothing else, so the arctic fox is a wild animal that ECL § 11-0512 bars as a pet, in the same sentence that frees a captive-bred fennec. California restricts "Family Canidae-All species" for welfare, and the department's caging table names the arctic fox beside the bat-eared fox. Connecticut makes every canid Category One, Maine every canid Category 1, and Iowa's dangerous wild animal definition reaches "a member of the family canidae of the order carnivora". New Jersey's N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.8(a) makes "Canidae--Nondomestic dogs" potentially dangerous and 4.9(a)3 says such a species "shall not be kept as a pet". New York City's Health Code names "fox" in its wild dog clause, and the District of Columbia's closed list of seven pet categories has no canid but the dog.

Six more states ban it without a fox word at all. Georgia's license rule takes all of Carnivora and § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues licenses only to dealers and exhibitors. Oregon's ORS 609.305 makes "any nonwolf member of the family Canidae not indigenous to Oregon" an exotic animal, and the permit window closed in 2010. Colorado's Parks and Wildlife will not license private possession of regulated wildlife, which is anything on none of its lists. New Hampshire's Fis 804.04(b) says "All species not specifically listed under the categories of non-controlled, prohibited, or controlled shall be designated as prohibited", and its controlled table names the gray, fennec and red foxes and no arctic fox. Rhode Island restricts every carnivore "for which there is no USDA conditionally licensed or unconditionally licensed rabies vaccine" to researchers, exhibitors and AZA facilities, and no fox has one. Alaska is the one state where the arctic fox is native, and that only confirms it is game; 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars the department from issuing a permit for any game animal "for use as a pet".

<FunFact>
Arkansas is the only state that wrote the arctic fox into a permit list by name. Addendum R1.02 of the Game and Fish Commission Code says "Permits for breeding, sale, and importation may be issued for the following species", and the mammal list includes "Arctic Fox (Vulpes lagopus)". The fennec is on no Arkansas list at all, which under R1.03(B) makes it "prohibited until evaluated". A keeper with a Commission-permitted breeder's receipt, a veterinary certificate and the sexes housed apart can hold an arctic fox in Arkansas and cannot hold a fennec.
</FunFact>

## The Permit States

Nine states issue a permit, and one of them does not mean it: MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard. The other seven are real. Florida's agency treats foxes as Class III, the free personal use permit, despite the Canidae parenthetical in its Class II list. Virginia's permit table restricts "Canidae | All Species" with the common name column reading "Wild Dogs, Wolves, Coyotes or Coyote hybrids, Jackals and Foxes", and because the arctic fox is non-native that table is its rule, where the native red fox is barred outright instead. Delaware's regulation 903-6.2 requires an Individual permit, $25, for any exotic animal not on the exempt list, and the live red fox statute at tit. 7 § 789 names the red fox only. South Carolina's furbearer list reads "red and gray fox" but is "not limited to" them, so an arctic fox reads as a furbearer needing the import permit. South Dakota's possession permit covers all nondomestic Canidae at ten dollars an animal. Wyoming's statute names the red fox as a predatory animal and not the arctic, so the arctic fox is not barred from import the way the red fox is; it lands on Chapter 10 § 13(d), where a permit "may be authorized upon approval". Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora, and Utah's Table 3b-1 lists the red fox as domestic and the fennec as controlled and never mentions the arctic fox, so it is presumed prohibited until the Division assesses it.

## Eight States Where the Word Will Not Settle It

This is the row that makes the arctic fox its own page. Eight states have a definition that says "fox" and a scheme that turns on whether a non-native fox is inside it, and none has said. Ohio's R.C. 1531.01(W):

> "Fur-bearing animals" includes minks, weasels, raccoons, skunks, opossums, muskrats, fox, beavers, badgers, otters, coyotes, and bobcats.

No species, no nativity limit, inside a definition of wild animals that takes in "all other wild mammals". On that reading the arctic fox, farmed for fur as the blue fox, needs the § 1533.71 license at $25 a year; read as Ohio's own foxes, it needs nothing. Texas is the same question in a chapter headed "wild": § 71.001(1) names "fox" among fur-bearing animals and § 71.005(c) requires a propagation license to possess any live one, and the fennec cell reads "wild ... fox" as not reaching a captive-bred non-native, on the word and on the chapter being written for Texas populations, while the text arguably reaches it anyway. North Carolina's game animal definition names "fox" with no species, and § 113-129(16) extends the wildlife definitions to animals of wild species kept in captivity; read that way, an arctic fox needs a captivity license that is not issued for a pet, and read as North Carolina's own foxes, nothing reaches it. Mississippi classes "fox" as a nuisance animal and bars possessing a live one outside a hound pen; read literally that reaches the arctic fox, read as a list of the state's pest species it does not, and Rule 8.3 names no fox.

The other four turn on a different word. Pennsylvania's canid line names the coyote, the red and gray fox and the wolf, so the arctic fox is not on it; what arguably reaches it is § 137.1(a)(11), the catch-all for wildlife captive bred in another state, which rests on an agency reading of the word wildlife. Missouri's 3 CSR 10-9.110(2) clears non-native mammals to be bought, sold and possessed without permit, and 3 CSR 10-9.223(2) says "Live ... raccoons, foxes, and coyotes may not be imported into this state", with no species limit, so an arctic fox bred in Missouri may be kept and whether one may be brought in from elsewhere is the gap. North Dakota's category 1, which needs no license, includes "ranch foxes", a term the rule never defines, while category 2 names kit, swift, red and gray foxes; the arctic fox is in neither by name, and an animal "not otherwise referred to" goes to the board for review before import. Oklahoma's exemption list clears "Dogs (except coyotes and native foxes)", which implies a non-native fox is an exempt dog, and then gives the fennec its own line at (35), which implies the Commission did not read "Dogs" as covering foxes at all. Either way the arctic fox is not banned there. In every one of these eight, the answer is a letter to the agency, kept with the animal's papers.

## Where the Fox Rules Name Their Own Foxes

Nine states place no rule on the arctic fox, and the reason in every one is that their fox rule is a native-wildlife rule. Michigan's captive game list names "red and gray fox", the two Michigan species, and leaves non-native exotics to the import rules. Indiana's Class II list carries "Gray fox (Urocyon cinereoargenteus)" and "Red fox (Vulpes fulva)", and 9-11-1 limits the permit requirement to animals the rule names. Illinois protects the red and gray fox by binomial in its Wildlife Code and its dangerous animal list names no fox. Minnesota's small game definition names only the red and gray fox, and a farmed arctic fox two or more generations from the wild is a domestic animal under §§ 17.351 and 17.352 in any case. Nebraska's import list admits "Fox Vulpes spp.", which takes the arctic fox on its current name, and the Captive Wildlife Permit scheme reaches only Nebraska natives. Wisconsin exempts non-native wild animals that are not harmful from licensing. West Virginia's dangerous wild animal list names gray wolves and African wild dogs and no fox, and Chapter 20 reaches only native wildlife. Kansas treats a non-native species as exotic wildlife that "may be possessed without limit in time and number". Tennessee's Class I canids are the wolves, and the arctic fox falls into the Class III catch-all that requires no permit. The [interactive map of arctic fox laws](/exotic-pet-laws/arctic-fox/) shades every jurisdiction that restricts one.

The split is the whole lesson. The [red fox](/blog/red-fox-legal-guide/) is banned or permitted in all nine of those states, because it is the animal their native-wildlife chapters were written about, and a captive-bred arctic fox walks past the same chapters untouched. Where a state instead wrote "fox" or "Canidae", the arctic fox and the [fennec fox](/blog/fennec-fox-legal-guide/) get the red fox's answer. Nothing about the three animals explains any of that; it is entirely an artifact of which word the drafter reached for.
