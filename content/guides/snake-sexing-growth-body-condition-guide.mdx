# Sexing, Growth, and Body Condition in Pet Snakes

<VetDisclaimer />

Ask most snake owners how to find out if their animal is male or female and the honest answer is that they don't know, because nobody's told them there isn't a safe way to find out at home. The same gap shows up around growth: owners reach for a tape measure because length is easy to picture, when weight is the number that actually tells you something. And body condition, whether a snake is too thin, too heavy, or right where it should be, gets judged by eye with no real framework at all. This guide covers all three, and it's honest about where the reliable methods stop and the guesswork begins.

## Sexing a Snake: Why This Isn't a Do-It-Yourself Project

Snakes don't carry visible external genitalia the way many mammals do, so confirming sex means either checking internal anatomy directly or reading a trait that correlates with sex closely enough to trust. Two hands-on methods do the former, and both are procedures with real technique behind them, not something to attempt cold.

**Probing** is the standard method a reptile vet or experienced breeder uses on an adult or subadult snake. A well-lubricated, blunt metal probe of the right diameter is inserted into the cloaca and guided gently backward, toward the tail tip, along the side of the tail. Males have paired hemipenes that sit inverted in pockets at the base of the tail, so in a male the probe slides in deeper, to a depth of 6 to 14 subcaudal scales. In a female, the probe only reaches a shallow cloacal gland and stops at roughly 3 to 6 scales. That gap in depth is what makes the method work, but getting a clean, accurate reading depends on using a probe of the correct size and directing it at the correct angle, which is exactly the kind of judgment call that comes from doing it under supervision first, not from a first attempt on your own animal.

**Popping**, or manual eversion, is used mainly on hatchlings and very young snakes, where probing is harder to do safely on such a small tail. A 2025 review of reptile sex determination methods published in the journal Animals describes the technique as applying gentle, controlled pressure to the base of the tail just behind the cloaca, which everts the hemipenes in a male so they become visible. The same review is direct about the risk: improper technique can injure the reproductive structures or the tail itself, and in clinical settings light anesthesia is sometimes used specifically because it relaxes the retractor muscles and lowers the odds of causing damage from too much force.

Neither method is complicated to describe, and that's part of the problem. They're easy to read about and much harder to execute correctly on a live animal that can be hurt by too much pressure, the wrong angle, or a probe that's the wrong size for that individual snake. The reliable path for a pet owner is not to attempt either one, but to ask a reptile-experienced vet to do it at a routine visit, or to have an experienced breeder walk through it in person before you try. If you're already comfortable handling your snake for routine checks, our [ball python handling guide](/blog/ball-python-handling-guide/) covers the kind of calm, supported grip that also makes a vet's job easier during any hands-on exam, sexing included.

### External Cues Are Not a Substitute

Plenty of home guesses circulate among keepers, and it's worth saying plainly how weak most of them are. Tail shape and how far the tail tapers past the vent is the most common one, the idea being that males carry a thicker, longer tail base to house the hemipenes. It's not baseless as a concept, but there's no reliable accuracy behind it as a stand-alone visual check, and it's easy to misjudge on a single animal with nothing to compare it against.

Cloacal spurs, the small claw-like projections near the vent, are a genuine sex-linked trait, but only in boas and pythons (families Boidae and Pythonidae). Ball pythons are pythons, not boas, and both groups carry spurs, while colubrids like corn snakes and kingsnakes don't have them at all, so a spur check simply doesn't apply outside those two families. Even within boas and pythons, "just look at spur size" is a shakier cue than it sounds. A 2021 study in Herpetology Notes by Hoefer, Robinson and Pinou looked at pelvic spurs in the Bahamian boa (*Chilabothrus strigilatus strigilatus*) and found that males possessed spurs nearly twice as long, and notably more curved, than those of females. That is a real and measurable difference, but note what it is: one subspecies of wild boa, measured as a population. It is not a published cue for the species most people keep, and nothing in it tells you where the cutoff falls between a long female spur and a short male one on the single animal in your hands. An owner eyeballing one snake's spurs against a mental average has no reference data to work from at all.

If you want certainty without a hands-on procedure, ultrasound is the documented alternative. The same 2025 Animals review describes ultrasonography as a reliable, non-invasive technique for sex determination specifically in snakes, a notably better result than the same imaging method gets in some lizard species. It requires a reptile-experienced exotics vet and the right equipment, but it sidesteps the injury risk that comes with probing or popping done by an untrained hand.

<KeyTakeaway>
Probing and popping both work, and both carry real injury risk when attempted without training. Leave sexing to a vet or an experienced breeder, and treat tail shape and spur size as rough guesses at best, not confirmation.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Tracking Growth by Weight, Not Length

Length is the number most owners default to because it's intuitive and easy to eyeball against a memory of "how long it used to be." It's also the wrong number to build a growth record around. Corn snakes, kingsnakes, and garter snakes typically reach 3 to 6 feet as adults, and that ball pythons hatch out around a foot long and grow to roughly 3 feet by age three, reaching their full adult length of 5 to 6 feet by 3 to 5 years old. Those are useful benchmarks for what a species eventually looks like, but they say nothing about whether a given animal is healthy along the way, because length and body mass don't grow in lockstep.

That mismatch is well documented. A 2017 study in PLOS ONE that measured 248 wild Burmese pythons in Florida found the species shows positive allometry, meaning mass increases disproportionately faster than length as a snake gets bigger. In practical terms, two snakes of identical length can carry very different amounts of muscle and fat, and a snake that has "grown" several inches since last season may have added little real body mass, or the reverse. The same study found that body condition indices built on simple mass-to-length ratios carried a strong size bias and were inappropriate for comparing animals of different sizes, while regression-based indices and the scaled mass index showed little correlation with body size and held up far better. That is a technical way of confirming something practical: length alone is a weak stand-in for how well an animal is actually doing.

A gram or kitchen scale solves this more directly than a tape measure ever will. Weight captures muscle, fat, and gut content together in one number, and set on a schedule, monthly for a growing juvenile is a reasonable default, it builds something a single reading can't: a trend line. One weigh-in tells you almost nothing by itself. Three or four spaced-out readings that are climbing steadily, holding flat, or dropping tell you a great deal, and it's the direction of that line, not any one number on it, that should drive a decision to change feeding frequency or call a vet.

We were not able to source verified species-by-species weight ranges for snakes from any peer-reviewed or veterinary source for this guide, so we're not going to invent numbers here. That's a genuinely fine outcome: the method, a consistent scale, a regular schedule, and judging the trend rather than any single reading, works regardless of species or the exact numbers involved, and it's the same approach our [bearded dragon growth and weight-check guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-growth-weight-checks-guide/) walks through in more species-specific detail for a reptile where those benchmark ranges are better documented. If you want to compare growth against a scale reading rather than fight a tape measure around a coiled body, that guide's method transfers directly.

## Body Condition: The Cross-Section Test

Weight tells you the trend. Body condition tells you what that weight actually looks like on the animal in front of you, and for snakes it comes down to a simple visual and hands-on check rather than a numbered scale.

Pick the snake up, support it well, and look at it end-on, imagining a slice straight across its body. A well-conditioned snake reads as a rounded loaf or a slightly rounded rectangle in that cross-section, full and even with no sharp angles, and a gentle hand run along the back should feel like muscle over bone rather than a sharp ridge or a padded-over gap. An underweight snake reads triangular instead, with muscle sloping away from a visible ridge running down the center of the back where the spine sits closer to the surface. An overweight snake reads round in a different way, with skin folds along the sides or a soft, doughy feel over the ribs where fat has built up under the skin.

It's worth saying directly that the 9-point body condition scale familiar from dog and cat checkups does not apply here. That scale was built around a mammalian skeleton and a very different fat-distribution pattern, and mapping it onto a snake's elongated, limbless body doesn't work. The cross-section and spine-palpation approach above is the check that actually fits a snake's anatomy, and it's one you can run at home between vet visits, at every handling session or weigh-in, without any special equipment.

A snake that reads triangular over several checks, especially alongside a falling weight trend, refused meals, or lethargy, is worth a vet visit rather than just more food, since thinness in a reptile is as often a symptom of parasites, an underlying illness, or a husbandry problem as it is simple underfeeding. Our [ball python health issues guide](/blog/ball-python-health-issues-guide/) covers several of the conditions that show up first as exactly this kind of unexplained weight or condition change, and the same logic applies broadly across species even where the specific disease list differs. On the other end, a snake that's consistently round with visible fat pads is a case for adjusting feeding size or frequency, not for skipping meals outright, since sudden fasting brings its own problems in an animal already carrying extra weight.

## Putting It Together

None of these three checks work as well alone as they do together. Sexing tells you what the animal is, not how it's doing, and it's a job for a vet or experienced breeder rather than a weekend project. A weight trend built from regular readings on the same scale tells you the direction things are moving. And the cross-section test, run at the same time as that weigh-in, tells you what that weight actually looks like on the animal's frame. Run all three the way they're meant to be run, sexing by a trained hand, growth by a scale and a log, condition by eye and touch, and you'll catch far more real problems than a tape measure and a guess ever would.
