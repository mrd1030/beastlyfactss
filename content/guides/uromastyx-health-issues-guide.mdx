# Common Uromastyx Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Uromastyx are hardy desert specialists, and most health problems trace directly back to getting this species' unusually specific heat, humidity, or dietary needs wrong.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

The most common issue in captivity. Watch for a soft or hanging jaw, swollen limbs, tremors, deformed bones, and lethargy. Caused by inadequate calcium or vitamin D3, insufficient UVB, or a diet too high in phosphorus or oxalates. Always see a vet, severe cases can be fatal. The early signs are behavioral. Poor appetite, weakness, and an inability to walk normally come ahead of the swollen or distorted jaw and limbs that most keepers picture, with fractures, muscle spasms, and cloacal prolapse following. Nothing about this is treatable at home beyond fixing the UVB and the calcium, and a uromastyx that has started to wobble needs a vet to stage it.

## Impaction

Bloating, no bowel movements, appetite loss, and lethargy point here. Caused by ingesting loose substrate, particularly calcium sand or walnut shell products, or eating food that's too large. See a vet if it doesn't resolve, this can become fatal. The [substrate that prevents it](/blog/uromastyx-tank-setup-guide/) is the fix that matters. Choosing an appropriate <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0002DIZKC?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Zoo Med ReptiSand, 10 Pounds, Desert White">sand substrate</AffiliateLink> and avoiding calcium sand or walnut shell products entirely is part of avoiding this risk.

General veterinary guidance for lizards puts it more broadly: sand, dirt, and even wood chips can cause impaction when swallowed, and the default there is tile, reptile carpet, or paper. Uromastyx are the exception that guidance was not written for. They come from sand and very sandy soil across North Africa and the Middle East, they dig burrows for heat and cold refuge, and a bare floor takes that away. So the setup keeps a packed sand, soil and clay mix and removes the other causes: no calcium sand or walnut shell, greens chopped small, and a basking surface hot enough to keep digestion moving. Bloating, or stool that has stopped, is a vet visit rather than a wait.

## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, open-mouth breathing, and mucus at the nostrils. Caused by humidity running too high or temperatures too low. Always see a vet.

The early version is a lizard that sits with its mouth slightly open, or a bubble at a nostril. Unsanitary conditions, malnutrition, and vitamin A deficiency also cause reptile respiratory infection, so a clean enclosure and a varied diet are part of keeping this off the list. For a desert lizard the trigger is the two numbers above: air that is too damp, or a basking surface that has cooled.

## Parasites

Diarrhea and weight loss, more common in wild-caught or stressed animals. See a vet for a fecal exam and treatment.

An annual wellness exam that includes fecal testing for parasites is the right cadence even for a uromastyx that looks fine, since the signs start with appetite loss and weight loss rather than anything visible. Weighing monthly on a gram scale catches that earlier than your eye will.

## Hydration: Where the Water Comes From

A uromastyx almost never drinks from a bowl, and that confuses new keepers into thinking it is not drinking at all. These lizards get almost all of their water from food. Fresh greens that have not dried out are the water supply, so feed them before they wilt, and mist the greens lightly if the lizard is shedding or the room is running dry. Veterinary guidance adds three more methods: dropping water onto the nose once or twice a week, which the lizard licks off; a shallow soak of 15 to 20 minutes about once a week; and a light fog of the enclosure once or twice a week at night, so the lizard can lick condensation. A water bowl stays available too, even if it goes untouched, in an enclosure that otherwise has to stay dry.

Dehydration looks like loose skin, sunken eyes, dull color, and lethargy. A lizard showing those needs a vet, not a bigger water bowl.

## When to Call a Vet

Four signs are each on their own a reason to call:

- Lumps or swelling in the joints or under the skin
- Runny or foul-smelling stools
- Dull or darkened coloration
- Sunken eyes or lethargy

Two more belong on the same list: open-mouth breathing or discharge from the nose, and any tissue protruding from the vent.

A white, crusty buildup around a uromastyx's nostrils can look like a sign of illness, but it's actually normal: specialized glands let this species excrete excess salt through the nose, and wiping it away is all the maintenance it needs.
</FunFact>

## Salt Crusting: Not a Health Problem

A white, crusty buildup around the nostrils is completely normal, it's how uromastyx excrete excess salt through specialized glands. Manageable simply by wiping it away. The Center for Bird and Exotic Animal Medicine describes the same thing: a gland near the nose that excretes mineral salts to conserve water, so occasional white deposits at the nostrils are expected.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Inadequate UVB and calcium cause MBD, the most serious and most preventable issue on this list. Loose or inappropriate substrate causes impaction. And humidity or temperature running outside this species' genuinely narrow comfort zone causes respiratory infection.
