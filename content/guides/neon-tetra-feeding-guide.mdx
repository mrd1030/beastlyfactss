# Neon Tetra Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and the Rule That Prevents an Outbreak

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A neon tetra grows to about 1.2 inches, feeds in the middle of the water, and takes its food in small bites. Feeding one is simple in principle: small food in small amounts. The rule that protects the school has nothing to do with portions at all.

## Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

Neons start on live food and frequent meals, then step down to two meals a day as adults.

Live food is not optional for fry. A newly hatched neon has no working stomach: its gastric glands only appear about 20 days after hatching. In a University of Florida lab trial, fry raised on live Artemia nauplii alone had 22% survival at 32 days, and fry fed only a prepared microdiet had 0.8%.

Small, frequent feedings suit their tiny stomachs and fast metabolism, and protect water quality far better than one large feeding. Leftovers are the signal to cut back. Uneaten food decays and spikes ammonia and nitrite, the waste a mature tank's [nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) exists to clear, and neons are very sensitive to both.

## What Neons Eat, in the Wild and in the Tank

In the forest streams of the Amazon basin, neons are most likely omnivores, picking off small invertebrates and crustaceans, filamentous algae and bits of fallen fruit. In the wild their diet runs roughly 20 to 30% plant matter and algae alongside insect larvae and small invertebrates.

Build the tank diet around a high-quality <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004PBB5TK?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Semi-Floating Micro Pellets">tropical flake or micro-pellet</AffiliateLink> designed for small, nano fish. Around it go small frozen or live foods: baby brine shrimp, daphnia, cyclops and finely chopped bloodworms, plus some plant or algae content like spirulina flakes or the occasional blanched vegetable.

<KeyTakeaway>
Their mouths are very small. They are only about 1 to 2mm across. Crush flakes into fine pieces, and stick to "micro" or "nano" sized pellets.
</KeyTakeaway>

The frozen and live foods that come closest to the wild menu are the same small prey: bloodworm (chironomid larvae), mosquito larvae, Daphnia and Moina. None of them needs to be the staple, since dried food alone can keep a neon going, but the fish does best on a varied menu, and a school that gets them a few times a week shows it in color. Size is the limit on every one of them: bloodworm goes in chopped, and anything a neon has to wrestle with is too big.

Live food brings a risk that dried food does not. Cyclops and daphnia are common intermediate hosts of Camallanus, a parasitic worm, and fish pick up tapeworm larvae by eating infected invertebrates.

No specific supplement is required beyond a varied, vitamin-fortified diet. Variety is how a fish's nutritional needs get met, and carotenoid-rich foods with some plant matter maintain color on their own, so a properly fed community tank needs no separate vitamin or mineral product.

Dried food goes stale once opened. A container kept at room temperature is finished or thrown out within about 2 months, and frozen food keeps about a year.

## Foods to Avoid

- **Oversized flakes or pellets.** Anything bigger than the mouth goes in and comes straight back out.
- **Too much protein-rich live or frozen food.** Keep it to 2 to 3 feedings a week to avoid digestive issues.
- **Low-quality live foods from questionable sources.** They can transmit disease. Tubificid worms, sold live as tubifex, host myxozoan parasites such as the one behind whirling disease.
- **Too much vegetable or fruit.** It causes bloating in this small a fish.

<FunFact source="Merck Veterinary Manual; Sanders et al. 2010, Diseases of Aquatic Organisms">
Never leave a dead fish in the tank or let tankmates scavenge a visibly sick fish's body. Neon tetra disease is caused by the microsporidian parasite Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, and if a fish dies of it and its body is eaten by others, those fish contract it too. There is no cure and no effective treatment. Prompt removal of dead or sick fish is the only control that exists, and leaving one in the tank is how an outbreak spreads to the whole school.
</FunFact>

## Feeding a Neon Among Faster Fish

A neon eats in the middle of the water column and eats in small bites, which puts it last in line beside quick surface feeders like [zebra danios](/blog/zebra-danio-feeding-guide/). Food that sinks slowly through mid-water reaches the school before it reaches the bottom, and a pinch dropped at each end of the tank splits the rush. Watch the neons at feeding time rather than the tank: a school that hangs back while others eat is a school that is going hungry.

## Why Neons Stop Eating

1. **Food too large.** Food that's too large gets taken and spat back out repeatedly, which functionally starves the fish even while food is visibly going into the tank. Flake made for bigger fish is the usual culprit, and crushing it fixes the problem.
2. **Internal parasites.** Spironucleus, an intestinal flagellate, brings weight loss and a lost appetite, and kills fry and juveniles.
3. **Neon tetra disease.** A neon that turns restless, shows white patches in the skin, or sets itself apart from the school fits the warning signs of the disease, which is virtually untreatable once it is advanced.

## When to Worry

Color is a poor hunger gauge. A neon turns duller, with a violet stripe, at night, and lighter in bright light over a pale substrate in clear water, so a pale school at the morning feed is showing its night coloring. The signs that need action:

- **One fish hanging apart and skipping meals.** Move it out of the main tank straight away. Columnaris, a treatable bacterial infection, can mimic neon tetra disease, which is why [the lookalike gets ruled out](/blog/neon-tetra-health-issues-guide/) before a fish is written off.
- **A red thread at the vent.** Camallanus shows as blood-red worms in the gut and protruding from the vent, most often in a tank fed live cyclops or daphnia.
- **A swollen belly or stalled growth.** Heavy tapeworm infections cause both, though many infected fish show no sign at all.

## When You Go Away

Time away means a person who feeds the fish or an automatic feeder, and both can overfeed a school this small.

For a sitter, measure out every meal before you leave, for example in a labeled pill organizer with one compartment per feeding, so the portions cannot grow. Walk them through it once, and leave your number.

For an automatic feeder, set it up and run it at home for several days before the trip, with a food it dispenses cleanly, and check that it neither jams nor drops in too much.

A fish this small eats in bites a keeper can barely see, and getting the size of each bite right does more for a neon school than any brand of food.
