# Common Crested Gecko Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Nearly everything on this list traces back to husbandry, specifically calcium, humidity, and substrate choices. Get those right and most of these problems never show up.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Caused by a deficiency in calcium or vitamin D3. It's progressive, which is why catching it early matters.

**Early sign:** a soft or rubbery lower jaw is often the first thing owners notice.

**Advanced signs:** floppy or weak limbs, a visibly misaligned jaw, a kinked spine, tremors, and difficulty climbing. Once the disease reaches this stage, some of the damage can be permanent even with treatment.

Prevention is straightforward: feed a [complete crested gecko diet powder](/blog/crested-gecko-feeding-guide/) (Pangea or Repashy are widely used), supplement with calcium, and <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TiqeC4" product="UVB - Arcadia Forest 6% (ProT5 kit, 36in/39W)">provide low-level UVB</AffiliateLink>. A vet diagnoses MBD through bloodwork and x-rays, so if you notice a soft jaw, don't wait to see if it gets worse on its own. The UVB half is the part that slips quietly, since [output falls off with distance and with bulb age](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) long before the lamp looks dim.

## Floppy Tail Syndrome (FTS)

The tail hangs over the gecko's head or sits at an odd angle relative to the body, typically from chronic upside-down resting against glass over time. It's sometimes associated with calcium or pelvic issues and occasionally overlaps with MBD, though it isn't diagnostic of it on its own. If you notice this, it's worth a vet visit to rule out an underlying calcium problem.

## Impaction

This is the most common health problem in pet geckos generally, and crested geckos are no exception. It's a blockage in the digestive tract, usually caused by loose substrate (sand or bark), prey that's too large, or shed skin that wasn't fully passed.

**Watch for:** no bowel movement for a week or more, a visibly bloated abdomen, straining, lethargy, and appetite loss.

Prevent it by using solid substrate (paper towel or tile) for young geckos, and by keeping feeder insects no larger than the space between your gecko's eyes. A warm 15-minute soak can sometimes help a mild case. If symptoms persist beyond 24 to 48 hours, see a vet.

## Retained or Stuck Shed (Dysecdysis)

Almost always a humidity problem. Shed skin sticks around the toes, tail tip, or eyes instead of coming off cleanly, and if left alone it can cut off circulation, in severe cases costing a gecko a toe. ## Respiratory Infection

Caused by an enclosure that's <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3QHrM81" product="Digital thermometer/hygrometer combo">too cold or too wet</AffiliateLink>. Look for open-mouth breathing, visible mucus, wheezing, and lethargy. This needs a vet promptly; it does not clear up with waiting.

## Gravid Females and Infertile Eggs

A female crested gecko does not need a male to produce eggs. A mature female kept alone can still develop and lay infertile clutches, and producing an infertile egg is harder on her than a fertile one. A laying female typically drops two eggs about once a month through the season, so any adult female needs a lay box whether or not she has ever met a male: a container with a few inches of slightly moist coconut fiber and fir bark, roughly half and half.

Each clutch draws on her calcium. Crested geckos store calcium in two small sacs at the back of the mouth, and sacs that shrink noticeably over a season mean her reserves are running down. Keep calcium in the diet, and have a vet check her if the sacs look small.

Laid infertile eggs can simply be removed. The concern is the egg that does not come out. A gravid female may eat less but should stay bright and alert. One that stops eating, turns weak or lethargic, or strains with a swollen belly after her expected laying date may be egg-bound, and that is a vet visit the same day. Most reptiles seen by vets for egg binding are single females kept with no male, so the risk applies to every adult female. Low calcium, dehydration, wrong temperatures or humidity, and no suitable place to lay are the usual causes.

## A Few Other Things to Know

- **Dehydration** shows up as sunken eyes and repeated stuck sheds.
- **Skin and scale issues** can develop from chronically high humidity combined with poor enclosure hygiene.
- **Internal parasites** and, less commonly, **cryptosporidiosis** are also on the list. Crypto in particular is serious and often difficult to treat, which is part of why a fecal exam is worth doing at your gecko's first vet visit.

## When to See an Exotic Vet

Bring a new gecko in within the first 30 days of ownership, and plan on an annual wellness exam with a fecal test after that as a baseline, not just when something looks wrong. See a vet promptly for a soft or misshapen jaw, no bowel movement for a week or more, breathing difficulty, ongoing weight loss, or any neurological symptoms.

A gram scale is one of the most useful tools you can own for this species. Weigh your gecko at least monthly and log it, more often while it is off its food. A slow loss is often the earliest sign that something's off, well before other symptoms appear, and our [reptile stool, urates, and hydration guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) covers the other check worth doing alongside it.
