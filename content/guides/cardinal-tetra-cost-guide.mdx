# How Much Does Cardinal Tetra Ownership Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cardinal tetras cost a little more than neon tetras to buy and about the same to keep, and the budgeting variable is the same one: this is a schooling fish, so you're never pricing out a single animal. The cardinal is a small characin from the Rio Negro and Orinoco drainages of Brazil, Colombia and Venezuela, and part of the supply is still caught in those rivers with dip nets and baited traps.

## The Fish: $3.30 to $8 Each

Tank-bred cardinal tetras run about $3.30 to $8 a fish from online sellers, a little less per fish in a larger pack. Since this is a schooling species, you're buying a group, not one fish. A starting school of 10, the number this species needs to settle, runs about $35 to $80 before shipping. Eight is the least to buy, and a mixed-sex group of 8 to 10 is the recommended start.

That is noticeably more than ten [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-cost-guide/), which sell for $1 to $3 each, so a cardinal costs two to three times what a neon does. It's worth budgeting for rather than discovering after the fact. The gap has a supply-chain reason: neon tetra is bred at scale and rarely wild-caught, while captive breeding of the cardinal is possible but difficult, so a meaningful share of the trade still comes from a wild fishery on Brazil's Rio Negro.

<FunFact source="Chao (2001), Project Piaba review">
Cardinal tetra alone has made up roughly 80% of the ornamental fish exported from the Rio Negro region, averaged across the export records out of Manaus. More than 1,600 fishers from the riverine communities of the middle Rio Negro take part in the fishery, which makes this one small fish the indicator species for the whole trade.
</FunFact>

## Upfront Setup: %%setup:cardinal-tetra%% for a 20 Gallon Long

<CostTable guide="cardinal-tetra" section="necessities" />

A 20 gallon long is the practical start for the school of 10 this species needs, on a base of at least 24 x 12 inches. Basic decor comes on top of the table. The sand is black because cardinals turn lighter in bright conditions over a pale substrate in clear water, and a sandy floor is the look the species suits best.

The test kit is the one item worth budgeting for on purpose. Cardinal tetras are sensitive to nitrite and need ammonia at zero, both of which show up in an incompletely cycled tank: in laboratory tests, half the fish died within 96 hours at 1.1 mg/L of nitrite. Confirming your cycle before stocking, not after, is the cheapest insurance this species has, and what a finished cycle reads on a test kit is a different question from how long the tank has been running.

## The Extras: A Spare Tank for Quarantine

<CostTable guide="cardinal-tetra" section="extras" />

New cardinals get at least 30 days in a bare tank of their own before they join the display. In tropical fish, disease often shows 1 to 3 weeks after shipping, which is why 30 days is the floor and not a generous margin. How to run that tank, and what to treat in it, is the subject of the [fish quarantine guide](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/).

Topping up a school later is a second purchase with its own quarantine. Each new batch, two fish or ten, spends its own 30 days apart while the display waits, which is the practical case for buying all ten in one go.

## Buying Checklist: Wild-Caught, Captive-Bred and Healthy

Cardinals reach the shop from two sources: a significant proportion of the fish in the trade are still wild caught or come from community-led projects such as Project Piaba on the middle Rio Negro, and the rest are bred. The difference that matters to the budget is that wild fish are less tolerant of deteriorating water than commercially produced ones, so a wild school is the one that most needs a cycled tank and a quarantine tank waiting for it.

Whatever the origin, the fish itself shows its condition. These are the changes that mark a sick fish:

| Look at | Walk away if |
|---|---|
| Body | Thin, or bloated |
| Eyes | Sunken in, or popped out |
| Fins | Clamped down, frayed or bloody |
| Skin | Lesions or growths |

## Shipping Fish Online

A shipped school spends the trip in a sealed bag, and the water changes on the way: the fishes' breathing and waste push the pH down and the ammonia up. Cold is the cardinal's own risk. In laboratory tolerance tests, half the fish died within 96 hours at about 67°F (19.6°C), only 6 degrees under the bottom of the 73 to 84°F range the species is kept in, and none survived 59°F (15°C).

## Ongoing Costs: Food, Conditioner, Test Kit Refills and Power

A micro-pellet food runs roughly $15 to $30 a year and water conditioner $10 to $15 a year, close to what neon tetra costs to run. The test kit bought with the setup gets refilled as each reagent runs out. The heater, filter and light also add to the power bill every month, by an amount that depends on the equipment and the room.

## Vet Costs

A fish vet is a real option, and the one worth paying for when a school keeps losing fish and the cause is not clear. Professional directories list the [vets who treat fish](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/), and a fish disease lab works from live, sick fish and a water sample. Day-to-day health is still mostly water quality, with over-the-counter treatment where the cause is clear. Where it is not, fish are often given one product after another when the first fails, which wastes time and money and usually does more harm than good.

## Lifespan

A well-kept cardinal tetra lives about five years in captivity under good, stable conditions. That is dramatically longer than what this species gets in the wild, where it lives about a year in its native blackwater streams and wild fish probably die after spawning, so most don't survive to see a second year. An aquarium with stable water is an easier life than the one this fish evolved for.

## Why the School Sets the Bill

Cardinal tetra and neon tetra are both priced per fish, so the schooling requirement sets the total. Budgeting for a full group from the start, rather than starting with two or three and adding more later, is both the more humane approach and the more cost-effective one, since an understocked, stressed school is more prone to health issues, which cost more to correct than simply stocking correctly on day one.
