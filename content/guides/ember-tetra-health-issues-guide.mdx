# Common Ember Tetra Health Issues (and Why the Ich Heat Cure Stops at 82°F)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

No disease belongs to the ember tetra in particular. This fish of about 0.8 inch, from the soft, weakly acidic backwaters of the Araguaia River basin in central Brazil, meets the problems any small soft-water tetra meets, and poor diet and poor water quality raise the risk of every one of them. Two things set it apart from some of its relatives. The top of its temperature range sits below the heat that kills ich, which changes how that disease gets treated, and the soft water it needs is the water where copper, a standard fish medication, is most dangerous.

## Signs That Need Action Today

A head count at feeding time is the daily check in a school of 10. These are the signs that cannot wait for tomorrow.

**Test ammonia and nitrite first:**

- Fish gasping at the surface or breathing fast.
- Two or more fish showing the same sign at once.
- A fish found dead. Take the body out of the tank before anything else.

**Start ich treatment today:**

- White spots like grains of salt on the body or fins.
- Fast breathing that continues once the water tests clean, since ich can infect the gills.

**Watch closely and check again tomorrow:**

- Flashing: rubbing against plants, glass or substrate.
- A fish off its food or hanging listless.
- Extra mucus on the skin.
- Color fading to dull.
- Ragged or bloody fin edges, or a cotton-like tuft on the skin or fins.
- A fish growing thin while it keeps eating.

The first four are the early signs of ich, and they show up before the spots do, so a fish showing them gets a close look for spots every day until the signs pass. Torn fins and cotton-like growths point to fin rot or fungus, and a fish that thins while it eats points to internal parasites or worse. Gasping, lethargy and a lost appetite are just as often the water as a parasite, which is why [telling a water problem from an illness](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) starts with the test kit and only then moves on to the fish.

## What a Healthy Ember Looks Like

Every sign above is a change from normal, so normal is worth knowing. A healthy fish has smooth, unblemished scales, fins and skin, with fins intact and no bloody or ragged edges. Its eyes are bright and clear, and it swims normally and steadily around the tank. A sick fish tends to swim erratically, circling or drifting, or hangs listless.

| Check | Healthy | Worth a closer look |
|-------|---------|---------------------|
| Skin and scales | Smooth and unblemished | White spots, a film, extra mucus, cotton-like tufts |
| Fins | Intact, with clean edges | Ragged, bloody or wearing away |
| Eyes | Bright and clear | Cloudy or bulging |
| Swimming | Steady, moving with the school | Circling, drifting or hanging apart |
| Body | Even and full | Thinning while the fish still eats |
| Color | Its usual orange | Fading to dull |

Color needs reading against the fish itself. Adult males are more intensely colored than females, especially in spawning condition, so a school with a spread of orange is normal. The fish to watch is one fading from its own usual shade.

## Water Quality Comes First

Embers come from soft, weakly acidic water, and they do best kept in it: 75 to 82°F, pH 5.0 to 7.0 and 1 to 10 dGH. Holding the tank steady inside that range matters more than hitting one exact number, and a liquid test kit run before each weekly water change catches a drift before it harms the fish. In water this soft, [KH is what holds pH still](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/), and it is the reading to understand before adjusting anything. It is also the reading that decides whether copper can go anywhere near the tank.

A new tank is where water does the most harm. In new tank syndrome, ammonia or nitrite climbs high enough to poison the fish, and they suddenly turn lethargic, stop eating and often die. Ammonia and nitrite get tested until neither shows up any more, before the school goes in.

A dull ember usually means its care slipped somewhere along the way. Diet is the other half of it. Poor nutrition stunts growth, causes deformities and depresses the immune system, which is how a bad diet raises the risk of every infection below. Overfeeding brings a run of health problems too, and an ember's small mouth takes tiny, slow-sinking food, so a fine food fed in small amounts suits it better than a pinch of flake that drifts down uneaten.

Company is part of health too: an ember is a shoaling fish, and a school of 10 is part of keeping it well.

## Ich: Warm to the Ceiling, Then Treat

Ich, or white spot disease, usually reaches a tank through infected water or a newly added fish. The spots on the body and fins tend to appear long after the infection starts, so the watch list above is usually the first warning. A fish whose gills are infected breathes fast or gasps at the surface. A large infestation can kill, especially in a fish already weakened by something else.

Most strains of ich cannot survive water above 86°F, and for fish that live comfortably that warm, holding the tank there clears an outbreak. The ember's range tops out at 82°F, four degrees short, so the heat method is not for this fish. Heat still helps up to its ceiling, because the parasite multiplies faster in warmer water and a faster cycle brings each new generation into reach of the treatment sooner. Raise the tank slowly to 82°F, using a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">aquarium heater</AffiliateLink>, with an air stone running for the extra oxygen warm water needs, and let one of these do the killing:

- **Aquarium salt**, never table salt, at about a tablespoon per 5 gallons, added gradually so the fish have time to adjust. Once the infection clears, several water changes take the salt back out.
- **An ich medication**, dosed to the label or to a vet's directions. Vets typically treat the water with formalin or copper sulfate products over several doses. In a soft ember tank, copper is the one to avoid: it grows more toxic to the fish as alkalinity falls, and the section on copper below gives the line.

Keep the treatment going until every sign of illness is gone. Warming to the ceiling and treating is the same route [every fish whose ceiling stops short of 86°F](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/) takes, the neon tetra among them.

## Fin Rot and Fungus: Damage First, Then Infection

Both of these usually follow something else. Water molds such as Saprolegnia are secondary invaders: they move in after an injury, poor water quality or water that has run too cold, and they show as grayish-white, cotton-like growths on the skin, gills, eyes or fins. Fin rot is a bacterial infection, often Aeromonas, and it leaves the fins ragged. Columnaris, another bacterial disease, can pass for either, with skin lesions under a slimy or cotton-like covering.

For an ember, prevention is mostly the care it already needs. It is very peaceful and does not compete well with boisterous or much larger tankmates, so calm company keeps fins whole, and steady water and a sound diet keep its defenses up.

Treatment starts with the water, then moves the fish to the hospital tank. Once the environment is clean, a single potassium permanganate treatment can often control external Saprolegnia, and early potassium permanganate can help with columnaris. A bacterial infection past that point needs antibiotics, and lab testing picks the right one. Rotating or stacking several drugs in the hope one works, known as shotgunning, is dangerous and breeds resistant bacteria.

## Internal Parasites

Internal parasites work out of sight, and the common signs are weight loss, lethargy and a poor appetite. One can be seen. Camallanus are blood-red worms that can show in the gut and protrude from the vent of an infected fish, which gives the condition its name, red worm disease. The small crustaceans Cyclops and Daphnia are intermediate hosts for it, and fish can be heavily infected wherever those hosts are present, so live crustaceans are one way it gets in. The intestinal flagellate Spironucleus shows as weight loss and white, stringy feces, and heavy tapeworm loads leave fish thin and listless.

Each has its own drug: fenbendazole or levamisole for Camallanus, metronidazole for Spironucleus, and praziquantel for tapeworms. Which one is needed comes from a microscope, since fresh smears or biopsies with live parasites in them are how these are diagnosed. That is work for a fish vet, and a dewormer picked by guesswork may treat a parasite the fish does not have.

## Neon Tetra Disease and Its Lookalikes

Neon tetra disease is caused by a microsporidian parasite, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, that infects the skeletal muscle. It is named for its type host, the neon tetra, but it is recorded from other tetras and from angelfish, rasboras, barbs and zebrafish, so the name does not confine it to neons. Its spores are extremely resistant, it can pass from fish to fish within a tank, and microsporidia are considered untreatable. No study has looked at it in embers specifically.

Mycobacteriosis is the lookalike that matters most. Its signs are variable and nonspecific: wasting, paleness, skeletal deformities including a bent spine, bulging eyes, a swollen belly, and skin ulcers or bleeding. It has no effective treatment in fish either, it cannot be detected with nonlethal tests, and infected fish are usually destroyed. Low dissolved oxygen, low pH and a heavy organic load predispose a tank to it. An ember tank runs acidic by design, so the two of those a keeper can work on are oxygen and a clean tank. An infected aquarium is disinfected before other fish go in, and because these bacteria can cause a skin infection in people, the hands that work in that tank need the same care.

The rule that protects any school applies to both: a dead fish comes out of the tank as soon as it is found, whatever killed it.

## Copper, Soft Water and Shrimp

Copper is the medication to be most careful with around an ember, for two reasons. The first is the fish. Copper grows more toxic to fish as the water's total alkalinity falls, and below 50 ppm total alkalinity, about 2.8 dKH, copper treatments are not recommended at all because the risk of killing fish is high. An ember tank kept soft with little KH can sit under that line, so a KH test comes before any copper product is even considered.

The second is everything else in the tank. Copper is toxic to invertebrates such as snails, and dwarf shrimp often share an ember tank. Shrimp come out before any medication goes in, whatever the product and whatever its label says, because no medication should be assumed harmless to them. Formalin, the other common ich treatment, is toxic in its own right and is used only by its instructions.

The simplest way to keep both problems out of the display is the hospital tank in the next section. The sick fish is treated there, and the school, the shrimp and the plants never meet the drug.

## Quarantine Every New Fish

New fish are the usual way ich reaches an established tank, and a separate tank is worth having no matter where the fish come from. Quarantine starts at the store: a clean tank, with every other fish in it looking healthy too. Every new ember then spends [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before it meets the school, and longer periods may be necessary. Arrival day carries its own risk. On a long trip, ammonia builds in the bag from the fish's own waste and pH drops as carbon dioxide rises, so the fish arrive already under strain. The bag water stays out of the tank, since it can carry that waste and pest snail eggs along with it, and acclimating fish this small means checking the bag and the net afterward for any that got stuck.

## When a Fish Vet Is Worth It, and How to Find One

Most of the diagnoses above end at a microscope or a lab. Parasites are confirmed on fresh smears, the right antibiotic comes from lab testing, and a dead fish tells the most at a necropsy done by a clinic or diagnostic lab that knows fish. Guessing with store medicines skips all of that, and in an ember tank a wrong guess can mean copper in soft water or a string of antibiotics that leaves resistant bacteria behind.

Vet licenses do not cross state lines, but any vet can consult with an aquatic vet in any state. So the first call can go to a local exotic or small-animal vet, asking whether they will take the case with a fish vet's help. Two directories list fish vets: the American Association of Fish Veterinarians, searchable by state or country, and the World Aquatic Veterinary Medical Association, which is international. Advice online without an exam is limited, and it cannot legally set a treatment plan.

What to bring:

- **Tank water**, at least 1 quart, in a plastic bag or a clean plastic bottle, capped under the water's surface so no air bubbles are trapped.
- **A chlorine sample** if the tank is filled from municipal water: a second, smaller sample of under about 3 ounces in a glass container.
- **The history**: tank volume, water source, filtration, recent additions and whether they were quarantined, any medications already used, and how many fish of which species share the tank.
- **Video or photos** if the fish cannot travel, showing the tank and its filtration and both the affected fish and the healthy ones.
- **A water sample with any dead fish** sent for necropsy.

An ember that is eating, holding its orange color and moving with the school in steady, soft water is a healthy one. Nearly everything that goes wrong with the species starts in that water or arrives with the next fish.
