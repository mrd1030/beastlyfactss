# Rummy-Nose Tetra Feeding: Wild Diet, Staples, and How Often to Feed

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The rummy-nose tetra (Petitella bleheri, formerly Hemigrammus bleheri) eats almost anything put in front of it, as long as it fits. An adult reaches only 1.5 to 2 inches (4 to 5 cm), it has a small mouth, and the whole job of feeding one comes down to three things: small food, plenty of variety, and portions the school can finish.

## What Rummy-Noses Eat in the Wild

In the middle Rio Negro basin of Brazil, the stomach contents of 177 wild fish show an omnivore, and most of what it ate was produced inside the water itself rather than dropping in from the forest above. The menu shifted with the river. During the flood, microcrustaceans made up a larger share. In the dry season the fish leaned more on filamentous algae, chironomid (midge) larvae and fragments of plants.

That mix of tiny animal prey and plant material is the template for the tank: a dried staple that covers the basics, with small crustaceans and insect larvae offered alongside it.

## The Staple Diet

Base the daily diet on a quality dried food: flake, granules or a slow-sinking <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004PBB5TK?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Semi-Floating Micro Pellets, 0.77 oz">micro-pellet</AffiliateLink>. A varied diet is what brings out the best color, so rotate between two or three dried foods instead of relying on one tub.

<KeyTakeaway>
Size every food to the mouth. Pick nano or micro-pellet grades, crumble flake before it goes in, and choose live and frozen foods no bigger than baby brine shrimp or daphnia.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Live and Frozen Foods

Small live and frozen foods round out the diet and come closest to the microcrustaceans and midge larvae of the wild menu. Good choices include baby brine shrimp, brine shrimp, daphnia and mosquito larvae. Krill and salmon-based foods are also fed for color.

Food alone will not keep the red nose bright, though. It fades when a rummy-nose is stressed or the water is off, so a school that has gone pale needs a water test before anyone changes the menu. The [rummy-nose tetra health guide](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-health-issues-guide/) covers what a faded nose can point to.

<FunFact>
Wild rummy-noses breed on the river's schedule. In the middle Rio Negro, females ripen as the flood rises between December and May, the same season wild fish carry their fullest stomachs, and spawning peaks as the flood ends, from March to May.
</FunFact>

## How Often to Feed, and How Much

Feed adults at least once a day. For a maintenance diet, aquarium fish need about 3% of their body weight in food daily, which for a fish this size is a very small pinch spread across the whole school.

Judge the portion by watching the fish eat. If pellets are still sinking untouched once the school loses interest, cut the next pinch down, because leftovers rot into ammonia that the [nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) in a mature tank then has to process.

Watching also shows whether the rummy-noses are eating at all. This is a gentle fish that loses out at feeding time to rowdy or much larger tankmates, which can take most of a meal before it gets a share. Companions of similar size and calm temperament, matched using [the checks two species need before sharing a tank](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/), keep the food going to the fish it was meant for.

## Feeding Fry

Adult rummy-noses eat their own eggs given the chance, so breeders move the adults out once eggs or fry appear. The newly hatched fry start on infusoria, then move on to live vinegar eels and powdered fry food as they grow.

A rummy-nose fed small, varied food in portions it can finish has almost everything the wild Rio Negro gave it. The [rummy-nose tetra encyclopedia profile](/encyclopedia/animal/rummy-nose-tetra/) has the natural history.
