# How Much Does a Giant Millipede Really Cost? (And a Supply Note Worth Knowing)

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Giant African millipedes are inexpensive to keep month to month, though the animal itself costs more than you might expect for an invertebrate, and there's a real reason behind that.

## Why the Price Runs Higher Than Other Invertebrates

There is no import ban, but a USDA PPQ 526 plant pest permit is required to bring a giant African millipede into the US or move one across state lines, and imports have dried up, largely over the mites that live on wild animals, with the state-by-state picture in the giant millipede legal guide. Practically, this means the giant millipedes available in the US today are captive-bred, and the species has never bred well in captivity. That keeps supply genuinely limited and prices higher than you'd expect for an animal this low-maintenance to care for. Some states also have their own additional sales restrictions or permit requirements, so the state question comes before the purchase.

## The Millipede Itself: $75 to $150 or More

Compared to most pet invertebrates, this is a real step up in purchase price, driven entirely by the supply situation above. Nothing about the care is harder.

## Where to Buy One

In the US that means a captive-bred animal from an invertebrate breeder or specialty dealer, because the import route is closed. A seller who can say the animal was bred in the US, and roughly when it hatched, is selling the thing the price is paying for.

Two checks before paying:

- **What species it is.** Record-keeping in the trade is poor, so the label is not always accurate. Long-held zoo animals displayed as giant African millipedes have turned out to be *Spiropoeus fischeri*, a West and Central African species, so ask what the seller knows about where the line came from.
- **The shell.** Look over every segment for cracks. A cracked exoskeleton can leak hemolymph and let infection in, which makes it a life-threatening injury rather than a cosmetic one.

Buy one animal if you want no babies. Anyone hoping to breed buys a small group instead of a pair, because juveniles cannot be sexed and a "pair" of young animals is a guess. Every new arrival spends three months in its own tub before joining an established group, the same [invertebrate quarantine](/blog/invertebrate-quarantine-cleaning-and-escapes-guide/) window used for tarantulas and scorpions.

## Ongoing Costs: Minimal

Fresh produce and periodic substrate or leaf litter replenishment make up nearly the entire monthly cost, just a few dollars. The deep organic substrate is both the biggest line here and the animal's food, which is why topping it up is not optional the way a decoration would be. A breeding group costs more in substrate than a single animal, since deeper substrate means more food for a growing colony.

## Vet Costs

Essentially none. Invertebrate-experienced exotic vets exist but are rarely needed, and most issues are managed through husbandry alone. A millipede that does go to a vet travels in a ventilated container with several inches of its own substrate, kept warm and out of direct sun.

<FunFact>
A giant millipede typically lives 5 to 7 years in captivity, and some pass a decade. That's genuinely remarkable longevity for an invertebrate, well beyond what you'd expect from an animal this small and this inexpensive to feed.
</FunFact>

## Lifespan: Exceptionally Long for an Invertebrate

5 to 7 years is typical in captivity, and some individuals live upwards of 10. This is remarkable longevity for an invertebrate, considerably longer than the [praying mantis](/blog/praying-mantis-cost-guide/), [jumping spider](/blog/jumping-spider-cost-guide/), or [hissing cockroach](/blog/madagascar-hissing-cockroach-cost-guide/), and worth factoring into your decision as a real, multi-year commitment.

The [four-species overview](/blog/jacksonschameleon-canary-millipede-corydoras-overview/) sets it beside a Jackson's chameleon, a canary and a corydoras catfish as the one animal of the four that cannot bite or sting at all.
