# Boa Constrictor Feeding: Schedule, Prey Size, and Why Adults Eat So Rarely

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Boa constrictor feeding is simple in principle, whole prey covers this species' nutritional needs completely, the skill is getting size and frequency right as your snake matures.

## The Staple Diet

Boas eat whole, frozen-thawed rodents, mice for younger animals, progressing to rats as they grow. This isn't a simplified diet, it's a complete one, whole prey delivers the protein, fat, calcium, and vitamins a boa actually needs in the right balance, without requiring you to piece together a varied menu the way you would for an omnivore. Always use frozen-thawed rather than live prey, and offer it with <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CBKD64LP?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Lasnten Extra Long Stainless Steel Reptile Feeding Tongs">feeding tongs</AffiliateLink> rather than your hand.

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Prey size is the rule that matters most here: offer prey no wider than your boa's body at its widest point. A snake with a 1.5 inch midsection should get prey no wider than 1.5 inches, not bigger just because it looks like it could handle more. Frequency drops substantially as your boa matures:

- **Babies (0 to 6 months):** every 5 to 7 days, starting on pinkie mice.
- **Juveniles (6 to 12 months):** every 7 to 10 days, moving to fuzzies and hoppers.
- **Adults (3-plus years):** every 10 to 14 days, extending to every 2 to 4 weeks for mature animals, mature females roughly every 2 to 3 weeks, adult males every 3 to 4 weeks.

<KeyTakeaway>
After a properly sized meal, you should see only a subtle lump, not a dramatic bulge. That every-2-to-4-weeks adult interval surprises a lot of first-time keepers who assume more frequent feeding is better care. It isn't, for this species it's the opposite.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Foods to Avoid

Prey that's wider than your snake's body is the main thing to avoid, it's the leading cause of regurgitation, a genuinely traumatic event that can injure or kill a boa. Skip live rodents entirely, a live rat or mouse can bite and cause real injury to your snake, and skip wild-caught rodents too, given the parasite and toxin risk. Never feed by hand, this risks a feeding-response bite meant for the prey.

## Supplementation

Generally none needed. Whole prey is nutritionally complete on its own. Some keepers occasionally dust prey with a light calcium or vitamin supplement, since commercially bred feeder rodents can run slightly low in certain nutrients like vitamin D, but this is a genuine option, not a requirement.

## The Mistakes That Matter

Overfeeding is the single biggest issue with this species today, feeding too often or with prey that's too large produces obese boas with shortened lifespans. The second mistake is handling too soon after a meal, disturbance during digestion is a real trigger for regurgitation. And oversized prey remains a genuine, avoidable cause of regurgitation and internal injury on its own.

## When a Healthy Adult Refuses a Meal

An adult boa turning down a meal is common, and most of the causes are benign. The usual list: a shed coming on, breeding season (males in particular often go off food in spring, and gravid females eat less or stop), winter, the stress of a new home or a rearranged room, too little privacy, and an enclosure that is not warm enough to digest a meal. Adult snakes are built to fast. Studied species can drop their resting metabolic demand by as much as 72% while going without food, which is why a skipped month in a well-conditioned adult is rarely an emergency on its own.

Work through it in order:

1. **Check the heat.** Read the basking surface with an infrared thermometer and the warm-side air with a digital probe. Dial and gauge thermometers are not accurate enough to rule temperature out.
2. **Look for a reason.** Cloudy eyes, a recent move, a female that might be gravid, or the time of year often explain the refusal.
3. **Stop handling.** Leave the snake alone for as long as it is not eating. A new arrival should get at least a week to settle before its first offer.
4. **Weigh it weekly.** A gram scale gives the baseline that turns "it hasn't eaten" into a number.
5. **Keep offering on schedule.** Offer the usual frozen-thawed prey at the usual interval the snake knows.

The line between a fast and a problem is weight. A boa that keeps refusing while its weight drops, or one that refuses alongside mites, wheezing, a swollen mouth, or odd head movements, needs a vet, who will start with a physical exam and a fecal test. Parasites, infection, and viral disease all show up first as a snake that will not eat, and a younger boa has far less margin for a long fast than a mature one.

This species' feeding schedule stretches out further than most new keepers expect: a mature male eating every 3 to 4 weeks is on schedule. The [snake brumation guide](/blog/snake-brumation-guide/) covers the winter slowdown that explains many seasonal refusals in boids.
