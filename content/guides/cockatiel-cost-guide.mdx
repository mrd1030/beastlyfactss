# How Much Does a Cockatiel Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cockatiels are relatively affordable to buy, but the budgeting story here is how long you'll be caring for one. A 12 to 15 year lifespan means this is a long-term financial commitment even though the entry cost is modest, and a well-kept bird can reach about 25. The longevity record is 35 years in captivity, one exceptional bird.

Normal gray is typically the cheapest color mutation, and buying directly from a breeder is common. The cage is frequently the single biggest line item in the whole setup, often costing more than the bird itself, which is worth keeping in mind if you're tempted to cut corners there instead of elsewhere. The pellet, seed, millet and cuttlebone rows are the first supply; after that they move to the ongoing table below. Plain paper for the tray and the rest of a small first aid kit (gauze, a small towel, a flashlight) are household items most homes already have, so they are not priced here. Against a budgie, the difference that outlasts the purchase price is lifespan and cage size, and the [budgie vs cockatiel comparison](/blog/budgie-vs-cockatiel-guide/) sets those side by side.

<FunFact>
The "normal gray" cockatiel is actually the closest color to the wild type found across the Australian outback, and it's usually the least expensive mutation for a simple genetic reason: it requires no selective breeding to produce. Every other color variation carries a rarity premium built from generations of deliberate breeding work.
</FunFact>

## The Vet Cost That Catches People Off Guard

An avian exam is due when the bird comes home and at least yearly after that, and fecal testing or bloodwork adds to each one. Reproductive emergencies, like egg binding, come on top of that with no warning.

Avian vets are a genuinely specialized field, harder to find and often pricier than a general small-animal clinic, so it's worth locating one near you before an emergency forces the search, and writing that number into a [bird emergency plan](/blog/bird-emergency-travel-guide/) before the power goes out. Pet insurance for birds is uncommon, so most owners end up covering vet costs directly, which makes a dedicated emergency fund worth having.

## What the First Exam Pays For

The first visit is the most important one, and it belongs within one to two weeks of bringing the bird home. It starts with a physical exam: the bird is weighed, its mouth checked, and its heart and breathing listened to. The tests that make up the rest of the bill are chosen from there. Blood work covers a complete blood count and a chemistry panel (calcium, protein, organ function). A fecal exam looks for intestinal parasites. Swabs of the mouth and vent check for abnormal bacteria or yeast. Screens for psittacosis, polyomavirus and beak and feather disease are added depending on the exam and the bird's history. Whole-body X-rays need sedation or gas anesthesia, which carries its own charge.

After that, the floor is one checkup a year, and many avian vets recommend two. A twice-yearly schedule doubles the exam line before any tests are added.

## The Budgeting Takeaway

The bird itself is the smallest number in this whole picture. What determines your long-term cost is the quality of the cage you buy (worth investing in upfront, since a too-small cage becomes a welfare issue, not just an inconvenience) and how seriously you take avian vet care over a 12 to 15 year relationship.

Owners eyeing something bigger usually look at a cockatoo next, and every one of these lines grows with the bird: the [cockatoo comparison](/blog/cockatiel-vs-cockatoo-guide/) shows how much larger that commitment runs.
