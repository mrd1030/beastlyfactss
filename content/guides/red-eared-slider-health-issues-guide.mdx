# Common Red-Eared Slider Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most problems in captive sliders trace directly back to one of three husbandry failures: inadequate UVB, poor water quality, or an unbalanced diet.

## Metabolic Bone Disease and Soft Shell

VCA Animal Hospitals describes metabolic bone disease in aquatic turtles as "an imbalance of calcium and phosphorus due to an improper diet, lack of adequate ultraviolet (UV) light, or a combination of both," showing up as a misshapen shell or deformed leg bones and, in severe cases, stunted growth that keeps a turtle from ever reaching normal adult size. Watch for a soft or misshapen shell, deformed leg bones, lethargy, swollen limbs, and difficulty swimming or walking.

This is the classic result of weak or absent UVB combined with a poor diet. Early cases can genuinely improve once lighting and diet are corrected, alongside veterinary care, but advanced cases can be permanent or fatal. ## Pyramiding Is Not the Same as MBD

These two get merged constantly, including in a lot of care writing, so it is worth separating them. Metabolic bone disease gives you a soft shell. Pyramiding gives you a hard one with raised, peaked scutes. The shell is growing abnormally in both cases, but not for the same reason and not with the same fix.

<FunFact>
Pyramiding is widely assumed to be a calcium problem, and the one experiment that tested that directly found the opposite. Stancel, Dierenfeld and Schoknecht fed 48 young red-eared sliders four diets for 150 days, including calcium and phosphorus supplemented versions, and published the result in the title: supplementation decreased growth but did not induce pyramiding. Every turtle finished with a normally shaped shell.
</FunFact>

Pyramiding is multifactorial. Tree of Life Exotic Pet Medical Center lists excess dietary protein, overfeeding and high-calorie diets, calcium and phosphorus imbalance, low humidity, no nighttime temperature drop, insufficient UVB, lack of exercise, rapid captive growth rates, and concurrent illness as contributors. Note where calcium sits in that list: present, but one item among nine, and the slider feeding trial above found supplementation alone did not produce it.

For an aquatic species the practical lever is usually growth rate rather than lighting. Overfeeding a slider, particularly on protein-rich pellets and meaty treats, grows the shell faster than it can form correctly.

None of this makes UVB optional. Weak or absent UVB genuinely causes MBD, which is the more dangerous of the two conditions and the one that turns up on this list far more often.

## Shell Rot

VCA Animal Hospitals describes shell infections in turtles as "caused by bacteria, fungi, or parasites," often secondary to trauma, burns, or bites, and showing up as soft spots, pits, discoloration, a foul odor, or white and fuzzy patches, with ulceration reaching down to the bone signaling a severe case. Prolonged exposure to dirty water, an existing injury, poor basking access, or high humidity without a chance to fully dry are the usual causes. VCA Animal Hospitals warns that a shell fracture specifically "can become infected, or the blood supply permanently damaged, within hours of the traumatic event," which is why even a minor crack is worth addressing immediately rather than waiting to see if it heals on its own.

## Respiratory Infections

VCA Animal Hospitals notes that respiratory infections in turtles are "most often caused by bacteria and are often secondary to vitamin A deficiency" or poor water filtration, showing up as excess mucus bubbling from the mouth and nose, nasal discharge, lethargy, and wheezing, with a turtle tilting sideways while swimming if pneumonia sets in. Water or ambient temperatures running too cold, drafts, poor water quality, and vitamin A deficiency all contribute. This can progress quickly, treat it as urgent and see a reptile vet promptly rather than waiting to see if it resolves.

## Aural (Ear) Abscesses

Visible swelling on the side of the head, along with difficulty eating or swimming, VCA Animal Hospitals describes it as a "large swelling on the side of the head, just behind the eyes." Often linked to vitamin A deficiency combined with chronically poor water quality or a bacterial infection. This almost always requires surgical drainage and antibiotics from a vet, it isn't something that resolves at home.

## Vitamin A Deficiency

Usually tied to a poor diet, especially an all-pellet regimen without enough variety or fresh greens, VCA Animal Hospitals specifically flags iceberg lettuce, an all-meat diet, and poor-quality commercial food as the common culprits. This deficiency contributes to the eye swelling, respiratory problems, and ear abscesses covered above, it's a genuine root cause worth taking seriously rather than treating each symptom separately. ## Emergencies vs. Manageable at Home

Difficulty breathing, severe lethargy, an inability to dive or swim properly, deep shell lesions, large abscesses, or a complete refusal to eat for more than a few days are all emergencies. Early soft shell, once UVB and calcium are corrected, mild superficial shell issues, and minor scrapes, once water quality is fixed, are generally manageable at home. Annual or semi-annual wellness exams catch most of this early, our Red-Eared Slider cost guide covers what those visits and any emergency treatment typically run.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

No or inadequate UVB combined with low dietary calcium leads to MBD and soft shell, which is exactly why the Merck Veterinary Manual's husbandry table lists broad-spectrum UVB lighting as essential, not optional, for this species. Weak or undersized filtration with infrequent water changes leads to shell rot and ear or respiratory infections, the same water-quality-driven pattern that shows up in other aquatic pets like koi, see our [Koi health issues guide](/blog/koi-health-issues-guide/) for that parallel. An all-protein or low-variety diet leads to vitamin A deficiency and the secondary infections that follow. And an insufficient dry basking area, one that doesn't let the turtle fully dry off, directly promotes shell problems.
