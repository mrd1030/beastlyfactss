More than 90 percent of the ocean is deep ocean, water deeper than 200 meters, and it is the largest habitat on Earth. It is also the least seen. As of April 2026, 28.7 percent of the global seafloor had been mapped with modern high-resolution sonar, and people have seen less than 0.001 percent of the deep seafloor directly, an area about the size of Rhode Island. Most of what is known about the animals below comes from a handful of expeditions, and each of the five below was pinned down by a specific study.

## The anglerfish: males that fuse into females

Deep-sea anglerfish (the ceratioids) hang a bioluminescent lure from a modified spine on the head and wait for prey to swim toward the light. Their stranger trait is reproduction. In some species the male is a dwarf whose job is to find a female in the dark. When he does, he bites into her skin and stays. Over time the two fuse and share a blood supply, and the male becomes a permanent sperm source attached to her body. A female has been caught with eight males attached.

Fusing two individuals should trigger tissue rejection, the same problem that complicates organ transplants. A 2020 study worked out how the fish get around it. In species with permanent attachment, many genes for adaptive immunity are weakened or broken, and in the most extreme species the RAG1 and RAG2 genes that build antibodies and T-cell receptors no longer work. The researchers describe the male-female fusion as the only known case of natural parabiosis.

## The giant squid: seen alive only in 2004

The giant squid (*Architeuthis*) was known for centuries only from carcasses washed ashore or taken from sperm whale stomachs. The first images of a live one in its habitat came on September 30, 2004, when two Japanese researchers photographed a giant squid attacking a baited line at 900 meters off the Ogasawara Islands in the North Pacific. They published the images in 2005. The squid left behind a severed tentacle 5.5 meters long, and from it they estimated the animal at over 8 meters including its long tentacles.

Giant and colossal squid also have the largest eyes of any animal, two to three times the diameter of any other species', with giant squid eyes about 10 inches across. A 2012 study worked out why. Eyes that size give little advantage for spotting prey or mates. They are, however, suited to one task: seeing the faint glow of small animals set off as a sperm whale moves through the water, which gives the squid early warning of its main predator.

## Bioluminescence: the ocean's default

Making light is not a deep-sea novelty. It is the norm. Two researchers went through video from 240 remotely operated vehicle dives in and around Monterey Canyon and logged every animal larger than about a centimeter, more than 350,000 of them. They reported in 2017 that roughly three quarters were bioluminescent, and that the proportion barely changed from the surface to 4,000 meters.

The best-known glowing animal is not a deep-sea species at all. The crystal jelly (*Aequorea victoria*) lives in shallow Pacific Northwest water. Its protein aequorin produces blue light, and a second protein, green fluorescent protein (GFP), shifts that light to green. GFP became one of the standard tools of modern biology for tagging and tracking cells and proteins inside living organisms.

## The barreleye: a fish with a see-through head

The barreleye (*Macropinna microstoma*) lives 600 to 800 meters down off California. Its head is covered by a transparent, fluid-filled shield, and inside it sit two green, tubular eyes. For decades those eyes were thought to be fixed, pointing straight up to catch the silhouettes of prey above. A 2008 study showed otherwise. Researchers watching a live fish in a shipboard aquarium saw it rotate its eyes forward as it turned its body from horizontal to vertical, so it can look up to find prey and forward to eat it. The two dark spots above the mouth that look like eyes in photographs are its nostrils, olfactory organs called nares.

## The dumbo octopus: deepest octopus on record

Dumbo octopuses (genus *Grimpoteuthis*) swim with a pair of ear-like fins on the mantle. In 2020 researchers reported the first cephalopods filmed at hadal depths, including a dumbo octopus at 6,957 meters (22,825 feet) in the Java Trench, the deepest octopus on record.

How they start life was unknown until an egg case collected in 2005 on the New England and Corner Rise seamounts in the northwest Atlantic yielded a hatchling. It was described in 2018. The hatchling already had fins for swimming, developed eyes and brain, and sensory cirri, the features an adult uses to find and seize prey. Dumbo octopuses hatch as competent miniature adults rather than as drifting larvae.

## What the count leaves out

Five animals is a tiny sample of a habitat that covers most of the planet and has been seen on less than one thousandth of one percent of its floor. The strangeness is not confined to the deep, either. [The hagfish turns seawater into nearly a liter of slime in under half a second](/blog/hagfish-slime-how-it-works/), and up on the reef [every clownfish is born male and changes sex when the female above it dies](/blog/what-finding-nemo-got-wrong-about-clownfish/).
