# Common Red-Footed Tortoise Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Red-footed tortoises flip the usual tortoise-care script on one big point: too little humidity, not too much, is what tends to make them sick, alongside the same UVB, calcium, and diet fundamentals that matter for any tortoise.

## Respiratory Infection: The Opposite Risk From Arid Tortoises

This is the single biggest reversal from Russian and sulcata tortoise care, and it's worth flagging clearly. For an arid-species tortoise, damp and cold cause respiratory infection. For a red-footed tortoise, it's chronically dry, cool conditions that put it at risk, several reptile-vet sources describe a red-footed tortoise kept dry and cool as highly likely to eventually develop respiratory problems. Watch for wheezing, nasal discharge, lethargy, and open-mouth breathing. Always see a vet immediately, this does not resolve on its own and needs antibiotics, while you raise humidity and warmth in the meantime.

## Shell Rot: Humid Isn't the Same as Wet

Soft, discolored, or foul-smelling patches on the shell, sometimes with lifting or flaking scutes, a bacterial or fungal infection that typically enters through a cut, scrape, or shell lesion, according to a [breakdown of turtle and tortoise shell rot](https://reptiledirect.com/turtle-shell-rot/). Here's the nuance that matters specifically for this species: a red-footed tortoise's enclosure is supposed to run humid, so "humid" alone isn't the risk factor. What actually causes shell rot is a substrate that's perpetually soggy, dirty, or waterlogged rather than evenly moist, one detailed care resource puts it plainly: there's a real difference between humid and constantly wet. Keep the substrate damp and clean rather than stagnant, and see a vet promptly, this can progress to a genuine life-threatening infection if left untreated.

## Pyramiding

Abnormal, raised, cone-shaped growth of the shell scutes instead of a smooth, flat carapace. This is permanent once it happens, prevention is the only real strategy. A 2021 study on red-footed tortoise hatchlings specifically found that a high-starch diet drove faster growth, more pronounced pyramiding, and lower bone mass and density compared to a high-fiber diet with otherwise identical conditions (Mendoza et al., 2022). Low humidity during the growing years compounds the risk further, the same broad relationship documented in other tortoise species appears to hold here too. Correct humidity and a fiber-appropriate diet are your best defense.

## Hind-Leg Weakness From a Protein-Deficient Diet

A real, species-specific issue that doesn't come up with a strict herbivore like the sulcata or Russian tortoise. Red-footed tortoises raised on a plant-only diet commonly develop hind-leg weakness or outright paralysis, along with poor fertility and weak hatchlings. Unlike most of the issues on this list, this one is a diet gap rather than a husbandry mismatch.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Dry, cool conditions cause the respiratory infection that's the most urgent item on this list, the reverse of what threatens arid tortoises. Stagnant, dirty moisture, not humidity itself, causes shell rot. Low humidity and a starch-heavy diet during the growing years cause pyramiding. Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD. And a plant-only diet causes hind-leg weakness in this species specifically.
