# Common Ackie Monitor Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ackie monitors are hardy animals, and the health issues that do show up trace back almost entirely to a small number of preventable husbandry gaps. Females add one more: they produce eggs with or without a male, so a keeper needs a nest site ready long before any breeding plan.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

A disease of neglect, and one a properly kept ackie should never develop. Caused by inadequate UVB, insufficient calcium, or both together. Watch for swollen limbs, soft or malformed bones, lethargy, and difficulty moving normally. Always see a vet, and treat correct <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TipZXE" product="UVB - Arcadia Desert 12% (ProT5 kit, 22in/24W)">UVB</AffiliateLink> and calcium supplementation as non-negotiable from day one. This is one of the more avoidable conditions on this list.

## Obesity and the Weekly Weigh-In

A captivity-specific risk for this species. Ackies are naturally so active that they burn far more energy in the wild than most captive setups allow for. Combined with their strong food motivation, this creates real overfeeding risk.

Weigh weekly on a digital kitchen scale and log the number. Most adult ackies weigh under 300 grams, and about 350 grams is the ceiling for the species, so an adult climbing past that is carrying fat. Weight alone does not settle it, because builds vary between individuals. Check the body as well: a tail that looks creased or segmented, belly dragging when it walks, and a lizard that has gone lazy all point to an overweight ackie. The fix is fewer or smaller meals and more room to move. See a vet if a month of adjustment produces no weight change.

<FunFact>
Ackie monitors are active enough in the wild to burn through far more energy than most captive setups ever ask of them, which is why obesity, more than any disease or parasite, is one of the most common health issues keepers see in this species. A large enclosure and a weekly kitchen scale check do more preventive work here than almost anything else on this list.
</FunFact>

## Internal and External Parasites

Appetite loss, lethargy, unexplained weight loss, vomiting, diarrhea, and slimy, bloody, or off-color stool are the signs to watch for. Pet store ackies often carry parasites picked up from other animals in the shop, so ask the seller for a clear fecal result before buying. Quarantine any new ackie for at least 3 months, and keep it there until two separate fecal checks come back clean, run the way the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) sets out. A fecal sample for the vet should be under 24 hours old.

## Impaction

Can become an emergency if severe. Linked to inappropriate substrate ingestion or temperatures too low to support digestion, which an <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4flGgUg" product="Infrared temp gun">infrared thermometer</AffiliateLink> on the basking surface rules in or out in seconds.

## Respiratory Infection

The signs are specific: breaths you can hear, as a wheeze or click, breathing with the head raised, and bubbly or stringy mucus in the mouth. Lethargy, a runny nose, open-mouth breathing, and appetite loss often come with them. The causes are cool temperatures, humidity that is too high or too low, a missing thermal gradient, and prolonged stress. A respiratory infection needs a vet and antibiotics to clear. While the lizard is on treatment, raising the night temperature to 80 to 85°F supports its immune response, and the day and night temperatures and humidity should all be rechecked so the cause is gone before the course ends.

## Egg Cycling and Egg Binding

A female will lay infertile eggs with no male present, typically twice a year. A swollen belly, a bigger appetite, and more digging, often at night, mean eggs are on the way. She needs a nesting area at least 18 inches deep, damp enough to hold a burrow, and in the high 80s°F, preferably 86 to 87°F. Afterward she looks deflated, and a pinky mouse or two helps her recover. Infertile eggs look squishy and collapsed and can be discarded.

Egg binding is the risk when she cannot lay: loss of appetite, inactivity, straining, and a swollen cloaca. Shallow substrate with no suitable nest site, dehydration, obesity, and poor muscle tone are the usual causes. Egg binding is life-threatening and needs an experienced reptile vet the same day.

## The Husbandry Pattern

Weak or absent UVB, combined with poor calcium supplementation, is the direct cause of MBD, the most serious and most preventable condition on this list. An enclosure too small for this species' activity level contributes directly to obesity. Heat and substrate depth prevent impaction, respiratory disease, and egg binding at once. Nearly everything here comes back to building the ambitious setup this species needs, the same principle behind most of what shows up in a [bearded dragon](/blog/bearded-dragon-health-issues-guide/) or a [blue-tongued skink](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-health-issues-guide/).
