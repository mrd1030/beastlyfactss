# Is It Legal to Own a Box Turtle? State-by-State Guide

Box turtles get treated very differently from a species like the red-eared slider. There's no invasive-species angle here, since box turtles are native across most of the US. Instead, the law is almost entirely built around one idea: these turtles reproduce so slowly that even modest wild collection can crash a local population for good. That shapes both how you can acquire one and, in a few states, how you're allowed to keep one you already have.

### The Number Is the Rule, and It Runs From One to Six

Four states answer the box turtle with a cap rather than a ban, and lining them up shows how little agreement there is about what the right number is.

Alabama is the strictest. 220-2-.92(4) makes it unlawful "to possess more than one (1) box turtle or to offer for sale, sell, or trade for anything of value any box turtle (Terrapene spp.), box turtle part, or reproductive product except by permit". One turtle, no sale, and the rule says Terrapene spp., so it reaches the ornate as well as the eastern. It carries no origin qualifier either, so a captive-bred turtle bought out of state counts towards the one. The sale bar is the sharper half in practice: a keeper may lawfully own a box turtle and nobody may lawfully sell them one.

Oklahoma looks harsher than it is. Rule 800:15-9-3(3) says flatly that "the possession, buying and/or selling of any terrestrial turtles commonly known as 'box turtles', is prohibited". But that section sits inside the commercial aquatic-species harvest chapter, and the Wildlife Department's own noncommercial turtle page reads it as a sale bar, listing six of each species in possession and stating that "terrestrial (land) turtles may not be sold". The Department's page says outright that it is an interpretive summary and not a legal document, so this is a genuine gap between the words and the practice rather than a settled six.

Indiana works by a different mechanism again. It does not cap box turtles specially; 312 IAC 9-5-6(f) caps every native reptile and amphibian at four of any one species. What it adds is the sale bar at 9-5-7, which applies "regardless of place of origin" and so follows a captive-bred turtle in from an out-of-state breeder, and a turtle possession permit at 9-5-11 that an Indiana resident must apply for within ten days of taking possession, with a receipted invoice or bill of lading showing lawful acquisition and a conservation officer inspecting the enclosure before the first permit issues.

### Texas Caps the Number and Bans the Sale

Texas is usually filed with the wild-collection states and belongs with Missouri instead, because the rule it uses is a number rather than a prohibition. Both box turtles that occur there, the eastern and the ornate, sit on the list at 31 TAC 65.331(e), which is the lower of the state's two nongame tiers. Under 31 TAC 65.327(b)(3)(A) a person with no permit may possess six or fewer of a species on that list, provided they do not engage in commercial activity, and 31 TAC 65.326(2) defines commercial activity as the sale, offer for sale, exchange, or barter of the animal. So six is the ceiling and selling one is out at any number.

The part that catches people is 31 TAC 65.325(a), which says nongame wildlife "includes captive-bred nongame wildlife." Buying from a breeder in another state does not lift the cap, exactly as in Maryland and Florida. Taking one from the wild is a separate requirement rather than a ban: 31 TAC 65.327(c)(1) preserves the obligation to hold a hunting license for any activity involving the take of nongame wildlife, and picking a box turtle off a road shoulder needs the reptile and amphibian stamp under Parks and Wildlife Code 43.901 as well.

### Connecticut Regulates the Species, Not the Collection

Connecticut is the one state on this list where being captive-bred does not help. The eastern box turtle was added to the state's List of Endangered, Threatened, and Special Concern Species in 1998 and is currently listed as a species of special concern. Under Regs. Conn. State Agencies § 26-55-6, any wild animal on that list is a "Category Four Wild Animal", and subsection (b)(5) provides that no person may import or possess one without a permit from the Department of Energy and Environmental Protection.

The permit is issued for a specific animal, lasts for that animal's life, and cannot be transferred. Nothing in the definition turns on where the turtle came from, so a captive-bred eastern box turtle bought from an out-of-state breeder is in the same position as one picked up off a Connecticut road. That is a meaningfully different rule from the wild-collection bans elsewhere on this page, and it is easy to miss because the listing lives in the endangered species regulations rather than the pet ones.

### The States Where Even Legal Ownership Comes With Strings

Most people assume that once a box turtle is legally yours, the law stops caring. Florida, West Virginia, Indiana, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Kansas all disagree to some degree. Florida's cap of two turtles per person applies no matter how you acquired them, and the state expects you to be able to show a receipt with the purchase date, species, and supplier. West Virginia's 2021 overhaul went furthest: even owners who'd had a pet box turtle for years needed to file for a state authorization letter by a March 2022 deadline just to keep it legally going forward.

### Why the Law Treats Box Turtles This Way

<FunFact>
Box turtles typically don't reach sexual maturity until they're 8 to 10 years old, and lay only three or four eggs a year, with very few hatchlings surviving. Wildlife agencies describe this as a species where "removing even a few adult turtles can destabilize a local population for decades," and long-term field studies have documented population crashes of 70 to 80 percent in some intensively studied areas over several decades. That slow-reproduction biology is the reason behind nearly every wild-collection restriction on this list, not an invasive-species concern like you'd see with a slider.
</FunFact>

## A Real, Recent Enforcement Case

In January 2026, a Mississippi man was sentenced to a year of federal probation and a $3,000 fine under the Lacey Act for possessing and selling three-toed box turtles without the state's required commercial propagation permit, then exporting them through New York to China. It's part of a broader, ongoing pattern; wildlife-trafficking researchers have tracked dozens of major turtle-trafficking cases across more than a dozen states since 2018, box turtles included.

## The Takeaway

Don't assume a box turtle you find in the yard is yours to keep; check your state first, since several genuinely restrict or ban this outright. And don't assume a legally purchased, captive-bred box turtle comes with zero conditions either; Florida, West Virginia, and a handful of other states attach real caps or paperwork requirements even to turtles you bought fair and square.
