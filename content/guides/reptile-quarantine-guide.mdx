# Reptile Quarantine: Why New Snakes and Lizards Need Months, Not Weeks

<VetDisclaimer />

A new snake or lizard can walk out of a pet store or a breeder's rack looking completely normal and still be carrying something that can hurt, or kill, the reptile already living in your house. That's not a scare line, it's the actual reason quarantine exists as a practice rather than a suggestion. Two problems drive most of the real-world advice here: snake mites (Ophionyssus natricis), which spread fast through direct contact and shared equipment, and inclusion body disease, a fatal virus in boas and pythons that can sit silently in a carrier for a long time before it does anything visible. Both are preventable with the same basic setup: real separation, real time, and a routine that puts the new animal last in line. If you're bringing home a tortoise or box turtle specifically, the [chelonian herpesvirus quarantine guide](/blog/chelonian-herpesvirus-quarantine-guide/) is the one to follow instead, since that species group carries its own well-documented herpesvirus risk with a somewhat different protocol. This guide covers snakes and lizards.

## Why "It Looked Fine" Isn't Good Enough

The entire argument for quarantine rests on one uncomfortable fact: outward appearance is not a reliable health test. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes boas as able to harbor the inclusion body disease virus, now confirmed as a reptarenavirus, for months to years with few or no clinical signs. Pythons go the other way: once signs start, the manual notes that most pythons die within days or weeks. Either way, "it seems fine" tells you almost nothing about what a new arrival might be carrying, which is exactly why quarantine trades a visual check for real time and real testing.

Snake mites are a more visible problem, but a new animal can still bring in an infestation that isn't obvious on day one. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes death from mite infestation as occurring "due to anemia or bacterial or viral infection," and names the mites in the mechanical transmission of Aeromonas hydrophila, a range of other bacteria, rickettsial agents and viruses. That includes inclusion body disease itself: on reptile viral disease the same manual says "breeding, fight wounds, fecal/oral contamination, and snake mites have been implicated as common ways of transfer." A mite problem you don't catch early doesn't stay contained to one animal, and it can hand off a much more serious disease while it's at it.

## How Long, and Why It Runs Into Months

General reptile-keeping advice sometimes puts a workable quarantine floor at 60 to 90 days. The veterinary literature runs longer. The Merck Veterinary Manual's husbandry guidance for reptiles is brief but unambiguous on the number: "quarantine periods of 3-6 months for new reptiles are recommended." That is 90 to 180 days, and the upper end is not padding.

The reason the window has to run that long comes straight from how the two headline problems behave. A mite infestation, checked daily on bare substrate, tends to declare itself within the first few weeks. Inclusion body disease does not play by the same clock. Because a boa can carry the virus for months to years without a visible sign, stopping quarantine the moment nothing looks wrong tells you almost nothing about that specific risk. Running the full 3 to 6 months, rather than treating it as a formality to get through, is what actually buys you the odds of catching a slow problem before it reaches an established pet.

## Setting Up a Space That Actually Separates

A second tank in the same room as your established reptile is not quarantine, it's proximity with a lid on it. The clearest statement of what separation is actually for comes from livestock biosecurity rather than from reptile keeping. Iowa State University's Center for Food Security and Public Health, in its Livestock Isolation and Quarantine Areas tip sheet, sets the standard as separate buildings, pastures, or lots where space allows, because "the goal is to not share air space or fence lines," and where that isn't possible, a buffer of at least six feet, with more distance always better. Reptiles are not livestock, but the principle transfers exactly: a different room is the reptile-room version of a different building, and two tanks on one shelf share air the way two pens share a fence line.

Inside that separate space, keep the enclosure itself deliberately plain. The Merck Veterinary Manual's guidance on reptile parasites gives the reason directly: "If mites are suspected, gently rubbing the reptile while it is standing over a piece of white paper will allow the mites to be observed after they have fallen off." A quarantine enclosure lined with plain paper towel is that diagnostic surface, running continuously, for the whole quarantine period. It does the same job for stool and for regurgitated food, both of which are visible on paper and invisible in three inches of bark chip. A tank layered with loose substrate and decor hides the two things you're actually trying to catch. Keep furnishings to a hide box and a water dish, items you're comfortable discarding or disinfecting once quarantine ends, rather than anything elaborate.

## Separate Tools, and the Order You Work In

Everything that touches the new animal needs its own version that never crosses over: feeding tongs, the water dish, a spot-cleaning scraper, even the paper towel roll if you can manage it. This mirrors long-standing biosecurity practice in animal keeping generally. The same Iowa State tip sheet is explicit: "Equipment and supplies should not be shared between areas," down to caretaker clothing and footwear, feed and water buckets, treatment equipment, and cleaning and disinfection supplies, and where sharing is unavoidable, items must be thoroughly cleaned between uses. On sequencing it is just as blunt, that the isolation and quarantine area is cared for last, and that anyone crossing between areas should at a minimum change clothing and boots and wash their hands.

That same order carries over directly to a reptile room. Feed, water, and spot-clean your established snake or lizard first, every time, and only open the new arrival's enclosure once everything else is done. Wash your hands, or change gloves, between the two, even on days when nothing looks unusual. It's a small habit that costs a few extra minutes and closes off the most obvious way a new animal's mites or a pathogen on your hands reach an animal you're trying to protect.

## Choosing a healthy reptile and a reptile vet

Quarantine catches what a buyer could not see. The first filter is the source. A breeder who hatched the animal can say when it hatched, what it eats, and when it last ate and shed. A pet store or a breeder is worth buying from only if its enclosures are clean and large enough to keep the animals comfortable. A rescue animal often arrives with no reliable history at all, and an expo table puts animals from many sellers in one room and one airspace for a day. Neither is a reason to refuse the animal. Both are reasons to run the full quarantine window with no shortcuts.

Captive-bred animals make the better pets. Compared with wild-caught animals they are less likely to bite, more likely to accept the food offered, and less likely to carry parasites. A seller who cannot say whether an animal was bred or caught has answered the question.

Then ask the seller four things: the hatch date, whether it was captive bred, what it eats and when it last ate, and whether it has been treated for anything, mites or a respiratory infection above all. A good breeder answers all four without looking anything up and stays reachable for questions afterward.

Find the vet before the animal comes home, because not every practice treats reptiles, and the time to learn that is not during an emergency. The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians (ARAV) runs a Find A Vet directory at arav.org, and at minimum the vet should be an ARAV member in good standing. When you call a clinic, ask how many reptiles it sees, whether it routinely treats your species, whether it runs fecal parasite exams in house, and who covers reptile emergencies after hours. Book the first exam for within 72 hours of bringing the animal home, and no later than two weeks. That visit sets a baseline weight and a record of the animal healthy, which is what every later visit is measured against.

## The Vet Workup During Quarantine

Quarantine time on its own is a waiting game unless it's paired with an actual exam. The first health check, ideally within those same 72 hours, is where the vet records weight, general appearance and mobility and palpates the animal. The part that matters most for quarantine is a fresh fecal sample, so the vet can check for intestinal parasites, since microscopic examination of feces is what detects coccidia, protozoa and intestinal worms. Treat that screen as non-negotiable rather than optional, because those parasites show no outward sign at all, and a quarantine that runs six months on observation alone will still miss them. If a normal stool or urate is unfamiliar territory, our [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) is a useful baseline to compare against before you're trying to judge a sample under stress.

Mites, checked against bare substrate daily, are usually the fastest thing to show up, often within the first couple of weeks if they're present at all. The signs tied to inclusion body disease behave on a much slower and less predictable clock, particularly in boas, which is the practical reason for running the full quarantine window rather than declaring victory the moment a few weeks pass without incident. If your new animal is a boa or python, weigh it weekly and keep a simple log of appetite and stool, the same way our [ball python health issues guide](/blog/ball-python-health-issues-guide/) recommends tracking a pattern rather than a single bad day. If retained shed shows up anywhere along the way, our [reptile shedding guide](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/) covers what's a normal rough patch versus a real problem. A pattern that only becomes visible after a month of entries is exactly the kind of thing daily memory alone will miss.

<FunFact>
  One of the more useful early warning signs of a mite infestation isn't a visible mite at all: the Merck Veterinary Manual notes that reptiles with a heavy mite burden will often spend an unusually long time sitting in their water dish, apparently trying to drown the parasites. A snake or lizard that suddenly can't seem to leave its water bowl is worth a closer look at its eyes and chin before anything else.
</FunFact>

## Ending Quarantine: Disinfection Before Introduction

Reaching the end of the quarantine window isn't the finish line by itself. Once the vet exam and fecal screens come back clear and the full window has actually passed, clean and disinfect the enclosure, dishes, and every tool that touched the new animal before any of it is reused near an established pet, and don't shortcut this step just because the calendar period is technically over. If anything flagged during quarantine, a positive fecal, a bout of regurgitation, unexplained weight loss, restart the clock rather than splitting the difference. A new animal that's ninety percent through a clean quarantine and then regurgitates a meal hasn't earned an exception, it's given you the one piece of information the whole process was designed to catch.
