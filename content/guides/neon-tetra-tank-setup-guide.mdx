# Neon Tetra Tank Setup: Soft Water, Dim Light, and a Blackwater Feel

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Neon tetras come from soft, dim, tannin-stained blackwater streams, and the closer your tank setup gets to that origin, the better this fish looks and behaves.

<Figure src="/assets/images/neon-tetra-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a neon tetra aquarium showing soft acidic water, dim lighting, and blackwater conditions from driftwood and tannins" caption="The closer your tank setup gets to this species' soft, dim, tannin-stained blackwater origin, the better it looks and behaves." shareable />

## Tank Size

Neons swim as a group, so buy the tank by length: at least 24 inches (60 cm), about 14 gallons or more, for ten fish. A standard 10-gallon tank is only 20 inches long and falls short. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">20-gallon long tank</AffiliateLink> clears it with room to spare. Schooling plays out horizontally, so favor a longer tank over a taller one.

Tankmates have to share the soft water and the 70 to 77°F band and be too small to eat a neon. The pairings that fail are [named species by species](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/): an adult angelfish, which eats neons once it passes 3 inches, any fin-nipper, and anything else with a mouth big enough. Ten neons also make a calmer tank than five neons and five rasboras, where neither group reaches the size that settles it.

## Temperature

Keep the tank at 70 to 77°F (21 to 25°C). As a tropical species, a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">heater</AffiliateLink> is necessary for any tank this size, and keeping the temperature stable matters as much as hitting the right number, swings are more stressful than a slightly imperfect but steady setting.

## Water Chemistry: The Detail That Separates This Fish From a Lot of Beginner Picks

Neon tetras want soft, slightly acidic water, a pH of 6.0 to 7.0, with hardness between 1 and 12 dGH. This is a step away from the harder, more alkaline water a [guppy](/blog/guppy-tank-setup-guide/) tolerates, and it's worth [testing your source water](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before assuming your setup is automatically suitable. Hard tap water comes down by blending it with RO water, never by dosing a pH-down product against a high KH, which only swings the pH back. Ammonia and nitrite both need to read zero.

## Filtration

Keep the flow gentle, neon tetras dislike strong current. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> is a common, effective choice, and a hang-on-back filter works too if you can baffle or reduce its output.

## Substrate

Fine gravel or sand suits them.

## Lighting

Neon tetras come from shaded, plant-covered forest streams, so bright, intense lighting over an open tank is the opposite of their home. Dense planting and floating plants soften it.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Recreate Blackwater Conditions

This is the detail that takes a neon tetra tank from adequate to great. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">Driftwood</AffiliateLink> and Indian almond leaves release natural tannins that stain the water slightly and lower pH, mimicking the blackwater habitat this species evolved in.

<FunFact>
The usual fix for hard tap water is the one that fails. Dosing a pH-down product against a high KH only swings the pH back, so a soft-water fish like the neon tetra needs the tap blended with RO water instead. Add dense midground plants and floating plants to dim the light, and the tank starts to resemble the shaded, stained streams the species comes from.
</FunFact>

## Let the Tank Mature Before the Fish Go In

A tank that has just finished cycling is still settling. A young tank drifts in pH and hardness as the driftwood leaches, the substrate settles and the bacteria colony grows into its load. Give the cycled tank time to hold steady readings from one weekly test to the next before the school goes in.

Water changes follow the same logic. Match the new water's temperature and chemistry to the tank, and keep changes modest and regular, so the school never meets a sudden jump.

Always quarantine new fish for at least 30 days before adding them, given the disease risk this species carries, and keep them well fed on a proper micro-pellet or micro-crisp food sized for their small mouths. Always use a water conditioner on tap water before it goes in the tank, dechlorinating and neutralizing chloramine and heavy metals that are otherwise harmful to fish.
