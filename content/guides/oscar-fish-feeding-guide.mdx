# Oscar Fish Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why Feeder Goldfish Are a Mistake

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Oscars will beg for food constantly and are easy to overfeed, but the single most damaging mistake with this species isn't portion size, it's building the diet around live feeder goldfish.

## The Staple Diet

Oscars are protein-preferring omnivores. The staple should be a high-quality <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LXX69CK?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Cichlid Gold Floating Pellets">cichlid pellet</AffiliateLink>, commonly cited at 35 to 45% protein, making up roughly 80% of the diet, sized appropriately for the fish. Supplement the remaining 20% with frozen or fresh items such as shrimp, krill, earthworms, mysis, and occasional plant matter like peas or zucchini. Variety here prevents dietary monotony and deficiency.

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Babies under 3 to 4 inches eat 3 times daily. Juveniles around 3 to 8 inches eat twice daily. Adults over about 8 inches, or a year old, eat once or at most twice daily. Feed only what's eaten in 2 to 3 minutes and remove anything uneaten.

The heavy early schedule matches the growth curve. An oscar reaches about two-thirds of its adult size within its first 6 to 12 months, so most of the food a keeper ever puts into building the fish goes in during that first year. Cutting back to adult rations once the fish passes about 8 inches is what keeps a grown oscar from turning that same appetite into fat.

<KeyTakeaway>
A healthy oscar has a slightly rounded, not bulging, belly. They beg constantly and are genuinely easy to overfeed, many keepers fast adults one day a week to keep portions honest.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Foods to Avoid

Live feeder goldfish and rosy-red minnows shouldn't be a staple. Feeder fish species commonly carry thiaminase, an enzyme that breaks down vitamin B1, and without adequate B1, symptoms include lethargy, weight loss, nerve control problems, and eventually heart failure and death. Feeder fish also carry a real risk of parasites, disease, and excess fat, and they go into the oscar's tank straight from a crowded store system, skipping [the hospital-tank quarantine any other new fish would get](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/). Avoid mammalian meats like beef heart too: oscars digest them poorly, and they can contribute to fatty liver disease. Overfeeding and low dietary variety are risks on their own, independent of any single bad food.

## Vitamin C: The Nutrient an Oscar Cannot Make

Oscars lack L-gulonolactone oxidase, the liver and kidney enzyme other animals use to make their own vitamin C, so every milligram has to come from the food. A 26-week feeding trial at the Smithsonian's National Zoological Park (Fracalossi and colleagues, 1998) showed what happens without it. Juvenile oscars on a diet with no vitamin C gained only 37% of their starting weight, against 91 to 112% for fish given 25, 75, or 200 mg per kg of diet. After 25 weeks the unsupplemented fish began to show deformed gill covers and jaws, bleeding in the eyes and fins, and a curved spine.

The lowest level tested, 25 mg per kg of diet, was enough to prevent both the stunted growth and the deficiency signs. The risk sits with a diet built on something else: feeder fish, raw meat, or a single frozen food with no fortified staple behind it. Plain ascorbic acid breaks down with oxidation, which is why the trial used a stabilized phosphate form of the vitamin.

## Supplementation

No separate supplement is needed if fed a quality, varied diet. Vitamin C and vitamin-fortified foods matter for preventing lateral-line disease specifically, and some keepers soak food in vitamin supplements when actively addressing Hole-in-the-Head disease, but the underlying fix is a balanced pellet plus varied whole foods rather than added powders.

## The Mistakes That Matter

A feeder-goldfish-based diet risks thiamine deficiency, fat accumulation, and introducing parasites or disease, all from a food source that seems natural but genuinely isn't a good staple.

<FunFact>
What drives [Hole-in-the-Head disease](/blog/oscar-fish-health-issues-guide/) is not settled. Poor water quality with nitrate climbing, overfeeding that fouls the water, a monotonous low-vitamin diet, internal flagellates such as *Hexamita* and *Spironucleus*, and activated carbon run full-time are all implicated, and none of them has been shown to lead. Cover every side of it: weekly 25 to 30% water changes with nitrate under about 40 ppm, and real variety in the food.
</FunFact>
