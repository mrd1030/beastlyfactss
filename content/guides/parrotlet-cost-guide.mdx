# How Much Does a Parrotlet Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Parrotlets are the smallest parrot commonly kept as a pet, smaller even than a budgie, but neither the price nor the personality scales down with the body. This is a bold, strong-willed bird, and that matters for budgeting as much as the sticker price does.

## The Bird Itself: $100 to $900

A common green or blue Pacific parrotlet runs $100 to $400 from a breeder. Rarer color mutations, American yellow, lutino, dilute turquoise, and pied among them, commonly run $350 to $900, with exceptional individuals passing $1,000. Adoption or rehoming typically runs $50 to $300, and is worth checking first.

That puts a parrotlet solidly above a [budgie](/blog/budgie-cost-guide/) ($20 to $80 for the bird itself) and below what you'd typically pay for a mid-size or large parrot, a conure, Quaker parakeet, or African grey. Hand-raised birds cost more than parent-raised ones and are generally the better choice if a tame, interactive pet is the goal.

The cage is worth prioritizing. A minimum of 18x18x24 inches works for one bird, but parrotlets are surprisingly active and playful for their size, closer to a large parrot's energy in a tiny frame, so wider and roomier is a genuine upgrade rather than an indulgence.

That works out to roughly $250 to $400 a year, or about $21 to $33 a month. The toy line item deserves a specific note: parrotlets are intelligent, active chewers that go through foraging and shreddable toys faster than their size suggests, budgeting for regular rotation isn't optional if you want to head off boredom-driven feather plucking.

## Vet Costs

A routine annual checkup with an avian vet runs about $60 to $90. Parasite treatment, if it comes up, adds $0 to $80. Ongoing medication for a chronic issue can run $0 to $150 a year. The real budgeting risk is emergency care, [egg-binding](/blog/parrotlet-health-issues-guide/) in a female or an injury from this bird's fearless, accident-prone streak, which can run $300 or more once diagnostics, fluids, or a procedure are involved. Avian-specific vets are scarcer than general small-animal vets, worth locating and saving the contact for before an emergency, not during one.

## Lifespan: One to Two Decades

8 to 12 years is the average, and individual birds are reported into their 20s and occasionally their 30s. Avian-specialist guidance for this species puts the upper end of a well-kept bird's life at around 20 years. In the wild, where the species is less thoroughly studied, one frequently cited estimate averages around 10 years.

The honest takeaway: budget for a bird that could plausibly be part of your household for one to two decades, and don't assume the lower end of that range is a given. A proper pelleted diet and routine vet care are the biggest levers separating a parrotlet that lives 10 years from one that lives 20.

## The Cost Decision That Matters Most: One Bird or Two

As with lovebirds, the single biggest factor in what kind of pet you end up with isn't a line item, it's whether you buy one parrotlet or two. A single, well-socialized bird bonds intensely to its person and can become a genuinely affectionate, hands-on companion. A bonded pair bonds mainly to each other instead, entertaining to watch but rarely as tame or reliably handleable.

<FunFact>
A bonded pair of parrotlets doesn't automatically mean a peaceful cage. Even a bonded pair can turn possessive, with a dominant bird shoving its mate away from food or a favorite perch, and two unfamiliar parrotlets introduced carelessly can fight seriously enough to injure each other. This is a species that's small enough to underestimate and feisty enough to prove you wrong.
</FunFact>

If two birds are the plan, budget for a noticeably larger cage than the single-bird minimum, and expect a slower, supervised introduction rather than assuming two parrotlets will simply get along.
