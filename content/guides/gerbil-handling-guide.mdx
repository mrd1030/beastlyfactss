# How to Handle a Gerbil (And Why It Needs a Companion)

Gerbils are gentle, curious, and genuinely easy to tame, and like the degu also covered on this site, they break from the hamster in two specific ways worth understanding before you buy. For the full care picture beyond handling, see our complete [Gerbil care guide](/guides/gerbil/), or our companion guides on [cost](/blog/gerbil-cost-guide/), [health issues](/blog/gerbil-health-issues-guide/), and [enclosure setup](/blog/gerbil-tank-setup-guide/).

## Social, Not Solitary, Unlike the Hamster

Gerbils need to live with company, same-sex pairs or small groups, and isolation genuinely causes anxious, stressed behavior in this species. This is the direct opposite of the solitary hamster already on this site, which must be housed alone. If you're introducing adult gerbils to each other rather than raising a pair together from a young age, be aware that adult introductions often fail, a slow, gradual "split-cage" introduction method works considerably better than putting two unfamiliar adults together directly.

## Active by Day, Unlike the Hamster

Gerbils are diurnal and crepuscular, active during the day and around dawn and dusk, rather than strictly nocturnal. If a mismatched schedule was ever a frustration with a nocturnal hamster, this species offers a genuinely better fit for daytime interaction.

## Getting Handling Right

Gerbils tame well with regular, gentle handling and are naturally curious rather than defensive. **Never pick one up by the tail.** Like several small rodents, gerbils can experience "tail slip," where the skin and fur sloughs away under grasping pressure, sometimes losing the whole tail. This genuinely warrants a vet visit if it happens. Scoop by the body instead.

<FunFact>
A failed adult introduction between gerbils isn't a minor squabble, it can escalate to real fighting fast. The gradual "split-cage" method, where two gerbils live in adjacent enclosures and swap bedding or scent before ever meeting directly, exists specifically because gerbils are territorial enough that skipping straight to a shared cage is a genuine risk, not just an impatient shortcut.
</FunFact>

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Keeping a single gerbil, assuming it'll do fine the way a solitary hamster would.
- Grabbing or restraining by the tail.
- Forcing two unfamiliar adult gerbils together without a gradual introduction.
- Housing in a wire cage that can't hold the deep bedding this species genuinely needs, more in the [tank setup guide](/blog/gerbil-tank-setup-guide/).

## The Relationship You're Actually Building

Because gerbils are social and day-active, you'll likely see and interact with them far more naturally than with a nocturnal, solitary small pet. Watching a bonded pair dig, forage, and interact is genuinely part of what makes this species rewarding, plan for two from the start rather than discovering the social requirement after the fact.
