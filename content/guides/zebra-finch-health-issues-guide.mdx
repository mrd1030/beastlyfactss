# Common Zebra Finch Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Zebra finches are a prey species that hides illness well, so the handful of conditions common in this bird are worth knowing before symptoms are obvious enough to notice on their own.

## Air Sac Mites

A respiratory parasite, Sternostoma tracheacolum, that lives in the trachea and air sacs, most frequently reported in canaries and Gouldian finches but capable of infecting other finches too, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual. In mild infections birds are often asymptomatic, but Merck notes that heavy infections bring on audible dyspnea, described as high-pitched noises and clicking, along with sneezing, tail bobbing, and open-mouthed breathing. Always see an avian vet, air sac mites need targeted antiparasitic treatment and won't resolve on their own.

## Egg Binding

A genuine emergency in laying hens. The Merck Veterinary Manual lists the warning signs plainly: sitting on the cage floor, weakness, low energy, straining, bloody droppings, tail bobbing from difficulty breathing, a sudden stop in egg laying, and a swollen abdomen. Merck names calcium deficiency as a key contributing cause, since "calcium is necessary to form the hard shell around an egg just before it is laid," and a hen that has laid several eggs in a row without adequate calcium intake is at real risk. This is exactly why a [cuttlebone](/blog/zebra-finch-feeding-guide/) is a non-negotiable part of the setup, not an optional extra.

<FunFact>
Egg binding can progress fast enough that veterinary sources treat any suspected case as same-day urgent, not a wait-and-see situation. A hen straining on the cage floor with a swollen abdomen needs a vet the same day symptoms appear.
</FunFact>

## Obesity and Fatty Liver Disease

An all-seed diet is a genuine, documented health risk, not just a nutritional shortcut. The Merck Veterinary Manual states that "obesity is common in companion birds," with "high-fat diets (seeds, nuts, and many table foods)" named as a leading contributor, and that obese birds face a higher risk of "arthritis, fatty liver disease (hepatic lipidosis), atherosclerosis, and cardiac disease." This is a core reason avian sources increasingly recommend a pellet-forward diet over a traditional seed-heavy one for finches.

## Scaly Face, Scaly Leg, and Feather Mites

Scaly face and leg mites cause crusty, roughened buildup around the beak, eyes, and legs, and are genuinely treatable, especially caught early rather than left to progress. Red mites and feather mites feed at night rather than during the day, part of why they're easy to miss initially, and cause restlessness, dull plumage, and visible scratching, along with small crusty feeding wounds often found tucked under the wing. Treatment needs to cover both the bird and its full environment, mites left in the cage or perches will simply reinfest a treated bird.

## Why Zebra Finches Hide How Sick They Are

This is worth taking seriously, and it's genuinely true of this species, not just a general bird cliché. VCA Hospitals is direct about the mechanism: "a bird's natural instinct is to not show any sign of weakness, or it risks being targeted by predators," since looking sick makes a wild bird a target. The consequence, per VCA, is that "by the time a pet bird begins to display symptoms of illness, it has likely been sick for several days to weeks." Zebra finches, being small and highly social, mask illness particularly well within a group, which is part of why quiet daily observation beats hands-on interaction with this species, it's how you'll catch problems early.

## Early Warning Signs Worth Watching For

VCA Hospitals lists a set of concrete signs to watch for rather than waiting for a specific diagnosis to become obvious: ruffled or unkempt feathers, reduced eating or drinking, sleeping more than usual, labored or open-mouth breathing, tail bobbing with each breath, diarrhea or abnormal droppings, balance problems, and head tilt. Any of these, especially in combination, is worth a same-day call to an avian vet rather than a wait-and-see approach, given how much of an illness head start this species already has by the time it shows.
