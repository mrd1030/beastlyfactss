# Russian Tortoise Enclosure Setup: Compact Housing With Real Requirements

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Russian tortoise care is manageable indoors for this species' entire life, and getting the enclosure type, temperature, and humidity right is most of what it takes to keep one thriving.

<Figure src="/assets/images/russian-tortoise-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a Russian tortoise enclosure showing an open-top tortoise table, grouped basking and UVB lighting, and a moist hide" caption="Getting the enclosure type, temperature, and humidity right is most of what it takes to keep one thriving." shareable />

## Enclosure Size: The Floor and the Target

A 4 ft by 2 ft by 2 ft tortoise table, about 8 square feet of floor, is the absolute indoor minimum for one adult, and it is genuinely a minimum rather than a goal. Aim well above it: at least 12 square feet of floor space with walls at least a foot high, and better still nearer 7 ft by 3.5 ft, roughly 24 square feet, because this is an unusually active tortoise that walks the whole enclosure every day and rearranges anything light enough to move.

Take the largest figure your room allows and treat 4 by 2 as the point below which the enclosure is failing rather than the point at which it is finished. For an adult, 7 by 3.5 ft is the working size, with at least 10 more square feet for each extra tortoise. A juvenile under about 4 inches of shell can live in a 4 by 2 ft table until it reaches about 6 inches, and then it moves up. Floor area is what matters here, not height; a foot of wall is plenty, and this species climbs better than it looks like it should, so the walls need to be solid and the top open rather than tall and glass. An <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rkp3S6" product="Tortoise table">open-topped tortoise table</AffiliateLink> is strongly preferred over a closed glass aquarium, better ventilation and considerably less stress for the tortoise. House alone, or with caution, two females together, but not multiple males, which turns territorial fast.

## Temperature

Basking spot around 95 to 100°F, measured at the surface with a temperature gun, cool side in the 70s°F, and the air across the enclosure around 75 to 85°F by day. This is a genuinely cold-hardy species, tolerating night drops to around 60°F without needing supplemental night heat in most homes. Nighttime temperatures should not fall below 60°F, and a room that will not hold that needs a ceramic heater on the enclosure. Sixty is the floor indoors and out, the same line the [reptile emergency plan](/blog/reptile-emergency-plan-guide/) and [outdoor housing](/blog/outdoor-reptile-housing-guide/) work from. A basking heat source, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000YS3H0I?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Exo Terra Sun Glo Halogen Basking Spot Lamp, 50W">halogen bulb</AffiliateLink> works well, is needed indoors during the day; tortoises kept outdoors in mild climates often need no additional heat at all.

## Humidity

Keep ambient humidity at the open surface relatively low, 40 to 50%, appropriate for this arid-adapted species, but provide a moist hide lined with peat moss to create a humid microclimate for hydration and shedding. A juvenile runs moister, 40 to 75%, lower by day and higher at night. High ambient humidity across the whole enclosure is a real cause of respiratory infection, so resist the instinct to keep everything damp.

The shape of it matters more than the exact percentage. The tortoise experiences two humidities, not one: a dry, well-ventilated surface it walks and basks on, and a genuinely humid microclimate underneath, in a burrow or a moist hide, where it spends a large part of its day and does most of its shedding. Build for both. An enclosure that is dry everywhere, with no damp retreat and no depth to dig into, is what produces a dehydrated, pyramided tortoise, and it is a more common failure in this species than an enclosure that is too wet.

## Substrate

A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CY23K32S?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="VCEPJH Loose Coconut Fiber Substrate, 2 Pack">50/50 mix of topsoil and coconut coir</AffiliateLink>, or a similar sand and loam blend, kept slightly moist rather than bone dry. Depth is what lets it burrow: at least 4 inches for a juvenile and 6 inches for an adult, and deeper again in one dig zone. Avoid pure sand, which can cause mobility and impaction issues, and skip rabbit pellets or newspaper-only setups, neither supports natural behavior well.

## Lighting

Daily UVB is essential. A T5 HO tube, Arcadia 12% or <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FF9GHCDC?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product='Zoo Med ReptiSun T5 HO Terrarium Hood with 10.0 Bulb 24" L'>Zoo Med ReptiSun 10.0</AffiliateLink> are both well-regarded choices, in a reflective fixture, sized to cover roughly a third to half the enclosure length. Target a basking-area UVI around 3.0 to 4.0, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076GXJFJG?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Solarmeter Model 6.5R Reptile UV Index Meter">UV index meter</AffiliateLink> takes the guesswork out of confirming that number. Distance sets the dose: with mesh between tube and tortoise, its back sits 14 to 16 inches below the lamp, and with no mesh, 17 to 20 inches. Avoid coil or compact UVB bulbs, they're not considered adequate for this species. Pair with a bright 6500K light, and run the lights on a timer, 12 hours on and 12 off, or a seasonal swing from about 14 hours in summer to 10 in winter.

## Cleaning

Pick out droppings and urates every day, and empty, wash and refill the water dish daily. Soiled patches of substrate come out as they happen, and the whole bed is replaced every 3 to 6 months. Scrub dishes and hides before disinfecting them, and do the whole job in a utility sink or a dedicated tub, never a kitchen sink, with the bleach dilution and contact time the [reptile Salmonella hygiene guide](/blog/reptile-salmonella-hygiene-guide/) gives.

## Diet Basics

This species is almost entirely herbivorous, and the staple should be unlimited grass hay and a rotation of wild weeds, dandelion, plantain, and clover among them, rather than cultivated grocery greens; our [herbivorous reptile safe plants guide](/blog/herbivorous-reptile-safe-plants-guide/) covers the fuller list along with why high-oxalate greens like spinach and chard should stay off the rotation. Fruit stays off the menu altogether: it is high in phosphorus and low in calcium, the opposite of what this arid-habitat grazer needs.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Brumation

Russian tortoises are physiologically programmed to brumate. A captive brumation is held at 35 to 50°F, as close to 41°F as the setup manages, and its length is a ceiling rather than a target: no more than 10 weeks for a small tortoise and 14 for a large one. Wild-caught adults especially may refuse food or show real stress if prevented from doing this entirely. Here's the genuine advantage of indoor keeping though: by maintaining full lighting, heat, and feeding year-round, indoor keepers can choose to keep their tortoise active through winter rather than brumating it, a real option this species offers that many others don't. If you do choose to brumate your tortoise, a pre-brumation vet health check comes first, and the [tortoise brumation guide](/blog/tortoise-brumation-guide/) lays out the fast, the weigh-ins and the warm-up.

There is more on the species in the [Russian Tortoise encyclopedia profile](/encyclopedia/animal/russian-tortoise/).
