# Russian Tortoise Enclosure Setup: Compact Housing With Real Requirements

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Russian tortoise care is manageable indoors for this species' entire life, and getting the enclosure type, temperature, and humidity right is most of what it takes to keep one thriving.

<Figure src="/assets/images/russian-tortoise-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a Russian tortoise enclosure showing an open-top tortoise table, grouped basking and UVB lighting, and a moist hide" caption="Getting the enclosure type, temperature, and humidity right is most of what it takes to keep one thriving." shareable />

## Enclosure Size: The Floor and the Target

A 4 ft by 2 ft by 2 ft tortoise table, about 8 square feet of floor, is the absolute indoor minimum for one adult, and it is genuinely a minimum rather than a goal. The sources disagree about how far above it to aim, and the gap is wide enough to be worth printing rather than averaging away. ReptiFiles, republished by Zen Habitats, asks for at least 12 square feet of floor space at least a foot high. Our own functional recommendation is larger still, nearer 7 ft by 3.5 ft, roughly 24 square feet, because this is an unusually active tortoise that walks the whole enclosure every day and rearranges anything light enough to move.

Take the largest figure your room allows and treat 4 by 2 as the point below which the enclosure is failing rather than the point at which it is finished. Floor area is what matters here, not height; a foot of wall is plenty, and this species climbs better than it looks like it should, so the walls need to be solid and the top open rather than tall and glass. An <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rkp3S6" product="Tortoise table">open-topped tortoise table</AffiliateLink> is strongly preferred over a closed glass aquarium, better ventilation and considerably less stress for the tortoise. House alone, or with caution, two females together, but not multiple males, which turns territorial fast.

## Temperature

Basking spot around 95 to 100°F, measured at the surface with a temperature gun, cool side in the 70s°F. This is a genuinely cold-hardy species, tolerating night drops to around 60°F without needing supplemental night heat in most homes. ReptiFiles, via Zen Habitats, sets the same floor from the other direction: nighttime temperatures should not fall below 60°F, and a room that will not hold that needs a ceramic heater on the enclosure. Sixty is the floor indoors and out, and our [reptile emergency plan guide](/blog/reptile-emergency-plan-guide/) and [outdoor housing guide](/blog/outdoor-reptile-housing-guide/) both use it. A basking heat source, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000YS3H0I?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Exo Terra Sun Glo Halogen Basking Spot Lamp, 50W">halogen bulb</AffiliateLink> works well, is needed indoors during the day; tortoises kept outdoors in mild climates often need no additional heat at all.

## Humidity

Keep ambient humidity relatively low, 30 to 50%, appropriate for this arid-adapted species, but provide a moist hide lined with peat moss to create a humid microclimate for hydration and shedding. High ambient humidity across the whole enclosure is a real cause of respiratory infection, so resist the instinct to keep everything damp.

Sources sit slightly higher than that on the ambient figure. ReptiFiles, republished by Zen Habitats, gives 40 to 50%, which overlaps our 30 to 50% at the top rather than contradicting it. What matters more than the exact percentage is that both answers have the same shape, and it is a shape that "keep it dry" alone gets wrong. The tortoise experiences two humidities, not one: a dry, well-ventilated surface it walks and basks on, and a genuinely humid microclimate underneath, in a burrow or a moist hide, where it spends a large part of its day and does most of its shedding. Build for both. An enclosure that is dry everywhere, with no damp retreat and no depth to dig into, is what produces a dehydrated, pyramided tortoise, and it is a more common failure in this species than an enclosure that is too wet.

## Substrate

A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CY23K32S?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="VCEPJH Loose Coconut Fiber Substrate, 2 Pack">50/50 mix of topsoil and coconut coir</AffiliateLink>, or a similar sand and loam blend, deep enough to support burrowing and kept slightly moist rather than bone dry. Avoid pure sand, which can cause mobility and impaction issues, and skip rabbit pellets or newspaper-only setups, neither supports natural behavior well.

## Lighting

Daily UVB is essential. A T5 HO tube, Arcadia 12% or <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FF9GHCDC?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product='Zoo Med ReptiSun T5 HO Terrarium Hood with 10.0 Bulb 24" L'>Zoo Med ReptiSun 10.0</AffiliateLink> are both well-regarded choices, in a reflective fixture, sized to cover roughly a third to half the enclosure length. Target a basking-area UVI around 3.0 to 4.0, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076GXJFJG?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Solarmeter Model 6.5R Reptile UV Index Meter">UV index meter</AffiliateLink> takes the guesswork out of confirming that number. Avoid coil or compact UVB bulbs, they're not considered adequate for this species. Pair with a bright 6500K light.

## Diet Basics

This species is almost entirely herbivorous, and the staple should be unlimited grass hay and a rotation of wild weeds, dandelion, plantain, and clover among them, rather than cultivated grocery greens; our [herbivorous reptile safe plants guide](/blog/herbivorous-reptile-safe-plants-guide/) covers the fuller list along with why high-oxalate greens like spinach and chard should stay off the rotation. Fruit should be minimal to none: the Tortoise Trust's position on feeding Mediterranean tortoises, an arid-habitat pattern this species shares, is that fruit "should not be given" at all, since it is high in phosphorus and low in calcium, the opposite of what this animal needs.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Brumation

Russian tortoises are physiologically programmed to brumate, entering a period of dormancy for roughly 2 to 4 months over winter at cooler temperatures, around 40 to 55°F. Wild-caught adults especially may refuse food or show real stress if prevented from doing this entirely. Here's the genuine advantage of indoor keeping though: by maintaining full lighting, heat, and feeding year-round, indoor keepers can choose to keep their tortoise active through winter rather than brumating it, a real option this species offers that many others don't. If you do choose to brumate your tortoise, a pre-brumation vet health check is worth doing first, and our [tortoise brumation guide](/blog/tortoise-brumation-guide/) covers the full protocol.
