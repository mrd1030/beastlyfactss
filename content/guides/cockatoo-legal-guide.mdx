# Is It Legal to Own a Cockatoo? State-by-State Guide

Cockatoos are legal to own across nearly every US state, but "is a cockatoo legal here" turns out to be the wrong question in at least one place. Maine's parrot rules don't treat cockatoos as one group; they single out specific species by conservation status, which means the answer can depend on which cockatoo you're asking about.

### Massachusetts Answers It Species by Species

Massachusetts is the second state we checked, after Maine, where the answer changes with the cockatoo. It runs a closed exemption list at 321 CMR 9.01, and parrots are on it as a family, "Psittacidae (parrots and their allies)". That looks like the end of it, and it is not, because the bird section opens by removing three groups from whatever follows, the first of which is any species "categorically non-exempt pursuant to 321 CMR 9.01(3)". Clause (3)(b) then bars exempting anything listed in the IUCN Red Book.

That clause splits the pet cockatoos down the middle. The umbrella cockatoo and the Moluccan are both assessed as endangered and the yellow-crested group includes critically endangered species, so all of them sit outside the family exemption and in a permit tier that MassWildlife says it does not issue for pets. The Major Mitchell's and the long-billed corella are least concern and clear it. Goffin's cockatoo is near threatened, which is the one place the language runs out: the statute bars listing an animal "in any category" of the IUCN Red Data Books and the agency paraphrases that as "any rarity category", and neither says whether near threatened is one.

The practical version is short. In Massachusetts, look up your species before you look up the parrot rule.

### Cockatoos Are an Easy Yes in Most States

In the great majority of states, a pet cockatoo doesn't raise any legal questions at all. Idaho and Colorado clear pet birds as a category outright, Colorado by putting "all Psitticine birds" on its unregulated wildlife list, and Oklahoma's noncontrolled exotic-species exemption names cockatoos specifically, alongside cockatiels, macaws, and canaries. Others, including New Hampshire and Montana, name the order Psittaciformes or the family Psittacidae directly as unregulated. Where a state does restrict a parrot species, it's almost always the same one: the monk parakeet, named specifically in California, Connecticut, New Jersey, and Virginia, among others, and prohibited outright in Colorado on a separate list that overrides the parrot exemption, while every other parrot, cockatoos included, is left alone. New Jersey is the exception that proves how the reading goes wrong, and it gets its own section below.

### New Jersey Restricts by Silence, Not by Listing

New Jersey looks like an easy yes and is not, and the reason is worth understanding because the same trap is set in every closed-list state. Its potentially-dangerous-species table at N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.8(a) does name three parrots by genus, the ring-necked parakeets, the monk parakeets, and the Patagonian conure, and no cockatoo appears on it. That is a real fact and it answers the wrong question. Being off the dangerous table only means a bird is not banned.

What decides whether a bird is free is the exempt list at 7:25-4.4, and it is closed. Nine birds are on it: the budgerigar, cockatiel, peafowl, rock dove, canary, house sparrow, European starling, zebra finch, and society finch. Every other bird falls to 7:25-4.5, which requires a permit for "any other exotic mammals, birds, reptiles, or amphibians, or nongame species not specifically exempted" by that list. A cockatoo is not on the nine, so it needs an individual hobby permit. So does a macaw, and so does the [African grey](/blog/african-grey-parrot-legal-guide/), which the state names on its permit-required list outright.

There is a second layer for the same species Massachusetts and Maine catch. New Jersey Fish and Wildlife says in its restricted-species notice that it classifies any animal as endangered when it appears on "any relevant State or Federal endangered species list, including but not limited to" the federal list, the IUCN Red List, and the state's own, and that endangered species may not be kept as pets for any reason. The umbrella cockatoo and the Moluccan are both IUCN Endangered, so for those two the answer may be harder than a permit. As in Massachusetts, look up your species first.

### Maine's CITES and IUCN Carve-Out

<FunFact>
Maine clears the entire parrot order, Psittaciformes, from its captivity-permit requirement in one broad stroke, then takes a chunk back out: any species also listed on CITES Appendix I or the IUCN Red List's Endangered, Critically Endangered, or Extinct in the Wild tiers still needs a permit. That single clause catches some of the most commonly kept pet cockatoos by name. The Moluccan, yellow-crested and Goffin's cockatoos are all CITES Appendix I; Goffin's, the smallest cockatoo commonly kept, has been since 1992. The umbrella cockatoo is IUCN Endangered, even though it's only CITES Appendix II, which means the IUCN half of Maine's rule catches it on its own. Species on neither list, like the cockatiel or the galah, clear Maine's list with no permit at all, so size is no guide: the small Goffin's needs the permit. So "is a cockatoo legal in Maine" isn't a single yes-or-no, it depends entirely on which cockatoo you're asking about. Arkansas took the opposite approach: its unrestricted list names five cockatoo species outright, Moluccan and yellow-crested included, with no conservation-status carve-out at all, the same CITES Appendix I birds Maine treats as needing a permit.
</FunFact>

### Hawaii: A Process, Not a Ban

Hawaii doesn't ban cockatoos, but it doesn't treat them as an ordinary pet either. White cockatoos, along with the galah, gang-gang, black cockatoos, and palm cockatoo, are named on the state's list of conditionally approved animals. Getting one into the state legally means securing an import permit, completing pre-departure quarantine, providing health certification, and having the bird permanently identified, all before it's allowed to land. It's a real, working process rather than a ban, but it's a meaningfully higher bar than most states set.

## Conservation Status Sorts the Cockatoos

In 47 jurisdictions a cockatoo is an ordinary pet bird, and the species never comes up. In MA and ME the species decides it, because a CITES or IUCN listing pulls the Moluccan, the umbrella and their kin out of a parrot exemption, while NJ and VT want a permit for any cockatoo and Hawaii runs an import process. Look up the species before the state rule. The [interactive map of cockatoo laws](/exotic-pet-laws/cockatoo/) gives the rule behind every state.
