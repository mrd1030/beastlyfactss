# How Much Does a Fire Skink Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Fire skinks are affordable, and the price gap between captive-bred and wild-caught animals is worth paying attention to for reasons beyond just cost.

## The skink: %%animal:fire-skink%%, more for captive-bred

Common wild-caught or imported fire skinks run %%animal:fire-skink%%. There's no real color-morph market here, this species is prized for its natural red, black, and gold banding rather than selectively bred variants. Captive-bred individuals cost more but make genuinely better pets, healthier on arrival and more receptive to taming, worth the premium if you can find one.

## Upfront setup: a moderate reptile cost

A 36x18x18 inch enclosure, UVB kit, heat lamp and thermostat, deep moisture-retentive substrate, hides, and a hygrometer make up the setup, genuinely more affordable than a large lizard setup and comparable to other mid-size terrestrial reptile setups. ## The setup, line by line: %%setup:fire-skink%%

The enclosure is the big line, and the [thermostat is the one people try to skip](/blog/reptile-heating-thermostats-guide/). It has to be a dimming model like the one linked above. A $130 on/off unit is the wrong buy here, however good: its maker says not to run an incandescent basking bulb on one. A 36x18x18 inch tank is the floor for a single adult, and the substrate has to be deep enough to burrow in, around 6 inches, which is why it takes eight or nine 8 quart bags to fill that footprint.

The whole bed is replaced every 3 to 4 months, so that shopping list comes round three or four times a year.

## Hides and small items

Hides run anywhere from a $4 plastic cave to a $30 resin shelter, so that row depends on taste more than need.

## Ongoing costs: modest

Live insects, crickets, roaches, and worms among the common choices, plus a calcium and D3 supplement and periodic substrate replacement, keep this a low-maintenance species to feed month to month.

An adult fire skink eats two or three times a week, and the feeder bill follows from that. Dubia roaches sell for $0.18 to $0.31 each at the smaller sizes, the low end on sale, and at five or six insects a feeding, nine to thirteen feedings a month lands at roughly $10 to $25 with some crickets and worms mixed in. Calcium with D3 is a $6 tub, and the care sheets call for a light dusting, so it is a purchase every few months. The line people forget is substrate: the care sheets call for a full replacement every three to four months, and at eight or nine bags a time that is $22 to $33 a month once it is spread out. Add the yearly vet visit below and a UVB bulb change on the maker's replacement schedule, and this is still one of the cheaper lizards to keep.

## Vet costs

Fire skinks are hardy with relatively few reported health issues. An annual exotic vet checkup is still worth doing, and parasite screening is genuinely important for wild-caught animals specifically, given how common wild-caught stock is in this species' trade. A new skink lives apart from any other reptile until that screen comes back clean, on the setup the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) describes, so budget a basic second tub and paper towels for the first months.

## Lifespan

15 to 20 years, a long commitment for a lizard this small and this affordable to acquire. The low purchase price shouldn't distract from the fact that this is a two-decade relationship.

## The cost consideration: captive-bred vs. wild-caught

Given how much of this species' trade is still wild-caught, the more meaningful cost decision isn't about morphs or price tiers, it's about sourcing. A wild-caught animal is the cheaper of the two and tends to arrive more stressed, more parasite-prone, and slower to settle. Paying the premium for verified captive-bred stock is the better value here.
