# How to Handle a Hermit Crab (Minimally, and On Its Terms)

Hermit crabs are prey animals, and they're not a pet built for regular handling the way a guinea pig or a chinchilla is. Minimal, gentle, purposeful handling is the right approach here, not casual daily interaction.

## The Correct Technique When You Do Handle One

Let the crab sit on a flat, open palm instead of closing your hand around it. If you need to move it, lift from the back of the shell, not by grabbing at the legs or claws directly. Keep your hands low, over a soft surface or close to the enclosure floor, in case the crab startles and slips. Keep sessions brief, this isn't a pet that benefits from extended handling the way some mammals do.

<FunFact>
Grabbing a hermit crab by a leg or claw during a startle can trigger autotomy, the defensive limb-dropping response where the crab sheds the limb rather than risk a worse injury. Lifting from the shell itself isn't just gentler, it avoids setting off that response in the first place.
</FunFact>

## When Not to Handle, No Exceptions

Never handle a crab that's molting, has recently changed shells, or is buried in the substrate. Molting crabs in particular are extremely vulnerable, and disturbing one can be fatal, this is one of the clearest rules in hermit crab care. A freshly molted crab stays soft and weak until the new exoskeleton hardens, the same defenseless stretch that [molting tarantulas and other invertebrates](/blog/invertebrate-molting-guide/) go through, and a hand in the substrate can crush or expose it.

## Signs of Stress

- Retreating deep into the shell for an extended period
- Staying unusually still well after handling has ended
- Dropping a limb
- Increased pinching behavior
- Refusing food
- Constantly trying to escape when handled
- Burying and staying hidden

## How Long a Stressed or New Crab May Hide

This is worth knowing so you don't panic unnecessarily. A newly disturbed crab, or one that's just arrived in a new home, commonly digs down and stays out of sight for days, sometimes over a week, while it settles in. That's normal, and not something to worry about. A molting crab can stay buried considerably longer, anywhere from several weeks up to around three months, and should not be dug up or disturbed during that time no matter how long it's been.

A crab that's staying still or buried is almost always still alive. The real warning signs of a genuine problem are a strong, rotten odor, or a body that's gone limp or fallen out of the shell entirely.

## Telling Males from Females

Sex is read from the gonopores, the small openings at the base of the walking legs, and never by pulling a crab from its shell. A female has a pair of small, dark, round gonopores on the underside of the base of the third pair of legs, the second pair of large walking legs. A male's gonopores sit on the tiny fifth pair of legs, which normally stay tucked inside the shell, so a male usually shows nothing where a female shows two dots.

The only way to look is to wait for the crab to walk far enough out on its own. A crab crossing a clear container, or climbing the glass, often extends enough to show the leg bases. Adult females also carry three egg-holding pleopods on the left side of the abdomen, but that part stays inside the shell and is not something to check on a live pet.

## Picking a Healthy Crab

Most crabs in shops have been stressed by collection, shipping and poor holding conditions, so the pick matters. Pass on any crab that:

- Is missing several limbs, or sits in a tank with shed legs scattered on the floor
- Lives in a cracked, painted, or visibly too-small shell
- Stays limp and unresponsive when its shell is lifted
- Shares a tank that smells foul or fishy, or shows mold, mites or other pests

A good candidate pulls back into its shell when lifted, comes out to explore soon after, and wears an intact natural shell it fits into fully. Even a crab that passes every check may take weeks or months to recover from the trip, and it is still in the post-purchase risk window until its first molt at home.

## A Pet Built on the Enclosure

Hermit crabs do not come around to handling the way a rat or a [bearded dragon](/blog/bearded-dragon-handling-guide/) can. What a keeper gets instead is the nightly traffic of a group that feels safe: climbing, shell trying, digging and feeding, most of it after the lights go down. Handling stays brief and purposeful, and the rest of the relationship is the environment. The [Hermit Crab encyclopedia profile](/encyclopedia/animal/hermit-crab/) covers the wild background behind all of it.
