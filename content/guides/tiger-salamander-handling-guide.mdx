# How to Handle a Tiger Salamander (And Why You Mostly Won't See One)

Tiger salamanders are more outgoing than a lot of amphibians, they'll learn feeding routines and emerge from their burrow when they sense you nearby. That personality doesn't translate into a handling relationship though, and understanding why comes down to biology, not temperament.

## A Largely Underground Animal

This species is fossorial, spending most of its time burrowed underground rather than visible on the surface the way a more aquatic amphibian might be. Don't mistake a salamander that's rarely visible for one that's unhealthy or hiding from stress, this is genuinely normal behavior for the species, and it's exactly why deep, burrowable substrate matters so much, a case the [tiger salamander enrichment guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-enrichment-guide/) makes at length.

## Why Handling Stays Minimal

Direct handling is avoided with this species. Oils on human skin interfere with the mucus layer a salamander breathes and drinks through, and like many amphibians, tiger salamanders produce skin secretions that are toxic if swallowed. Excessive handling also stresses the animal and warms it, and this is a species whose enclosure should never pass about 78°F: a warm hand is well above that.

There is a second reason that has nothing to do with the salamander's skin. A tiger salamander has rows of short, sawtooth ridges on both jaws for gripping prey, and one that associates a hand with food will strike at fingers the same way it strikes at a worm.

## If Handling Is Necessary

Wear powder-free gloves moistened with dechlorinated water and keep any handling brief. Without gloves, wash and rinse hands thoroughly so no soap is left, then wet them with spring or conditioned water right before contact. For moving the animal during a clean-out or into a quarantine tub, a container beats hands: guide the salamander into a damp plastic tub, the same container-first method the [amphibian quarantine guide](/blog/amphibian-quarantine-and-water-guide/) uses for every new amphibian. This isn't a species built for regular physical interaction, and treating it that way risks harm to an animal with permeable skin.

## Protecting Yourself

Even a healthy tiger salamander can carry Salmonella in its gut. Wash with soap and running water after handling the animal, its feeders, or anything from its enclosure, and before eating. Children younger than 5 should not handle amphibians or touch their enclosures at all, because they are at the highest risk of serious illness. Clean the dish and decor in a laundry sink or bathtub, never a kitchen sink or anywhere food is prepared.

<FunFact>
Tiger salamanders spend so much time burrowed that a keeper who barely sees theirs for weeks at a stretch might assume something is wrong. It usually isn't, this is completely normal fossorial behavior for the species, not a sign of stress or hiding.
</FunFact>

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Handling too often, assuming an outgoing personality means tolerance for regular handling.
- Using bare, dry hands instead of wet gloves when handling is unavoidable.
- Feeding prey that's too large, which can cause impaction or even paralysis.
- Overfeeding generally, this species doesn't reliably self-regulate portions.

## How This Compares to the Axolotl

This is the most useful comparison to understand. Juvenile and larval tiger salamanders look and are cared for almost exactly like axolotls, fully aquatic, gilled, and living entirely in water, in [much the same setup](/blog/axolotl-tank-setup-guide/). But unlike the axolotl, which stays aquatic its entire life, tiger salamanders metamorphose as they mature, losing their gills and becoming the terrestrial, burrowing adult described above.
