# How to Handle a Tiger Salamander (And Why You Mostly Won't See One)

Tiger salamanders are genuinely more outgoing than a lot of amphibians, they'll learn feeding routines and emerge from their burrow when they sense you nearby. That personality doesn't translate into a handling relationship though, and understanding why comes down to biology, not temperament. For everything else about day to day care, see our full [Tiger Salamander care guide](/guides/tiger-salamander/).

## A Largely Underground Animal

This species is fossorial, spending most of its time burrowed underground rather than visible on the surface the way a more aquatic amphibian might be. Don't mistake a salamander that's rarely visible for one that's unhealthy or hiding from stress, this is genuinely normal behavior for the species, and it's exactly why deep, burrowable substrate matters so much, covered in our [Tiger Salamander tank setup guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-tank-setup-guide/).

## Why Handling Stays Minimal

Direct handling should genuinely be avoided with this species. Oils naturally present on human skin can interfere with a salamander's sensitive mucus layer, and like many amphibians, tiger salamanders produce toxic skin secretions that are dangerous if ingested. Excessive handling also causes real stress and can lead to overheating, since amphibians regulate temperature very differently than reptiles or mammals do.

## If Handling Genuinely Is Necessary

Wear wet, powder-free nitrile gloves and keep any handling brief. This isn't a species built for regular physical interaction, and treating it that way risks genuine harm to an animal with permeable, sensitive skin.

<FunFact>
Tiger salamanders spend so much time burrowed that a keeper who barely sees theirs for weeks at a stretch might assume something is wrong. It usually isn't, this is completely normal fossorial behavior for the species, not a sign of stress or hiding.
</FunFact>

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Handling too often, assuming an outgoing personality means tolerance for regular handling.
- Using bare, dry hands instead of wet gloves when handling is unavoidable.
- Feeding prey that's too large, which can cause impaction or even paralysis, more on feeding-related health risks in our [Tiger Salamander health issues guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-health-issues-guide/).
- Overfeeding generally, this species doesn't reliably self-regulate portions.

## How This Compares to the Axolotl Already Covered on This Site

This is genuinely the most useful comparison to understand. Juvenile and larval tiger salamanders look and are cared for almost exactly like axolotls, fully aquatic, gilled, and living entirely in water, similar to what we describe in our [axolotl tank setup guide](/blog/axolotl-tank-setup-guide/). But unlike the axolotl, which stays aquatic its entire life, tiger salamanders metamorphose as they mature, losing their gills and becoming the terrestrial, burrowing adult described above. If you've kept an axolotl and are considering a tiger salamander expecting similar lifelong care, know that the adult setup, covered in our [tank setup guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-tank-setup-guide/), looks completely different from an axolotl tank.
