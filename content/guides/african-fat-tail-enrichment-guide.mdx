# African Fat-Tailed Gecko Enrichment: The Closest Cousin Was Studied

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Nobody has run an enrichment study on this species, but its nearest relative got one of the better ones in reptile keeping. For everything else about them, see our complete [African Fat-Tailed Gecko care guide](/guides/african-fat-tail/).

<KeyTakeaway>
The leopard gecko study tested five enrichment types and ranked them: thermal and feeding at the top, mirrors and visual novelty at the bottom. Fat-tails are eublepharids, the same family, so that ranking is the best available steer. The one thing that does not transfer is humidity, which this species needs considerably more of.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Actually Says

Nothing on *Hemitheconyx caudicinctus*. The nearest evidence, covered in full in our [Leopard Gecko enrichment guide](/blog/leopard-gecko-enrichment-guide/), is that Bashaw and colleagues tested **five types of environmental enrichment** on leopard geckos and found thermal and feeding enrichment produced the strongest responses, while **mirror-based visual enrichment was the weakest**.

A separate paper compared reaction to novel objects and faecal glucocorticoid metabolite levels in two nocturnal gecko species, finding that individuals engaging sooner and longer with novel objects had lower baseline stress-hormone levels.

<FunFact>
Leopard geckos and fat-tails are both eublepharids, the eyelid geckos, which makes this one of the shorter evidence borrows in this series. It is still a borrow, and the humidity difference between the two species is a real reason not to apply it blindly.
</FunFact>

## The Humid Hide, Which Is Not Optional Here

The biggest divergence from the leopard gecko playbook. Fat-tails come from more humid West African habitats, and a persistently damp retreat is a requirement rather than a shedding aid you set up occasionally.

Maintain it. A humid hide that has dried out is a hide, not a humid hide. Our [tank setup guide](/blog/african-fat-tail-tank-setup-guide/) covers the humidity numbers.

## Thermal Choice, the Top-Ranked Item

Three hides minimum: warm, cool, and humid. The point is that the animal can be concealed **and** at its chosen temperature at the same time, rather than trading one for the other. That is what the leopard gecko study ranked first, and there is no reason it would differ here.

## Feeding as Hunting

Ranked second. Tong feeding with <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FB3PRD2Y?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Short soft-tip feeding tongs, 4 pack">soft-tipped tongs</AffiliateLink>, short supervised insect hunts inside the enclosure, and varying when and where prey appears all beat a static bowl.

Feed in the evening. Fat-tails are crepuscular and nocturnal, and offering food during the day gets a duller response from an animal that should be asleep. Our [feeding guide](/blog/african-fat-tail-feeding-guide/) covers prey sizing and schedule.

## Floor Space Over Height

Terrestrial species. Low branches, cork flats and stacked slate give somewhere to rest off the ground, and that is genuinely all the vertical structure this animal needs. Floor area and a working hide setup do more.

## Priority Order

1. A properly maintained humid hide, which this species needs more than leopard geckos
2. Thermal choice with hides at both ends
3. Feeding and hunting presentation
4. Floor space
5. Low climbing and cover
6. Novelty objects, last, since visual enrichment ranked bottom

## What Not to Do

Do not let the humid hide dry out, which is the one place the leopard gecko playbook underserves this species. Do not raise humidity across the whole enclosure to compensate, since that risks respiratory infection, covered in our [health issues guide](/blog/african-fat-tail-health-issues-guide/). Do not bother with mirrors, which ranked last in the closest study available. Do not feed during the day.
