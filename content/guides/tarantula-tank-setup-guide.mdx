# Tarantula Enclosure Setup: Shape, Substrate, and a Humidity Debate

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Tarantula enclosures are refreshingly simple compared to most exotic pets, no complex lighting, no elaborate temperature gradients. The details that do matter, though, matter quite a bit, especially enclosure shape and substrate depth.

<Figure src="/assets/images/tarantula-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a tarantula enclosure showing the secure ventilated lid, deep substrate, hide, and water dish" caption="A tarantula enclosure stays horizontal and low to limit fall risk, with a ventilated acrylic lid and at least 3 inches of coconut fiber or soil substrate, never sand." shareable />

## Enclosure Shape and Size

Most commonly kept pet tarantulas are terrestrial, and the enclosure should be **horizontal, wider than it is tall**, specifically to limit fall risk. A reasonable adult minimum is around 20 by 10 by 10 inches, or roughly a 10-gallon tank, sized to about three times the tarantula's leg span. Keep the overall height low, under about 12 inches, since falls are a genuine danger for most tarantulas.

Use a secure lid, but avoid mesh tops. A tarantula's feet can get caught in mesh and cause real injury. Acrylic with drilled ventilation holes is the better choice.

## Substrate

Use at least 3 inches of depth, filling roughly half to two-thirds of the enclosure. Coconut fiber, peat, or organic pesticide-free soil all work well, they hold moisture appropriately and support natural burrowing behavior.

<FunFact>
Many wild terrestrial tarantulas spend much of their time in burrows they dig themselves or take over from other animals. Substrate depth in captivity isn't just about holding moisture, it lets a tarantula express a burrowing instinct it retains even in an enclosure where it will never actually need to escape a predator.
</FunFact>

## Furnishings

Provide at least one hide, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3SKmfOx" product="Cork bark round hide">cork bark</AffiliateLink>, a half-log, or half of a terracotta pot all work. Add a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/44hAzR9" product="Small/shallow water dish">shallow water dish</AffiliateLink>, changed daily, and keep it shallow enough that there's no drowning risk.

## Temperature

Room temperature, roughly 70 to 80°F, is fine for most homes with no supplemental heat needed. If your space runs cold, use a heat mat mounted on the side of the enclosure, never the bottom, paired with a thermostat. ## Humidity

Published targets run from 40 to 60% up to 65 to 75%. Use the lower end for the dry-adapted species most keepers start with, like the Chilean rose hair, because ventilation matters more than humidity and stagnant damp breeds mold. The most consistent approach is a reliable water dish combined with lightly moistened substrate, using a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vPKMjX" product="Fine mist spray bottle">fine mist spray bottle</AffiliateLink> sparingly, rather than heavy misting, which raises mold risk without a clear benefit.

## Lighting

None required. Most pet tarantulas are nocturnal, and bright light or direct sun should be avoided rather than added.

## Diet

Feed live crickets or <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4uFr6hU" product="Dubia Roaches (feeder bag)">dubia roaches</AffiliateLink> no larger than the tarantula's abdomen, and remove anything uneaten within 24 hours, a live insect left in the enclosure can injure or stress a tarantula, particularly one close to a molt. The [invertebrate molting guide](/blog/invertebrate-molting-guide/) shows how to spot that premolt window coming.

## Housing a Sling

A spiderling, or sling, does not go in the adult enclosure. Most terrestrial species start out around a quarter inch across the legs, and in a big tank the sling and its prey never find each other and nobody can tell whether it is eating or molting.

- **Container.** A 40 dram vial raises most slings to about a 1 inch leg span. After that, a container two to three times the spider's leg span across. A 16 oz deli cup with a mesh insect lid is the better choice for any young tarantula, since it ventilates far better than a vial and holds more substrate. Use 12 to 16 oz cups for terrestrial species and 32 oz for arboreal ones.
- **Substrate.** Fill the container about halfway. Moisten it until it just clumps when squeezed in a fist, and if water runs out it is too wet. Err toward dry: even slings of humid-forest species die quickly in wet, stagnant containers. Poke a 1 inch starter burrow near the side so the spider is visible from outside.
- **Water.** Slings are too small for a dish and get their water from prey, as long as the substrate never dries out completely. A 2-liter bottle cap becomes the first dish once the spider's leg span matches it. Skip damp sponges, which breed bacteria.
- **Heat.** Room temperature of 72 to 78°F raises most species. Where extra heat is needed, keep the containers off the heat source and below 82°F.
- **Arboreal slings.** A taller container, only a quarter filled with substrate, with a sprig of artificial plant or a piece of bark to climb. A light weekly misting that dries within a day or two, never sprayed onto the spider.

Remove uneaten prey the next morning, since a live cricket can kill a sling that is fasting before a molt. Move slings with a soft artist's paintbrush, never by hand. Each step up to a bigger container is a small version of the adult [catch cup and card move](/blog/invertebrate-rehousing-guide/), with the brush doing the steering.

## One Spider per Enclosure

House tarantulas singly. Most species are solitary and cannibalistic, cohabitation isn't a workable setup regardless of enclosure size.
