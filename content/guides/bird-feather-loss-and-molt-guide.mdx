# Why Is My Bird Losing Feathers? Molt vs. Plucking vs. PBFD vs. Giardia

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

A bird missing feathers sends most owners straight to the internet, and the internet mostly answers with the scariest option first. Before jumping to plucking, boredom, or a rare disease, it helps to know what an ordinary molt actually looks like, because that's the baseline everything else gets compared against. This guide walks through that baseline first, then the three ways feather loss actually diverges from it: a behavioral plucking habit, the viral disease psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD), and a cockatiel-specific parasite that causes an itch owners routinely mistake for a personality problem.

## What a Normal Molt Actually Looks Like

Every bird sheds and regrows most of its plumage on a roughly annual cycle. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that birds normally lose and replace most of their feathers at least once a year through molting, and that many species also go through a smaller partial molt about six months after the main one. The defining feature of a normal molt, per Merck, is that it's "a slow, gradual process," and the feathers that come out are generally whole and undamaged, not shredded, chewed, or snapped off partway.

The new feathers replacing them start as pin feathers: a growing feather still encased in its own protective sheath. Lafeber's pet bird care team describes that sheath as a waxy covering that stays soft while the feather is still actively growing and supplied with blood, then dries out and flakes away on its own once the feather matures and the blood supply withdraws. Most birds manage this themselves through ordinary preening, working the dry sheaths loose bit by bit as each feather finishes coming in. Our [wing clipping guide](/blog/bird-wing-clipping-guide/) covers the same pin and blood feather anatomy in more depth, including why a still-growing blood feather is a genuine bleeding risk if it gets cut or snapped.

Molting is also physically demanding, and it shows in a bird's mood. Lafeber's team notes plainly that molting "takes a lot of their resources," and that the pin feathers themselves can be uncomfortable while they're growing in, which is enough to make an otherwise easygoing bird cranky or short-tempered for a stretch. A bird that suddenly doesn't want to be handled, or fusses when you try to help along a pin feather that clearly isn't ready to come loose, is usually just having a normal, if grumpy, molt.

Bathing helps. The Merck Veterinary Manual recommends bathing or misting molting birds regularly, since raising humidity keeps skin from drying out, may lessen feather plucking, and encourages the kind of normal preening that helps a bird work sheaths off on its own. <KeyTakeaway>
A normal molt is slow, made of whole feathers, and never produces bald skin. If your bird is missing patches, has damaged or ragged feathers, or is losing feathers from an area it shouldn't be able to reach itself, you've moved past a normal molt and into one of the three conditions below.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Plucking: The Bird That Can't Reach Its Own Head

Feather-destructive behavior, plucking, over-preening, self-mutilation of feathers and skin, is the condition most owners jump to first, and it's genuinely common. But it has one built-in physical limit that makes it easy to rule in or out: a bird can only chew, pull, or over-preen feathers it can physically reach with its own beak. The Merck Veterinary Manual is specific about what that damage looks like up close, describing feathers that are "chewed at the skin level, chewed with down feathers left behind, or chewed only at the tips, giving a ragged appearance," with more severe cases pulled out completely.

That reach limitation is the single most useful diagnostic detail in this whole picture. A bird with bald or damaged feathers across its chest, back, or under its wings, but a completely full, undisturbed set of feathers on top of its own head, is very likely doing that damage to itself, because the chest and back are exactly where a bird's own beak can reach and the top of its own head is exactly where it can't. Flip that pattern around, a bird losing feathers specifically from the top of its head while everything else looks fine, and self-inflicted plucking stops being a plausible explanation on its own, since the bird physically cannot pluck a spot it cannot reach.

Plucking itself is rarely one simple cause. The [cockatoo screaming and feather plucking guide](/blog/cockatoo-screaming-feather-plucking-explained/) goes deep on the behavioral side: over-bonding, boredom, anxiety, and what actually reduces established plucking once medical causes are ruled out. Sleep matters here too. The [bird photoperiod and sleep guide](/blog/bird-photoperiod-sleep-guide/) covers how an inconsistent light schedule and too little uninterrupted darkness contribute to the same feather-destructive behavior, alongside boredom and hormonal stress. What both of those guides agree on, and what's worth repeating here, is that plucking always deserves a medical rule-out before it gets treated as a purely behavioral problem, because both an underlying illness and a viral disease with no cure can produce feather loss that looks identical to a habit at first glance.

## PBFD: The Viral Cause That Isn't Behavioral At All

Psittacine beak and feather disease is the condition that punishes anyone who assumes all feather loss is behavioral. It's caused by a circovirus that, per VCA Animal Hospitals, "attacks fast-growing epithelial (skin) cells, causing visibly abnormal formations of the beak and/or feather follicles." Unlike plucking, PBFD doesn't care what a bird can or can't reach with its own beak, because the damage comes from inside the follicle itself, not from the bird's own actions.

That's exactly what makes PBFD show up in places plucking can't. The Merck Veterinary Manual lists "feather loss in areas the bird can't reach (such as the top of its head)" as a symptom, alongside abnormal-looking pin feathers, abnormal mature feathers, and a lack of the fine powder down that species like African greys and cockatoos normally produce. VCA's own description of the feather changes is similarly specific: affected feathers can appear "stunted, club-shaped, or pinched off," with premature molting and abnormal feather formation occurring symmetrically across the body, a pattern a purely behavioral habit doesn't typically produce.

The disease earns the "beak" half of its name too. VCA notes the beak can become "elongated, excessively flaky or shiny," and will "easily break with simple handling," a presentation seen especially in white cockatoo species. Host range matters for how worried to be: VCA documents PBFD mainly in parrots native to Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and Indonesia, and states plainly that it "is rare in neotropical parrots, such as macaws, amazon parrots, and conures." The Merck Veterinary Manual names African grey parrots, Eclectus parrots, lovebirds, and lorikeets among the species where it still occurs. Over 50 species have been documented as susceptible, so it isn't confined to one or two birds, but it also isn't an equal risk across every parrot in every household, and a macaw owner reading about PBFD is working from a very different baseline risk than a cockatoo or African grey owner.

There's no good news on treatment. VCA is direct that there's no cure, and that most affected birds eventually die from secondary infections that take hold once the virus has suppressed their immune system, sometimes within months, sometimes over a longer stretch of years. Diagnosis and any supportive care for a suspected case belongs entirely with an avian vet, ideally before a new bird is ever introduced to an existing collection, since the virus sheds readily in feather dust and droppings.

## Giardia in Cockatiels: The Itch That Looks Behavioral

This is the case owners get wrong most often, and it's specific to one species for a documented reason. VCA Animal Hospitals states it directly: "Another organism that commonly affects cockatiels is the internal parasite Giardia lamblia. Birds infected with Giardia may have loose stools or show signs of being very itchy, violently attacking themselves, especially under the wings."

Read that sentence carefully, because the "or" is doing real work. A Giardia infection doesn't guarantee obvious diarrhea. Some cockatiels get loose stool, but plenty show only the itch, with a completely normal-looking dropping tray the entire time. That itch, when it's the only sign, is intense enough that an infected bird will attack its own skin hard enough to damage feathers, especially in the hard-to-monitor area under the wings, and from the outside that looks exactly like a bird that has developed a stress or boredom habit. Nothing about a Giardia-driven itch looks parasitic from across the room. It looks like a behavior problem, right up until someone runs the right test.

That's the practical instruction worth taking away from this section: a cockatiel plucking or digging at its skin, particularly under the wings, gets a fecal test before anyone starts rearranging the cage, adding foraging toys, or assuming a new stressor moved in. Environmental and behavioral fixes genuinely help plucking that's actually behavioral, covered in the cockatoo guide linked above, but none of them treat a parasite, and weeks spent on cage rearrangement and enrichment while an underlying Giardia infection goes untreated is weeks the bird spends still being miserably itchy for the wrong reason. This susceptibility is documented specifically in cockatiels, not parrots generally, so it's a cockatiel-specific line item to raise with your vet rather than a blanket concern for every pet bird species. <FunFact>
The same "can it reach that spot" logic that flags PBFD also explains why a well-meaning owner can misread a healthy bird. A bird preening a partner's head feathers, or a solo bird using a perch or cage bar to scratch its own head, is doing normal maintenance a beak alone can't manage, not showing an early symptom of anything.

## When to Call an Avian Vet

Any feather loss that isn't a slow, whole-feather molt is worth a call. That includes bald skin appearing quickly, feathers that look chewed, pinched, or stunted rather than simply shed, feather loss on the head or another spot a bird can't reach itself, and any plucking paired with loose stool or visible skin irritation. An avian vet can examine the feather follicles and skin directly, run bloodwork or a fecal test as needed, and rule PBFD or a parasite like Giardia in or out before any environmental changes get made on a guess. Getting the diagnosis right first is what actually prevents weeks of the wrong fix.
