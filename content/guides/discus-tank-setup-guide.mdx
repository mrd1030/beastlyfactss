# Discus Tank Setup: Why the Water-Change Routine Matters More Than the Gallon Number

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Discus tolerate a narrower range of water conditions than most community fish, and the tank size number matters less than most people expect, the water-change routine is what separates a discus tank that works from one that doesn't.

What it all costs is in our [Discus cost guide](/blog/discus-cost-guide/), and everything outside setup is in the full [Discus care guide](/guides/discus/).

## Tank Size

55 gallons is commonly cited as the practical entry point for a small group. That said, several experienced discus-keeping sources push back on treating 55 gallons as anything more than a floor, recommending 75 gallons or larger instead, since a 55-gallon tank forces meaningfully more frequent water changes to keep pace with a proper group's waste output. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08BJB7FX4?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Tetra Glass Aquarium, 55 Gallons (NV52018)">standard 55-gallon tank</AffiliateLink> runs about 20 inches tall, which suits this species' broad, tall body shape well, taller and deeper tanks are generally preferred over long, shallow ones for discus. Whichever size you land on, plan for a real group, 5 to 6 fish or more, since this is a genuinely social species that does poorly alone or in a pair outside of breeding, covered in more detail in our [Discus handling guide](/blog/discus-handling-guide/).

## Temperature

82 to 86°F, warmer than nearly every other common community fish and warm enough that it genuinely limits realistic tankmate options. Wild Heckel discus tolerate water closer to 90°F, but 82 to 86°F is the standard range cited for keeping captive-bred discus long term. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">reliable heater</AffiliateLink> is essential, and given how much warmer this range runs than a typical community tank, many keepers budget toward the higher end of standard wattage guidance, or run two heaters, since a heater failure is a bigger problem at this temperature and tank size than in a cooler setup.

## Water Chemistry

Soft and slightly acidic, roughly pH 6.0 to 7.0 for keeping, though sources vary somewhat on the exact edges of that range. This mimics the Amazon blackwater this species comes from, tributaries of the Rio Negro in particular, where wild pH commonly runs 6.0 to 6.5 and drops as low as 4 in certain blackwater spots, with very soft water overall. Hard tap water often needs an RO or RO/DI unit to bring it into a workable range for discus, since chasing pH down chemically without addressing hardness tends to be unstable. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">test kit</AffiliateLink> is the only reliable way to confirm your actual numbers rather than guess at them.

<FunFact>
The pH range recommended for keeping discus, roughly 6.0 to 7.0, is already on the acidic side for a community tank. Their native water is more extreme than that. In the blackwater creeks and flooded forests off the Rio Negro where wild discus actually live, pH commonly sits around 6.0 to 6.5 and can drop as low as 4 in some spots, water soft enough to carry almost no measurable hardness. Captive discus don't need conditions that extreme to thrive, but it's a useful reminder of just how far this species is from a typical hard-water community tank.
</FunFact>

## Filtration

This is a genuine point of disagreement rather than a settled answer. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vNGY2v" product="Canister filter (Fluval 407, up to 100 gal)">Canister filters</AffiliateLink>, sized well above the tank's actual volume, are the most common modern choice and handle this species' heavy bioload from frequent feeding and large water changes well. At least one discus-specialty source pushes back on canister filters specifically, arguing they're sealed systems that compete with the fish for dissolved oxygen and can fail dangerously during a power outage, recommending a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000260FUW?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="AquaClear 70 Power Filter (40-70 gal)">hang-on-back filter</AffiliateLink> instead, or a canister paired with extra surface agitation or an air stone for supplemental oxygenation. A sponge filter is the standard choice for fry and breeding tanks, gentle enough not to pull in small fry while still providing real biological filtration. Whatever you choose, avoid under-gravel filters, decomposing waste trapped underneath is a real risk given how much this species eats and produces.

## Substrate

Genuinely a matter of priorities rather than one correct answer. Bare-bottom tanks are the standard choice among breeders and serious keepers specifically because they're the easiest to keep clean given how often discus tanks get water changes, nothing traps waste that then has to be vacuumed out from between gravel. A layer of fine sand is the common middle ground, more natural-looking than bare glass while still showing uneaten food clearly enough to remove before it fouls the water, unlike gravel, where food settles between pieces and is easy to miss.

## Decor and Plants

Build in cover without crowding the swimming space this tall-bodied fish needs. Driftwood arranged vertically and broad-leafed plants, Amazon sword and anubias are commonly recommended, suit this species' natural blackwater look and give a genuinely shy fish somewhere to retreat, which matters given how easily discus stress, covered in our [Discus handling guide](/blog/discus-handling-guide/). Floating plants that soften the light from above are a worthwhile addition for the same reason.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Frequent Water Changes

This is the detail that actually defines a working discus tank more than any single equipment choice. Weekly water changes in the 25 to 50% range are commonly recommended, and some serious keepers change water every 2 to 3 days rather than weekly. Ammonia and nitrite are highly toxic to fish even at low concentrations, and discus tolerate less margin for error here than a lot of hardier community fish, which is exactly why this isn't a species for an uncycled or newly established tank. Budget the electricity and water use behind this routine into your ongoing costs, covered in our [Discus cost guide](/blog/discus-cost-guide/), it's a bigger recurring expense here than for most fish on this site. Small, frequent meals rather than one large feeding also keep the bioload between changes more manageable, our [Discus feeding guide](/blog/discus-feeding-guide/) covers portioning in more detail.

## Lighting

A standard LED on an 8 to 10 hour daily cycle works well. Discus don't have unusual lighting needs beyond what supports any live plants in the tank, and given how easily this species startles at bright light, a fixture with a dimmable or gradual-ramp setting is a genuine nice-to-have rather than a necessity.

## The Bottom Line

None of the individual pieces here are exotic, warm water, soft acidic chemistry, strong filtration, and a real commitment to frequent water changes are all achievable with standard equipment. What trips people up is treating discus like a slightly warmer version of a typical community tank rather than accepting that the maintenance routine itself needs to be genuinely more frequent. Get that right and this is a long-lived fish, commonly cited at 10 to 15 years, covered in more detail in our [Discus cost guide](/blog/discus-cost-guide/). Getting the water right also heads off most of the problems in our [Discus health issues guide](/blog/discus-health-issues-guide/), water quality and stress drive nearly everything on that list.
