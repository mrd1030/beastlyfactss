# Discus Tank Setup: Why the Water-Change Routine Matters More Than the Gallon Number

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Discus tolerate a narrower range of water conditions than most community fish, and the tank size number matters less than most people expect, the water-change routine is what separates a discus tank that works from one that doesn't.

## Tank Size

55 gallons is commonly cited as the practical entry point for a small group. That said, several experienced discus-keeping sources push back on treating 55 gallons as anything more than a floor, recommending 75 gallons or larger instead, since a 55-gallon tank forces meaningfully more frequent water changes to keep pace with a proper group's waste output. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08BJB7FX4?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Tetra Glass Aquarium, 55 Gallons (NV52018)">standard 55-gallon tank</AffiliateLink> runs about 20 inches tall, which suits this species' broad, tall body shape well, taller and deeper tanks are generally preferred over long, shallow ones for discus. Whichever size you land on, plan for a real group, 5 to 6 fish or more, since this is a genuinely social species that does poorly alone or in a pair outside of breeding.

## Temperature

82 to 86°F, warmer than nearly every other common community fish and warm enough that it genuinely limits realistic tankmate options. Wild Heckel discus tolerate water closer to 90°F, but 82 to 86°F is the standard range cited for keeping captive-bred discus long term. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">reliable heater</AffiliateLink> is essential, and given how much warmer this range runs than a typical community tank, many keepers budget toward the higher end of standard wattage guidance, or run two heaters, since a heater failure is a bigger problem at this temperature and tank size than in a cooler setup.

## Water Chemistry

Soft and slightly acidic, roughly pH 6.0 to 7.0 for keeping, though sources vary somewhat on the exact edges of that range. This mimics the Amazon blackwater this species comes from, tributaries of the Rio Negro in particular, where wild pH commonly runs 6.0 to 6.5 and drops as low as 4 in certain blackwater spots, with very soft water overall. Hard tap water often needs an RO or RO/DI unit to bring it into a workable range for discus, since chasing pH down chemically without addressing hardness tends to be unstable. Straight RO has almost no GH or KH, so it gets [remineralized or blended back with tap](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before discus go in, or the pH has nothing holding it steady. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">test kit</AffiliateLink> is the only reliable way to confirm your actual numbers rather than guess at them.

<FunFact>
The pH range recommended for keeping discus, roughly 6.0 to 7.0, is already on the acidic side for a community tank. Their native water is more extreme than that. In the blackwater creeks and flooded forests off the Rio Negro where wild discus actually live, pH commonly sits around 6.0 to 6.5 and can drop as low as 4 in some spots, water soft enough to carry almost no measurable hardness. Captive discus don't need conditions that extreme to thrive, but it's a useful reminder of just how far this species is from a typical hard-water community tank.
</FunFact>

## Filtration

This is a genuine point of disagreement rather than a settled answer. <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vNGY2v" product="Canister filter (Fluval 407, up to 100 gal)">Canister filters</AffiliateLink>, sized well above the tank's actual volume, are the most common modern choice and handle this species' heavy bioload from frequent feeding and large water changes well. At least one discus-specialty source pushes back on canister filters specifically, arguing they're sealed systems that compete with the fish for dissolved oxygen and can fail dangerously during a power outage, recommending a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000260FUW?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="AquaClear 70 Power Filter (40-70 gal)">hang-on-back filter</AffiliateLink> instead, or a canister paired with extra surface agitation or an air stone for supplemental oxygenation. A sponge filter is the standard choice for fry and breeding tanks, gentle enough not to pull in small fry while still providing real biological filtration. Whatever you choose, avoid under-gravel filters, decomposing waste trapped underneath is a real risk given how much this species eats and produces.

If a canister does sit without power for hours, unplug it so it cannot restart on its own and [clean it before it runs again](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/). A sealed canister that sat dead goes anoxic inside and pushes an ammonia spike into the tank the moment it restarts, and water held at discus temperatures carries less oxygen to begin with.

## Substrate

Genuinely a matter of priorities rather than one correct answer. Bare-bottom tanks are the standard choice among breeders and serious keepers specifically because they're the easiest to keep clean given how often discus tanks get water changes, nothing traps waste that then has to be vacuumed out from between gravel. A layer of fine sand is the common middle ground, more natural-looking than bare glass while still showing uneaten food clearly enough to remove before it fouls the water, unlike gravel, where food settles between pieces and is easy to miss.

## Decor and Plants

Build in cover without crowding the swimming space this tall-bodied fish needs. Driftwood arranged vertically and broad-leafed plants, Amazon sword and anubias are commonly recommended, suit this species' natural blackwater look and give a genuinely shy fish somewhere to retreat, which matters given how easily discus stress. Floating plants that soften the light from above are a worthwhile addition for the same reason.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Frequent Water Changes

This is the detail that actually defines a working discus tank more than any single equipment choice. Change 10 to 25% every week as the baseline, and let nitrate set the pace: keep it under 20 ppm in a tank without plants and under 40 ppm in a planted one, changing more water or changing it more often whenever the test creeps toward that line. A 55-gallon tank hits the limit sooner than a 75, which is why the smaller tank means more water changes. Discus tolerate less margin for error on ammonia and nitrite than a lot of hardier community fish, which is exactly why this isn't a species for an uncycled or newly established tank. Budget the [electricity and water use](/blog/discus-cost-guide/) behind this routine into your ongoing costs, it's a bigger recurring expense here than for most freshwater fish. Small, frequent meals rather than one large feeding also keep the bioload between changes more manageable.

## Growing Out Juveniles

Most discus are sold young, and a group of juveniles needs housing planned for the adults it will become. For a 75-gallon, buy 10 to 12 juveniles at once, all close to the same size so none gets outcompeted at feeding time. As they grow, the rowdier males show themselves and can go back to the store, which leaves a settled group of about six adults, mostly females. A 55-gallon can grow out a group too, at the price of noticeably more water changes.

Grow-out tanks are usually bare-bottom for the same reason breeders keep adults that way: a growing discus is fed heavily and often, and a bare floor shows every leftover for the siphon. A 2024 controlled trial by Navarro-Morales and colleagues shows what research-grade grow-out looks like. Juveniles 45 days old and about 1.3 inches long were kept 16 to a 50-liter tank (roughly 13 gallons), fed four times a day at 6% of body weight, with 80% of the water changed every day at about 84°F and pH near 6.9. Over 63 days, fish whose commercial feed was split half and half with live *Moina* or a homemade wet paste outgrew fish on commercial feed alone.

A home tank does not have to match a lab schedule. The pattern it copies is the pairing: heavy, frequent feeding goes with large, frequent water changes, and a mixed diet outgrows pellets alone.

## Lighting

A standard LED on a plug-in timer, so the day runs the same length every day, works well. Discus don't have unusual lighting needs beyond what supports any live plants in the tank, and given how easily this species startles at bright light, a fixture with a dimmable or gradual-ramp setting is a genuine nice-to-have rather than a necessity.

## The Bottom Line

None of the individual pieces here are exotic, warm water, soft acidic chemistry, strong filtration, and a real commitment to frequent water changes are all achievable with standard equipment. What trips people up is treating discus like a slightly warmer version of a typical community tank rather than accepting that the maintenance routine itself needs to be genuinely more frequent. Get that right and discus repay the routine for many years.
