# Is It Legal to Own a Leopard Gecko? State-by-State Guide

The leopard gecko has been captive-bred in the United States for long enough that the animals in shops are many generations removed from anything wild-caught, and the law mostly reflects that. It clears 46 of 52 jurisdictions with no permit and no conditions. The exceptions are worth knowing exactly, because two of them are absolute and four of them are paperwork.

## Hawaii Named This One Individually

Most state lists that restrict geckos do it by family, which leaves keepers arguing about whether a given species falls inside the group named. Hawaii removed that argument for this animal by listing it in the Gekkonidae block by scientific name: Eublepharis macularius, gecko, leopard.

The heading on that list matters as much as the entry. Part A is designated for research and exhibition, so the effect is not a permit a private keeper can apply for. A university or a federally licensed exhibitor can hold the animal. A household cannot, and no amount of captive-bred documentation changes it.

That is a different mechanism from the one that catches the [crested gecko](/blog/crested-gecko-legal-guide/), which Hawaii bars by never listing at all. Same outcome, arrived at from opposite directions.

## Maine Clears Geckos at a Rank That Survives Reclassification

Maine's Unrestricted Species List clears "all species in infraorder Gekkota", with a single named exception. Infraorder is an unusually sensible rank to write this rule at, because the geckos have been split and re-split across families in recent decades. Eublepharidae, Diplodactylidae and Gekkonidae all sit inside Gekkota, so a clearance written there survives the taxonomy moving underneath it.

The asterisk on the entry is the part to read. It removes anything the IUCN rates endangered, critically endangered or extinct in the wild. The leopard gecko is least concern, is not CITES Appendix I, and appears on neither federal list, so the asterisk passes over it. The whole reptile section also sits under a captive-bred origin heading, which is the real condition attached: a wild-caught animal of a cleared species is not cleared.

## The Two Places With No Route

Hawaii and the District of Columbia are the only jurisdictions where a private keeper has no path at all. DC gets there by omission rather than by naming the species. Section 8-1808(j)(1) bars possession of wildlife with seven exceptions, and the exceptions reach zoos, licensed exhibitors, rehabilitators, veterinarians, shelters, and goats and sheep brought in to graze. No lizard fits any of them, and there is no permit sitting behind the bar to apply for.

Nothing about the animal being small, captive-bred, or unable to survive a Washington winter changes that reading.

## West Virginia Regulates the Shipment, Not the Species

West Virginia never named the leopard gecko, and the rule that reaches it is not on any species list. W. Va. Code § 20-2-13 says that "no person shall transport into or have in his possession within this state any live wildlife or viable eggs thereof from without the state, except as authorized by an importation permit issued by the director." Wildlife is defined in § 20-1-2 as wild birds, wild animals, game and fur-bearing animals, fish, reptiles, amphibians, mollusks and crustaceans, and the reptile and amphibian clause carries no nativity qualifier and no captive-bred exception. A leopard gecko shipped in from a breeder is therefore live wildlife from without the state.

The argument that an out-of-state animal sits outside the chapter is closed before it can be made: § 20-2-4(b) says wildlife lawfully taken outside of this state is subject to the same laws and rules as wildlife taken within it. What is missing is the other half. No rule in Title 58 implements § 20-2-13 outside aquaculture, the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for an importation permit, and the only penalty the section spells out is written for coyotes. The requirement is clear on the page and invisible in practice, which is the honest thing to tell someone crossing the state line with a tank in the car. Generations of captive breeding make no difference here, because § 20-1-2 defines wildlife by taxonomic group and never mentions where an animal was hatched.
