# Halloween Pet Safety: The Candy Bowl, the Candles and the Door

<VetDisclaimer>
This article is for educational purposes only and is not veterinary advice. If your pet has eaten something it should not have, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435) or the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661) before signs appear.
</VetDisclaimer>

**A dog does not need to see the candy bowl.** It can smell a bag of fun-size chocolate through a cabinet door, so the candy goes up high or behind a latch, not just out of sight. A kitten will test a candle flame with a paw. A rabbit or guinea pig hears forty doorbells in an evening and reads every one as a predator at the burrow mouth. A cockatiel two rooms from a scented pumpkin candle is breathing the same air as the person who lit it. Halloween is one night, but it stacks more of the ordinary household hazards into one evening than any other date on the calendar.

The animals most at risk are the ones with the least to do with the holiday: the pets sitting in the house while it happens around them.

<KeyTakeaway>
The one rule that covers every hazard below: call before symptoms appear. Xylitol can drop a dog's blood sugar within 10 to 60 minutes, chocolate signs usually start 6 to 12 hours after eating, and pancreatitis from a fatty binge can take two to four days. A pet that looks fine an hour after the candy bowl went over has not been cleared. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435) and the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661) both answer 24 hours a day, both may charge a consultation fee, and both want the wrapper, a rough amount, the time and the animal's weight.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Candy Bowl

Four separate problems live in a bowl of Halloween candy, and they do not behave alike.

### Chocolate

Chocolate poisons dogs and cats through two related stimulants, theobromine and caffeine, together called methylxanthines. Theobromine is the larger share, and dogs clear it slowly: its half-life in a dog is 17.5 hours, so a large dose keeps working through the next day. Dogs account for most cases because they eat without judgment and can reach what cats ignore.

How much matters depends entirely on which chocolate. The darker and less sweet, the more methylxanthine per ounce.

Mild signs begin at roughly 20 mg per kilogram of body weight, about 9 mg per pound. Effects on the heart start at 40 to 50 mg per kilogram, and seizures at 60 mg per kilogram and above. Put those together and a 22 pound dog reaches the mild-signs line on a little over 3 ounces of milk chocolate and on under half an ounce of baking chocolate. About 1 ounce of milk chocolate per pound of body weight is considered potentially lethal.

Signs usually begin 6 to 12 hours after the chocolate goes down: thirst, vomiting, diarrhea, a swollen belly and restlessness first, then hyperactivity, a racing or irregular heartbeat, tremors and seizures. The delay is the trap. An owner who finds the empty bag at 7 pm and sees a normal dog at 9 pm has learned nothing yet.

White chocolate is almost free of theobromine, but it is mostly fat and sugar, and a big enough fatty binge of any candy can set off pancreatitis, which may not show for two to four days.

### Xylitol

Xylitol is the sugar substitute in sugar-free gum, breath mints, "keto" and diabetic-friendly candy, some protein bars, chewable vitamins, toothpaste and some peanut and nut butters. In a dog it is absorbed far faster than in a person and triggers a surge of insulin out of all proportion to what a human pancreas releases. Blood sugar can crash within 10 to 60 minutes. The sequence runs vomiting, then weakness, staggering, collapse and seizures, and a dog that has eaten xylitol may need 12 to 24 hours of hospital monitoring because some of the serious effects arrive late.

Cats do not appear to react to xylitol the way dogs do. Ferrets do: they can develop low blood sugar and seizures after xylitol products, which matters in a house where a ferret has the run of the floor during a party. Ferrets already carry a high rate of [insulinoma](/blog/ferret-health-issues-guide/), a tumor that drives blood sugar down on its own, so a xylitol crash lands on an animal that may have no reserve.

The peanut butter line is the one most owners miss. Some nut butters contain xylitol, and peanut butter is the standard way to hide a pill. Check the label of the jar you already own.

### Raisins and grapes

There is no known safe amount of grape or raisin for a dog. A small handful has caused kidney failure in some dogs while others eat more and seem fine, and for years nobody knew why. The leading explanation now is tartaric acid: ASPCA toxicologists connected a dog with acute kidney injury after eating homemade play dough made with cream of tartar, which is a form of tartaric acid, to cases in their database involving tamarinds and grapes, and the kidney lesions matched. Tartaric acid in grapes runs anywhere from 0.35% to 2% depending on the variety and ripeness, which is why one box of raisins is survivable and the next is not. Chocolate-covered raisins pack two poisons into one treat. Signs are vomiting, nausea, refusing food, lethargy and belly pain.

### Wrappers, sticks and the rest

Foil, cellophane and lollipop sticks go down with the candy, and in the gut they can cause a bowel obstruction that needs surgery. Vomiting, going off food, straining or not passing stool in the days after Halloween are the signs. If the bag is short several wrappers, assume they were swallowed along with the candy.

One more thing about the bowl itself: a candy haul big enough to upset a dog's stomach is big enough to be a pancreatitis case even with no chocolate in it.

## Candles, Glow Sticks and Decorations

A lit jack-o'-lantern on the floor is a candle in a paper bag at tail height. A dog's tail or a cat's leap can knock it over and start a fire, and kittens, who test everything with a paw, get burned or singed by candle flame. Battery tea lights make the same pumpkin with none of that. The pumpkin has a second life as a hazard once the night is over: left out until it molds, it can grow mycotoxins that cause tremors and seizures, so the jack-o'-lantern goes in the trash before it softens.

Scented candles are a different hazard for a different animal. A bird's one-way respiratory system makes it vulnerable to fumes long before a person notices them, and votive candles, wax melts and plug-in air fresheners are on the list of things that irritate or poison caged birds in an ordinary home, the same list as an overheated [nonstick pan](/blog/bird-household-hazards-guide/). A pumpkin-spice candle burning in the living room reaches a cockatiel in the next room. On a night with the front door opening every few minutes, the simplest move is to leave the scented candles unlit until November.

Glow sticks and glow jewelry are mostly a cat problem. The liquid inside is bitter and causes mouth pain, heavy drooling and foaming, and cats make up most of the glow stick calls to the Pet Poison Helpline because they bite the sticks and then groom the liquid off their fur. It is usually not life-threatening, but it looks alarming, and the helpline wants the call so they can talk you through it.

The decoration box holds three more. Chewed batteries, from light-up props and string lights, cause corrosive burns to the mouth and digestive tract, and a swallowed battery is a same-hour call. Dry ice in a punch bowl burns on contact. Fur dyes and sprays labeled non-toxic for people can still harm a pet that licks them off, so the only dye that touches a pet is one made for pets.

## Costumes

Do not put a dog or cat in a costume unless you know the animal loves it. If it does, the costume has four rules: it must not restrict movement, vision, breathing, barking or meowing; it must have no small, dangling or easily chewed pieces; it gets tried on days ahead so a bad fit shows before the night; and the animal wearing it is never left on its own. Metal beads, snaps and bells on costumes can contain zinc or lead, both serious poisonings if swallowed.

A pet that freezes, scratches at the outfit, tries to back out of it or goes to hide is not enjoying the costume. The fallback is a festive bandana. Human costume pieces count too: a dropped vampire fang or a plastic pirate hook is a choking hazard the moment it hits the floor.

## The Door

For most of the evening the front door is the hazard. It opens dozens of times onto strangers in masks, and a dog that bolts or a cat that slips past a leg is outside at night on the busiest pedestrian evening of the year.

All but the most social dogs and cats should spend peak trick-or-treating hours in a separate, comfortable room away from the front door, with a favorite toy or a food puzzle to work on. Cats stay indoors. Any pet that leaves the house after dark goes out on a secure leash wearing a reflective collar and ID. Check that the microchip registry has your current phone number before the night, because a tag can come off and a chip cannot.

The quiet room matters more for prey animals than for dogs. A rabbit or guinea pig does not understand a doorbell as a doorbell. It understands a loud noise followed by strange voices, forty times, and it responds the way a prey animal does, by freezing and refusing to eat. A rabbit that stops eating is on a clock: no food and no droppings for 8 to 12 hours is [GI stasis](/blog/rabbit-health-issues-guide/) territory, an emergency, and stress is one of the things that starts it. The room for the rabbit is the farthest one from the door, with a hide box and the hay topped up, and someone checks that the hay is going down before bed.

A dog that paces, pants or hides from the doorbell is not being difficult. That behavior is [anxiety with a cause](/blog/when-your-pet-wont-stop-pacing-or-hiding-what-anxious-pet-behavior-is-really-telling-you/), and the fix on the night is distance from the trigger, a closed door, a radio and something to chew, not a reprimand.

## What To Do First

If a pet eats something from any list above, the order is the same every time.

1. **Take the food away** and pick up what is left so no second animal gets it.
2. **Keep the wrapper or packaging.** The poison lines assess by product, and the label settles whether that gum had xylitol or that bar was dark or milk.
3. **Estimate how much is missing**, roughly. Count the empty wrappers against the bag.
4. **Note the time and the animal's weight.** Both decide what dose it took and how long the window is.
5. **Call now, before signs.** Your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661. Both are open 24 hours a day, every day. The ASPCA line may charge a consultation fee, and the Pet Poison Helpline charges a one-time per-incident fee that covers every follow-up call with you and your vet.
6. **Do not make the animal vomit** unless the person on the line or your vet tells you to.

Save both numbers in your phone tonight, under a name you will find in a hurry. The call costs less than an emergency visit, and the people answering it have the product database and the dose tables in front of them, which is the part no owner can do from the kitchen floor at 9 pm with a dog who looks, for now, completely fine.
