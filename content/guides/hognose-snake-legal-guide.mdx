# Is It Legal to Own a Hognose Snake? State-by-State Guide

Hognose snakes get flagged by first-time keepers for one reason: they're mildly venomous, in the sense that they have small rear fangs and a weak venom evolved to subdue toads. That's led to a persistent assumption that they must fall under the same permits as cobras or vipers. In practice, every venomous-reptile law we checked draws its line well above hognose. The real legal wrinkles are elsewhere.

### The "Venomous Snake" Myth

A real medical case report on a keeper bitten by a Western hognose during feeding documented local swelling, bruising, and mild cellulitis, with a full recovery in about five months and no systemic effects, which is [what a bite from this species actually looks like](/blog/hognose-snake-handling-guide/). That's consistent with how state law treats the species: Florida's wildlife agency states outright that rear-fanged colubrids like hognose "are not regulated as venomous" in the state, and Alabama, Kentucky, and Louisiana all explicitly name Heterodon as an exception to their venomous-reptile bans. Even a 2025 South Carolina bill to expand venomous-reptile regulation was drafted to exclude hognose.

Alabama is worth a second look, because clearing the venomous rule is not the same as clearing the state. 220-2-.26(1) does except "hognose snakes (genus Heterodon)" from its ban on venomous reptiles that have never naturally existed in Alabama, and that is the clause the exemption lists all quote. A different rule then protects one of the three hognoses: 220-2-.92(1)(c) lists the southern hognose, *Heterodon simus*, among the nongame species nobody may possess without a scientific collection permit or a written permit from the Commissioner. The eastern hognose, *H. platirhinos*, is an Alabama animal and is not on that list, and the western, *H. nasicus*, is the pet-trade species and is not native there at all. So in Alabama the answer turns on which hognose you have, which is exactly the kind of thing a venomous-reptile carve-out will not tell you.

### Georgia's Real Exception

Georgia is the clearest genuine restriction here. Its Department of Natural Resources lists Eastern and Southern hognose under species that "may not be held as a pet," and states the restriction applies regardless of the animal's origin or morphology, meaning captive breeding doesn't create an exception the way it does in most other states.

### Colorado's New Split Rule

<FunFact>
Colorado's 2026 herpetofauna overhaul (Chapter W-7, effective May 1, 2026) legalized possessing, selling, and transporting captive-bred Western and Plains hognose with proof of origin, but pointedly excluded Eastern hognose from that legalization. At the same time, wild collection of any native Colorado hognose is now banned outright. It's a clean, current example of the captive-bred-versus-wild-collected distinction that runs through hognose law generally.
</FunFact>

## What's Still Unverified

California is frequently cited on aggregator sites as requiring a hognose permit, but we couldn't confirm this against the state's actual restricted-species list, and the format of that claim doesn't match how California's list is normally structured. Texas is settled now, and it turned out not to be a venom question at all. The state's controlled exotic snake rule reaches six named constrictors and venomous species that are not native to Texas, and a hognose is neither. What does reach it is the nongame rule at 31 TAC 65.331(d), which names both the western and the eastern hognose, so a Texas keeper may hold 25 of a species without a permit and may not sell, offer for sale, exchange, or barter any of them. The cap follows captive-bred animals too, because 31 TAC 65.325(a) defines nongame wildlife to include them. Treat California alone as the open question, and confirm it with the state agency rather than an aggregator.

## The Takeaway

### West Virginia Is the Second Ownership Ban

Georgia is not the only place hognose ownership is off the table. West Virginia gets there by a different route and reaches further, because its rule is a permitted list rather than a prohibited one. W. Va. C.S.R. § 58-73-1.1 prohibits "the take or possession of any native West Virginia reptile or amphibian species not listed hereafter," and section 4 lists ten salamanders, two frogs and two turtles. No snake is on it.

The Eastern Hog-nosed Snake, Heterodon platirhinos, is on WVDNR's checklist of the state's twenty-two native snakes, so it is inside the prohibition. The department's summary of the rule puts the total at 80 species of reptiles and amphibians whose possession the regulations bar. Nothing here turns on the venom question that decides this species everywhere else: West Virginia is not treating the hognose as dangerous, it is treating it as its own wildlife.

If you're getting a hognose snake, the venomous-reptile angle almost never applies to you, no matter where you live. Georgia and West Virginia are the two states where hognose ownership itself is off the table. Colorado owners of Eastern hognose specifically should be aware their subspecies wasn't part of the 2026 legalization, and Massachusetts draws the same line harder: its exemption list clears the Western hognose by name and its special-concern listing puts the Eastern one in a permit tier the state says it will not open for a pet, captive-bred or not. Everywhere else, this is one of the more broadly legal reptiles on this list.
