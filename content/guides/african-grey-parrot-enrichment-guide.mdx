# African Grey Enrichment: Foraging Is the One With Real Evidence

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most enrichment advice is plausible. Parrot foraging research is something rarer: an intervention with a measured outcome on a condition owners actually search for.

<KeyTakeaway>
Eighteen feather-plucking African greys were fed through pipe feeders instead of bowls in a controlled crossover study, scored on a ten-point plumage system. In Amazon parrots, foraging opportunity given from weaning prevented psychogenic feather picking from developing at all.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Foraging Studies Found

Two studies tested foraging in this exact species. Lumeij and Hommers assigned **18 pterotillomanic African greys** to experimental and control groups in a crossover design across two one-month periods. The experimental group received food in pipe feeders; controls received a bowl, with two empty pipe feeders present so the cage furniture matched. Plumage was scored with Meehan's ten-point system. Separately, van Zeeland and colleagues tested how effectively different foraging enrichments increased actual foraging time in greys.

Behind both sits the foundational work. Meehan, Millam and Mench gave young Orange-winged Amazons foraging opportunity and increased physical complexity, and found both **prevented and reduced** psychogenic feather picking. Foraging-based enrichment was preferred over non-foraging physical enrichment such as plastic toys.

<FunFact>
The mechanism is arithmetic. A parrot given food in a bowl finishes in minutes and has the rest of the day to fill. In the wild that time goes to searching, extracting and processing food. Feather destruction is what fills the vacuum when the searching is deleted.
</FunFact>

The honest limit: prevention in young Amazons is a stronger result than treatment in established adult pluckers, and plucking has medical causes as well as behavioral ones.

Start easy. A bird that has always been bowl-fed does not automatically know that food is inside a device, and the first stage is often food sitting visibly on top of a foraging toy before any of it goes inside. Increase difficulty as competence grows, and weigh the bird while you do it.

Rotate. A puzzle that has been solved and left up for a month is furniture.

## Destructible Material

For a parrot, shredding is closer to foraging than to play. Material that survives the week has failed at its job. Softwood, palm, paper and untreated natural fiber all give a grey something to dismantle, which is both an outlet and a way to deliver food inside something that has to be destroyed first.

## Training, and What It Is Evidence For

Worth its own section, and worth being precise about the claim.

The parrot training literature is not about feather destruction. It is about **voluntary participation in husbandry and veterinary care**. Laboratory macaws trained with positive reinforcement came to take part in veterinary procedures voluntarily, which the authors framed as reducing the stress of capture and restraint. In a more recent case, two scarlet macaws given **ten minute sessions once a day, four to six times a week for four months** learned to follow a target, enter a carrier, stay in it, step onto a scale, step onto a towel and drink from a syringe, and the behaviors transferred to handlers who had not done the training.

Those were macaws, so treat it as a borrow within the parrots. What it supports is a specific and unglamorous claim: a bird that will step onto a scale is a bird that gets weighed, and weight is usually the first thing to change when a parrot is becoming unwell.

What this is **not** is a second treatment for plucking alongside foraging. The nearest evidence for that is a [sulphur-crested cockatoo study](/blog/cockatoo-enrichment-guide/) where training outperformed medication and feeding enrichment, and that is a different species again. Foraging is still the one with direct evidence in greys.

## Priority Order

1. Food delivered through foraging devices rather than a bowl
2. Enough daily out-of-cage time and interaction, which for this species is not optional
3. Varied perch diameters and materials
4. Destructible shreddable material, rotated
5. Short daily training sessions, aimed at cooperative care
6. Bathing opportunity
7. Novel objects, which the Amazon study found were preferred less than foraging

## What Not to Do

Do not assume plucking is behavioral before a vet has ruled out the medical causes. Do not leave the same foraging toy up indefinitely and count it as enrichment. Do not make the first foraging device difficult: a bird that cannot solve it eats less. Do not treat plastic toys as equivalent to foraging, because the Amazon study directly compared them and foraging won. And do not use cage size as a substitute for occupation, since a large cage with a food bowl in it still produces a bird with nothing to do.
