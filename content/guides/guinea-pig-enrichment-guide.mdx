# Guinea Pig Enrichment: Hideouts, Foraging, and Floor Time

A guinea pig is a prey animal first. Enrichment that ignores that, novelty for its own sake, misses what actually lowers stress and keeps them active: reliable cover and food that takes some work to find. For everything else about keeping one, see our complete [Guinea Pig care guide](/guides/guinea-pig/).

<KeyTakeaway>
Hiding places aren't a decoration, they're what lets a prey animal feel safe enough to relax, explore, and forage in the first place. A guinea pig with nowhere to retreat stays on alert even in a completely calm room.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Hideouts and Cover

Constant access to a secure hiding place is close to a baseline need, not an extra. PVC pipes, hide huts, and fleece tunnels all work, and a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F37K1Z91?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Small Pet Select Tiny Paws Seagrass Play Pack">seagrass hideout or tunnel</AffiliateLink> adds a chewable option in the same footprint. In a cage with more than one guinea pig, provide multiple hides so a lower-ranking animal always has somewhere to go, a single hide in a shared space tends to become something one animal guards rather than something everyone can use.

## Foraging Comes First

Wild guinea pigs can spend up to 80% of the day foraging and feeding, most actively in the morning and evening. That behavior is one of the most restricted in a pet guinea pig fed from a single bowl. Scatter hay across the enclosure instead of piling it in one corner, and hide small amounts of chopped vegetables in a foraging mat or tunnel rather than placing them in a dish. Since hay should already be unlimited and fresh in the diet, spreading it out does double duty as both nutrition and enrichment.

<FunFact>
Hiding places for retreat aren't just about physical safety, research on captive guinea pig welfare links secure hiding options directly to reduced stress indicators, on top of whatever protection they offer from an actual threat.
</FunFact>

## Chew and Gnaw Items

Guinea pig teeth grow continuously, so gnawing is both a physical need and a natural behavior worth supporting directly. Untreated wood chews, hay-based chew sticks, and cardboard tunnels all hold up to regular use. Rotate a few options rather than relying on one, guinea pigs vary in what they'll actually engage with.

## Supervised Floor Time

Even a large cage can't fully replace open floor space. Supervised time in a guinea-pig-proofed room or playpen lets them run, popcorn, and investigate new smells and textures, all real enrichment beyond what the enclosure itself offers. Keep the space free of cords, gaps they could squeeze into, and anything toxic, and always supervise directly rather than leaving them unattended outside the cage.

## Reading the Warning Signs

A guinea pig that stays hidden constantly, shows little interest in food or its surroundings, or develops repetitive bar-chewing is signaling that something in the current setup isn't working, whether that's a missing hide, not enough foraging opportunity, or an underlying health issue. Since illness can produce some of the same withdrawal, a vet check is worth pairing with any enrichment changes.
