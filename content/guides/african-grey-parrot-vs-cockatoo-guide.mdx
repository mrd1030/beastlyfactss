# African Grey Parrot vs. Cockatoo: Which Big Parrot Commitment Fits You

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Both are large, long-lived, exceptionally intelligent parrots that get recommended to experienced bird owners and almost no one else, and for good reason. The real differences come down to talking ability, how physically clingy each one is, and just how loud your home is prepared to get.

## Talking Ability Isn't Close

If conversation is what draws you to a parrot, this comparison has a clear answer. African greys are widely considered the single best-talking parrot species that exists, a reputation cemented by Alex, the grey studied for over 30 years by Dr. Irene Pepperberg, who built a working vocabulary past 150 words and demonstrated genuine understanding of concepts like color, shape, and quantity, not just mimicry. Cockatoos can pick up some words, but they're chosen overwhelmingly for personality and affection rather than vocabulary.

<FunFact>
Alex the African grey, the parrot at the center of Dr. Pepperberg's decades-long research, could reportedly identify colors, shapes, and quantities up to six, and understood the concept of "zero" well before he died in 2007, a level of abstract reasoning researchers had rarely documented in a non-primate.
</FunFact>

<KeyTakeaway>
Choose an African Grey if real conversational ability is the priority and you don't need a bird that's touching you all day. You get the most cognitively advanced talker in the parrot world, loud but not the loudest bird in the room, in exchange for a more reserved, independent personality than a cockatoo offers.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Affection Level Is Almost Reversed

Cockatoos are famous, almost notorious, for how intensely they bond to their person, earning the nickname "velcro bird" for wanting to be in physical contact for hours a day. That's not a small ask, it's a defining feature of the species that shapes an owner's entire daily schedule. African greys bond just as deeply in their own way, but they're generally more reserved and observant, content with real interaction without demanding constant physical touch the way a cockatoo does.

<KeyTakeaway>
A Cockatoo is the better fit if you want a parrot that wants to be touching you for hours a day and you can build your schedule around that. If you can't offer that much direct contact, the same bond that makes cockatoos so affectionate is what drives the screaming and feather plucking they're most notorious for.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Noise: One Is Loud, the Other Is Louder

Both species can vocalize loudly, but cockatoos are widely regarded as the loudest of the commonly kept pet parrots, capable of screams that genuinely carry through walls and floors, especially when bored or seeking attention. ## Feather-Destructive Behavior Is a Real Risk for Both

This isn't a minor footnote for either species, it's one of the defining welfare concerns avian vets see in captive large parrots. An under-stimulated bird of either species can develop feather plucking or outright self-mutilation, and cockatoos are especially well documented for it given how much daily interaction they need to feel secure. ## The Lifespan Commitment Spans Generations

Both species commonly live 40 to 60 years, with some cockatoo species cited even longer. That's not just "a long time for a pet," it's long enough that a bird bought as a young adult can realistically need to be rehomed to a family member, friend, or dedicated parrot rescue decades down the line. Anyone considering either bird should have at least a loose plan for what happens to it after they no longer can provide care.

## Bottom Line

Pick an African grey if exceptional talking ability and a slightly more independent, lower-noise temperament matter most to you. Pick a cockatoo if you want the most physically affectionate, people-oriented parrot commonly kept, and can genuinely offer hours of daily contact along with tolerance for serious noise. Neither is a casual pet or a good first bird, both demand an experienced, deeply committed owner for decades.
