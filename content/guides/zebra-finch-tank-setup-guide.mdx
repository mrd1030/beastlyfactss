# Zebra Finch Cage Setup: The Pair Comes Before the Cage

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cage size and bar spacing matter, but the setup decision that determines whether a zebra finch thrives is how many birds go into it, never just one.

## The Non-Negotiable Part: A Pair or Small Group, Never Solo

This has to come before cage dimensions, because it changes what the cage is actually for. Zebra finches are a [flock-oriented species](/blog/zebra-finch-enrichment-guide/), in the wild they live in flocks of dozens up to around 100 birds, and in captivity that means a bird that should never be housed alone without a compelling veterinary reason. Long-term solo housing isn't a design choice here the way it can be for, say, a solo [canary](/blog/canary-tank-setup-guide/), it's a welfare problem.

Plan the cage size, perch layout, and even the number of feeding stations around at least two birds from the very start.

## Cage Size and Shape

A pair needs at least 24 inches long by 14 inches deep by 18 inches tall. Treat that as a floor, not a target, since more length is genuinely better. Communal aviary housing is the ideal setup, and where space is limited, the compromise is on width, with vertical height and length kept generous to allow free flight. The width given up there is depth, the front-to-back measurement: the side-to-side length is the flight path and the one dimension not to cut. Zebra finches fly rather than climb, so a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4wuT808" product="Large bird flight cage">long flight cage</AffiliateLink> that lets the birds cross the length of it in real flight suits this species far better than a tall, narrow cage built with a climbing bird in mind, the same principle that applies to canaries.

## Bar Spacing

No wider than 3/8 inch. That's tighter than the roughly 1/2 inch that works for a canary, since zebra finches are a genuinely smaller bird, and wider spacing carries a real risk of a head getting caught or a bird squeezing through entirely.

## Temperature

Normal household temperature, 65 to 80°F, works well for zebra finches, the same comfortable range that works for canaries. Keep the cage away from drafts, air conditioning vents, and direct sun. No supplemental heat is needed indoors.

## Light and Dark Cycle

Pet birds generally settle into a rhythm of approximately 12 hours of light and 12 hours of darkness each day, with natural daylight or full-spectrum artificial lighting during the day. A consistent daily schedule matters more than hitting an exact hour count, covering the cage at a predictable time each evening (or moving it to a dark, quiet room) is a simple way to keep that rhythm steady.

<FunFact>
Zebra finches breed opportunistically, timed to rainfall in the wild rather than a fixed season, and a [stable light cycle](/blog/bird-photoperiod-sleep-guide/) at home can inadvertently cue breeding behavior in a mixed-sex group. That's worth knowing if you're not intending to breed, since a hen that lays eggs regularly faces a real, ongoing calcium demand.
</FunFact>

Keep the overall furnishing simple, this is a bird that needs open flight space more than a cage full of toys.

## Substrate and Cage Lining

A paper liner on the cage floor, spot-cleaned daily and fully changed weekly, is a low-maintenance standard that also makes it easy to monitor droppings for early signs of illness, worth doing given how well this species hides being sick.
