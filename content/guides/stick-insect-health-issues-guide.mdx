# Common Stick Insect Health Issues (Molting Is the Biggest Risk)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Stick insects are hardy, and nearly everything that goes wrong traces back to one of two opposite mistakes: an enclosure too dry, or one too wet. Getting that balance right is most of what this species' health actually depends on.

## Molting Failure (Dysecdysis)

This is the single biggest risk for a pet stick insect. Watch for a shed that doesn't complete properly, resulting in deformity or death. It's caused by insufficient vertical space, since a stick insect needs clear height of at least three times its adult body length to hang freely while shedding, or by humidity that's too low leading up to the molt. An Indian stick insect molts five times on the way to adulthood, so a nymph faces this risk five separate times. This isn't something to treat after the fact, prevention through correct enclosure height and misting, tracked with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3QHrM81" product="Digital thermometer/hygrometer combo">digital hygrometer</AffiliateLink> rather than guesswork, is really the only effective approach.

When a molt is under way, the answer to whether to step in is no. A stick insect hanging from the roof is not touched while it sheds, since the new skin is soft for hours afterward and handling tears it. What a keeper can do is move tankmates out of reach, because other insects knock legs off a molting one, and keep humidity steady. One that stays stuck half out of its old skin with no progress is a case for an experienced keeper or an exotic vet, never an untrained attempt to pull it free, the same rule the [molting guide for pet invertebrates](/blog/invertebrate-molting-guide/) sets for every species it covers.

<FunFact>
A stick insect molts by hanging from the top of its enclosure, and if the space beneath it is too short or the humidity too low in the days before, the result can be a permanently deformed or fatal molt.
</FunFact>

## Dehydration and Drowning Risk

Two opposite problems worth knowing about together. Dehydration comes from insufficient misting. Most stick insects drink water droplets rather than from a dish, so a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vPKMjX" product="Fine mist spray bottle">spray bottle</AffiliateLink> and a thorough misting of the enclosure and food plants each evening prevents it. Humidity needs vary widely: the Indian stick insect is happy in a fairly open cage at room temperature from about 63°F, while some tropical species need a nearly closed cage at around 80% humidity and warmth near 77°F. Tap water harms some species in some areas, so rainwater or tap water left to stand for a day or two is the safer choice. Drowning, meanwhile, is a real risk for small nymphs if you're using an open water reservoir to keep cut host-plant foliage fresh, cover or otherwise secure any standing water.

## Mold and Fungal or Bacterial Infection

From an enclosure kept too damp combined with poor ventilation or infrequent cleaning. Prevented with good airflow and a regular substrate change, roughly weekly is a reasonable habit. Fungal infection is also one of the common causes of lost legs. When it shows up, clean the whole enclosure thoroughly and move any affected insects into a separate one. Eggs mold too: any egg with visible mold comes out, gets cleaned, and goes into its own container.

## Leg Loss

Not a disease, but worth including here since it's a common outcome of three things: rough handling, fungal infection, and overcrowding, where cagemates bite or knock off each other's legs, most often during a molt. A stick insect can regrow a lost leg only at its next molt, so a nymph may partially regrow one while an adult's lost leg is permanent. Some species, the pink-winged stick insect among them, drop legs far more easily than others. Room to molt and gentle, body-only handling are what prevent it.

## What a Vet Visit Looks Like

Few vets see insects, so a vet visit for a stick insect is the exception. It makes sense for a molt stuck with no progress, fungal growth that keeps spreading after the enclosure has been cleaned, or losses across a colony with no husbandry explanation. The [Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians directory](https://arav.org/find-a-vet/) lists exotic vets by region, and some treat invertebrates. Call first and ask whether the practice sees insects.

Expect the visit to center on husbandry. Bring the enclosure's temperature and humidity readings, the food plant and where it came from, and dates of recent molts and losses, since most answers for this species sit in those numbers. The other thing a vet offers is a humane end for an insect that cannot recover. Veterinary guidance for invertebrates puts anesthesia first, usually an inhaled anesthetic, with any second step such as freezing used only once the animal is unconscious.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Low humidity combined with insufficient enclosure height causes the molting failures that are this species' biggest risk. Excess dampness with poor airflow invites mold instead. And rough handling and crowding cause preventable leg loss. Balance and gentleness, more than any complex intervention, are the whole story with keeping stick insects healthy.

The same balance runs through [praying mantis health](/blog/praying-mantis-health-issues-guide/): a failed molt from dryness and mold from excess dampness sit at the top of that list too.
