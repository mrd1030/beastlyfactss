# Cardinal Tetra Tank Setup: A 20-Gallon Long, Warm Soft Water, and Dim Light

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cardinal tetras are native to the upper Orinoco basin and the Negro River in the Amazon basin, where they live in forest streams and minor tributaries with slowly moving water. In the Rio Negro that water is soft, dim and stained brownish by tannins and humic substances released by decomposing organic matter, the same kind of blackwater country many neon tetras come from. In the Orinoco the species turns up more often in clear-water habitats over sand. Wild cardinals move through flooded forest and over litter-covered stream beds in groups of a few dozen. This species just runs a few degrees warmer than the neon.

## Tank Size: A 20-Gallon Long for the School of Ten

A 10-gallon tank is too small for the school of 10 this species needs. The recommended base is at least 60x30 cm (24x12 inches), about 14 gallons, which lines up with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">20-gallon long tank</AffiliateLink>, and that is the practical minimum for a school of at least 8 to 10. Schooling behavior plays out horizontally, so favor a longer tank over a taller one.

## Temperature: 77 to 82°F Day to Day

73 to 84°F is the range for this species, with 77 to 82°F as the everyday target, and cardinal tetra tolerates water reaching 30°C (86°F) in short spells, warmer than the roughly 25°C (77°F) ceiling neon tetra is comfortable with. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">heater</AffiliateLink> is necessary for any tank this size, and keeping the temperature stable matters as much as hitting the right number.

The short-spell tolerance comes from short tests. In lab tolerance testing on small wild juveniles, every fish survived 96 hours at about 88°F (31°C). The same testing found the real danger at the other end: deaths climbed once the water fell below about 19.6°C (67°F), and every fish died at 59°F (15°C). A failed heater in a cool room is a bigger threat to this fish than a warm summer day.

## Water Chemistry: Soft, Acidic and Low in Minerals

Cardinal tetras want soft, acidic water, and wild-caught specimens do best toward the more acidic end. Aim for roughly pH 5.0 to 7.0 for captive-bred stock, and toward the low end of that range for wild-caught fish, at a hardness of 1 to 12 dGH, again toward the lower end for wild fish. It's worth testing your source water before assuming your setup is automatically suitable.

The water these fish are caught from is softer and more acidic still. Across 14 collection sites along the middle Rio Negro, the pH ran from 3.35 to 5.82, with only one site above 5.0, and conductivity sat between about 8 and 23 µS/cm, water with very few dissolved minerals in it.

Hold that chemistry with a [weekly 25 to 30% change](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/), testing nitrate first: under 20 ppm the routine is right, and past 40 ppm the next change is 50%. The new water goes in dechlorinated, at tank temperature and close to the tank's chemistry, which in a soft-water tank means mixing the change water the same way the tank was first filled. Always use a water conditioner on tap water before it goes in the tank, dechlorinating and neutralizing chloramine and heavy metals that are otherwise harmful to fish.

## Filtration: Gentle Flow

Keep the flow gentle. This species' native habitat is slow-moving forest streams full of fallen branches and leaf litter, not open water with strong current, and wild cardinals are netted from the low-flow parts of those streams. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> is a common, effective choice, and a hang-on-back filter works too if you can baffle or reduce its output.

## Substrate: A Soft-Water Floor Under Leaf Litter

A cardinal is a predator that browses small prey off the surfaces of submerged leaves, wood and plants. The detritus that turns up in its stomach comes in with that prey, and the fish does not dig. Fine gravel or sand suits them, the same as for the closely related [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-tank-setup-guide/), and sand is the natural fit: a biotope tank for this species is sand with driftwood roots and branches, and the Orinoco's clear-water cardinal habitats have sandy beds. Colors turn lighter over a pale substrate or background in clear water and bright light, so a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0018CLX3C?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Seachem Flourite Black Sand, 7 kg (15.4 lb)">black sand</AffiliateLink> keeps the fish looking deep. That is a color effect; it is not a stress treatment.

The floor must be inert to leave soft water soft. Even standard pea gravel carries enough calcium to raise pH and hardness, coral sand pushes the pH past 8.0, outside every cardinal range, and calcareous rock such as limestone does the same. About 1 inch of sand is plenty; a bed planted into runs 2 inches or more of gravel, with root tabs beside rooted plants or an acidic aquasoil in its place. The setup price covers about 30 lb of sand, roughly an inch across a 20 gallon long; a 2-inch bed for rooted plants takes about 60 lb, double the sand in the setup price. Dried leaf litter and alder cones go on top, where the microbes on the decaying leaves become a secondary food for fry.

## Plants: Shade From Ferns, Moss and Floaters

The plants that suit cardinals are the ones that make shade: java fern (Microsorum), moss such as Taxiphyllum, Cryptocoryne, and floating water sprite (Ceratopteris). Pair them with dense midground live plants to dim the light further and give the school somewhere to retreat.

## Lighting: Dim, Measured in Lux

Keep the light dim. This species comes from shaded, densely vegetated streams.

The study held cardinals at five light levels, from darkness up to 2,700 lux, for up to 72 hours. Under intense light the black and neon stripes paled as the dark pigment cells in them thinned out, while the red stripe grew stronger. At 0 to 250 lux those pigment cells multiplied in the black and neon stripes and the color came out more vibrant. Dim light under floating plants, roughly 8 to 10 hours a day, suits them.

## Tankmates: Schooling Fish for Warm, Soft Water

A school of at least 8 to 10 cardinals is the base, and other small schooling species in the tank add to its sense of security. Every companion has to live in the cardinal's water too: warm, soft and acidic, at 77 to 82°F, a band a neon tank only touches at its very top.

## Blackwater or Clear Water

Cardinals live in both. Most cardinals on sale in the UK are captive-bred, though wild-caught fish are available there too, and the species tolerates water up to pH 7.5. Still, this is the detail that takes a cardinal tetra tank from adequate to great.

<AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">Driftwood</AffiliateLink> and Indian almond leaves release natural tannins that stain the water and lower pH, mimicking the blackwater habitat of the Rio Negro, the same fallen branches and leaf litter that define its native streams. They only move the pH where the KH is already low. Natural peat is not needed for any of this: its collection is unsustainable and environmentally destructive.

A soft, acidic tank has little buffer, so the same weekly change that holds its chemistry also keeps the tint from deepening and the pH from drifting past where they were set.

## Cycle First, Then Lower the pH

A new cardinal tank is [cycled](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) at the tap water's own pH and hardness, and only acidified afterward. A cycle takes about four to six weeks, and the bacteria that run it need a stable pH and enough carbonate hardness. A drop in either can stall them, and stripping the KH and pH out of the water with RO water, wood and leaves on day one is exactly that drop. So the chemistry change waits until ammonia and nitrite both read zero, and then the RO blend that softens the water goes first, with the wood and leaves after it.

Ammonia and nitrite both need to read zero before stocking, and nitrite is the reading to watch with this fish. In lab testing, 1.1 mg/L of nitrite killed half the cardinals within 96 hours, and 2.0 mg/L killed every one. A significant share of the trade is still wild-caught, and wild fish are less tolerant of deteriorating water than commercially produced ones, so a cardinal school belongs in a tank that has finished cycling and is holding steady.

Always quarantine new fish for at least 30 days before adding them, given the disease risk this species carries, and a bare hospital tank is what that means, not a spare corner of the display tank. Keep them well fed on a proper micro-pellet food sized for their small mouths.
