# Ball Python Enrichment: What 35 Snakes in a Study Actually Showed

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most reptile enrichment advice is extrapolated from general principles about mammals. Ball pythons are one of the rare exceptions, because the single most contested question in how they are kept has been tested directly.

<KeyTakeaway>
Researchers compared 35 ball pythons in a conventional rack system against furnished terrariums and concluded that racks are not a welfare-equivalent alternative. The furnishings that made the difference were substrate, hides, climbing structure, a bathing-sized water basin, elevated spots and live plants. Enrichment for this species is mostly a housing question.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Housing Study Found

Kaufmann and colleagues, publishing in *PLOS ONE*, evaluated two housing systems for ball pythons on a behavior basis. Thirty-five snakes were kept in a conventional rack system and compared against furnished terrariums containing substrate, hiding places, climbing opportunities, a water basin large enough for bathing, elevated basking spots and living plants.

Their conclusion is unusually direct for a welfare paper: from an animal welfare perspective, rack systems are not an alternative to furnished terrariums. The reasoning they give is that housing should let an animal express its full behavioral repertoire and cope with the challenges that arise, and enriched conditions can produce positive emotional states rather than merely the absence of bad ones.

<FunFact>
The furnished enclosures in the study deliberately included climbing structure. Ball pythons are usually described as terrestrial, and most commercial setups give them nothing to climb at all, which quietly removes a behavior they will use when it is available.
</FunFact>

This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to overstate. The study compared two housing systems. It did not test individual enrichment items against each other, and it does not tell you that a particular branch or plant is what mattered. What it establishes is that the whole furnished package outperformed the bare one.

## Cover Everywhere, Not Just Inside a Hide

A ball python is an ambush predator that spends most of its life concealed, and the standard reading of that is "give it a tight hide". The more useful reading is that the whole floor should offer cover, so the snake can be somewhere other than inside a box without being exposed.

That means <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3SKmfOx" product="Cork bark round hide">cork bark tubes and flats</AffiliateLink>, leaf litter, and enough plant cover to break up sightlines. Two secure hides, one on the warm side and one on the cool side, remain the baseline so the snake never has to choose between the temperature it wants and feeling safe. ## Climbing, Which Most Setups Skip Entirely

Give a ball python a sturdy <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3QQNV3L" product="Climbing branch (Mopani wood)">climbing branch</AffiliateLink> braced firmly at both ends and it will use it. They are heavy-bodied and not agile, so the requirement is stability: something a snake can push against without it shifting, positioned low enough that a fall is not a fall.

Cork rounds stacked to create a raised platform work as well as a branch and double as cover. The point is not that ball pythons are secretly arboreal, it is that vertical structure adds usable space to an enclosure that otherwise only has a floor.

## Water Big Enough to Get Into

The furnished terrariums in the study included a basin for bathing, not a drinking cup. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eKyN0W" product="Large soak-able water dish">dish the snake can fully enter</AffiliateLink> supports shedding, gives it a hydration option it controls, and is one of the few enrichment items that is passive: you provide it and the animal decides.

Keep it stable enough that it cannot be tipped, and clean, since a soaking snake will also defecate in it. Our [health issues guide](/blog/ball-python-health-issues-guide/) covers what a bad shed actually indicates.

## Scent, Which Costs Nothing

Snakes navigate chemically, so novel scent is closer to a puzzle for them than a novel object is. A shed skin from another enclosure, a scent-marked piece of cork, or dragging the prey item across the substrate before offering it all give a ball python something to investigate with the sense it actually leads with.

This is reasoning from natural history, and the guide should say so. No study result sits behind it. It is free, it is low risk, and it is more plausible than most of what gets sold as reptile enrichment.

## Priority Order

1. A properly furnished enclosure, which is the part with a study behind it
2. Cover across the whole floor, not just two hides
3. Stable climbing structure
4. A water basin large enough to soak in
5. Scent novelty
6. Rearrangement and new objects, genuinely last

## What Not to Do

Do not treat a rack as enriched by adding one item to it, since the study compared whole systems and the rack is the thing that lost. Do not stack decor so densely that the snake cannot reach the warm spot, because thermal access outranks everything here. Do not use handling as enrichment, especially around a shed cycle, and see our [handling guide](/blog/ball-python-handling-guide/) for what safe interaction looks like. And do not let feeding response drive enclosure design, since a snake that strikes at movement is not asking for a more exciting room.
