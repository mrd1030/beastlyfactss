# How to Handle a Western Hognose Snake (And Why the Drama Is Mostly Bluff)

Western hognose snakes have built their reputation on a dramatic defensive display that looks alarming and means almost the opposite of what it appears to. Understanding that display is the key to handling this species well.

## Meet the "Puff Adder" Act

When a hognose feels threatened, it puts on a full show: inflating its body, flattening its neck into a cobra-like hood (the source of its "puff adder" nickname), hissing loudly, and making closed-mouth mock strikes that never make contact. If that doesn't work, some hognoses escalate further, playing dead entirely, rolling onto their back, mouth open, tongue out, sometimes releasing musk. Flip one back over mid-performance and it will often roll itself belly-up again, as if it hasn't noticed.

This is all bluff. A hognose putting on this full display is doing everything possible to avoid biting you. Aggression in this species is close to unheard of. What looks dramatic is a small, harmless animal trying to look bigger and scarier than it is.

## The Venom Question

Western hognoses are rear-fanged and mildly venomous. Bites are uncommon and their effects stay local, but a snake that holds on can do more than a pinprick. Weinstein and Keyler's 2009 case report describes a keeper bitten while offering a mouse to a snake that kept its grip for several minutes: marked swelling, bruising and mild cellulitis, no systemic effects, and a full recovery in about five months. The paper reviews several other documented Heterodon bites alongside it. A 2026 survey by Damm and colleagues collected 63 usable bite reports from keepers. Most of those bites came from a hungry snake or while moving one into a feeding container, and swelling was the most common symptom. More than a third of quick bite-and-release cases caused no symptoms at all, while a snake that held on or chewed caused symptoms in about 80% of cases. Two of the reports described effects that lasted, one of them weakness for more than a year. Both papers conclude the species should not be considered dangerous, just handled thoughtfully, especially around feeding time. Rare allergic reactions are possible, as with any bite. None of that puts this species in the category state venomous-reptile laws are written for, and a bite that breaks skin is still worth showing to a doctor.

## When Bites Happen

Bites are rare and typically tied to a feeding response, the snake mistaking a hand for food, rather than defense. Wash your hands before handling to remove any prey scent, and leave the snake alone for 48 hours after it eats.

## How to Pick One Up

Give a new hognose about two weeks to settle before the first session. After that:

1. **Signal that this is not food.** Lift the snake out of the substrate with a small tool such as a snake hook, then move it onto your hands.
2. **Come in from the side.** A hand or tool dropping from above looks like a predator and sets off the hood-and-hiss.
3. **Support as much of the body as you can.** Let the snake rest along your palms and forearm with most of its length held. Never grab the tail and never restrain the head.
4. **Let it move.** Keep your hands under it as it crawls, moving one hand ahead of the other.

The first sessions last no more than 5 minutes, and they end on a calm snake, so don't put it back mid-display. Build up to 10 minutes, then to as much as half an hour, and never past an hour. Once or twice a week is plenty, and never more than once a day. Skip a session when the snake has eaten in the last 48 hours.

If a feeding-response bite does happen and the snake hangs on, get it off promptly, because in the bite survey the symptoms tracked how long the snake held on. Cool water poured over its face usually makes it let go. Don't yank it off, which can tear the skin.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Panicking at the hood-and-hiss display and handling roughly or dropping the snake, when calm, steady handling works better
- Handling within 48 hours of a meal
- Not washing hands beforehand, raising the odds of a feeding-response bite

## How They Compare to Other Beginner Snakes

Hognoses get compared most often to corn snakes and ball pythons. Care-wise, they're closer to a [corn snake](/blog/corn-snake-handling-guide/), active and suited to a dry, arid setup, than to a [ball python](/blog/ball-python-handling-guide/). Corn snakes are generally the most reliable eaters of the three; hognoses and ball pythons can both go through finicky feeding phases. Temperament-wise, a stressed ball python curls into a tight ball, while a stressed hognose puts on the hood-and-hiss show instead, different strategies, similarly harmless.

A hognose that hoods and hisses at every routine interaction is usually reporting something about its enclosure. Those displays are anti-predator responses and say nothing fixed about its personality. Deep substrate and [cover at both ends of the gradient](/blog/hognose-snake-enrichment-guide/) reduce how often the snake feels it needs them.

## Reading the Bluff for What It Is

Everything in the hognose display, from the hood to the belly-up roll, is meant to make a threat leave without a bite. The bites that do happen come from a snake that smells food, so calm hands that come in from the side and carry no prey scent take away most of the risk.
