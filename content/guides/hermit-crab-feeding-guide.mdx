# Hermit Crab Feeding: Scatter It, Vary It, and Watch the Label

A land hermit crab in the wild works a beach. They are omnivorous scavengers that eat whatever they find, although not the former occupants of the shells they use, and in the wild that means fruit, fallen leaves, seeds, animal droppings and carrion. It is a range no single tub of pellets reproduces.

## What Goes In

The working list for a pet crab is commercial hermit crab diets, fruits, vegetables, nuts and brine shrimp, split by how often each should appear.

Vegetables are the near-daily item, six or seven days a week: spinach, carrots, kale, romaine lettuce, bell peppers and cucumbers. Fruit comes one to three times a week, with mangoes, coconut, papaya, strawberries, apples and bananas named. Nuts, seaweed, brine shrimp and fish flakes are treats at two to three days a week.

Zoo practice is the same shape at a larger scale: crab food alongside assorted fruits and vegetables.

## Why One Food Is Not the Diet

The published nutrition standards for this species build around three categories: regular access to appropriate animal-based protein, access to natural calcium sources, and safe plant matter. The stated principle is that "No single food source meets all needs", and the standards explicitly reject pellet-only feeding and static diets.

That is the argument for rotation, and it is a stronger one than novelty. A crab offered the same commercial pellet every day is being fed a food designed to be complete, by a standard that says completeness across a week comes from variety, and no one product supplies it.

Feeding also works better as scattering than as serving. Spreading food across the substrate and on climbing surfaces turns eating into searching, which is what the [enrichment guide](/blog/hermit-crab-enrichment-guide/) covers in more detail.

## Calcium and the Molt

Calcium is the nutrient with a deadline. A crab rebuilding its exoskeleton after a molt needs it, and the supply has to already be in place when that happens, not arrive afterwards.

The route is a powdered calcium supplement added to food, or a crab-safe natural source such as crushed cuttlebone. One caution: skip reptile products with added vitamin D unless a vet recommends them, and skip human calcium chews, tablets and flavored mineral blocks.

Cuttlebone has the advantage of being available continuously, so a crab takes what it needs on its own schedule instead of on the keeper's.

## The Two Additives Worth Reading Labels For

This is the part most care sheets skip, and it is specific.

Two substances belong nowhere near a hermit crab's food. **Copper sulfate**, which is toxic to invertebrates. And **ethoxyquin**, a synthetic preservative also toxic to invertebrates, which can be present in fish meal without being disclosed separately on the label.

That second one is the reason a fish-based treat can be a problem even when nothing on the ingredient panel looks alarming. Fish flakes and dried shrimp are ordinary hermit crab foods, and the preservative can ride in with the fish meal.

Produce carries its own version of the same issue. Hermit crabs are sensitive to pesticides, so wash anything fresh before it goes in, or source it knowing how it was grown.

## Water Is Part of Feeding

Both dishes, always: constant access to shallow bowls of saltwater and freshwater, one fresh and dechlorinated, one mixed to an appropriate salinity, and both no deeper than a quarter to half an inch.

That depth limit is not a detail. A crab that cannot get out of a water dish drowns in it, and the dishes are in the enclosure permanently, which makes their depth a standing condition rather than a one-time setup choice.
