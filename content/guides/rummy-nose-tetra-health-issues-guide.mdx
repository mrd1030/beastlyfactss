# Common Rummy-Nose Tetra Health Issues (and What a Fading Red Nose Is Telling You)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

The rummy-nose tetra (Petitella bleheri, formerly Hemigrammus bleheri) is a schooling fish of 1.5 to 2 inches from the soft, acidic, tea-colored blackwater rivers of the Rio Negro and Rio Meta basins. Nothing that sickens it is peculiar to the species, but it reacts to stress more readily than most small tetras, and it has an unusual way of showing it: the deep red of its head and snout drains toward pink when the fish is unwell. That color, and a ceiling temperature well short of the usual ich cure, shape how its health gets handled.

## Signs That Need Action Today

A count of the school at feeding time, with a look at every nose, is the daily check. These are the signs that cannot wait until tomorrow.

**Test the water first:**

- A red nose gone pale, on one fish or across the school.
- Fish gasping at the surface or breathing fast.
- Several fish showing the same sign together.
- A dead fish. Lift the body out before doing anything else.

**Start treatment today:**

- White specks like grains of salt on the body or fins: treat for ich.
- Rapid breathing that keeps going after the water tests clean, since ich can settle in the gills.

**Watch and check again tomorrow:**

- Flashing: darting at glass, wood or sand to scrape the body.
- A slimy look from extra mucus on the skin.
- A fish that turns sluggish or skips its food.

That third group is how ich usually starts, and the white specks often show only well after the infection took hold, so a fish with any of those signs gets checked for spots every day until it looks normal again. A struggling fish and a struggling tank look much alike from outside the glass, and [sorting a water problem from a disease](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) begins with the test kit before it turns to the fish.

## The Red Nose Is a Cue to Test the Water

The red on a rummy-nose fades when the fish is stressed or its conditions are poor, and in a healthy, settled school it stays strong. That makes the nose a useful early prompt. It is not a precise gauge: it does not say which reading is off, or by how much, and a pale nose alone is not a diagnosis. What it does say is that something has changed, and the first response is always the same. Test ammonia, nitrite and pH with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">liquid test kit</AffiliateLink>, then look further.

Water that has drifted alkaline is one known cause of a dulled head, and in harder, higher-pH water nitrate takes a bigger toll on this fish, with lost color often the first thing a keeper sees. When the water reads clean, the other suspects are the tank's company and the diet. Bullying is one of the stresses that drains the red, and a calm community matters for this fish, which is why [settling a new group in before any boisterous tankmates](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-handling-guide/) is part of keeping it healthy. Color also depends on what goes in at the bowl: the best red comes from [a varied mix of small dried, frozen and live foods](/blog/rummy-nose-tetra-feeding-guide/) sized for a small mouth.

## Water Quality Comes First

Rummy-noses do best in soft, acidic water: 73 to 79°F, pH 5.5 to 7.0 and 2 to 12 dGH. Stability inside that window counts for more than chasing one exact figure, and testing before each [weekly partial water change](/blog/aquarium-water-changes-guide/) catches a slide early. Soft water carries little buffering, so [the KH reading](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) is the one to understand before trying to move pH in either direction.

A new tank is the riskiest place for this fish. Ammonia and nitrite both need to read zero before a school goes in, which means [finishing the nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) first. A school of 10 or more is part of the picture too, since a lone or short-handed rummy-nose is a stressed one.

## Ich: Warm to 79°F, Then Treat

Ich, also called white spot disease, most often enters a tank in infected water or on a newly added fish. Its early signs are the ones in the watch group above, with the spots arriving later. A fish with infected gills breathes quickly or hangs at the surface, and a heavy infestation can kill, above all in a fish already weakened by poor water.

Most ich strains die in water above 86°F, and fish that tolerate that heat can be cured by holding the tank there. The rummy-nose's range ends at 79°F, seven degrees short, so the heat method is not an option for it. Warmth still helps up to that ceiling: the parasite reproduces faster in warmer water, and a quicker cycle exposes each new generation to treatment sooner. Bring the tank up slowly to 79°F with a dependable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">aquarium heater</AffiliateLink>, add an air stone because warmer water holds less oxygen, and let one of these clear the infection:

- **Aquarium salt**, never table salt, at roughly a tablespoon per 5 gallons, dissolved in gradually. Once the fish are clear, a run of water changes brings the tank back to fresh water.
- **An ich medication**, dosed to the label or a vet's instructions. Vets usually treat the water with formalin or copper sulfate over several rounds.

Carry on until no fish shows any sign of illness. The same warm-then-treat route serves [every species whose ceiling sits below 86°F](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/), and in a mixed tank the treatment stops at the lowest ceiling among the fish.

## Neon Tetra Disease

Neon tetra disease comes from a microsporidian parasite, Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, whose primary host is the [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-health-issues-guide/). It has turned up in around 20 species of freshwater fish across four orders, and characins as a group are among its hosts, so the name gives a rummy-nose school no protection. Any fish that dies comes out of the tank as soon as it is spotted, whatever the cause.

## Quarantine Every New Fish

Rummy-noses sometimes arrive from the store already carrying ich, and a newcomer added straight to the display tank brings it to every fish there. Each new fish spends [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before joining the school. A small <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4pjiyeT" product="10 gallon tank">separate tank</AffiliateLink> running a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink>, heated to the same range as the main tank, covers it, and the same setup serves as the hospital tank whenever a fish falls ill.

A rummy-nose school that eats eagerly, swims as one and holds a deep red head in steady, soft water is a healthy school. When the red starts to fade, the water is the first place to look.
