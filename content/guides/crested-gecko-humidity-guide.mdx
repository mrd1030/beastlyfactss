# Why Humidity Is Everything for Crested Geckos

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Crested geckos (*Correlophus ciliatus*) are native to the tropical forests of New Caledonia, where humidity regularly exceeds 70%. The pattern to copy is wet nights and drier days. A crested gecko drinks by licking droplets off leaves and glass, sheds cleanly only when its skin has been moist, and breathes best in air that moves. Humidity is where all three of those meet, which is why it is the number most new keepers get wrong.

## The Target Range: 60 to 80%, With a Daily Swing

Aim for 60 to 80% relative humidity across the enclosure as the working range. PetMD's care sheet puts the ideal band at 70 to 80%, and the number that matters is not the average but the shape of the day. Humidity should spike right after the evening mist, hold high overnight, and fall through the day before you mist again. That wet-dry cycle mimics the forest and keeps mold and respiratory problems away.

The afternoon low is not a failure. Zen Habitats' humidity guide puts the dry-down at 45 to 50% before re-misting, and that drop is what stops mold and the bacterial or fungal infections that come with it, and lets the gecko's skin keep its water-shedding surface. An enclosure pinned at 80% around the clock is the mistake to watch for.

## A Misting Schedule That Works

One heavy mist at lights-out does most of the job. Soak the walls, the leaves, and the substrate until droplets bead on the glass, and let the gecko drink from them after dark. If your home runs dry in winter, a humidifier for the room helps the enclosure hold humidity longer. With correct temperatures, ventilation, and substrate, you should not need to mist several times a day; if you do, something else in the setup is leaking humidity.

## Monitoring Humidity

A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3QHrM81" product="Digital thermometer/hygrometer combo">**digital hygrometer**</AffiliateLink> is non-negotiable. Analog dial gauges drift and read wrong in moist air. Use a digital probe, place it mid-enclosure away from the misting point, and read it twice: after the evening mist, and again in the late afternoon. Those two readings tell you whether the cycle is working.

## Water

Crested geckos take most of their water from droplets, so the mist is the main water source. Keep a shallow dish anyway as a backup, and fill it with tap, filtered, or spring water. Distilled water carries no minerals and is not a good sole source.

## Signs of Low Humidity

- Retained shed, especially on the toes, where a ring of old skin can constrict and cost a digit. See our [complete guide to reptile shedding](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/) for how to handle it safely.
- Wrinkled or sunken skin, sunken eyes, or skin with a dry sandpaper texture.
- Sticky saliva, reduced activity, and a gecko that stops eating.

## When Humidity Runs Too High

The other failure is quieter. An enclosure that never dries grows mold on the substrate and decor, and the Merck Veterinary Manual is blunt about the next step: cutting ventilation to hold humidity "frequently causes skin and respiratory disease." Watch for mold on wood or soil, droplets that never clear between mistings, and on the gecko itself the respiratory signs Merck lists: open-mouth breathing and discharge from the nose. Either of those is a vet visit.

To bring it down, mist less rather than sealing more, unblock every vent, and add a small fan across the top if the air is stagnant. Then read the hygrometer again the next afternoon.

Keep it wet at night, let it dry by day, and your crested gecko will pay you back with years of flappy-footed company. For natural history, see the [Crested Gecko encyclopedia profile](/encyclopedia/animal/crested-gecko/).
