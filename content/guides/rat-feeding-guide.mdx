# Rat Feeding: Diet, Portions, and Why Hamster Food Isn't Rat Food

A bag labeled "Hamster and Gerbil Food" looks close enough for a rat too, but the two formulas are built to different specs for a reason, and a rat fed the wrong one misses nutrients it needs.

## The Staple Diet

A nutritionally complete, rat-specific pelleted diet or lab block should be the base of every meal. Pellets made for rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters and other plant eaters are not a stand-in, because they are short on nutrients a rat needs. The labels show why. One widely sold adult rat food guarantees a minimum 15% crude protein, 4% crude fat, and a fiber range of just 2 to 5%, while the same maker's hamster and gerbil food runs a meaningfully higher 10 to 15% fiber alongside 16% protein, a formula built for a more herbivore-leaning digestive system than a rat has.

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Two meals a day, one in the morning and one in the evening, sized so the rat finishes what's offered and holds a healthy weight. An always-full bowl is how a rat gets fat. Supplement daily with small amounts of fresh vegetables, and offer fruit, grains, and seeds more sparingly. Keep all of that supplemental food, vegetables, fruit, grains, and seeds combined, under about 10% of the total diet, with the rat-specific pellet or lab block doing most of the nutritional work.

Scatter feeding counts toward that twice-daily portion. It is a way of serving the ration, not an extra meal on top of it: weigh out the usual amount and throw it across the cage, or mix some into fresh bedding at cleaning time, instead of tipping it into a bowl. The same goes for food hidden in cardboard tubes or a dig box, or a boiled egg left in its shell to be broken into. Enrichment food comes out of the daily allowance, never on top of it, which is how a rat can forage every day without drifting toward the obesity this species is prone to. Seeds and nuts given for the fun of handling them come out of the 10% share for extras. Uneaten food still gets picked out afterward, scattered or not.

## Foods to Avoid

Onion, citrus fruit, walnuts, rhubarb, grapes, raisins, and chocolate are off the menu entirely. Also skip caffeine, alcohol, raw beans, unripe tomato, green or raw potato, and anything salty, sugary, or heavily processed, none of it is worth the risk for an animal this small. Water goes in bottles, which stay cleaner than an open bowl, and a cage gets at least two of them. A clogged spout or one pushy cage mate then never leaves a rat dry. Check the spouts for blockages every day.

## Rats Are True Omnivores

This is where rats differ from several other small pets. Chinchillas and guinea pigs are strict herbivores built around a hindgut-fermentation system that fresh meat would actively disrupt. Rats are not built that way. In the wild, brown rats eat insects, small vertebrates, and carrion alongside grain, seeds, and plant matter, and research on wild Norway rat diet has found they actually prefer meat when given the option between the two. A pet rat can safely have small amounts of cooked lean meat, egg, or legumes a couple of times a week as a genuine, species-appropriate protein source rather than an indulgence, something that would be entirely inappropriate to offer a chinchilla or a guinea pig on the same shelf.

## The Mistakes That Matter

Reaching for whatever small-pet pellet is already in the house, hamster food, gerbil food, rabbit food, is the single most common feeding mistake with rats, and it's an easy one to make since the bags all look similar on a store shelf. It isn't a trivial substitution: those formulas are built around a more fiber-forward, herbivore-leaning digestive system, not the one a rat has, and a mismatch here shows up over time as poor coat condition and a diet that isn't doing what the label implies.

<FunFact>
Hamster food is the swap first-time owners make most easily, since a bag reading "Hamster and Gerbil Food" sits on the very same shelf as rat food and looks like a reasonable substitute. It isn't quite that simple: a rat formula runs lean on fiber, and a hamster and gerbil mix can carry several times as much, built for a meaningfully different gut than a rat's.
</FunFact>

Overfeeding fatty extras is the second big one. Obesity is common in pet rats, and calorie restriction, without compromising overall nutrition, is one of the few interventions actually shown to extend their lifespan, which makes weighing out a measured daily portion a meaningful habit rather than a fussy one. [A high-calorie diet also raises the risk of the pituitary tumors older rats develop](/blog/rat-health-issues-guide/).
