# White's Tree Frog Feeding: Reading the Ridges Above the Eardrum

<VetDisclaimer />

Two facts about this species collide at the food dish. It is sedentary by temperament, spending most of the day sitting still, and it has an appetite that does not moderate itself. Put those together and a well-meaning keeper produces an overweight frog without ever feeling like they were overdoing it.

## What to Offer

Crickets carry the diet. Around them, the useful additions are moths, beetles, cockroaches, grasshoppers and earthworms, with dubia roaches a common staple in their own right.

The fatty feeders need a ceiling. Waxworms and superworms go in once a week at most, and a pinkie mouse belongs in the same category: an adult may take one occasionally, and nothing about the diet requires it. This is a species that puts on weight easily, so the rich items are the ones to count rather than the ones to reach for.

Presentation is worth a thought. A frog that hunts naturally does better with prey it can find at its own level, so insects that climb the planting and the branches suit it better than something that immediately vanishes into the substrate. Insects can go loose in the enclosure or be offered from blunt, round-tipped forceps, whichever the individual frog responds to.

## How Often, and How Much

Growing frogs eat daily. That much is consistent across veterinary guidance.

Adults slow to two or three times a week, at roughly three to four appropriately sized insects a sitting. A frog over three inches takes a few larger crickets at that interval; a smaller adult takes about three-week-old crickets on the same schedule.

Those counts are a starting point, and the animal corrects them.

## The Ridge Test

This is the part worth learning, because it replaces guesswork with something you can check in a few seconds.

Look at the ridges that sit just above the frog's eardrum. Three readings:

- **No visible ridges.** The frog is likely underweight. Feed more, or feed more often.
- **Ridges present and defined.** This is the shape to aim for.
- **Ridges prominent, sagging, or folding over.** The frog is obese and should be eating less.

That last state is not merely cosmetic. In an overweight White's tree frog the excess fat shows up clinically: a cloudy appearance to the eyes caused by lipid deposits in the cornea, a coelom that looks enlarged from fat laid down inside it, and fat in the crests above the eyes heavy enough to start interfering with vision. The correction is unglamorous and effective, less food and more opportunity to move.

## Supplements

A feeder insect is a delivery mechanism, and an unfed cricket delivers very little. Both halves of fixing that, feeding the insects properly beforehand and dusting them on the way in, are covered in the [gut-loading guide](/blog/gut-loading-feeder-insects-guide/), including the timing window and why the two jobs are not interchangeable.

What is specific to this frog is the frequency. Calcium and a multivitamin on the insects runs about once a week for an adult and twice a week for a growing juvenile. Whether the calcium carries vitamin D3 depends on the lighting, since a frog under working UVB makes its own and one without it does not.

## Where This Connects

Overfeeding and underfeeding are both husbandry problems before they are dietary ones, and the [health issues guide](/blog/whites-tree-frog-health-issues-guide/) covers what a frog in poor condition starts to present with.

The other half is opportunity to move. A frog with height, dense planting and places to climb burns more than one sitting on a bare branch, which makes the enclosure itself part of the weight equation.
