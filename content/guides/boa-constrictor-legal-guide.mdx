# Is It Legal to Own a Boa Constrictor? Federal Withdrawal and State Rules

<LegalDisclaimer />

Boa constrictors spent years in federal regulatory limbo alongside pythons and anacondas, then the government explicitly declined to restrict them. That decision, plus a small number of genuinely restrictive states, is the entire legal picture for this species.

## The Federal History: Proposed, Then Withdrawn

FWS proposed listing nine large constrictor species, including the boa constrictor, as injurious wildlife back on March 12, 2010. In January 2012, the agency finalized a listing for four of those species, the Burmese and Indian python plus the Northern and Southern African python, but left the remaining five, including the boa constrictor, under continued consideration. After a 2014 comment period reopened for those five, FWS's March 2015 final rule listed the reticulated python and three anaconda species, and explicitly withdrew the boa constrictor from consideration entirely.

FWS's own stated reasoning is worth reading directly: although a boa "can be damaging to U.S. wildlife," the agency found that "the circumstances surrounding the species, which include widespread private ownership and domestic breeding, render importation and interstate transport prohibitions less effective." In other words, the boa constrictor was already too widely and legally established as a pet for a federal trade ban to meaningfully change anything, so FWS chose a cooperative approach with states, wildlife agencies, and the pet industry instead. As of 2026, the boa constrictor remains not federally listed as injurious wildlife.

<FunFact>
FWS noted that boas had been found "on the loose" in 46 states by the time of its 2015 decision, an indicator of just how widespread private ownership already was, and part of why the agency concluded a federal ban would do little beyond inconveniencing existing legal owners.
</FunFact>

## Louisiana Is Now the Strictest Mainland State

Louisiana rewrote its reptile rules in January 2025, and the result catches a lot of people still working from older summaries. The current LAC 76:XV.101 names Boa constrictor individually on the restricted nonnative species list, which means a Restricted Snake Permit is required to import, possess, sell or buy one at any size. A separate general provision covers constrictor snakes over eight feet, and that eight-foot line is the part most secondhand write-ups quote, but for a boa it is beside the point: the species is restricted by name whatever length it is. Permitted facilities are also open to inspection.

## New Jersey: Exempt, Not Permitted

New Jersey is worth stating plainly because the opposite claim circulates widely. The state's exempt species list at N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.4 names the boa constrictor outright, using the older binomial Constrictor constrictor, and makes no distinction between B. c. imperator, B. c. ortonii and the nominate subspecies. The potentially dangerous species list at 7:25-4.8 reaches only venomous families, Elapidae, Viperidae and Crotalidae, plus the usual mammals and crocodilians. No boa appears on it. What New Jersey does require a permit for is the ball python, which is the reverse of most states and the likeliest origin of the subspecies story.

## Hawaii and New York City: The Two Outright Bans

Hawaii bans every snake species without exception, boas included, a felony-level offense carrying fines up to $200,000 and up to three years imprisonment, with an amnesty turn-in program for anyone who already has one. The reasoning is ecological rather than punitive: Hawaii's native bird populations evolved with no natural snake predators, and any established snake population would be devastating to the island ecosystem.

New York City separately bans the entire family Boidae under its health code, which on its plain language captures boa constrictors even though the code's own illustrative examples focus on anacondas. This is a municipal rule layered on top of New York State law, which does not restrict boas at all, meaning the same snake is legal an hour outside the city and illegal within it.

## Florida Does Not Ban Boa Constrictors

This one needs saying because the opposite claim is common. FWC added 16 high-risk nonnative reptiles to Florida's Prohibited list in February 2021, effective that April, and the boa constrictor was not among them. The prohibited snakes are the brown tree snake, four anacondas in genus Eunectes, four Python species, two Morelia species and the sea snake family. The Conditional list's only reptile is the red-eared slider. Boa constrictor is on neither, so no Florida permit, microchip or caging standard attaches to keeping one.

## What About Interstate Transport and CITES?

Two more points that get muddled. Because the boa constrictor is not federally listed as injurious, the Lacey Act's import and interstate transport prohibitions do not apply to it, and no federal approval is needed to move one across state lines or sell one interstate. What does apply is CITES, and only to international movement: Boa constrictor is on Appendix II, and the Argentine subspecies B. c. occidentalis is on Appendix I. Neither touches domestic ownership.

## The Takeaway

The federal government looked at banning boa constrictors and decided against it, on the record, because the species was already too well established as a legal pet for a ban to accomplish much. That leaves state and local law as the only real barrier, and it's a short, specific list: Hawaii's total ban, New York City's municipal ban layered over an otherwise permissive state, permits in Louisiana, New Mexico and Delaware, Missouri's registration duty once the animal passes eight feet, and Minnesota's permitted-breeder condition. West Virginia turned out to be legal once the board's list was read: the statute told a three member board to consider constrictors over six feet, but the list the board actually adopted in April 2022 names no snake at all, only the Komodo dragon among reptiles. Delaware turned out to be a permit state once its regulation was read in full: no snake is exempt there, so a boa needs a $25 Individual permit, and the python and anaconda list that made it look like a ban is a restriction on what sales permit holders may breed. Everywhere else, boa constrictors are simply legal to own.
