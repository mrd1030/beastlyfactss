# Three Livebearers That Breed Whether You Plan to or Not

Platies, swordtails, and mollies show up in beginner community tanks more than almost any other combination, and it's not a coincidence. All three are livebearers, meaning females give birth to live, free-swimming fry instead of laying eggs, and all three are genuinely easy, hardy fish that forgive a new owner's mistakes better than most. Platies and swordtails are close enough to share a genus, mollies aren't far behind, and all three commonly live 3 to 5 years given reasonable care. What separates them is smaller than what connects them, but each one still has a real, distinguishing trait worth knowing before you buy.

## Platy: The Color Range Is Entirely Man-Made

Two closely related species, the southern platy and the variatus platy, are sold interchangeably under the same name, and decades of selective breeding between the two, plus hybridization with swordtails, have produced an enormous range of patterns, from Mickey Mouse and wagtail to calico, tuxedo, and bumblebee varieties. None of that color is natural. Wild platies are a fairly drab olive-brown, built for blending into the muddy, densely vegetated canals and ponds they actually live in. At 2 to 3 inches and hardy, it's one of the easiest first fish there is, and like its two relatives, it breeds prolifically with little encouragement. ## Swordtail: The Sword Is a Dating Profile, Not a Weapon

Swordtails share a genus with platies, Xiphophorus, Greek for "sword bearer," and the two species are close enough that they've been intentionally crossed for almost a century, so many swordtails and platies sold today already carry some hybrid ancestry. The sword itself, a long, colorful extension of the male's lower tail fin, plays no role in swimming at all. It's a mate-choice signal, and females show measurable preferences for males with longer swords and bolder patterning. In the wild, swordtails favor faster-flowing streams than platies do, and that shows up in the tank as a noticeably more active, energetic swimmer. Females are actually the larger sex, reaching 6.2 inches against a male's 5.5 including the sword.

## Molly: The Livebearer That Can Handle Salt

Mollies are close relatives of both guppies and platies, but they stand apart in one specific way: they naturally live in fresh and brackish coastal waters, and can tolerate salt concentrations well past normal seawater, a genuine point of difference from the other two fish here. Whether a healthy freshwater tank actually benefits from added aquarium salt is a separate, disputed question among experienced keepers rather than a settled one. Selective breeding has also produced plenty of color and finnage varieties, including the tall-finned sailfin molly, which reaches up to 6 inches against a standard molly's 3 to 4.5. <FunFact>
None of these three lay eggs. Platies, swordtails, and mollies are all livebearers, fertilization and development happen inside the female, and what she eventually releases into the tank is fully formed, free-swimming fry rather than eggs that need separate incubation somewhere else. That's a big part of why new owners get caught off guard: there's no nest, no visible clutch, nothing to notice in advance, just a normal-looking tank one week and a scatter of tiny fish darting for cover in the plants the next. Platies and mollies are both specifically described as prolific breeders that need little encouragement, and a swordtail, close enough to a platy to interbreed with one, isn't any harder to breed by accident. Leave an unplanned male and female of any of these three together, and a surprise batch of fry is usually just a matter of time.
</FunFact>
