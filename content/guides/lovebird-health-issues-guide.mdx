# Common Lovebird Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Lovebirds hide illness well, a survival instinct that means by the time symptoms are obvious, a problem is often more advanced than it would be in a more expressive pet. A handful of conditions come up often enough to know in advance. See our [Lovebird cage setup guide](/blog/lovebird-tank-setup-guide/) for the husbandry side, and our full [Lovebird care guide](/guides/lovebird/) for everything else.

## Psittacine Beak and Feather Disease (PBFD)

Lovebirds are particularly susceptible to this virus, which attacks feather and beak cells along with the immune system. Watch for feather deformities, feather loss, and abnormalities in the beak. This is often fatal, has no cure, and is highly contagious between birds. Always see a vet, and testing any new bird before introducing it to an existing one is genuinely important prevention, not excessive caution.

## Chronic Egg-Laying and Egg-Binding

A hen can lay eggs regularly even with no male present, and an all-seed diet, low in calcium and vitamin D, makes this worse and raises the risk of a genuinely dangerous complication. Watch for straining, lethargy, and visible abdominal swelling. Egg-binding can turn fatal quickly. This is an emergency, don't wait to see if it resolves.

<FunFact>
A female lovebird doesn't need a male present to lay eggs, she can do so regularly entirely on her own. It's part of why nest boxes, mirrors, and enclosed "happy hut" style hideaways are discouraged for pet lovebirds, they can trigger chronic egg-laying even in a single, unmated hen.
</FunFact>

### Why You Don't Remove the Clutch

The instinct when a hen won't stop laying is to scoop up each egg the moment it appears, so she isn't sitting on infertile eggs for weeks. That instinct usually backfires. According to VCA Animal Hospitals, removing eggs as soon as they're laid can push a hen to lay even more, because her body reads an incomplete nest as a signal to keep producing. A hen tracks clutch size partly through her brood patch, the bare, warm patch of skin on her abdomen that stays in contact with the eggs during incubation, and pulling eggs away keeps that feedback loop from ever closing.

<KeyTakeaway>
Pulling eggs as your hen lays them tends to make chronic laying worse, not better. Leaving the clutch in place, including with fake eggs, is what lets her body register that the clutch is done.
</KeyTakeaway>

The fix runs against instinct: leave the eggs where they are. VCA recommends letting a hen sit on her full clutch for a complete incubation period, generally 21 to 28 days depending on species, before removing the eggs, and even then only a few at a time, roughly one every one to two days, since clearing the nest all at once can restart the cycle. For lovebirds specifically, that window runs close to 23 days, based on breeding data compiled by the University of Michigan's Animal Diversity Web. If real eggs are a concern for breakage or spoilage, a LafeberVet client handout notes that hard-boiled eggs, frozen eggs, or craft-store fake eggs of a matching size and weight can substitute, letting her sit through the same behavior without a cage full of aging eggs.

### Cut the Hormonal Triggers

Managing the clutch correctly is only half of it. The bigger lever is removing the environmental cues that push a hen into laying. LafeberVet's chronic egg-laying handout lists long day length, warm temperatures, and an abundant food supply among the main triggers for companion parrots, and daylight is the one owners have the most direct control over. VCA recommends cutting a chronic layer's exposure to daylight or room light down to about 8 hours a day, and LafeberVet gives a similar 8 to 10 hour range, for a period your avian vet helps you set. Covering the cage earlier in the evening to extend the dark period is the standard way to do this; our [bird photoperiod and sleep guide](/blog/bird-photoperiod-sleep-guide/) covers setting up a consistent light and dark schedule.

Nest-like spaces matter just as much. Huts, boxes, dark enclosed hideaways, and anything she can shred paper into and burrow inside all read as nesting opportunities, and LafeberVet recommends removing them outright rather than managing around them. Cut back on rich, abundant food too, since a steady surplus mimics the conditions that would support raising a clutch in the wild.

Handling matters as well. Both VCA and LafeberVet flag stroking or petting along a hen's back, under the wings, or around the vent as behavior that sends a mate-like signal and encourages more laying. Keep contact to the head and neck, and keep sessions shorter during a laying episode.

## Egg Binding Is a Same-Day Emergency

Chronic laying isn't just an inconvenience to manage around. Producing eggs draws heavily on a hen's calcium stores, since calcium builds the shell and drives the uterine contractions that push each egg through the oviduct. On a calcium-poor, all-seed diet, that draw doesn't get replaced, and VCA links the resulting hypocalcemia to a broader decline in body condition and a sharply higher risk of egg binding, since a calcium-depleted oviduct is too weak to contract normally around a malformed or soft-shelled egg. A hen laying heavily for weeks, thin and slow to bounce back between clutches, is worth a vet visit before anything actually gets stuck.

Egg binding is what happens when that cycle catches up with her: an egg fails to pass and lodges in the oviduct. PetMD lists abdominal straining, a wide-based stance, over-fluffed feathers, and tail wagging or bobbing among the signs, along with a bird that stops perching and instead sits low on the perch or on the floor of the cage. VCA adds that an egg-bound hen may not have passed an egg in the last 48 hours and may struggle to stand or grip the perch if the trapped egg is pressing on the nerves that control her legs. Small birds decline fast once this starts: VCA notes that small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg-bound, as the trapped egg compresses circulation and puts pressure on the air sacs. There is no waiting this out at home. It needs a same-day avian vet, not an appointment scheduled for the next morning.

For hens who keep cycling back into chronic laying despite the environmental changes above, an avian vet has hormonal options for refractory cases. Leuprolide acetate, given by injection, is the standard first drug; VCA notes it typically halts laying for only three to four weeks, so it's usually repeated on a schedule your vet sets. For longer suppression, some vets place a deslorelin implant under the skin, which VCA reports can hold off laying for three to six months or longer. Both are prescription decisions that depend on the individual bird's history and bloodwork, not something to request by name on your own, and neither one replaces fixing the light, diet, and handling triggers that started the cycle to begin with.

## Respiratory and Bacterial Infections

Wheezing, breathing difficulty, and discharge point here. Psittacosis specifically is worth knowing about because it's zoonotic, transmissible to people. Always see a vet.

## Polyomavirus

Mainly affects young birds, causing feather abnormalities and other symptoms. A vaccine exists and is worth discussing with an avian vet, especially if you're acquiring a young bird or planning to keep multiple birds.

## Watch for Hidden Illness

Because lovebirds mask symptoms so effectively, any sudden change, lethargy, appetite loss, fluffed feathers held constantly, or a shift in normal behavior, is worth a prompt vet visit rather than a wait-and-see approach.
