# Common Lovebird Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Lovebirds hide illness well, a survival instinct that means by the time symptoms are obvious, a problem is often more advanced than it would be in a more expressive pet. A handful of conditions come up often enough to know in advance.

## Psittacine Beak and Feather Disease (PBFD)

Lovebirds are particularly susceptible to this virus, which attacks feather and beak cells along with the immune system. Watch for feather deformities, feather loss, and abnormalities in the beak. This is often fatal, has no cure, and is highly contagious between birds. Always see a vet, and testing any new bird before introducing it to an existing one is genuinely important prevention, not excessive caution. PCR testing can find the virus in a bird that still looks healthy, and the [feather loss guide](/blog/bird-feather-loss-and-molt-guide/) shows how PBFD differs from plucking and molt.

## Chronic Egg-Laying and Egg-Binding

A hen can lay eggs regularly even with no male present, and an all-seed diet, low in calcium and vitamin D, makes this worse and raises the risk of a genuinely dangerous complication. Watch for straining, lethargy, and visible abdominal swelling. Egg-binding can turn fatal quickly. This is an emergency, don't wait to see if it resolves.

<FunFact>
A female lovebird doesn't need a male present to lay eggs, she can do so regularly entirely on her own. It's part of why nest boxes, mirrors, and enclosed "happy hut" style hideaways are discouraged for pet lovebirds, they can trigger chronic egg-laying even in a single, unmated hen.
</FunFact>

### Why You Don't Remove the Clutch

The instinct when a hen won't stop laying is to scoop up each egg the moment it appears, so she isn't sitting on infertile eggs for weeks. That instinct usually backfires. Removing eggs as soon as they're laid can push a hen to lay even more, because her body reads an incomplete nest as a signal to keep producing. A hen tracks clutch size partly through her brood patch, the bare, warm patch of skin on her abdomen that stays in contact with the eggs during incubation, and pulling eggs away keeps that feedback loop from ever closing.

<KeyTakeaway>
Pulling eggs as your hen lays them tends to make chronic laying worse, not better. Leaving the clutch in place, including with fake eggs, is what lets her body register that the clutch is done.
</KeyTakeaway>

The fix runs against instinct: leave the eggs where they are. Let a hen sit on her full clutch for a complete incubation period, generally 21 to 28 days for parrots as a group, before removing the eggs, and even then only a few at a time, roughly one every one to two days, since clearing the nest all at once can restart the cycle. For lovebirds specifically, incubation runs 18 to 24 days. If real eggs are a concern for breakage or spoilage, hard-boiled eggs, frozen eggs, or craft-store fake eggs of a matching size and weight can substitute, letting her sit through the same behavior without a cage full of aging eggs.

### Cut the Hormonal Triggers

Managing the clutch correctly is only half of it. The bigger lever is removing the environmental cues that push a hen into laying. Long day length, warm temperatures, and an abundant food supply are the main triggers for companion parrots, and daylight is the one owners have the most direct control over. Cut a chronic layer's exposure to daylight or room light down to about 8 hours a day, 8 to 10 at most, for a period your avian vet helps you set. Covering the cage earlier in the evening is the standard way to do this, and the same covered-cage routine is how any bird gets its [10 to 12 hours of dark sleep](/blog/bird-photoperiod-sleep-guide/).

Nest-like spaces matter just as much. Huts, boxes, dark enclosed hideaways, and anything she can shred paper into and burrow inside all read as nesting opportunities, so remove them outright rather than managing around them. Cut back on rich, abundant food too, since a steady surplus mimics the conditions that would support raising a clutch in the wild.

Handling matters as well. Stroking or petting along a hen's back, under the wings, or around the vent as behavior that sends a mate-like signal and encourages more laying. Keep contact to the head and neck, and keep sessions shorter during a laying episode.

## Egg Binding Is a Same-Day Emergency

Chronic laying isn't just an inconvenience to manage around. Producing eggs draws heavily on a hen's calcium stores, since calcium builds the shell and drives the uterine contractions that push each egg through the oviduct. On a calcium-poor, all-seed diet, that draw doesn't get replaced, and the resulting hypocalcemia brings a broader decline in body condition and a sharply higher risk of egg binding, since a calcium-depleted oviduct is too weak to contract normally around a malformed or soft-shelled egg. A hen laying heavily for weeks, thin and slow to bounce back between clutches, is worth a vet visit before anything actually gets stuck.

Egg binding is what happens when that cycle catches up with her: an egg fails to pass and lodges in the oviduct. The signs are abdominal straining, a wide-based stance, over-fluffed feathers, and tail wagging or bobbing, along with a bird that stops perching and sits low on the perch or on the cage floor. An egg-bound hen may not have passed an egg in the last 48 hours and may struggle to stand or grip the perch if the trapped egg is pressing on the nerves that control her legs. Small birds decline fast once this starts: they can die within a few hours of becoming egg-bound, as the trapped egg compresses circulation and puts pressure on the air sacs. There is no waiting this out at home. It needs a same-day avian vet, not an appointment scheduled for the next morning. The vet gives heat, injected fluids, calcium and vitamin D3, and oxytocin to stimulate contractions. If the egg still does not pass, it can be removed under sedation, drawn out with a needle, or taken out surgically. Most birds treated promptly go on to live normal lives. A hen that has been egg bound needs rest from laying triggers for at least 2 to 4 weeks, and preferably a whole breeding season.

For hens who keep cycling back into chronic laying despite the environmental changes above, an avian vet has hormonal options for refractory cases. Leuprolide acetate, given by injection, is the standard first drug; it typically halts laying for only three to four weeks, so it's usually repeated on a schedule your vet sets. For longer suppression, some vets place a deslorelin implant under the skin, which can hold off laying for three to six months or longer. Surgery is the last resort for a hen who keeps cycling despite everything else. Both are prescription decisions that depend on the individual bird's history and bloodwork, not something to request by name on your own, and neither one replaces fixing the light, diet, and handling triggers that started the cycle to begin with.

## Vitamin A Deficiency and Calcium
All-seed diets, and half-seed, half-pellet diets that are not portion controlled, are low in vitamin A. The signs are nasal discharge, sneezing, swelling around the eyes, conjunctivitis, labored breathing, increased thirst and urination, poor feather quality, feather picking and appetite loss. Calcium runs short on a seed diet too, since seed is poor in calcium relative to phosphorus and indoor birds make less vitamin D3. Low calcium shows as weakness, tremors, seizures and fractures with no obvious injury. Both are fixed by formulated pellets and vegetables, which the [lovebird feeding guide](/blog/lovebird-feeding-guide/) lays out.
## Obesity and Fatty Liver
High-fat food, meaning seed, nuts and table food, plus too much of it and too little exercise, leads to obesity, and fatty liver disease follows. Lameness and breathing trouble are the signs a keeper sees. It is corrected with a pelleted diet, portion control, and exercise through a larger cage, more than one feeding station and climbing perches.

## Respiratory and Bacterial Infections

Wheezing, breathing difficulty, and discharge point here. Psittacosis specifically is worth knowing about because it's zoonotic, transmissible to people. It is caused by the bacterium *Chlamydia psittaci*, which sick birds and apparently healthy ones shed in droppings and secretions, and people usually catch it by breathing dust from dried droppings or secretions. In people, symptoms typically start 5 to 14 days after exposure: a dry cough, fever and chills, headache and muscle aches. Anyone in the house who gets them after exposure to a bird should tell their doctor about the bird. In the bird, bright lime-green droppings and urates are one sign, and a confirmed case is treated with a long course of doxycycline, which the [bird quarantine guide](/blog/bird-quarantine-guide/) covers along with the testing that keeps it out of the house. Always see a vet.

## Polyomavirus

Mainly affects young birds, causing feather abnormalities and other symptoms, and lovebirds are among the species commonly affected when young. Adults generally resist the disease but can shed the virus for up to 90 days, which is why a healthy-looking adult still goes through quarantine. A vaccine exists and is worth discussing with an avian vet, especially if you're acquiring a young bird or planning to keep multiple birds. The studied schedule gave the first dose at 35 days of age and a booster 2 to 3 weeks later, and the [avian polyomavirus guide](/blog/avian-polyomavirus-guide/) has the detail.

## Other Conditions the Species Is Prone To
Beyond the list above, giardiasis, avian gastric yeast, polyfolliculitis, poxvirus and so-called lovebird eye disease all turn up in the species. Lovebird eye disease is marked by discharge from the eyes, depression and weight loss. A bird that keeps eating and still loses weight is the pattern the [avian gastric yeast guide](/blog/avian-gastric-yeast-guide/) describes.
## Watch for Hidden Illness

Because lovebirds mask symptoms so effectively, any sudden change, lethargy, appetite loss, fluffed feathers held constantly, or a shift in normal behavior, is worth a prompt vet visit rather than a wait-and-see approach. Subtle is often all the warning you get.

Weight is the measurement that moves first. A healthy adult weighs about 40 to 60 g (1.5 to 2 oz), though the LafeberVet sheet gives 45 to 70 g with an average of 50 g, so the bird's own steady number matters more than either range. A kitchen scale that reads in grams, used every day before breakfast with each reading written down, catches a loss days or weeks before the bird looks thin, and the [sexing, weight and body condition guide](/blog/bird-sexing-weight-body-condition-guide/) shows how.
