# How to Handle a Green Iguana (And Why So Many Get Surrendered)

Green iguanas can become genuinely calm, interactive animals with consistent handling from a young age, but the honest picture also includes real physical risk from an adult, and a surrender rate that's worth understanding before you buy. For the full care picture, see our complete [Green Iguana care guide](/guides/green-iguana/).

## The size problem, stated plainly

An adult male green iguana reaches 6 to 7 feet including tail and up to 20 pounds, a completely different animal from the small, manageable baby most people bring home. This mirrors a theme already covered on this site with the [savannah monitor](/blog/savannah-monitor-handling-guide/) and [Argentine tegu](/blog/argentine-tegu-handling-guide/), all three are large lizards commonly surrendered when owners discover the adult doesn't match what they signed up for. The iguana-specific twist: unlike the omnivorous monitor or the dog-like, trainable tegu, an iguana is strictly herbivorous and, notably, can become genuinely more dangerous during breeding season.

## Three real physical risks

- **Claws.** Sharp and constantly used for climbing, capable of deep scratches during normal handling, not just defensive struggling.
- **Tail-whipping.** A real defensive strike, not a minor flinch. Large iguanas direct tail lashes at whatever's nearest, including a keeper's face, hard enough to cause welts and bruising.
- **Tail autotomy.** Like many lizards, the tail can drop under grasping pressure, even fairly light pressure. Never grab or restrain by the tail.

<FunFact>
Green iguanas have a pale scale on top of the head called the parietal eye, a genuine third eye with a lens and a retina. It's wired to the pineal gland rather than to vision, and its job is detecting shadows moving overhead, an early warning system for birds of prey that's been with this species far longer than captivity has.
</FunFact>

## Breeding season changes everything, temporarily

This is genuinely worth knowing in advance. Testosterone-driven aggression in mature males is real and can transform a previously calm, handleable iguana into an unpredictable one for a period each year, head-bobbing, color intensifying toward orange or red, and territorial chasing or biting. Approaching from directly above triggers a strong defensive response too, it reads as an aerial predator attack to the iguana, approach from the side instead.

## First-timer mistakes

- Buying a $30 baby without budgeting for what a 6-foot adult actually needs.
- Grabbing the tail during handling.
- Approaching from overhead instead of the side.
- Housing two males together, expect real fighting.

## The honest recommendation

If you're drawn to this species, go in with eyes open about the adult size and the real, if temporary, behavioral shift breeding season brings. This is not a species where the cute baby predicts the adult experience, and that mismatch is exactly why iguanas show up so often at reptile rescues.
