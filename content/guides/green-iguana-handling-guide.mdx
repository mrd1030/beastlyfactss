# How to Handle a Green Iguana (And Why So Many Get Surrendered)

Green iguanas can become calm, interactive animals with consistent handling from a young age, but the honest picture also includes real physical risk from an adult, and a surrender rate that's worth understanding before you buy.

## The size problem, stated plainly

An adult male green iguana reaches 6 to 7 feet including tail and up to 20 pounds, a completely different animal from the small, manageable baby most people bring home. This mirrors a theme that runs through the other big lizards, the [savannah monitor](/blog/savannah-monitor-handling-guide/) and the [Argentine tegu](/blog/argentine-tegu-handling-guide/), all three are large lizards commonly surrendered when owners discover the adult doesn't match what they signed up for. The iguana-specific twist: unlike the omnivorous monitor or the dog-like, trainable tegu, an iguana is strictly herbivorous and, notably, can become genuinely more dangerous during breeding season.

## Four real physical risks

- **Claws.** Sharp and constantly used for climbing, capable of deep scratches during normal handling, not just defensive struggling.
- **Tail-whipping.** A real defensive strike, not a minor flinch. Large iguanas use the tail as a whip and aim the lashes at a person's eyes. An adult's tail can do severe injury, the same as its claws and teeth.
- **Bites.** A frightened iguana, or a mature male in breeding season, will bite. If one breaks the skin, stop the bleeding with direct pressure, check that no tooth is left in the wound, and wash it under warm running water for five minutes. See a doctor for redness, warmth, swelling, pus or fever in the days after, or if your last tetanus shot was more than five years ago.
- **Tail autotomy.** Like many lizards, the tail can drop under grasping pressure, even fairly light pressure. Never grab or restrain by the tail.

<FunFact>
Green iguanas have a pale scale on top of the head called the parietal eye, a genuine third eye with a lens and a retina. It's wired to the pineal gland rather than to vision, and its job is detecting shadows moving overhead, an early warning system for birds of prey that's been with this species far longer than captivity has.
</FunFact>

## Breeding season changes everything, temporarily

This is genuinely worth knowing in advance. Testosterone-driven aggression in mature males is real and can transform a previously calm, handleable iguana into an unpredictable one for a period each year, head-bobbing, color intensifying toward orange or red, and territorial chasing or biting. Approaching from directly above triggers a strong defensive response too, it reads as an aerial predator attack to the iguana, approach from the side instead.

Males reach sexual maturity at about 18 months when they have been fed and housed well, and as late as 3 to 6 years when they have not, so the first season can arrive while the owner still thinks of the animal as a juvenile. After that it returns at the same time every year, so a keeper can mark it on the calendar. During it, keep handling to what care requires and wear long sleeves for enclosure work.

Castration is the veterinary option, and timing decides whether it helps. In a 10-month study of 16 male green iguanas by Brad Lock, R. Avery Bennett and Timothy Gross, males castrated in September, before the season, were significantly less aggressive through the December and January breeding months than intact controls. Males castrated in December, mid-season, showed only a nonsignificant trend. A keeper considering surgery books it for late summer.

## Telling Males From Females

Adult males are the larger animals, with bigger dorsal spines, a larger dewlap under the jaw, and larger femoral pores, the row of openings under the thighs that produce a waxy secretion. A mature male also shows a hemipenal bulge at the base of the tail, and a breeding male may take on an orange tinge.

## Taming takes months

A new iguana learns people before it learns hands. Do the daily enclosure maintenance around it without touching it, and talk while working so it gets used to a voice. Once it eats regularly and stops hiding, offer greens by hand, then light scratches on the back and neck. Lift from below and support as much of the body as possible, holding loosely without pinning, so nothing hangs by the tail. Supervised time out of the enclosure on a sturdy perch belongs in the process from the start.

## First-timer mistakes

- Buying a $30 baby without budgeting for what a 6-foot adult actually needs.
- Grabbing the tail during handling.
- Approaching from overhead instead of the side.
- Housing two males together, which leads to real fighting. Hatchlings group up in the wild, but adults are territorial.

## The honest recommendation

If you're drawn to this species, go in with eyes open about the adult size and the temporary behavioral shift breeding season brings. This is not a species where the cute baby predicts the adult experience, and that mismatch is exactly why iguanas show up so often at reptile rescues.
