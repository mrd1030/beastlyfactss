A fennec fox weighs about three pounds. It is the smallest canid alive, it comes from the Sahara, and it has never been implicated in a rabies outbreak in North America because there has never been a wild population here to have one.

None of that matters much to the law. Twenty-eight of the forty-two jurisdictions we have read restrict it, and in almost every case the reason is that the rule says "fox" or "Canidae" and stops there.

The split is worth noticing on its own. New York State, Texas, Ohio and Minnesota all place no restriction on a fennec fox while banning or permitting the serval, because their cat rules are drawn by family and their canid rules are drawn by species and aimed at wolves.

## Maryland Does It in One Word

The shortest ban in the matrix belongs to Maryland, which prohibits possession of a:

> fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear

No genus, no qualifier, no exception. Four common nouns. A fennec fox is a fox, so a fennec fox is prohibited.

That list is not really about exotic pets at all. Fox, skunk and raccoon are the three principal terrestrial rabies reservoirs in the eastern United States, and rules like this were written to stop people keeping wild-caught local animals that might be incubating rabies. The fennec gets caught because it shares a common name with the animal the rule was aimed at.

Kentucky is more precise and reaches the same place. 301 KAR 2:082 lists prohibited exotic wildlife by genus:

> Fox (Genus Cerdocyon, Genus Lycalopex, Genus Otocyon, Genus Urocyon, and Genus Vulpes);

*Vulpes zerda* sits in the last genus named, alongside the red fox and the arctic fox.

Nevada manages to name it twice. NAC 503.110 prohibits "all species in the genera Vulpes, Fennecus," and *Fennecus* is the older genus name for this species before it was moved into *Vulpes*. Whichever taxonomy you use, Nevada has it.

## The Rabies Problem Is the Real Story

The reason so many of these rules are absolute rather than permit-based comes down to a single fact that applies nationally: **there is no USDA licensed rabies vaccine for any fox species.**

Rabies vaccines are licensed per species, on the basis of challenge trials in that species. Dogs, cats, ferrets, horses, cattle and sheep have licensed vaccines. Foxes do not. A veterinarian can give a fennec a dog vaccine off label, and many do, but no health department can treat that animal as vaccinated.

The practical consequence is that if a fennec fox bites someone, the standard protocol for an unvaccinated wild carnivore applies, and that protocol ends with euthanasia and testing of the brain. It is why Rhode Island's rule bars the Department from granting any private person a permit for an exotic carnivore, and it is the single most important thing to understand before buying one, quite apart from where you live.

<FunFact>
Iowa's dangerous wild animal statute catches the fennec fox in a paragraph about "wolves, coyotes, and jackals," with the only written exception being the domestic dog. New Hampshire's controlled species table is blunter still: the entry reads "Fox, Gray; Fenec," pairing a North American native with a Saharan species under one row and one permittee category, and misspelling the second. The category is 5, exhibitor. There is no category for a private individual.
</FunFact>

## Where It Sits in the Permit States

Seven jurisdictions will consider a private permit, and the reasoning differs.

Virginia catches it with unusual clarity. Its permit table restricts "Canidae, All Species," and the common name column spells out "Wild Dogs, Wolves, Coyotes or Coyote hybrids, Jackals and Foxes," so the fennec is caught by the same row as a jackal and needs a special permit to import, possess or sell.

Idaho's Department of Agriculture rule reaches "All Non-native Canidae Species," which is about as clean a fit as the fennec ever gets.

South Carolina treats it as a furbearer. Wyoming has no fox on its prohibited list at all, which drops the animal into a discretionary category where a permit "may be authorized."

Maine and Arkansas both arrive by omission again. Maine's unrestricted carnivore list is one animal, the domestic ferret. Arkansas's unrestricted mammal list has that same ferret plus wolf and dog hybrids, and nothing else in the order.

## Montana Bans It and Clears the Serval

Montana produces the strangest single comparison in this matrix. Its noncontrolled species rule names the serval, a forty-pound African cat, as legal to keep as a pet without a permit. It names no canid at all, and because Montana runs a white list, that omission is a prohibition.

So in Montana you may keep a serval and you may not keep a fennec fox. Nothing about the two animals explains that. It is entirely an artifact of which species happened to be considered when the list was drawn up in 2005 and last amended in 2010.

## The Practical Answer

Fourteen states place no restriction on this animal, which is a better picture than the serval gets but well short of a clear yes nationally. Check your county and city as well: exotic animal ordinances catch foxes routinely, often in the same clause as skunks and raccoons for the same rabies reason.

Then find a veterinarian before you buy, not after. Ask specifically whether they will see a fennec fox, what they will do about rabies vaccination, and what happens if the animal bites someone. If you cannot get satisfactory answers to those three questions, the legal status where you live is not the binding constraint.
