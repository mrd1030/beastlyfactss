Imagine an animal the size of a medium dog that will aggressively defend a carcass against animals many times its size, haul chunks of a caribou away through deep snow to cache them, and range hundreds of miles without rest. That animal is real, it lives in the frozen wilderness of the Northern Hemisphere, and its name is the wolverine. Small in stature but absolutely enormous in attitude, the wolverine is one of the most jaw-dropping creatures on the planet.

## Built Like a Tank, Fights Like a Champion

The wolverine (Gulo gulo) is the largest land-dwelling member of the weasel family, yet it rarely weighs more than 40 pounds. Despite that modest size, wolverines defend carcasses aggressively. The line you will find everywhere, that they drive cougars, bears and wolf packs off kills, is worth pausing on, because it appears to come from a single uncited sentence on one reference site and then propagate. It does not hold up. A camera study of 58 puma kills logged 20 scavenger species and no wolverine, and when researchers looked at wolverines around lynx they found avoidance rather than dominance. Alaska Dept. of Fish and Game puts it flatly: a wolverine will not attack a larger predator. Wolves and bears, meanwhile, are genuine predators of wolverines. Alaska Dept. of Fish and Game reports that researchers have never observed a wolverine chase off a bear, and a 2023 study in Ecology and Evolution found a single wolf pack killed three of four collared wolverines in Alaska. Their jaws are powerful enough to crush frozen bone, and their thick, muscular bodies are built to absorb punishment. Wildlife researchers consistently cite the wolverine as one of the most ferocious animals alive relative to body weight.

## A Territory the Size of a Small Country

Wolverines are wanderers on a truly epic scale. A single male wolverine can patrol a home range of up to 240 square miles, which researchers describe as large for the animal's body size, though nobody has actually ranked wolverines against all land mammals on that measure. They mark that range mostly with urine and by rubbing a gland on the belly along the ground; there are scent glands on the footpads too. The anal glands near the tail base, the ones everyone reaches for, appear to be defensive rather than territorial. The musk is real, then, though its reputation outruns it: the one chemical analysis of wolverine anal gland secretion found none of the pungent thietanes that give skunks and other mustelids their carrying power, and mostly short-chain fatty acids instead. It marks territory from a considerable distance. Their territory is not just large, it is aggressively defended.

## A Nose That Sees Through Snow

Wolverines can smell prey buried under snow, and the number attached to that is one of the least reliable in popular nature writing. Twenty feet is the figure you will usually see, including in earlier versions of this article. It traces to a single PBS web page from 2010 that asserts it without a source. The Forest Service has said six feet. Other outlets say two metres. When the numbers disagree by a factor of three, none of them is a measurement. What an Alaska state biologist will tell you is that wolverines have a really good nose and can smell food buried well under the snow, with no depth attached, and that is as far as the evidence goes. Using it, they locate and dig out animals killed by avalanches or simply buried by winter snowpack. They will excavate several feet of compacted snow to reach a frozen carcass, making them one of the most efficient cold-weather scavengers on Earth. This superpower is a key reason they thrive in some of the harshest environments on Earth.

## Fur That Laughs at Frost

A wolverine's coat is the stuff of legend, and not just because it looks incredible. The fur is uniquely structured to resist frost and moisture. Ice and snow do not cling to it the way they do to other animal coats, which means wolverines can move through blizzard conditions without their fur becoming a frozen, heavy burden. Indigenous peoples of the Arctic have long prized wolverine fur for lining the hoods of parkas for exactly this reason.

## Gulo Gulo: The Glutton

<FunFact>
The wolverine’s scientific name, Gulo gulo, literally means “glutton” in Latin, and the animal has absolutely earned it. Wolverines are opportunistic omnivores that will eat almost anything: carrion, small mammals, birds, eggs, berries, and roots. When food is plentiful, they gorge themselves. When it is scarce, they can travel enormous distances to find it. They are also famous for caching food, burying surplus meals in the snow or under rocks to retrieve later. Nothing goes to waste in wolverine country.
</FunFact>

## The Ultimate Pound-for-Pound Champion

Boxing fans love the pound-for-pound ranking, and if the animal kingdom had one, the wolverine would be a serious contender for the top spot. Relative to their body weight, wolverines possess extraordinary muscular strength, bone-crushing bite force, and near-reckless aggression. What they do not do, despite the stories, is pick fights with animals that outclass them. The wolverine's real edge is refusing to be driven off something it has already claimed, in country where nothing else wants to work that hard. That is a narrower talent than the legend and a more useful one.

## Surprising Facts You Probably Did Not Know

Wolverines are full of surprises beyond their famous ferocity. Here are some lesser-known facts that make them even more remarkable:

- Wolverine claws are often called semi-retractable, and state agencies describe them that way, but the biologists at The Wolverine Foundation say the toe biomechanics only make them look that way and anatomically the claws are fixed. The cat comparison does not hold.
- Despite their fierce reputation, wolverines are largely solitary, coming together briefly to mate. Males are not entirely absent afterwards though: there is evidence that fathers spend time with their offspring, particularly once the young are past about eight months.
- A typical litter contains just two or three kits, born in late winter inside a snow den that the mother excavates herself.
- Wolverines live roughly 5 to 7 years in the wild, though individuals in captivity have reached 15 to 17 years, with the record standing at around 19.5 years.
- They were listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act following a USFWS final rule in November 2023, a status reflecting ongoing habitat loss driven by climate change, which is reducing the deep snowpack they depend on for denning.

## The Legend Is Real

<KeyTakeaway>
Low population density is the wolverine's defining problem, and it is not alone in that. [Every wild tiger left on Earth would fit inside a small town](/blog/every-wild-tiger-would-fit-in-a-small-town/), which is a useful reminder of how thin these numbers get for animals that need a lot of space.
</KeyTakeaway>

The wolverine is not a myth, not a comic book invention, and not an exaggeration. It is a real, living animal that has carved out a life in some of the most unforgiving places on Earth through sheer toughness, intelligence, and an almost supernatural refusal to quit. The next time someone calls something “wolverine tough,” know that the bar is set extraordinarily high. Few animals in the history of life on this planet have earned their reputation quite so completely.
