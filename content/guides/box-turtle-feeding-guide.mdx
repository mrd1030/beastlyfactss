# Box Turtle Feeding: The Diet That Changes With Age

<VetDisclaimer />

A box turtle does not eat the same way at forty as it did at four. VCA Animal Hospitals describes young turtles, up to roughly four to six years, as primarily carnivores, with adults tending toward herbivorous. That shift is the single most important thing about feeding this species, because a keeper who settles into one routine at the hatchling stage is feeding the wrong diet by the time the animal is grown.

## The Ratio, and Where the Sources Part

VCA gives a working guideline: "your box turtle's diet should be about 50% plant-based material and 50% animal-based material."

Wildwood Veterinary Hospital pushes the adult end further toward plants: "A majority (60-70%) of the adult's diet should consist of a variety of fresh dark leafy greens such as collard, mustard, and dandelion greens", with animal protein making up the remainder.

Both are veterinary sources and both are describing the same animal, so the honest reading is that the target is a band, not a point. A 50/50 adult sits inside VCA's guidance, a 65/35 adult sits inside Wildwood's, and neither is a mistake. What both agree on is direction: the plant share climbs as the turtle ages, and dark leafy greens carry it.

## How Often

Here the two part again, and the gap is worth knowing before setting a routine.

VCA: "Most young turtles eat daily, while mature, adult turtles can be fed either daily or every other day."

Wildwood: "Adults need to be fed every two to three days, while juveniles require daily feeding."

They agree completely on juveniles and disagree on adults, where VCA's slowest interval is Wildwood's fastest. An adult fed every other day is inside both. An adult fed daily is inside VCA's guidance alone, and for a species where obesity and overfeeding are ordinary problems, the slower end is the safer default.

## What Goes in the Bowl

Dark leafy greens are the base: collard, mustard and dandelion greens, with kale and bell peppers on VCA's acceptable list. Wildwood recommends mixing those with coarsely chopped vegetables, naming carrots, squash, green beans and broccoli, and adds a detail worth keeping: "Avoid regularly feeding frozen vegetables as the freezing process breaks down vital nutrients."

Fruit is the small end. VCA keeps it under 10% of intake and names figs, apricots and berries; Wildwood calls apples, cantaloupe, melon and berries an occasional treat to be fed in moderation.

For the animal share, VCA names crickets, grasshoppers, mealworms, earthworms and hard-boiled eggs, and adds that "Commercially available reptile pellets are an excellent protein source." Wildwood's list runs mealworms, kingworms, earthworms, slugs and snails, small pinky mice, or crickets.

One caution on collecting your own: Wildwood warns against worms from backyard or garden soil, which "can contain bacteria, parasites, and toxins."

## The Greens to Keep Off the Base

Two separate problems get collapsed into one warning on most care sheets, and they work differently.

Oxalates bind minerals. VCA names Swiss chard, spinach and beet greens as containing oxalates that inhibit mineral absorption. Goitrogens affect the thyroid, and VCA names cabbage, kale and mustard greens in excess.

Note that kale and mustard greens appear on the recommended list and in the goitrogen warning on the same page. That is not a contradiction, it is a quantity instruction: rotate them through, do not build the salad on them.

Iceberg lettuce and celery sit in a third category, named for poor nutritional content. They are not harmful so much as pointless, displacing food that would have done something.

Dog food and cat food are ruled out plainly. VCA: "Dog food and cat food are a poor nutritional source for box turtles."

## Calcium and Vitamins

The two schedules differ enough to matter, and both are defensible.

VCA dusts vegetables with a calcium powder two to three times per week, and adds a reptile multivitamin-mineral powder weekly.

Wildwood dusts food with calcium once weekly, and is specific about the product: a minimum calcium to phosphorus ratio of 2:1, avoiding preparations with added vitamin D.

The disagreement is about frequency, not about whether supplementation is needed. A keeper following either is supplementing; a keeper following neither is running the deficiency that produces the shell and bone problems on the [health issues guide](/blog/box-turtle-health-issues-guide/).

Supplements also do not work alone. Dietary calcium and usable [UVB exposure](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) are two halves of the same system, and a turtle getting the powder without the light still ends up short.
