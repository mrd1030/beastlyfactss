# Box Turtle Feeding: The Diet That Changes With Age

<VetDisclaimer />

A box turtle does not eat the same way at forty as it did at four. Young turtles, up to roughly four to six years, are primarily carnivores, and adults tend toward herbivorous. That shift is the single most important thing about feeding this species, because a keeper who settles into one routine at the hatchling stage is feeding the wrong diet by the time the animal is grown.

## The Ratio, and Where the Sources Part

Two veterinary sources describe the same animal and land in different places. VCA Animal Hospitals gives a working guideline: "your box turtle's diet should be about 50% plant-based material and 50% animal-based material." Wildwood Veterinary Hospital pushes the adult end further toward plants, at "A majority (60-70%) of the adult's diet" as fresh dark leafy greens, with animal protein making up the remainder.

The honest reading is that the target is a band, not a point. A 50/50 adult sits inside VCA's guidance, a 65/35 adult sits inside Wildwood's, and neither is a mistake. What both agree on is direction: the plant share climbs as the turtle ages, and dark leafy greens carry it.

## How Often

Here the two part again, and the gap is worth knowing before setting a routine.

One allows "either daily or every other day" for a mature adult. The other says "Adults need to be fed every two to three days."

They agree completely on juveniles, which eat daily either way, and disagree on adults, where the first source's slowest interval is the second's fastest. An adult fed every other day is inside both. An adult fed daily is inside only the looser of the two, and for a species where obesity and overfeeding are ordinary problems, the slower end is the safer default.

## What Goes in the Bowl

Dark leafy greens are the base: collard, mustard and dandelion, with kale and bell peppers acceptable alongside them. Mix those with coarsely chopped vegetables, carrots, squash, green beans and broccoli. Skip frozen vegetables as a routine item, since freezing breaks down some of what they carry.

Fruit is the small end, under 10% of intake. Figs, apricots, berries, apple, cantaloupe and melon all work as an occasional item.

The animal share can come from crickets, grasshoppers, mealworms, kingworms, earthworms, slugs and snails, small pinky mice, or hard-boiled egg. Commercial reptile pellets are a good protein source too.

One caution on collecting your own: worms out of backyard or garden soil "can contain bacteria, parasites, and toxins."

## The Greens to Keep Off the Base

Two separate problems get collapsed into one warning on most care sheets, and they work differently.

Oxalates bind minerals: Swiss chard, spinach and beet greens carry them, and they inhibit mineral absorption. Goitrogens affect the thyroid, and cabbage, kale and mustard greens carry those in quantity.

Note that kale and mustard greens turn up on the recommended list and in the goitrogen warning, on the same veterinary page. That is not a contradiction, it is a quantity instruction: rotate them through, do not build the salad on them.

Iceberg lettuce and celery sit in a third category, poor nutritional content. They are not harmful so much as pointless, displacing food that would have done something.

Dog food and cat food are ruled out plainly, as "a poor nutritional source for box turtles."

## Calcium and Vitamins

The two schedules differ enough to matter, and both are defensible.

Use calcium with D3, dusted on food two or three times a week, plus a reptile multivitamin-mineral powder weekly. That is the schedule the rest of this site's box turtle pages are built around, and it is the one to follow unless a vet says otherwise.

A second veterinary source runs lighter, dusting once weekly and preferring a supplement with a minimum 2:1 calcium to phosphorus ratio and no added vitamin D. The difference is really about D3, and D3 is about light: a turtle outdoors in real sunlight makes its own, while one indoors under a UVB lamp of uncertain age may not. If the turtle lives outside in the sun for much of the year, the lighter schedule is defensible. If it lives indoors, use the first one.

What both agree on is that supplementation is not optional. A keeper following neither schedule is running the deficiency that produces the shell and bone problems on the [health issues guide](/blog/box-turtle-health-issues-guide/).

Supplements also do not work alone. Dietary calcium and usable [UVB exposure](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) are two halves of the same system, and a turtle getting the powder without the light still ends up short.
