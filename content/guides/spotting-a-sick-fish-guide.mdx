# How to Tell If Your Fish Is Sick: Waste, Behavior, and Body Signs

<VetDisclaimer />

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most fish don't announce that something is wrong. There's no whimper, no limp, no obvious wound most of the time, just a series of small, easy-to-miss changes that add up over a day or two. A keeper who checks the tank once in the morning and once at night is working from incomplete information by design, since fish hide illness as a survival instinct, and the animal that looks fine at 8 a.m. can be clamped and hiding by dinner.

This guide is about noticing early rather than treating late. It covers the three things you can actually observe without touching the fish, its waste, its behavior, and its body, and it puts the most important piece of context first: a large share of what looks like disease is actually a water quality problem, and testing the water is the first move, not the last resort. What to actually do about a confirmed illness, including quarantine and the hospital tank, is covered in our [fish quarantine and treatment guide](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/). This article's job is spotting the problem, not treating it.

## Test the Water Before You Do Anything Else

Here's the framing that should sit ahead of every other section in this guide: if a fish looks off, test the water before you reach for a diagnosis, and definitely before you reach for medication. Ammonia and nitrite are the two most common invisible culprits, and both are completely undetectable by eye. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that unionized ammonia above roughly 0.05 mg/L is already enough to damage gill tissue, that lethargy and poor appetite follow chronic exposure, and that acute ammonia toxicity can produce neurologic signs like spinning and disorientation, symptoms that look a lot like an exotic disease to an owner who hasn't tested the water. Nitrite works differently but ends in a similar place: it converts hemoglobin into methemoglobin, a process Merck's manual calls "brown blood disease," and affected fish will seek out oxygen-rich water, congregating near filter outflows or gasping at the surface, exactly the same behavior you'd see from a fish that's simply short on oxygen.

That overlap matters because gasping at the surface is one of the most commonly misread signs in the hobby. It's easy to assume a fish gulping air at the top of the tank has a respiratory infection or a gill parasite. Far more often, it's plain physics: the University of Florida's IFAS program states that dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L supports optimum fish health, that most species become distressed once levels drop to roughly 2 to 4 mg/L, and that mortality typically follows below about 2 mg/L, with surface gasping, sometimes called piping, as the visible warning sign along the way. A fish piping at the surface after a heat wave, a power outage that stopped the filter, or an overstocked, overfed tank is telling you about the water, not about a pathogen.

New setups carry a version of this risk that's worth naming directly, since it catches so many new keepers off guard. Merck's manual describes "new tank syndrome," in which ammonia rises through the first two to three weeks of a new system because the biofilter's bacteria haven't finished establishing, a period made worse by overfeeding and overstocking. If a fish in a tank that's been running less than a month suddenly looks sick, the nitrogen cycle is the first suspect, not disease. Our [aquarium cycling guide](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) walks through what that establishing biofilter is actually doing and how to avoid crashing it.

The practical upshot is simple: keep a liquid test kit on hand and use it the moment something looks wrong, before you assume the worst or start treating for something specific. ## Reading Fish Waste: The Earliest Signal

Once the water checks out, waste is one of the few internal health signals a keeper can actually see without a microscope or a vet visit. Normal fish feces varies by species and diet, generally a solid, somewhat compact strand that roughly matches the color of what the fish has been eating and doesn't linger, trailing behind the fish for more than a few seconds before it drops or breaks off.

The signal worth paying attention to is a shift toward pale, clear, or white feces that trails behind the fish in a long, stringy thread rather than dropping normally. This is a real early-warning sign, but it is not, on its own, a diagnosis, and it's worth being honest about why. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes "mucoid fecal casts" as a clinical sign in infectious hematopoietic necrosis, a viral disease, while separately listing weight loss and a "pot belly" appearance as signs of internal worms like Capillaria and Pseudocapillaria, and weight loss and anorexia as signs of the intestinal parasite Spironucleus. In other words, abnormal, mucus-heavy feces shows up across several unrelated causes in the veterinary literature, not one single culprit. A fish that has simply stopped eating for a stretch, for any reason including a water quality problem, will often pass mostly mucus rather than digested food, which looks identical to a parasite-driven case from the outside.

That's the honest limit of reading waste at home: pale, stringy trailing feces tells you something is worth watching, most plausibly a fish that isn't eating well or is carrying an internal parasite load, but it doesn't tell you which. What it should trigger is closer observation over the next day or two, not an immediate treatment decision. Is the fish still eating at all? Is it losing visible weight or developing a pinched, sunken belly? Is anything else on this list showing up alongside it? A single odd dropping in an otherwise normal, eating, active fish is worth noting and rechecking, not panicking over. A pattern of it across several days, especially paired with weight loss or appetite loss, is the point where our [fish quarantine and treatment guide](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) becomes the next step, since isolating a sick fish and observing it closely in a hospital setup is the right response to a real pattern, not a single stool.

<KeyTakeaway>
Stringy white or pale feces is genuinely useful as an early flag, but it's a nonspecific one. It shows up with internal parasites, with viral disease, and with fish that have simply gone off their food. Treat it as a reason to watch closely, not as a diagnosis to treat.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Behavior Signs That Point to Trouble

Behavior changes tend to show up before anything visible does, because a fish can feel unwell long before a mark or growth appears on its body. The Merck Veterinary Manual's own descriptions cover most of them. It notes that "fish often rub against objects to relieve itching," a behavior it calls flashing or scratching, describes piping as "swimming near the surface of the water, trying to gulp air," and lists loss of appetite, loss of condition, dulled coloration and rapid breathing among the signs that something is wrong. Clamped fins comes from the same manual's parasitic disease page, which gives "lethargy, clamped fins, and dark coloration" as the clinical signs of ich.

Each of those has a slightly different likely meaning. Clamped fins, held tight against the body instead of spread naturally, are one of the more universal signs of stress or illness across species; Merck's manual lists clamped fins specifically among the signs of ich (white spot disease), but it appears well beyond that one parasite too. Flashing or rubbing against decor, gravel, or the glass usually points toward an irritant on the skin or gills, commonly an external parasite, though it can also follow simple water chemistry irritation. Gasping at the surface deserves a second mention here specifically because it's so often misread: as covered above, it's more often a dissolved oxygen or ammonia and nitrite problem than a disease process, so it's the one behavior sign that should send you to a test kit before anything else.

A fish that hangs persistently in the current near a filter outflow or airstone, rather than moving through the tank normally, is often chasing the same thing a piping fish is chasing: the most oxygen-rich water available. Merck's description of nitrite-stressed fish "congregating in oxygen rich areas" applies just as well to fish under general water-quality stress, so this behavior belongs in the same "test the water" bucket as gasping.

Loss of appetite and isolation round out the behavioral picture. Merck's general list of fish illness signs includes "not eating" and abnormal swimming positions, floating, drifting, or resting somewhere a fish normally wouldn't, as core indicators, alongside outright lethargy. A normally social, mid-water fish that starts hiding behind decor or sitting alone at the bottom, even briefly, is worth a closer look the same day rather than a "let's see how it looks tomorrow." Abnormal buoyancy, a fish that can't hold a normal position in the water column, floats nose-down, or struggles to stay off the surface or the substrate, is a related sign that points toward an internal problem, whether that's a swim bladder issue, bloating, or something systemic; our [goldfish health issues guide](/blog/goldfish-health-issues-guide/) covers swim bladder disorder in more depth for the species where it shows up most often.

## Physical Signs You Can See From Outside the Tank

Physical, visible signs are the ones most keepers wait for, but by the time several of these appear together, a fish has usually been unwell for a while. Merck's general overview of fish illness lists loss of color, discoloration or splotchiness, spots, ulcers, loss of scales, scales standing out from the body, swelling or bloating, and puffy or fuzzy growths as the core visible signs worth tracking.

White spots, small and evenly distributed across the body and fins, are the hallmark of ich, and Merck's manual pairs that sign with lethargy, clamped fins, and dark coloration in affected fish. Greyish-white, cotton-like growths describe fungal infection, most often Saprolegnia, typically appearing on damaged skin, a healing wound, or already-stressed tissue rather than on a fish with intact, healthy skin. Ulcers or red, inflamed patches on the body point toward a bacterial process, and fin erosion, fins that look shortened, ragged, or reddened at the edges compared to how the fish normally looks, follows a similar bacterial pattern, generally worsening from the fin's edge inward if left unaddressed. Our [betta fish health issues guide](/blog/betta-fish-health-issues-guide/) covers fin rot specifically, since long-finned species show it clearly and often.

Bloating and popeye both point toward something happening internally rather than on the surface. A swollen abdomen can mean overfeeding and simple digestive backup, dropsy-like fluid retention, or in some cases the internal worms discussed in the waste section above, since Merck describes a "pot belly" appearance alongside Capillaria and Pseudocapillaria infections. Popeye, one or both eyes visibly protruding or enlarged, is associated with bacterial infection in Merck's overview and, like most of the signs on this list, isn't specific enough on its own to name the cause without a closer look. Color loss, a fish looking washed out, paler, or duller than its normal coloration, is listed by Merck as a general illness indicator that often shows up before anything more specific does, making it one of the more useful "something's wrong, keep watching" signs precisely because it isn't tied to one disease.

## When It's a Vet or Aquatic Specialist Situation

Most of the signs above are meant to prompt closer observation and, where relevant, a trip to the [fish quarantine and treatment guide](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) for next steps once water quality is ruled out. Some situations are past that point.

Call in an aquatic veterinarian or a fish-health specialist when illness is spreading across multiple fish in the tank rather than staying isolated to one, when you're looking at an open ulcer, significant bloating, or a badly protruding eye rather than a milder sign like clamped fins, when fin erosion or a white-spot outbreak is visibly advancing day over day despite a clean water test, or when a fish is listing to one side, resting on the bottom unresponsive, or otherwise showing signs well beyond the early behavioral changes covered here. These are the cases where guessing at a cause and treating for it at home risks losing time on the wrong problem, and where the lab work is the only way to move from a nonspecific sign to an actual answer. Merck is direct that for many of these parasites, "microscopic examination of diseased tissue is required to confirm the diagnosis," which is not something an owner can do by looking at the tank.

It's also worth calling a specialist, rather than reaching for a general medication, whenever the honest answer to "what caused this" is "I don't know, and the water tested clean." Reading a stringy dropping, a clamped fin, or a pale patch correctly means recognizing what those signs can mean, not pretending any one of them, on its own, tells you what's wrong. That's the whole case for observing carefully and testing the water before treating: the earlier and more accurately a keeper reads these signs, the less guessing anyone has to do once real treatment starts.
