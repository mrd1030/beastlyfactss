# Cherry Shrimp Feeding: Diet, Portions, and the Overfeeding Trap

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cherry shrimp mostly feed themselves. In an established, planted tank, a colony spends most of its day picking biofilm and algae off every surface it can reach, which is exactly why it's so easy to overfeed one on purpose without realizing how little it actually needs on top of that. For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Cherry shrimp care guide](/guides/cherry-shrimp/).

## The Staple Diet: Mostly Grazing, Lightly Supplemented

Cherry shrimp are omnivorous scavengers. [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/cherry-shrimp-care) and [Aquariadise](https://www.aquariadise.com/caresheet-cherry-shrimp/) both describe algae, biofilm, and detritus as the backbone of their diet in a mature tank, supplemented with commercial shrimp or sinking foods, and occasional blanched vegetables like zucchini or spinach. That grazing habit is a big part of why cherry shrimp shouldn't go into a brand-new tank, our [tank setup guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-tank-setup-guide/) covers why a tank needs real biological maturity, not just a finished nitrogen cycle, before shrimp go in.

On top of that natural grazing, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00025JZAY?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Sinking Wafers for Catfish, Loaches and Bottom Feeders, 3.88 oz">sinking wafer food</AffiliateLink> a couple of times a week rounds out the diet with protein and plant matter shrimp can't get from grazing alone. It's formulated for bottom feeders generally, catfish and loaches included, but shrimp graze on it readily since it sinks and softens right where they already forage.

<AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">Driftwood</AffiliateLink> and dried Indian almond leaves are worth adding for the same reason. According to [Aquarium Co-Op](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/indian-almond-leaves), a decomposing almond leaf grows a layer of biofilm and infusoria that shrimp and their offspring graze on for days, on top of releasing tannins that gently soften and tint the water. It's a genuinely useful feeding aid disguised as tank decor.

## Portions: Smaller Than You'd Guess

A piece of food no larger than a pea, or a quarter of a small wafer, is enough for a colony of 10 to 20 shrimp. [Shrimp Science](https://www.shrimpscience.com/articles/causes-of-dying-shrimp/) puts the practical rule simply: feed only what the colony clears within 2 to 3 hours, and if food from the last feeding is still sitting there, skip the next one rather than adding more on top of it.

## The Overfeeding Trap

Cherry shrimp themselves won't gorge, but that's exactly what makes overfeeding easy to do by accident: their stomachs are so small that it takes very little leftover food to start decomposing. According to [Shrimp Science](https://www.shrimpscience.com/articles/causes-of-dying-shrimp/), uneaten food breaks down into ammonia and nitrite, both toxic to shrimp at levels that wouldn't bother a lot of fish, while underfeeding mostly just risks lower survival among the tiniest baby shrimp, who lean more heavily on biofilm than adults do. When genuinely unsure, underfeed. A healthy colony grazing an established, planted tank can go a day or two between deliberate feedings without any issue.

## Calcium and Minerals: Feeding for the Molt, Not Just the Meal

Molting is constant for this species. [Shrimp Science](https://www.shrimpscience.com/articles/molting-and-common-problems/) notes adult shrimp molt roughly every 3 to 4 weeks, with younger, faster-growing shrimp shedding as often as every 1 to 2 weeks. Every one of those molts pulls calcium and minerals out of the water and diet to build the next shell, so a calcium-poor diet is a genuine, common cause of a failed or soft molt, covered in more depth in our [health issues guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-health-issues-guide/). A quality sinking food, occasional blanched vegetables, and stable water hardness (more on GH in our [tank setup guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-tank-setup-guide/)) cover most of that need without a dedicated supplement.

<FunFact>
Part of a cherry shrimp's calcium strategy is remarkably direct: it eats its own shed shell. [Shrimp Science](https://www.shrimpscience.com/articles/molting-and-common-problems/) confirms it's safe, and generally a good idea, to leave a molted exoskeleton in the tank for a day or two rather than removing it, since the shrimp that just molted (and its tankmates) will pick it apart to reclaim the calcium and minerals already locked inside it, giving the fresh, soft new shell underneath a real head start on hardening. The only real exception is a tank already dealing with a parasite or bacterial problem, where old shells are better removed just in case.
</FunFact>

## Foods and Habits to Avoid

Skip anything treated with pesticides, live plants from a store are sometimes treated with snail-killing chemicals that are just as deadly to shrimp, our [health issues guide](/blog/cherry-shrimp-health-issues-guide/) covers copper specifically, which shows up in some of those treatments. Avoid dumping in large amounts of vegetable matter at once, and never add more food just because a previous portion is untouched, that's the overfeeding trap described above in miniature.
