# How to Handle a Cockatoo (And Why They're So Often Surrendered)

Cockatoos are extremely social, intelligent, and deeply bonded to their people, affectionate as babies. That same intensity of bonding is also exactly what makes this species one of the most difficult parrots to keep long-term, and one of the most commonly surrendered.

## The Honest Reputation, and Why It's Earned

A parrot behavior consultant who specializes in this exact issue put it plainly: cockatoos are among the most consistently relinquished parrots, and clients with cockatoos make up over half of her entire consulting practice. Rescue organizations echo this directly, cockatoos are frequently described as their most often surrendered bird. This isn't a reputation to dismiss, it reflects a real, well-documented pattern.

## Why This Happens

Cockatoos form unusually intense bonds with their owners, and that intensity drives genuine separation anxiety when the bird is left alone, even briefly. Combined with being extremely loud, screaming and contact-calling are normal cockatoo behaviors, and a real tendency toward feather-destructive behavior, sometimes escalating to self-mutilation, when their emotional and social needs aren't fully met, the day-to-day demands of this species are genuinely higher than most people expect going in. Our [deep dive on cockatoo screaming and feather plucking](/blog/cockatoo-screaming-feather-plucking-explained/) goes further into the psychology behind both behaviors if you want the fuller picture.

<FunFact>
Chicago Exotics puts it plainly: a full work day with no one home is too long for a cockatoo to be alone. For a bird whose separation anxiety starts within minutes, an ordinary nine to five is the problem.
</FunFact>

## Bite Risk

Large cockatoos bite more readily and with less restraint than many other parrots, and a bite from a bird this size can require stitches. Aggression often increases at sexual maturity, roughly 5 to 7 years old, sometimes catching owners of a previously sweet, cuddly bird off guard.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Over-bonding early on, spending intense one-on-one time in the first weeks in a way that cements an unhealthy, anxious dependency rather than a healthy one.
- Inadvertently reinforcing screaming by responding to it with attention, even negative attention.
- Body-petting in ways that mimic mating cues, which can trigger hormonal behavior and aggression.
- Underestimating the daily time commitment. Real engagement with this species means 4 to 6 hours of interaction most days, not an occasional check-in.

## Reading a Cockatoo Before It Bites

The crest is the first thing to read, and it does not mean one thing. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital's cockatoo care sheet lists a raised crest as anything from courtship and curiosity to fear, frustration, "or a warning that a bite will follow soon," and a lowered crest as calm and approachable. LafeberVet's body language handout agrees: the crest goes up for excitement, good or bad.

- **Eye pinning.** The pupils expand and contract to a pinpoint. LafeberVet calls it a sign of strong excitement, good or bad. Keep fingers away from the face until they settle.
- **Tail fanning.** LafeberVet calls a flaring tail a serious warning signal that can often go with aggression. Do not offer a hand.
- **Head lowered and body leaning forward, beak held open, lunging, foot tapping.** All on LafeberVet's excitement-or-aggression list, with the instruction to let the bird calm down first, because an overstimulated parrot can bite as hard as an aggressive one.
- **Feathers held flat, a stiff skinny posture, head high and forward.** That is fear. Find the cause and remove it, or introduce it gradually.
- **Beak grinding, soft fluffed plumage, body flattened out, facial feathers fanned over the beak.** Contentment, and the bird you can pick up.

LafeberVet does not rank these and warns that the clues are not foolproof, and easiest to misread in a bird's first 5 to 10 years. Until the crest comes down and the pupils stop pinning, the bird stays on its perch.

### Over-bonding, and the household rules that prevent it

A cockatoo that is petted like a cat decides it has a mate. Chicago Exotics says these birds often bond to an owner and misidentify that person as a mate, and an owner who fosters that relationship may occasionally end up with a bird that turns aggressive toward the rest of the household. Pamela Clark, the consultant cited above, writes that cockatoos are not cuddly, we are, and that once a cockatoo has formed a pair bond with a person, that person's quality of life often tanks. Her fix is to cut the hours of shoulder and lap time and reward the bird for any independent behavior it shows.

VCA's household version: too much attention may produce an overly dependent bird, so it has to be able to entertain itself, friends and family should work with it the same way you do, and meet different people over time. Chicago Exotics says a full work day with no one home is too long for a cockatoo to be alone.

## How This Compares to Other Large Parrots

Macaws are demanding too, and often have a stronger raw bite force, but cockatoos are generally considered needier, louder, and more prone specifically to separation anxiety and feather-destructive behavior. The [cockatiel](/blog/cockatiel-handling-guide/), technically a small cockatoo, is the clear exception, genuinely one of the easier pet birds to keep well. The [cockatiel and cockatoo side by side](/blog/cockatiel-vs-cockatoo-guide/) is the sharpest version of that gap. The large white cockatoo species are a different proposition entirely.
