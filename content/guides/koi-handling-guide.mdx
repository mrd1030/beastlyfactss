# How to "Handle" Koi (Mostly, You Don't)

Koi are social, hardy, and genuinely food motivated, many learn to recognize their keeper and swim to the surface at feeding time. But this is not a fish you pick up and hold. Physical handling is stressful for koi and carries real risk, both to the fish and, less obviously, to you. For the full care picture, see our complete [Koi care guide](/guides/koi/).

## Why Handling Is Minimized

Direct contact damages a koi's protective slime coat, raising infection risk, and a thrashing fish can bruise or injure itself against a container or your hands. The practical rule from specialist koi sources is simple: handle as little as possible, ideally only for necessary treatment, transport, or showing, never for casual interaction. A lot of the bacterial and fungal problems covered in our [Koi health issues guide](/blog/koi-health-issues-guide/) trace back to exactly this kind of opening.

## When Handling Genuinely Is Required

Guide the fish into a floating bowl or soft container using a net, rather than lifting it directly. Keep your hands or any towel wet before contact, dry surfaces strip the slime coat. Cradle gently, don't grip tightly. For transfers, a sock net or a plastic bag filled with water works well and minimizes stress.

## Personality Varies More Than You'd Expect

Most koi stay fairly reserved around people, but one variety, Chagoi, stands out for bold, genuinely interactive personalities.

<FunFact>
Chagoi can be trained to hand feed and will actively approach their keeper, and some koi enthusiasts compare their friendliness to a large, food motivated dog. Don't expect this from every koi in your pond, it's a notable exception, not the norm.
</FunFact>

## First Time Keeper Mistakes

- Over-handling or rough netting, which strips the slime coat and opens the door to secondary infections
- Handling with dry hands
- Forcing restraint on a fish that's trying to get away
- Introducing new fish without a proper quarantine period
- Poor water conditions, which make fish skittish or unusually aggressive at feeding time

Getting the pond itself right, covered in our [Koi pond setup guide](/blog/koi-tank-setup-guide/), heads off most of these problems before they start.

## Bites and the Goldfish Comparison

Koi aren't defensively aggressive the way some reptiles can be, they have pharyngeal teeth used for processing food, not defense, so bites in the traditional sense aren't really a concern. Look, don't touch, is the practical daily rule, your real relationship with a koi is built through feeding, observation, and water management, not physical contact.

Koi are often kept alongside or compared to goldfish, and while both are peaceful and can share a pond in adequate space, koi grow substantially larger, commonly 24 to 36 inches or more as adults, need considerably more water volume and filtration, and are more powerful foragers and diggers. The barbels near a koi's mouth, whisker like feelers goldfish don't have, are the easiest visual way to tell the two apart.
