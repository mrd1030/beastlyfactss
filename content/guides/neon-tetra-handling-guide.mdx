# Neon Tetras Aren't Handled, But the School Size Isn't Optional

A neon tetra is a fish to watch, and the only time a keeper's hands come near one is the minute it moves from a bag or a net into water. The welfare question with this species is group size, and getting that number right changes how these fish behave.

## The Number That Matters: Ten

Neon tetras are obligate schooling fish: they need a group to feel secure and behave normally. Eight is the least to buy, and ten is better. In a home aquarium study, neon tetras in smaller groups showed more darting and aggression and were slower to come out to feed than neons in a group of ten. For this species the number works as a welfare requirement.

## What a Properly Schooled Group Looks Like

Confident, brightly colored fish swim together in a loose group and work the mid-water of the tank instead of hiding in corners. It is one of the more rewarding things to watch in a community aquarium, and it only happens reliably once the school is big enough.

## The Minutes Between the Bag and the Tank

The one real handling job is getting new neons into the tank, and it starts with the same [float, net and release](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) every aquarium fish gets. Floating the bag takes 30 to 60 minutes, with a little tank water added every few minutes, and the lights stay dim for the first hour or two. When the shop's water and the home tank differ much in pH or hardness, slow mixing earns its time. Buy the whole school in one trip if the tank is ready for it. A pair of neons added ahead of the rest has no school to hide in.

Two more rules protect the school. Put neons into a tank that has run for a while after cycling, never one that finished cycling last week, and keep each water change to a size the fish never notice.

## Telling Males From Females

Sexing a neon is hard until the fish are mature. Adult females are rounder-bodied and a little larger than the slimmer males. In a school of ten, the plumpest few are the females.

## Breeding Neons

A community tank almost never produces young neons, and the reason is chemistry and light. Spawning needs very soft, acidic water, hardness around 1 to 5 dGH and a pH of 5.5 to 6.5, in a very dimly lit tank. Successful home spawnings have run at 75°F and 78°F. The eggs and fry are light sensitive, and the adults eat any eggs they find.

That shapes the setup. A small bare tank, a clump of Java moss or a layer of leaf litter for the eggs to fall into, soft water, and the tank kept dark. Add a pair or two of conditioned adults, and take them out once eggs appear. The eggs hatch in about 24 hours. The fry start on infusoria-sized food and move to microworm or newly hatched brine shrimp once they can take it. Young neons color up at about four weeks.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Buying two or three neons and calling it a school. It's the most common mistake with this species.
- Adding them to a tank that has only just cycled, which stresses an already sensitive fish.
- Mixing them with fin-nipping tankmates, which disrupts schooling even in an adequate group.

## How Neon Tetras Compare to the Guppy

The two look interchangeable on a beginner's shopping list: small, colorful, community friendly. Their care diverges. Guppies are hardy livebearers that tolerate harder, more alkaline water and breed without help. Neon tetras are egg scatterers that want soft, slightly acidic, steady water. A keeper who has done well with guppies should check that assumption before buying neons, because [guppies](/blog/guppy-handling-guide/) put up with a wider range of water than neons do.

<FunFact>
Neon tetras are often confused with the closely related cardinal tetra, and the red stripe tells them apart. It runs the full body length on a cardinal, but only the back half on a neon.
</FunFact>
