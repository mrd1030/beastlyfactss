# Tortoise Brumation: Which Species, and How to Do It Right

<VetDisclaimer />

Brumation is the reptile version of a winter slowdown: cooler temperatures and shorter days push a tortoise into weeks of near-dormancy instead of true hibernation's total shutdown. Done correctly, it costs nothing and can be good for a tortoise's long-term condition. Done on the wrong species, on an unfasted or sick animal, or at the wrong temperature, it is one of the more reliable ways a keeper loses an otherwise healthy pet. This guide is specific to Testudo tortoises, the Russian, Hermann's, and spur-thighed species most commonly kept as pets, and to North American box turtles. It is not a bearded dragon or a snake, and the numbers here don't transfer between them any more than our own [bearded dragon brumation guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-brumation-guide/) would apply to a chelonian. Every figure below is attributed to the specific page it came from and scoped to the species that source actually covers.

## Which Tortoises Brumate, and Which Never Should

Not every tortoise species evolved with a cold season to sleep through, and forcing one that didn't is a straightforward way to kill it. The Royal Veterinary College's exotics service names the species it considers unsuitable for hibernation outright: leopard tortoises, African spurred (sulcata) tortoises, the Southern Turkish spur-thighed variant, Egyptian, Libyan, and Tunisian Testudo graeca populations, and red-foot and yellow-foot tortoises. These are tropical or subtropical species with no cold-season adaptation, and cold dormancy strips away the heat they depend on to digest and fight infection.

On the other side, the same RVC guidance lists "most Mediterranean tortoises," Horsfield's tortoise (the Central Asian species sold as the Russian tortoise), and the American desert tortoise as suitable candidates. That covers the species this guide is built around: Testudo hermanni (Hermann's), Testudo graeca (spur-thighed, outside the North African populations named above), and Testudo horsfieldii (Russian). If you're unsure which species a tortoise actually is, our [Russian tortoise health issues guide](/blog/russian-tortoise-health-issues-guide/) covers identifying features alongside the illness signs that matter here.

North American box turtles aren't tortoises, but they brumate naturally in the wild across most of the US, so this guide covers them under the same seasonal umbrella. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital documents a full box turtle protocol, given in its own sections below, with different numbers for temperature, duration, and fasting length than the Testudo figures. Don't average the two together. For everything else that can go wrong with a box turtle across the year, see our [box turtle health issues guide](/blog/box-turtle-health-issues-guide/).

Even within a suitable species, RVC is explicit that any individual hibernated "should not have had significant health problems within the previous year and be of a sufficient size," which rules out hatchlings and recently recovered animals regardless of what the species chart says. The Texas Veterinary Medical Foundation puts a number on that size cutoff, keeping any turtle or tortoise under about 4 years old out of brumation entirely, on top of excluding any animal currently showing signs of illness.

## The Pre-Brumation Vet Check Is Not Optional

Both RVC and the Texas Veterinary Medical Foundation open their guidance with the same step: a vet exam before anything else changes. RVC recommends an annual pre-hibernation check in autumn along with a fecal parasite screen, specifically so a tortoise doesn't go into a three-month cold, low-metabolism period carrying a parasite load or an infection its immune system can no longer fight. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital states the same requirement for box turtles: "an exam done by a qualified reptile veterinarian should be completed" before brumation starts.

The reasoning connects to what brumation does to the immune system generally. VCA Animal Hospitals, writing about aquatic turtles, notes that hibernation is stressful on the body and that "sub-clinical illnesses can manifest during this time, when they may become immunocompromised." That's a different species than the ones this guide covers, but the physiology is the same reason a Testudo tortoise or box turtle needs a clean bill of health first: a mild, easily missed respiratory infection or parasite burden that a warm, active tortoise's immune system was holding in check can become a serious problem once that tortoise's metabolism drops for weeks at 40-something degrees. Skip the vet visit and you're finding out the hard way, mid-brumation, instead of in a heated exam room where something can still be done about it.

<KeyTakeaway>
  A tortoise that is underweight, showing any respiratory symptoms, recovering from a recent illness, or under roughly 4 years old should not brumate this year. RVC's advice for that animal is to overwinter it instead in a heated vivarium, never let temperatures there drop below 60°F (15°C), and try again the following year once it's cleared a vet check.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Fast: Why Food in the Gut Is the Classic Fatal Mistake

A tortoise digests using body heat it doesn't generate on its own. Cool it down with food still moving through its gut and that food simply stops moving, sitting there to rot and ferment while gut bacteria carry on working in an animal too cold to expel or process the result. That's the mechanism behind what keepers call the classic brumation mistake: stopping heat before stopping food, rather than the other way around.

For Testudo tortoises, the Texas Veterinary Medical Foundation gives a fasting window of 1 to 3 weeks depending on the animal's size, with temperature held at 70 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit throughout that fast so digestion can actually finish. Heat is only removed about a week before brumation itself begins. RVC describes essentially the same order of operations for its own Mediterranean species: feeding stops first, but the tortoise is "kept warm for the first week to allow digestion," and only after that does the temperature start easing down, by roughly 5°C (9°F) per week, toward hibernation.

Box turtles get a shorter runway. Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital calls for withholding food for 10 to 14 days before brumation, with a warm water soak every other day during that fasting period to keep the turtle hydrated while its gut empties. Whichever species you're working with, the fast comes first and the heat drop comes only once digestion has caught up, never the other way around.

## The Temperature Band, and What Happens Outside It

For Testudo tortoises, the Texas Veterinary Medical Foundation's working range is 35 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit, with 41°F as the ideal, and it flags anything below 35°F or above 60°F as unsafe. RVC's own numbers land in almost the same place from a different angle: it holds hibernating tortoises at a constant 2 to 9°C (roughly 35.6 to 48.2°F), targeting as close to 5°C, or 41°F, as it can manage, the same figure TVMF names as ideal. Box turtles run slightly warmer: Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital specifies 45 to 50°F, held for 3 to 4 months.

Both directions of temperature failure carry real risk. Too cold, and RVC is blunt about it: tortoises kept below freezing "can lose their eyesight or at worst their lives." Too warm, and the tortoise doesn't stay properly dormant, RVC notes that a tortoise kept too warm "becomes too active" and burns through the fat and liver glycogen reserves it needs intact to get moving again once brumation ends, the same stores an overheated brumation quietly drains before spring. That's why a probe or a max/min thermometer inside whatever setup you use matters more than the container itself.

## Methods: Fridge, Insulated Box, and Why Unmonitored Outdoor Cold Isn't One

RVC describes three approaches, and only two of them are ones it actually recommends.

**Fridge hibernation** is, in RVC's words, "good in principle" because it holds a steady temperature around 5°C. The catch is that a fridge's internal temperature varies by shelf position, runs drier than a tortoise needs, and ventilates poorly with the door shut. RVC's fix for all three: use a thermal probe or max/min thermometer to confirm the real reading rather than trusting the dial, open the door for about 30 seconds daily for fresh air and a visual check, and keep a topped-up bowl of water in the bottom for humidity. No insulation is needed inside a fridge, though some keepers add substrate so the tortoise can bury itself. The fridge used should have no other job, not cooling food while a tortoise lives in it, both for hygiene and because a door opened for groceries undoes the point of controlling temperature and airflow.

**Insulated box hibernation** is RVC's other recommended method: a large, rodent-proof outer box, wood or a similarly sturdy material, with the top and any air holes covered in wire mesh to keep vermin out. The base and sides get lined with thick padding, polystyrene or newspaper, and the tortoise itself goes into a smaller inner box with its own air holes, filled one to three-quarters with polystyrene chips, dry leaves, or shredded newspaper. RVC specifically says to avoid hay or straw. That inner box then sits inside the outer one, positioned somewhere the temperature actually holds in the target range and where it's easy to open for regular check-ins. Chicago Exotics describes a similar small-scale setup for box turtles: a ventilated plastic container filled two-thirds with a moistened mix of shredded newspaper and peat moss.

What RVC does not recommend is "self-hibernation," a tortoise left to find its own cold spot outdoors or in an unmonitored garage with no way to confirm the temperature it's actually experiencing. The risks RVC lists for hibernation overall, dehydration, freezing, and rodent attack, are exactly what an unmonitored outdoor or garage setup can't guard against. If a garage is the only option, it still needs the same probe thermometer and box-in-a-box insulation as an indoor setup, not just a cold corner and a hope.

## Weekly Weighing and the Weight-Loss Ceiling

Every credible protocol here treats the scale as the main tool for catching a brumation going wrong before symptoms show. RVC has keepers weigh "individually or complete with inner box every one to two weeks," and gives a concrete rate to watch: an adult tortoise loses about 1 percent of its pre-hibernation weight per month, so a 1,000 gram tortoise losing around 10 grams a month is normal. RVC calls anything past roughly 8 to 10 percent total loss "drastic," and its instruction is direct: bring the tortoise out of hibernation immediately, have it checked, and investigate for disease.

Box turtles get a different number from Chicago Exotics Animal Hospital: weigh in grams every two weeks, and past a 10 percent loss, stop brumation and get veterinary care. The Texas Veterinary Medical Foundation, covering turtles and tortoises together, is more conservative, treating 6 to 7 percent as the acceptable range and anything past 7 percent as a concern. Where sources disagree, treat the lower threshold as your action point rather than waiting for the higher one.

## Signs That Mean End Brumation Right Now

Stop and warm the tortoise up immediately, rather than waiting for the next scheduled check, if any of the following show up: weight loss past the action point for its group above, any discharge from the nose, eyes, or vent found during a routine check, the enclosure temperature having dropped below freezing even briefly, or general illness signs RVC and Chicago Exotics both flag as reasons to stop. None of these are "wait and see" situations. RVC's own guidance for a below-freezing exposure is that a tortoise "can lose their eyesight or at worst their lives," which is not a risk worth confirming by waiting for a scheduled weigh-in.

## Waking Up: The Gradual Warm-Up and the Immediate Soak

Coming out of brumation isn't as simple as flipping the heat lamp back on. RVC's sequence: bring the tortoise out slowly, check it over for discharge from the nose, eyes, and tail end while it's still cool and easy to handle, then leave it at room temperature for the first few hours before any direct heat. Once it's had that adjustment period, give it a warm bath for at least half an hour, repeatable twice daily. The soak isn't optional pampering, RVC explains it's how the tortoise empties its bladder of the toxic waste that built up over brumation and replenishes water it hasn't had access to in months. Watch for actual urination and defecation in the days that follow; if neither happens, RVC calls for a vet visit to get the tortoise rehydrated and checked. A tortoise that isn't eating or hasn't urinated within about seven days of waking, or that looks ill at any point, needs a vet without delay.

Box turtles follow a similar arc on a shorter clock. Chicago Exotics has the ambient temperature raised gradually over 4 to 6 days rather than all at once, with a soak every other day through that stretch. Only once the turtle has held at a normal temperature for 3 full days does feeding resume as usual. In both species, feed easy, succulent foods first rather than a full normal diet on day one, RVC recommends exactly that as the transition food before easing back to the regular menu.

<FunFact>
  RVC notes that for every 10°C (18°F) drop in body temperature, a tortoise's heart rate roughly halves, and by around 4°C (39°F) its respiratory movements become negligible, close to undetectable without careful observation. That's not a sign of distress at a proper brumation temperature, it's the whole point: a tortoise brumating correctly is running its body about as slowly as it's built to run.
</FunFact>

Brumation is worth doing right precisely because doing it wrong is so quiet. A tortoise that's too warm, too cold, unfasted, or the wrong species for the process doesn't usually announce the problem loudly until real damage is already done. The vet check, the fast, the thermometer in the box instead of a guess, and the weekly scale are the whole system for catching that early, and none of them are steps worth skipping to save a season.
