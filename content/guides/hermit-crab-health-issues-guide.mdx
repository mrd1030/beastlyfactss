# Common Hermit Crab Health Issues (There's Almost No Vet Care, So Prevention Is Everything)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Here's the thing to understand upfront about hermit crab health: there's essentially no practical veterinary care available for this species, similar to the situation with pet tarantulas. That reshapes everything about how to approach their wellbeing, husbandry and prevention do nearly all of the work.

Get the enclosure right first: humidity and substrate depth prevent most of what follows.

## Post-Purchase Stress (PPS), the Leading Cause of Death

Every pet hermit crab is wild-caught, not captive-bred, which means every single one goes through a real shock from capture, shipping, and a completely new environment. This adjustment period is where most hermit crab deaths actually happen.

**Watch for:** lethargy, burrowing and staying hidden, refusing food, and rapid loss of multiple limbs, which is a particularly strong indicator of advanced PPS. Once it's progressed significantly, there's often not much that can be done.

**The prevention approach:** quarantine and isolate new crabs, three months in a tank of their own before they join the group, the window [invertebrate quarantine](/blog/invertebrate-quarantine-cleaning-and-escapes-guide/) uses to let mites and other slow problems show themselves. Improve humidity and temperature gradually rather than making sudden changes. A hermit crab generally isn't considered past the PPS risk period until it has successfully molted once in your care, that first successful molt is a real milestone. A newly arrived crab that buries itself and disappears for days is behaving normally rather than dying.

## Limb Loss

Can happen from stress, fights with cage-mates, mites, or generally wrong conditions. The good news is that lost limbs do regenerate over successive molts, first appearing as small buds before growing back to full size.

<FunFact>
Before a molt even begins, a hermit crab breaks down part of its old inner shell and stores the calcium it recovers inside its body, sometimes visible as pale patches called molt sacs through the soft abdomen, ready to build the new exoskeleton. That quiet, internal rebuilding process is a big part of why a molting crab needs total darkness and zero disturbance, it isn't just resting, its body is mid-renovation.
</FunFact>

## Dehydration and Gill Suffocation

Hermit crabs breathe through modified gills that must stay moist to function properly. Signs include an ashy appearance, lethargy, and an inability to fully retract into the shell. ## Bad or Interrupted Molts

Often caused by inadequate humidity, <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0B52DC9VB?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Fluker's Hermit Crab Sand Substrate">substrate</AffiliateLink> that's too shallow or dry, or a crab being disturbed while molting. This is frequently fatal, which is exactly why leaving a buried crab alone matters so much, no exceptions, regardless of how long it's been.

Once it resurfaces, leave the shed exoskeleton where it lies. The crab eats much or all of it to take back the calcium and minerals it contains, the same recycling seen across [molting pet invertebrates](/blog/invertebrate-molting-guide/), and removing it takes those nutrients away during recovery.

## Mites and Mold

Generally traceable to poor hygiene or inadequate ventilation in the enclosure. Mites crawling on the crab itself are a question for a vet with invertebrate experience, never a mite spray, since nearly every pesticide kills the crab along with the mites.

## What "Seeking Help" Looks Like for This Species

Since clinical vet care is rarely an option, the real response to a sick or stressed hermit crab is correcting the husbandry issue behind it, and isolating any crab that's aggressive, injured, or showing PPS signs from the rest of the group while it recovers.
