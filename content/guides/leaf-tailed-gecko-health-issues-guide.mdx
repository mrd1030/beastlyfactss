# Common Leaf-Tailed Gecko Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Leaf-tailed geckos are genuinely delicate, and dehydration specifically deserves more attention with this species than with almost any other reptile on this site. See our [tank setup guide](/blog/leaf-tailed-gecko-tank-setup-guide/) for the husbandry side of prevention.

## Dehydration: The Fastest-Moving Danger

Specialist breeders describe dehydration as a genuine fast killer of this species. Watch for sunken or dull eyes, wrinkled or loose-looking skin, lethargy, difficulty shedding properly, and appetite loss.

Manageable at home if caught early, raise humidity and increase misting frequency immediately. If your gecko doesn't respond, or shows real lethargy or weight loss, see a vet promptly, this isn't a condition to wait out.

<FunFact>
A gecko kept at correct humidity sheds in one clean piece with no sunken eyes or a drooping tail afterward, a simple visual check that's one of the fastest ways to catch early dehydration before it turns dangerous.
</FunFact>

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Watch for limb weakness or tremors, a soft "rubber jaw," bone deformities, and fractures that can occur from even gentle handling. Caused by calcium deficiency, inadequate UVB or vitamin D3, and a poor calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in the diet. Always see a vet immediately, specialist sources note this can worsen rapidly once it starts.

## Respiratory Infection

Watch for wheezing or clicking sounds, open-mouth breathing, mucus, and lethargy. Caused by poor ventilation, temperatures running too low, or humidity that's mismanaged. Always see a vet, this needs prescription antibiotics.

## Internal Parasites

Genuinely common in wild-caught imports specifically, one more reason captive-bred is strongly preferred for this species, something our [cost guide](/blog/leaf-tailed-gecko-cost-guide/) covers in more detail. Watch for weight loss, diarrhea, regurgitation, and lethargy. A vet is required for diagnosis and treatment, but there's a useful nuance worth knowing: if you've just acquired a wild-caught or freshly imported gecko, waiting 1 to 2 months to let it acclimate before starting parasite treatment is often the safer approach, treating an already-stressed animal too soon can do more harm than the parasites themselves in the short term. Discuss timing with your vet.

## Tail Drop and Wound Care

Usually manageable at home simply by minimizing handling and disturbance going forward, something our [handling guide](/blog/leaf-tailed-gecko-handling-guide/) covers in full. See a vet only if the site where the tail dropped shows swelling, redness, discharge, or a foul odor, signs of infection rather than a normal healing process.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Low humidity is directly responsible for the dehydration that's the single biggest real threat to this species. Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD. And buying wild-caught rather than captive-bred substantially raises parasite risk. Getting humidity right, covered in full in our [tank setup guide](/blog/leaf-tailed-gecko-tank-setup-guide/), does more preventive work here than with almost any other reptile on this site.
