# Common Leaf-Tailed Gecko Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Leaf-tailed geckos are delicate, and dehydration specifically deserves more attention with this species than with almost any other reptile.

## Dehydration: The Fastest-Moving Danger

A leaf-tailed gecko gets most of its water from droplets on misted surfaces rather than from a bowl, so a lapse in misting can turn into dehydration quickly. Watch for sunken or dull eyes, wrinkled or loose-looking skin, lethargy, difficulty shedding properly, and appetite loss.

Manageable at home if caught early, raise humidity and increase misting frequency immediately. If your gecko doesn't respond, or shows real lethargy or weight loss, see a vet promptly, this isn't a condition to wait out.

<FunFact>
A gecko kept at correct humidity sheds in one clean piece with no sunken eyes or a drooping tail afterward, a simple visual check that's one of the fastest ways to catch early dehydration before it turns dangerous.
</FunFact>

## Stuck Shed

A gecko coming into shed turns greyish or faded and eats and moves less for a few days. Leaf-tails eat their own shed skin, so a clean shed usually leaves nothing behind by morning, and the thing to look for is what is still attached: rings of old skin on toes, the tail tip or the dermal fringe along the body.

Stuck shed is a humidity problem. Hold the enclosure at 80 to 100% for 24 hours, or put the gecko in a ventilated plastic tub lined with a lukewarm wet paper towel for 20 to 30 minutes, then lift off any loosened pieces with fine-tipped tweezers. Never pull skin that is still attached, which can injure the animal.

## Mites

Most often arriving on wild-caught animals or from another reptile in the house. Look for tiny red dots around the eyes, ears, vent and inside the skin flaps, ash-like dust on the body, rubbing and lethargy, though a light infestation can show nothing at all. Treatment covers both the gecko and the enclosure: a vet-guided plan for the animal, and a full strip-down of the setup, with substrate thrown out, surfaces washed, disinfected with a 10 minute contact time and rebuilt on paper towel until the mites are gone. A 3 to 6 month quarantine on paper towel for every new arrival is what keeps them out, and the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) sets out the separate room and tools.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Watch for limb weakness or tremors, a soft "rubber jaw," bone deformities, and fractures that can occur from even gentle handling. Caused by calcium deficiency, inadequate UVB or vitamin D3, and a poor calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in the diet. Always see a vet immediately, specialist sources note this can worsen rapidly once it starts.

## Respiratory Infection

Watch for wheezing or clicking sounds, open-mouth breathing, mucus, and lethargy. Caused by poor ventilation, [temperatures running too low](/blog/leaf-tailed-gecko-tank-setup-guide/), or humidity that's mismanaged. Always see a vet, this needs prescription antibiotics.

## Internal Parasites

Genuinely common in wild-caught imports specifically, one more reason captive-bred is strongly preferred for this species. Watch for weight loss, diarrhea, regurgitation, and lethargy. A vet is required for diagnosis and treatment, but there's a useful nuance worth knowing: if you've just acquired a wild-caught or freshly imported gecko, waiting 1 to 2 months to let it acclimate before starting parasite treatment is often the safer approach, treating an already-stressed animal too soon can do more harm than the parasites themselves in the short term. Discuss timing with your vet.

## Tail Drop and Wound Care

Usually manageable at home simply by minimizing handling and disturbance going forward. See a vet only if the site where the tail dropped shows swelling, redness, discharge, or a foul odor, signs of infection rather than a normal healing process.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Low humidity is directly responsible for the dehydration that's the single biggest real threat to this species. Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD, and the feeding guide has the light every-feeding dusting that covers the calcium half. And buying wild-caught rather than captive-bred substantially raises parasite risk.

Almost everything on this list is preventable through husbandry, the same pattern that runs through [tokay gecko health problems](/blog/tokay-gecko-health-issues-guide/).
