The most common types are the English or American shorthair, the Peruvian (long-haired) and the Abyssinian (swirled hair), and the long-haired types add daily brushing to the routine.

The cage is sized by head count: about 7.5 square feet is the minimum for one guinea pig and 10.5 square feet for a pair. An 8 sq ft single-level habitat at around $100 to $110 covers a single pig, so a pair needs a larger modular or C&C habitat, which runs closer to $140. This is the biggest line item, and it's worth getting right the first time rather than upgrading later. Every row from the cage down adds up to $300 to $470, not counting the animals. The hay, pellet, supplement and bedding rows are the first supply; after that they move to the monthly table below. The pellet bag is the small one on purpose: pellets should be used within 90 days of their manufacture date, and a pair takes far longer than that to get through a 10 lb bag.

For a pair, the recurring food lines are pellets, fresh vegetables and timothy hay, and hay is the one that grows with the number of animals because it is unlimited. Annual routine spending for a pair commonly lands between $400 and $1,620 depending on bedding quality and food brands, with around $1,200 a year being a fairly typical middle-ground figure.

Guinea pigs were domesticated more than 3,000 years ago in the Andes, originally raised as livestock rather than pets. That deep history as a group-living animal is part of why they're so social today, exactly the trait that turns a single-guinea-pig budget into a two-guinea-pig one.

## Vet Costs

An annual wellness exam runs $50 to $100. Sick or emergency visits run $100 to $300 or more, and complex issues like [GI stasis](/blog/guinea-pig-health-issues-guide/) can push well past that. Guinea pigs need a cavy-savvy exotic vet, not a general small-animal clinic, so it's worth finding one before you need one. No vaccines are required for this species.

## Why the Pair Matters for Your Budget

Guinea pigs kept alone tend to become withdrawn and depressed, so nearly every credible source recommends keeping them in pairs or small groups from the start. That effectively doubles your food and vet-visit costs compared to what a single-guinea-pig budget might suggest, but it's not really optional if you want a guinea pig that's thriving. Budget for two from day one rather than adding a second later and being surprised by the jump.

## Which Pair You Choose Changes the Vet Bill

The pairings that work are two females, brothers raised together, or a neutered male with one or more females. The first two need no surgery at all. A boy and a girl do: a male must be neutered before he lives with a female, because pregnancy and birth carry real risks for a sow. Neutering does not change a male's behavior or stop fighting, so it buys nothing in a same-sex pair. Spaying a female is the more complicated operation and is done less often. The exception is the preventive spay some exotics vets now recommend for young, healthy sows, since ovarian cysts affect more than half of females at some point. Neuter and spay fees are set clinic by clinic, and a quote should include the anesthesia and pain relief, so get one from a cavy-savvy vet before settling on a mixed pair.

## The Budgeting Takeaway

The guinea pig itself is the cheap part. What actually determines your long-term cost is the size and quality of the cage and whether you're budgeting honestly for two animals instead of one from the start. That need for a companion is the one rule that sets the guinea pig apart among [four common beginner pets](/blog/guinea-pig-cockatiel-betta-tarantula-overview/), and it is the rule that doubles the budget. Chinchillas sit in the same budget range with a far longer lifespan, which is where [a chinchilla set against a guinea pig](/blog/chinchilla-vs-guinea-pig-guide/) comes down to more than price.
