# Russian Tortoise Feeding: Fiber, Calcium, and Growing Slowly

<VetDisclaimer />

In the wild this animal spends its active hours moving through low vegetation, taking a bite here and a bite there. The diet is soft-stemmed, water-rich plant growth, grasses, flowers and leaves, high in fiber and low in concentrated energy. Almost every feeding mistake made in captivity comes from replacing that with something richer and faster.

## Graze First, Bowl Second

The best version of this diet is one the tortoise harvests itself. Where the enclosure allows it, safe growing plants beat anything chopped into a dish, because grazing spreads intake across the day and keeps the fiber high. Fiber is not filler here: microbial fermentation of it in the gut is a major nutrient source for a tortoise.

There is a practical catch worth knowing before planting an enclosure: tortoises will often eat a plant down to the roots and finish it. The workaround is to grow forage somewhere else, in a pesticide-free spot, and bring it in.

For which plants are safe to pick and which are not, the [safe weeds and wild plants guide](/blog/herbivorous-reptile-safe-plants-guide/) covers the list, where to avoid collecting, and how the calcium to phosphorus balance differs between one weed and another.

Two presentation details come from veterinary guidance and both are easy to miss. Mix the food together rather than laying items out separately, because a tortoise given the choice will eat selectively and leave the fiber. And put the food on a tile or in a dish, so the animal is not swallowing substrate with every bite.

## No Animal Protein

This is a herbivore, and the point is not a soft preference. Animal protein does not belong in the diet at all.

It also causes a second problem that takes longer to show up. Protein-rich food pushes fast growth, and shell deformity has long been attributed to exactly that, rapid growth on a high-protein diet. Growth that is too slow does damage as well, which is why a young tortoise is worth weighing regularly and tracking against a sensible curve instead of being pushed along or starved.

Pyramiding, where the scutes rise into raised humps instead of staying flat, is the visible result. Humidity and temperature feed into it too, so it is not purely a feeding fault, but protein and overfeeding are the parts a keeper controls at the dish.

## Calcium, and the Ceiling on It

Calcium runs the shell and the skeleton, and a deficiency here is one of the serious problems in the species: soft shells, shell and bone deformities, kidney damage, and worse if it goes far enough.

The usual delivery is calcium carbonate sprinkled lightly onto food. Human-grade calcium carbonate, citrate or phosphate mixed into the salad all serve.

What gets said less often is that there is a ceiling. Wild diets for these tortoises can run a calcium to phosphorus ratio at or above 4:1, but the target in captivity sits nearer 2:1, because too much calcium causes medical problems of its own. This is a nutrient to supply reliably, not maximally.

Alongside calcium, the diet wants carotenoids, the vitamin A precursors that come from dark leafy greens and from orange and yellow vegetables like squash, carrot and sweet potato.

## How Much

The honest answer is that published sources give proportions and ingredients far more readily than they give portions, and the reason is that the right amount depends on the animal in front of you.

So the check is the animal. Russian tortoises tend toward overweight in captivity, and the test is what happens when one withdraws: a tortoise that looks chubby pulling back into its shell, with flesh crowding the openings, is being fed too much. Cut the amount back.

That is the direction of error to expect. Underfeeding a tortoise that grazes is unusual; overfeeding one that would happily eat all day is the default.

## Water

Tortoises drink, and the belief that they do not is a persistent one that causes real harm. Without water, urates accumulate, and they can reach dangerous levels.

Provide water in a shallow bowl, something like a plant saucer, sunk so its lip sits level with the surrounding surface. It doubles as the soaking dish, and an indoor tortoise benefits from a weekly soak in chin-deep lukewarm water.
