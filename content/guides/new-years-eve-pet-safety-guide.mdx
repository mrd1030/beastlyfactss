# New Year's Eve Pet Safety: The Fireworks, the Door and the Morning After

<VetDisclaimer>
This article is for educational purposes only and is not veterinary advice. If your pet has eaten something it should not have, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435) or the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661) before signs appear. A dog with a severe noise phobia needs a vet appointment before the night.
</VetDisclaimer>

**A dog hears a firework the way a person hears a gunshot in the next room, and it does not know the display ends at 12:15.** In a study of 13,700 Finnish pet dogs, noise sensitivity was the most common anxiety trait of all, at 32 percent, and fear of fireworks the most common form of it, at 26 percent. A Swiss owner survey that used a stricter cutoff put the figure at 52 percent. Cats are worse at hiding it than anyone assumed: in a Dutch survey of 622 cat owners, 77 percent said their cat showed fear during fireworks, and three quarters of those cats hid. New Year's Eve is the one night a year when every animal in the house is subjected to the same thing at the same time, and the house is also full of strangers, open doors and drinks at floor level.

The fireworks are the headline, but the door is what loses the dog.

<KeyTakeaway>
Three things decide how the night goes. The quiet room is ready before dark: interior, curtains drawn, a radio or television on, water, a bed, the cat's hiding box and the rabbit's hay already in place. The door is handled at midnight: every pet is behind a closed door before the countdown, wearing a collar and tag, with the microchip registry showing a current phone number. And the morning is a sweep before it is a walk: spent fireworks, abandoned cups and ice melt are all on the sidewalk on January 1st. For anything eaten, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435) and the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661) answer 24 hours a day, for a fee.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Noise

Fear of fireworks is not rare, not a character flaw and not something a dog grows out of. It is the most common fear dogs have.

### What it looks like

The Dutch survey of 3,009 dog owners found the same picture most owners already know. During the bangs, 74 percent of fearful dogs sought their owner's eyes, 69 percent moved close, 63 percent pressed against them, 64 percent trembled and 60 percent panted. Cats did the opposite: 76 percent hid, 65 percent fled, and 43 percent froze. A cat that vanishes at 9 pm and reappears at breakfast has had a bad night, even though nobody saw it.

In the Finnish study, 53 percent of dogs afraid of one noise were afraid of several, and noise sensitivity rose with age, so a dog that coped at three may not at nine. Mixed-breed dogs, Lagotti Romagnoli and wheaten terriers were the most noise sensitive in that sample, miniature schnauzers and Staffordshire bull terriers the least, but the breed figures are averages and the individual in front of you is the one that matters.

### What helps on the night

The Swiss survey asked 1,225 owners what they had tried and what worked. Two things stood out. Giving the dog something it loves straight after each bang, a high-value treat or a favorite game, was rated effective in more than two thirds of cases. So was cueing a relaxation the dog had already been taught. Both work on the same principle: the noise starts to predict something good, and the dog's body learns the bang is not the beginning of a disaster. That means the owner is in the room, calm, with the treats in hand, for the whole display.

The rest of the plan is management, and it is the same for every species. The quiet room is interior, with the curtains drawn so the flashes do not show, and something steady playing to blur the bangs: a radio, a fan, the television. The dog's bed goes in it, and so does the owner. A cat gets what Dutch cat owners most often provided, a hiding place, a covered bed or a box in a closet, and is left alone in it. Nobody drags a hiding cat out for comfort. The animal that wants contact will come and get it.

What does not help is most of what is sold for the purpose. The Swiss survey found pheromone diffusers, nutraceuticals, herbal and homeopathic products and essential oils no more effective than placebo. Dutch cat owners reported a lasting benefit from pheromone products in 6 percent of cats. Fewer than 30 percent of all the measures owners tried had any lasting effect, and the one with the best record, 29 percent, was leaving: a night somewhere quieter. For a dog with a true phobia, the one that drools, shakes, claws at doors and will not eat, the answer is a vet appointment in December. Prescription anxiolytics exist for exactly this, and they need to be in the house before the 31st.

### What helps for next year

The Swiss survey's most useful finding is about timing. Forty-five percent of owners said the fear began before the dog was a year old, and without intervention it tends to stay or get worse: a third of fearful dogs were unchanged over time, and 27 percent got worse. Dogs that had been trained as puppies to associate the noise with something good scored a median of 1 on the fear scale. Dogs trained as adults before any fear scored 2. Untrained dogs scored 4. The cheapest New Year's Eve plan is the one that starts in January, with a puppy, a recording of fireworks at low volume, and a handful of chicken.

## The Door

The second hazard of the night is a door that opens at midnight onto a street full of bangs.

Guests arrive and leave in waves, the last of them in the loudest half hour of the year, and a dog that bolts at that moment is outside in the dark, in the cold, in a panic. The ASPCA's winter advice is blunt: more dogs are lost during the winter than during any other season, a dog on snow or ice can lose its own scent trail, and a dog never goes off leash in it. The fixes are the same ones that work for every holiday. Every animal is behind a closed door before the countdown. The collar carries a tag with a number someone will answer at 1 am. The microchip registry has that number too, checked that week, because a collar comes off in a hedge and a chip does not. Cats stay indoors from dusk on the 31st to the morning of the 1st, with the litter tray in the quiet room.

The last walk of the year happens before dark, on a leash, and the first walk of the new one waits for daylight.

## The Party

Three things from the party itself end up inside pets.

Party poppers, noisemakers and the confetti strings they throw frighten animals and scatter string. A thrown confetti string is one more thing a cat can swallow, and inside a cat a string behaves [the way tinsel does](/blog/christmas-pet-safety-guide/), anchoring at one end while the gut bunches along the rest, which is an abdominal surgery. The strings go in the bin with the paper hats before any animal is let back into the room.

Drinks get left at floor level and on low tables all night. Alcohol is absorbed fast and does to a dog or cat what it does at [Thanksgiving](/blog/thanksgiving-pet-safety-guide/) in a glass of mulled wine, and a champagne flute at the edge of a coffee table is at the height of a cat's nose and a rabbit's. Glow sticks and glow jewelry are a cat problem, and the bitter liquid inside them does what it does [on Halloween](/blog/halloween-pet-safety-guide/). Guests' bags carry medication, and the ASPCA's instruction is that medication, yours and theirs, goes in a closed cabinet, never left in a bag or a coat on a chair.

The house is loud until 2 am, and that matters for the animals that sleep. A cockatiel is a light sleeper built to watch for predators, and a bang in the dark, a light switched on during its sleep hours or someone walking into its room can send it into a night fright, a blind panic in which the bird throws itself around the cage and can hurt itself on the bars. The bird's room is closed, dark and off limits from its usual bedtime, with a night light on so a startled bird can find the perch again instead of the bars, and the cage well away from any window the flashes reach. The [same list of household hazards](/blog/bird-household-hazards-guide/) that applies on any evening applies twice over on this one: no scented candles, no smoke, no party fog in a room a bird shares.

Rabbits and guinea pigs hear fireworks the way they hear a predator, repeatedly, for hours. They freeze, and a frozen rabbit stops eating, and a rabbit that stops eating is on the clock for [GI stasis](/blog/rabbit-gi-stasis-guide/). The hutch or pen moves to the quietest interior room in the afternoon, with the hay piled high and the hide box in place, a blanket over the top to cut the flashes, and someone checking before bed that the hay is going down. An outdoor hutch comes inside for the night. A ferret, which sleeps through most things, gets the same closed room so the party does not reach the cage.

## The Morning After

January 1st is a different hazard, and it is on the sidewalk.

Spent fireworks lie everywhere on the morning walk: casings, cardboard, unexploded pieces, half-burnt sparklers. A dog that eats one is poisoned by whatever the firework was made of, and the effects run from stomach upset to injury to the kidneys, liver or bone marrow, and seizures. A recently lit firework burns a nose that investigates it. The morning walk is a short one on a leash, on a route that was quiet the night before, and anything a dog picks up comes out of its mouth.

The cold adds two more. Antifreeze is lethal to dogs and cats, and a spilled puddle in a driveway after a night of visitors' cars is exactly where a dog puts its tongue. Ice melt and road salt get licked off bare paws on the way home, and the chemicals in them are dangerous swallowed. Feet and belly get washed and dried at the door, and the dog that walked through the party's aftermath gets checked between the toes for anything lodged there. A cup of flat beer on the porch step counts as a hazard too.

The morning is also when the lost dogs come in. A dog that bolted at midnight is often found by someone at dawn, which is why the tag and the chip registry were checked the week before, and why the first call on the 1st, for an owner with an empty bed, is to the local shelter and the chip registry, before any drive around the block.

## What To Do First

For anything eaten, the order is the same as every holiday.

1. **Take it away** and clear what is left so no second animal gets it.
2. **Keep the packaging, the casing or a photo.** The poison lines assess by product.
3. **Estimate how much**, roughly.
4. **Note the time and the animal's weight.**
5. **Call now, before signs.** Your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661. Both are open 24 hours a day, every day, and both charge a consultation fee.
6. **Do not make the animal vomit** unless the person on the line or your vet tells you to.

For a dog that has gone through the door, the order is different: the microchip registry first, to confirm the number is live, then the local shelter and animal control, then the neighbors along the streets the dog knows from its walks.

And for the fear itself, the thing most owners wait to deal with until the next December, the work starts on the first quiet day in January, with a recording at the lowest volume the dog can ignore and a reward every time it does. A dog that is afraid of fireworks at one is, more often than not, still afraid at ten. A dog taught as a puppy that bangs mean chicken scored lowest on the fear scale of any group in the survey.
