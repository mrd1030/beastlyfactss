# Ball Python Feeding: Everything You Need to Know

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ball pythons are one of the most popular pet snakes in the world, and one of the most notorious food refusers. Understanding their feeding behavior will save you a lot of stress. For the rest of their housing and enrichment needs, see our full [Ball Python care guide](/guides/ball-python/).

## Feeding Schedule

- **Hatchlings (0-6 months):** Every 5-7 days
- **Juveniles (6-18 months):** Every 7-10 days  
- **Adults (18+ months):** Every 10-14 days

## Prey Size

Feed prey that is roughly the same width as the widest point of your snake's body. For most adult ball pythons, that's a medium rat. Too large = regurgitation; too small = nutritional inadequacy.

## Frozen/Thawed is Best

Always feed **frozen/thawed (F/T)** rodents. Live prey can injure your snake. Thaw in warm (not hot) water or a bag submerged in warm water. Never microwave. The prey should feel warm to the touch.

## Why Ball Pythons Refuse Food

1. **Breeding season (Oct-March):** Males especially go off food during this period - completely normal
2. **Shedding:** Most snakes refuse food when in shed (opaque eyes, dull skin) - see our [complete guide to reptile shedding](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/) for the full process

## Thawing Method and Temperature

This is the part most keepers get sloppy on, and it matters more than the schedule.

**How to thaw.** Put the frozen rodent in the refrigerator until it's fully thawed, or seal it in a bag and submerge it in cool to lukewarm water, changing the water as needed until there's no frozen core left. Refrigeration is the method the Merck Veterinary Manual points to as standard practice. Never thaw at room temperature on a counter for hours, and never use a microwave. A microwave heats unevenly, which can leave the inside still frozen while pockets on the outside are hot enough to burn your snake's mouth or, in the worst cases, cause the prey itself to rupture.

**How warm is warm enough.** Once thawed, don't feed it straight from the refrigerator. The Merck Veterinary Manual is direct about this: prey "should not be fed cold but at room temperature, or preferably warmer." Ball pythons hunt partly by detecting heat through the pit organs along their upper lip, which pick up on infrared radiation from a source that's warmer than its surroundings. A cold, thawed rodent doesn't register as prey the same way. Warming it gently, for example under a lamp for a few minutes or by running warm water over the outside of the sealed bag, gets it into a range the snake's heat pits can actually detect.

Do not overheat it trying to compensate. Hot water or a lamp left too long can cook the prey internally, which risks the body cavity rupturing when the snake constricts or swallows it, and it creates a real scald risk to the inside of your snake's mouth. If you don't have a way to measure surface temperature directly, warm gradually and check by touch often rather than leaving it under heat and walking away.

## Freezer Storage

Frozen prey doesn't stay in ideal condition forever, and how you store it affects both your snake's food and your own safety in the kitchen.

**Storage life and freezer burn.** According to the FDA, food held at a steady 0°F stays safe to eat indefinitely, but quality declines the longer it sits in the freezer. Freezer burn, those grey, dried-out patches you sometimes see on long-frozen prey, is a quality issue rather than a safety one, and it happens when packaging isn't airtight. Keep prey in its original sealed packaging or rebag it well, and don't let stock sit for a year or more just because the freezer says it's technically fine.

**Thaw once.** Once a rodent has been thawed, do not refreeze it. Refreezing thawed meat degrades quality further and gives bacteria more opportunity to multiply during the second thaw cycle. If you thaw more than your snake will eat, that prey item is a loss, not leftovers to put back in the freezer.

**Handling it safely yourself.** Frozen feeder rodents carry a real Salmonella risk to the people handling them, not just to the snake. The CDC has documented multiple Salmonella outbreaks tied to frozen feeder rodents and recommends washing your hands thoroughly with soap and water immediately after handling frozen rodents or anything in the area where they were stored, thawed, or prepared. Keep frozen rodents away from areas where human food and drink are stored, prepared, served, or consumed, and use storage containers and utensils that are dedicated to feeder rodents and never touch human food. Don't thaw prey in the kitchen sink you use for dishes, and never use a microwave or kitchen utensil meant for human food to handle it.

## When to Actually Worry

A healthy adult ball python can go 3-6 months without eating. Worry if: rapid weight loss, lethargy plus refusal, mucus around the mouth, or wheezing accompanies the fast.
