# Common African Fat-Tailed Gecko Health Issues

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Fat-tailed geckos are hardy when their two biggest needs, heat and humidity, are met.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Widely described as the most common serious issue in this species, and it can be fatal if it progresses far enough. Caused by calcium or vitamin D3 deficiency, often alongside missing UVB.

**Watch for:** lethargy, difficulty standing or walking, swollen joints, muscle twitches, bones that feel unusually flexible, and spinal curvature.

## Retained Shed (Dysecdysis)

Fat-tailed geckos, like leopard geckos, go through a complete shedding cycle and are genuinely prone to shed getting stuck, particularly on the toes and tail tip, when humidity runs too low or there's no moist hide available. This is the single most common fat-tail-specific problem, and it's almost entirely preventable.

Retained skin that constricts can cut off circulation and cause tissue loss if not addressed. ## Impaction

Terrestrial geckos feeding on loose substrate can accidentally ingest it, leading to a gut blockage.

**Watch for:** bloating, lethargy, appetite loss, and straining.

Reduce risk with appropriate substrate choices and correctly sized prey.

## Parasites

Fat-tails should have fecal exams on a regular schedule, starting at purchase, and they matter most for any gecko that was wild-caught. A parasite load shows up in a fecal test long before it shows up as weight loss. A new gecko goes through [quarantine](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) away from any other reptiles until its fecal comes back clean.

## Respiratory Infection

Caused by an enclosure kept too cool, too damp, or both, so a reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BGS517FT?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="REPTIZOO Dimming Thermostat, PID Temperature Controller with Memory Function, Reptile Thermostat for Reptile Heat lamp">thermostat</AffiliateLink> matters here too.

**Watch for:** wheezing, mucus, open-mouth breathing, and lethargy.

This needs a vet and antibiotic treatment, it won't resolve on its own.

## Egg-Binding

Female reptiles can form eggs with no male present, so any adult female is at risk. Egg-binding (dystocia) is a female that cannot pass her eggs.

**Watch for:** restlessness, repeated digging attempts in different spots, straining, a swollen vent, and a female that stops eating.

Causes include a missing or unsuitable nesting site, the wrong temperature gradient or humidity, poor nutrition and illness. A laying box of moist substrate she can dig into is the main prevention. A vet confirms retained eggs by X-ray or ultrasound, and treatment ranges from a proper nesting site and hormone injections to surgery, so this is a same-day call, not a wait-and-see.

## Tail Loss and Eye Issues

Tail loss happens through rough handling or fright. Eye problems are also relatively commonly reported in this species and are worth a vet visit if you notice cloudiness, swelling, or discharge that isn't tied to a normal shed cycle.

## When to Book an Exam

Book a vet visit if the gecko stops eating, loses weight, has retained shed that will not come off in the humid hide, or shows weakness. Adults weigh 40 to 90 grams, so a gram scale and a monthly log turn "seems thinner" into a number. Healthy geckos still get an annual wellness exam to check body condition, hydration and nutrition.

## The Husbandry Connection Worth Remembering

No moist hide, or an enclosure that's simply too dry overall, is the direct cause of the most common fat-tail health complaint: stuck shed on the toes and tail. Keeping the enclosure too cool and too damp at the same time causes respiratory infection. Skipping calcium, D3, and UVB causes MBD. Get the setup right and most of this list never shows up.

The same husbandry-first pattern holds across the other commonly kept geckos: [gargoyle geckos](/blog/gargoyle-gecko-health-issues-guide/) and [mourning geckos](/blog/mourning-gecko-health-issues-guide/) get sick for husbandry reasons far more often than for anything else.
