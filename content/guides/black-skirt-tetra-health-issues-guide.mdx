# Common Black Skirt Tetra Health Issues (and Why Ich Heat Stops at 79°F)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

The black skirt tetra (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi), also sold as the black widow tetra, comes from the Rio Paraguay basin of South America, and every fish in the trade today is captive bred. Generations of tank breeding have made it relaxed about water chemistry, but not about disease. It gets ich, fin rot and gill flukes like other community tetras, a microsporidian infection first described in it in 2021, and ich in this fish can turn fatal in two days.

## Signs That Need Action Today

A quick count at feeding time is the daily check on a school of 12. These are the signs that set the order of what happens next.

**Test the water first:**

- Two or more fish off their food or moving slowly at the same time.
- A fish found dead. Take the body out of the tank before anything else.
- The whole school gone sluggish and eating less. Read the thermometer along with the test kit, because this fish starts slowing down once the water drops to about 63°F.

**Start treatment today:**

- White spots on the body or fins.
- A fish that turns darker, keeps apart from the school and refuses food while the water tests clean. Those were the signs that came with ich in the outbreak described below, and the deaths followed fast.

**Watch and check again tomorrow:**

- A torn or ragged fin on a single fish.
- One fish eating less than the others but otherwise behaving normally.
- A slow run of losses, one fish every week or two, with no clear cause.

Each fish on the watch list gets a close look every day until the sign clears or turns into one from the lists above. A fading black is not on any of them: the black on a young skirt tetra is at its deepest early on and fades toward silvery gray as the fish ages.

## Water: Steady Inside a Wide Range

Captive breeding has given this species an easy range: 68 to 79°F (20 to 26°C), pH 6.0 to 7.5 and 5 to 19 dGH. Holding the tank steady inside it matters more than any one number, and the readings that kill fish quickly are ammonia and nitrite, which a tank only stops producing in harmful amounts once it has [finished cycling](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/). A liquid test kit answers the first group of signs above before anything gets treated.

## The Cold Limits Are Measured

Temperature is the one part of this fish's tolerance that has been tested directly. White skirt tetras, the pale form of the same species, were cooled by 1°C a day starting from 68°F (20°C). At about 63°F (17°C) they fed and moved less. Close to 54°F (12°C) they stopped eating, they stopped moving just above 50°F (10°C), and at 9.95°C, a fraction under 50°F, they lost their balance.

The bottom of the normal range sits only 5°F above the point where appetite drops, so a failed heater in a cool house shows up in the school within days. ## Ich: Fast in This Fish, Treated Below 86°F

Ich, or white spot disease, is the condition best recorded in black skirt tetras, and a 2025 case report shows how quickly it moves. A batch of widow tetras bought at a market in India was found heavily infected. The sick fish darkened, separated from the group, slowed down and quit eating. Some had lost fin tissue, others every gill filament, and the fish died within 48 hours of the first symptoms. The water at the time was 73°F (23°C) at pH 7.5, well inside the range the species is kept in, so a tank in good order does not hold ich off once an infected fish arrives.

Most strains of ich die in water above 86°F, and some fish are kept warm enough to be cured by heat alone. The black skirt's range ends at 79°F, 7°F short of that, so heat is a helper here and not the cure. Warmer water makes the parasite multiply faster, which shortens the wait for each new generation to reach a stage the treatment can hit. Raise the tank slowly to 79°F, run an air stone for the oxygen that warm water holds less of, and let aquarium salt or an ich medication kill the parasite. The doses, the timing and the full course are in [the ich treatment guide](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/). Repeated treatment over several doses is what brings an outbreak under control.

Salt has one exception in this species. In a tank holding eggs or fresh hatchlings, salt at 1 and 3 parts per thousand killed black skirt fry in large numbers while they still carried their yolk sacs, yet barely touched fry that were already swimming. A breeding tank with young fry in it is treated with something else, or the sick adults move to a hospital tank.

## Fins: Nipping, Tears and Fin Rot

The black skirt has a reputation as a fin nipper, mostly toward slow or long-finned tankmates. A school of 12 is the fix, since the fish then keep their squabbling among themselves. Which tankmates are safe with it, and which to leave out, is a question of temperament that the [handling guide](/blog/black-skirt-tetra-handling-guide/) settles.

Fin rot is recorded in this species at both an early and a late stage, so a torn fin is worth watching until it either regrows or starts to erode. The difference between a clean tear and [fin tissue being lost to a bacterial infection](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) is what decides whether to treat. Ich can strip fins too, so a fish with ragged fins and white spots goes on the ich treatment first.

Gill flukes of the genus Dactylogyrus are the fourth problem on the species' disease record, alongside ich and the two stages of fin rot.

## Tetra Disseminated Microsporidiosis

In 2021 researchers described a new microsporidian disease in two aquarium fish, the black tetra and the [cardinal tetra](/blog/cardinal-tetra-health-issues-guide/). The parasite, named Fusasporis stethaprioni and placed in a new genus, spreads through most of the internal organs and into the gills. In black tetras it was linked to a steady, drawn-out run of deaths in a population farmed in the United States, while in cardinals it turned up in dying fish held in quarantine at a public aquarium.

It is a separate parasite from the one behind [neon tetra disease](/blog/neon-tetra-health-issues-guide/), though both are microsporidians. The pattern it leaves in a tank is the slow trickle of losses on the watch list above, which is the point to take a fish to a vet who treats fish, and every dead fish comes out of the tank as soon as it is found.

## Quarantine Every New Fish

Every new black skirt spends [at least 30 days in quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) before joining the school. A month is long enough for ich to show, and it gives a slow disease a chance to surface where only the newcomers are exposed, which is exactly where the microsporidian turned up in cardinals. Artificially dyed skirt tetras are a health problem from the start. The dye is injected, the process puts the fish under heavy stress, and it often cuts their lives considerably short. The plain black, white and long-fin forms carry no such cost.

A black skirt that eats eagerly, swims with the school and keeps its fins whole in steady water is a healthy fish. Most of what goes wrong with the species arrives with a new fish or follows a slip in the water, and both can be caught early.
