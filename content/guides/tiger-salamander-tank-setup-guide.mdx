# Tiger Salamander Enclosure Setup: Deep Substrate for a Species That Lives Underground

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Tiger salamander husbandry is genuinely straightforward once you accept the central fact about this species: it's built to burrow, and the setup needs to support that, not fight against it. This covers the setup itself, for the cost of building it, see our [Tiger Salamander cost guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-cost-guide/), or the full [Tiger Salamander care guide](/guides/tiger-salamander/) for everything else.

<Figure src="/assets/images/tiger-salamander-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a tiger salamander enclosure showing deep substrate, a burrow, and the fully aquatic larval stage" caption="It's built to burrow, and the setup needs to support that, not fight against it." shareable />

## Enclosure Size

A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">20-gallon long tank</AffiliateLink>, 24 inches long by 18 inches wide by 12 inches tall, works as a minimum for a single adult. Favor horizontal floor space over height, this is a ground-dwelling, burrowing species, not a climber. Cohabitation generally isn't recommended, competition and even cannibalism are real risks, though some very large enclosures can occasionally support a small group, worth researching carefully before attempting.

## Temperature

60 to 75°F, and genuinely never above about 78°F, heat stress is a real concern for this species. Most rooms need no supplemental heat at all if they stay above roughly 60°F. Keep the enclosure out of direct sunlight entirely, a window can heat a tank to dangerous levels surprisingly fast. A digital thermometer and hygrometer combo makes both temperature and humidity easy to track at a glance, see our [Tiger Salamander cost guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-cost-guide/) for typical pricing on one.

## Humidity

70 to 75%, maintained through consistently moist, never soggy, substrate and a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/44hAzR9" product="Small/shallow water dish">shallow water dish</AffiliateLink>. A moisture gradient, one end of the enclosure slightly damper than the other, gives your salamander some choice in where to settle.

<FunFact>
Extra substrate depth beyond the 3 to 4 inch minimum is basically never wasted on this species, given how much it actually burrows in the wild. A shallow layer that looks fine on the surface can still leave a tiger salamander without enough room to do what comes naturally.
</FunFact>

## Substrate: The Single Most Important Decision in This Setup

This is genuinely the defining requirement for the species. Provide at least 3 to 4 inches of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CY23K32S?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="VCEPJH Loose Coconut Fiber Substrate, 2 Pack">moisture-retentive, burrow-supporting material</AffiliateLink>, coconut fiber, a commercial reptile soil product, organic fertilizer-free topsoil, or cypress mulch all work, and deeper is better given how much this species actually burrows in the wild. Avoid peat moss specifically, its acidity can cause real harm over time, and skip gravel, sand, small bark chips, and anything containing vermiculite or perlite, all carry genuine impaction or skin irritation risk, the kind of risk covered in more detail in our [Tiger Salamander health issues guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-health-issues-guide/).

## Water

A shallow dish of dechlorinated water should always be available. Treat all water with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4tXcbib" product="API Tap Water Conditioner (16 oz)">conditioner</AffiliateLink> before use, or use spring or distilled water instead.

## Lighting

This species is largely nocturnal and fossorial, and doesn't require UVB, though a low level is considered optional and potentially beneficial by some sources. A low-wattage light on a normal 10 to 12 hour day and night cycle supports a healthy rhythm without adding unnecessary heat.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Plan for a Life Stage Change

If you're starting with a young larval salamander, its early setup looks essentially identical to an [axolotl's](/blog/axolotl-tank-setup-guide/), fully aquatic. As it metamorphoses into its terrestrial adult form, you'll need to transition the enclosure entirely, from a water-based setup to the deep, burrowable terrestrial one described above. This is a normal part of the species' development, not something gone wrong, but it does mean planning for a genuine setup change partway through your salamander's early life, more on the difference in day to day care in our [Tiger Salamander handling guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-handling-guide/).
