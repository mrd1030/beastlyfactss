# Tiger Salamander Enclosure Setup: Deep Substrate for a Species That Lives Underground

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Tiger salamander husbandry is straightforward once you accept the central fact about this species: it's built to burrow, and the setup needs to support that, not fight against it.

<Figure src="/assets/images/tiger-salamander-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a tiger salamander enclosure showing deep substrate, a burrow, and the fully aquatic larval stage" caption="It's built to burrow, and the setup needs to support that, not fight against it." shareable />

## Enclosure Size

A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">20-gallon long tank</AffiliateLink>, 24 inches long by 18 inches wide by 12 inches tall, works as a minimum for a single adult. Favor horizontal floor space over height, this is a ground-dwelling, burrowing species, not a climber. Cohabitation generally isn't recommended, competition and even cannibalism are real risks, though some very large enclosures can occasionally support a small group, worth researching carefully before attempting.

## Temperature

60 to 75°F, and genuinely never above about 78°F, heat stress is a real concern for this species. Most rooms need no supplemental heat at all if they stay above roughly 60°F. Keep the enclosure out of direct sunlight entirely, a window can heat a tank to dangerous levels surprisingly fast. A digital thermometer and hygrometer combo makes both temperature and humidity easy to track at a glance.

## Humidity

70 to 75%, maintained through consistently moist, never soggy, substrate and a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/44hAzR9" product="Small/shallow water dish">shallow water dish</AffiliateLink>. A moisture gradient, one end of the enclosure slightly damper than the other, gives your salamander some choice in where to settle.

<FunFact>
Extra substrate depth beyond the 3 to 4 inch minimum is basically never wasted on this species, given how much it actually burrows in the wild. A shallow layer that looks fine on the surface can still leave a tiger salamander without enough room to do what comes naturally.
</FunFact>

## Substrate: The Single Most Important Decision in This Setup

This is the defining requirement for the species. Provide at least 3 to 4 inches of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CY23K32S?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="VCEPJH Loose Coconut Fiber Substrate, 2 Pack">moisture-retentive, burrow-supporting material</AffiliateLink>, coconut fiber, a commercial reptile soil product, organic fertilizer-free topsoil, or cypress mulch all work, and deeper is better given how much this species actually burrows in the wild. Depth is the setup number that decides which behaviors are available to the animal at all, which is the whole argument of the [tiger salamander enrichment guide](/blog/tiger-salamander-enrichment-guide/). Avoid peat moss specifically, its acidity can cause real harm over time, and skip gravel, sand, small bark chips, and anything containing vermiculite or perlite, all carry genuine impaction or skin irritation risk.

## Water

A shallow dish of dechlorinated water should always be available. Treat all water with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4tXcbib" product="API Tap Water Conditioner (16 oz)">conditioner</AffiliateLink> before use, or use bottled spring water instead. Distilled and reverse-osmosis water are the two to leave on the shelf, because stripping out essentially everything dissolved removes what an animal that exchanges water and salts across its own skin depends on, and the [amphibian water and quarantine guide](/blog/amphibian-quarantine-and-water-guide/) has the documented version of that.

## Lighting

This species is largely nocturnal and fossorial, and doesn't require UVB, though a low level is considered optional and potentially beneficial by some sources. A low-wattage light on a normal 10 to 12 hour day and night cycle supports a healthy rhythm without adding unnecessary heat.

## Diet Basics

A tiger salamander eats invertebrates, and crickets and earthworms or nightcrawlers make up the bulk of a good captive diet. Feed a growing juvenile every one to two days and an adult two to three times a week, at night, in small measured quantities. This species does not stop when it has had enough, which is why obesity is the problem it runs into most often.

Dust feeders with a calcium and vitamin D3 supplement plus a multivitamin, at every feeding while the animal is growing and every second to fourth feeding once it is adult. A frozen and thawed pinkie or fuzzy mouse is a rare treat at most for a large adult, never a staple and never fed live. Offer food off the substrate or from tongs, since a salamander that strikes hard at a worm will take a mouthful of whatever the worm was sitting on.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Plan for a Life Stage Change

If you're starting with a young larval salamander, its early setup looks essentially identical to an [axolotl's](/blog/axolotl-tank-setup-guide/), fully aquatic. As it metamorphoses into its terrestrial adult form, you'll need to transition the enclosure entirely, from a water-based setup to the deep, burrowable terrestrial one described above. This is a normal part of the species' development, not something gone wrong, but it does mean planning for a genuine setup change partway through your salamander's early life.
