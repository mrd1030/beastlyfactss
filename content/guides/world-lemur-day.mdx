An aye-aye finds its food by knocking. It walks along a branch tapping the wood rapidly with a thin, flexible third finger, listens with oversized ears for the hollow note of a beetle larva's tunnel, gnaws an opening with incisors that never stop growing, and hooks the grub out with that same finger. It is the largest nocturnal primate in the world, it weighs under 3 kilograms, and it is a lemur.

Lemurs get a day of their own at the end of October. Almost everything else about them is harder news.

## A Festival First, a Friday After

World Lemur Day falls on the last Friday of October, which makes it October 30 in 2026, the day before Halloween, and October 29 in 2027. It sits inside a longer World Lemur Festival, an annual celebration of lemurs and Madagascar that runs through the surrounding weeks.

The festival has a documented start. The first one was held in 2014 in Antananarivo, Madagascar's capital, organized by Professor Jonah Ratsimbazafy of GERP Madagascar. From 2015 it spread worldwide, to zoos, schools and online events. No single body runs it today. The Lemur Conservation Network, a US nonprofit that began as a project of the IUCN's primate specialists in Madagascar, promotes the events on its calendar and supplies organizers with activity guides, flyers and a lemur card game. The day itself has no recorded founder.

## Two Counts, One Verdict

In 2018 more than 50 primate specialists met in Antananarivo for an IUCN Species Survival Commission workshop led by the Primate Specialist Group. They reassessed all 111 lemur species and subspecies, every one of them endemic to Madagascar, and provisionally rated 105 as threatened: 38 Critically Endangered, 44 Endangered and 23 Vulnerable. That is about 95%. Russ Mittermeier, chair of the group, called it "without a doubt, the highest percentage of threat for any large group of mammals, and for any large group of vertebrates."

Those were workshop ratings, published ahead of the formal review every Red List assessment goes through. The official update came in July 2020 and counted species only, leaving subspecies out. It put 103 of the 107 lemur species in a threatened category and 33 of them, almost a third, in Critically Endangered. Thirteen species moved up a threat category. Two of them, Verreaux's sifaka and Madame Berthe's mouse lemur, the smallest primate in the world, went from Endangered to Critically Endangered.

The cause is forest. Slash-and-burn farming and logging for charcoal and fuelwood keep clearing what is left. Illegal hunting adds to it for species such as Verreaux's sifaka. The northern sportive lemur is severely depleted, and the specialist group's chair says it might already be gone without the more than 7.5 million dollars raised for lemurs through an IUCN fund.

The ring-tailed lemur, the face on most World Lemur Day posters and the one most people picture, is rated Endangered. A Red List rating carries no legal weight on its own, and in the US, [whether a private owner can legally keep one](/blog/ring-tailed-lemur-legal-guide/) comes down to the federal Endangered Species Act and state primate laws.

## The Lemur Nobody Recognizes

The aye-aye is the one that surprises people. It is listed as Endangered. Habitat loss and hunting are the main pressures, and in some communities it carries an older one: many villagers regard the aye-aye as an evil omen, fady, to be killed on sight.

Its hand turned out to be stranger than anyone had described. In a study published in late 2019, Adam Hartstone-Rose and colleagues at North Carolina State University dissected the wrists and hands of seven aye-ayes and found a pseudothumb in every one: an enlarged sesamoid bone with a dense cartilage extension, worked by three muscles, with its own pad on the palm complete with its own fingerprint ridges. No primate had been documented with one before. The closest comparison is the giant panda's famous false thumb.

The authors' explanation is the tapping. The aye-aye's fingers are so specialized for knocking and probing that they are poor at gripping, and the extra digit makes up some of the hold they lose. The rest of the kit, [teeth, ears and finger working as one foraging system](/beastlypedia/aye-aye/), is built around the same grubs.

Lemurs belong to the strepsirrhines, the branch of primates that also holds the lorises of Asia, and the branch has a habit of unlikely equipment. [The slow loris is the only primate known to be venomous](/blog/slow-loris-the-only-venomous-primate/), mixing a secretion from a gland on its arm into its saliva. The aye-aye settled for an extra thumb. Every piece of that equipment is built for trees, and trees are what Madagascar keeps losing. A lemur's specializations are a bet on the forest staying.
