# Common Canary Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Canaries face a handful of conditions common enough in this species to know before they show up, several of them mite-related and easy to overlook until song or breathing is already affected.

## Air-Sac Mites

A parasite of the respiratory tract, found most often in canaries and Gouldian finches. Watch for open-mouth breathing, clicking or wheezing sounds, and, tellingly, a loss of song in a previously singing male. Always see an avian vet, this needs targeted treatment and won't resolve without it. Caused by exposure to an already-infected bird, and worsened by stress or overcrowding.

<FunFact>
A previously singing male canary going quiet is one of the more telling early signs of air-sac mites. The loss of song can show up before more obvious breathing symptoms do, which makes it worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as a mood change.
</FunFact>

## Scaly Face and Leg Mites

Crusty, roughened buildup around the beak, eyes, legs, and feet. See a vet. It is treatable, and easier caught early.

## Feather and Red Mites

Bald patches and visible nighttime itching, since these mites feed at night rather than during the day, which is part of why they're easy to miss initially. A related sign, unexplained lethargy that gets worse over time, can point to anemia from a heavy infestation. Treatment needs to cover both the bird and its full environment, cage, perches, and surrounding area, mites left in the cage will simply reinfest a treated bird otherwise.

## Respiratory Infection

Ruffled feathers, discharge, and labored breathing. Caused by [poor air quality](/blog/canary-tank-setup-guide/), a dirty cage, household fumes, or drafts. Always see a vet.

## Canary Pox and Atoxoplasmosis

Two infections show up in canaries far more than in pet parrots. **Canary pox** is a virus carried by mosquitoes. It raises lumps around the head and feet, and cases climb in wet years when mosquitoes are plentiful, so canaries housed outdoors carry the most risk. An avian vet can immunize an outdoor canary against it.

**Atoxoplasmosis** is a coccidian parasite that invades the liver and spleen as well as the gut. It shows as diarrhea and deaths, mostly in juveniles, and it is still diagnosed in canaries. Neither infection announces itself in a bird that has just arrived, which is why a new canary goes through [quarantine](/blog/bird-quarantine-guide/) before it shares air or a cage with the bird already at home.

## Other Conditions Worth Knowing

Cataracts, feather cysts and baldness in males are the non-infectious problems that turn up in this species. Feather cysts are most common in varieties with thick, dense feathering such as the Norwich, and sometimes need surgical removal. Feather-picking can signal mites, a diet deficiency, boredom, or general stress, worth a vet visit to rule out a medical cause before assuming it's purely behavioral.

## Egg Binding in Hens

Egg binding turns up in canary hens as it does in other small birds. Large eggs, too little calcium, very young or old hens, and cold weather are the usual contributors. A hen sitting fluffed on the cage floor, straining, with a swollen abdomen and no egg passed is a same-day emergency, because a bird this small can die within hours of an egg lodging. The triggers that keep a single hen laying, and the full sign list, are the same ones [budgie, cockatiel and lovebird hens](/blog/bird-chronic-egg-laying-guide/) face: long days, unlimited rich food, and a spot that feels like a nest. A hen on a normal day length with calcium from pellets and a cuttlebone has the margin to pass an egg.

## The Early Warning Signs Worth Watching For

A canary that stops singing, shows fluffed or dull feathers, or becomes generally lethargic is signaling something's wrong, well before more specific symptoms might appear. Given how many of this species' issues trace back to mites and air quality, both preventable, paying attention to these early, general signs matters more than waiting for a specific symptom to confirm what's going on.

Most of what is on this list starts in the dish: the vitamin A deficiency behind the beak and foot crusting, the fatty liver behind a quiet, heavy bird, and the calcium a laying hen runs short of all come from a seed-heavy diet, and the feeding guide has the pellet share that prevents them.
