# Common Giant Millipede Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Giant millipedes are hardy, and nearly every serious issue in this species traces back to substrate moisture, either too little or, in a couple of specific cases, conditions that stay too consistently wet.

## Dehydration

The number one cause of death in this species. Watch for a shriveled, sluggish appearance and dry, cracking segments along the body. Caused by insufficient humidity or substrate that isn't holding moisture properly. Manageable early, raise humidity, moisten the substrate more deeply, and provide a shallow water dish. Stopping it from recurring is a humidity and substrate depth question. Prevention through consistent humidity matters more here than any at-home fix once dehydration is already advanced.

## Failed or Incomplete Molt

Often fatal. Caused by low humidity, poor nutrition, or disturbance during the vulnerable molting process. Never assist a molt or attempt to remove shed skin yourself, this can cause real harm. The only effective response is maintaining correct humidity beforehand and leaving your millipede completely undisturbed during the process itself. A burrowed, seemingly unresponsive millipede is very often just molting rather than in distress.

## Mite Overgrowth

<FunFact>
Not every mite on a pet millipede is a problem. Many species living alongside a captive millipede are harmless and genuinely part of a normal bioactive setup, it's only a heavy infestation, usually clustering visibly near the head or legs, that signals real trouble.
</FunFact>

A heavy infestation shows up as unusually frequent cleaning behavior, thrashing, or visible mite clusters concentrated near the head or legs. Gently wipe affected areas with a damp cotton swab if needed. Never use pesticides or mite sprays, these are toxic to millipedes and can kill the animal you're trying to help. Caused by substrate that's too wet combined with poor ventilation.

One commensal mite species arrives on wild-caught *Archispirostreptus* and is harmless. A new arrival is the other way problem mites get in. A millipede bought to join an established group spends three months in its own tub with its own tools first, the same [invertebrate quarantine](/blog/invertebrate-quarantine-cleaning-and-escapes-guide/) window used for tarantulas and every other pet invertebrate.

## Foot Rot

Legs that turn black at the tips, then look clipped, then wear down to stumps are foot rot, a bacterial infection that can also reach the antennae and damaged patches of shell. It turns up mostly on wild-caught millipedes and anything housed with them, it spreads between animals, and it is usually fatal over the long run unless the millipede molts out of it. The molt is the only known cure: the infected legs go with the old exoskeleton.

Management until then:

- Move the affected animal to its own enclosure, and keep healthy tankmates apart.
- Clean the legs and segments gently with a soft toothbrush under a thin stream of room-temperature water, never submerging the animal, since it breathes through spiracles along the sides of each segment. Use a separate brush for sick and healthy animals.
- Keep the top layer of substrate drier than usual, because warm, wet conditions speed the rot, while the bottom layer stays damp so the millipede can burrow down to rehydrate.
- Once it has molted clean and hardened, clean it once more and move it into a disinfected enclosure with all-new substrate.

## Exoskeleton Injury

Cracks or lost legs and antennae, usually from a fall or a difficult molt. Lost legs and antennae grow back over a few molts, often a little shorter than before. Dented or scratched segments are common, especially on wild-caught animals, and rarely dangerous. A cracked segment is the serious one: it complicates molts, lets infection in, and a millipede with a cracked segment that gets dropped usually dies from loss of hemolymph. A major crack calls for isolating the animal in a clean, simplified hospital enclosure while it recovers.

## Phorid Flies

Small humpbacked flies that look like fruit flies but walk more than they fly are phorid, or coffin, flies. They lay eggs in the millipede's body, and the maggots cause a long, slow decline and death. Keep the enclosure lid fine-meshed, clear rotting produce promptly, and deal with any fly population in the room before it reaches the tank.

## Necrotic Lesions and Soft Exoskeleton

Dark lesions point to substrate that's swampy and poorly ventilated, worth correcting immediately. A soft, weak-feeling exoskeleton points to calcium deficiency, add a genuine calcium source like cuttlebone or crushed oyster shell to the enclosure, not chalk, which isn't an appropriate calcium source for this species.

## When a Vet Visit Makes Sense

Few vets treat millipedes, but an exotics practice that sees invertebrates can advise on safe mite control and look at an injury or a failing animal. The millipede travels in a ventilated container with several inches of its own substrate to hold humidity, kept warm and out of direct sun and drafts. The container rules are the same ones that keep any invertebrate safe on the road, set out in the [invertebrate travel and transport guide](/blog/invertebrate-emergency-travel-shipping-guide/).

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Low humidity causes both the dehydration and failed molts that account for most serious health problems in this species. Excess wetness combined with poor airflow invites mite overgrowth, foot rot and necrotic lesions instead. And falls, entirely preventable through careful handling, cause most physical injuries. Balanced humidity, more than anything else, is the key to this species' health.
