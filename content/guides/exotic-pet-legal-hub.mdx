# The Pet Bans That Are Real, and the Ones That Aren't

Ask the internet whether a given exotic pet is legal, and you'll usually get one of two wrong answers: a confident "it's banned everywhere" that turns out to be one state's rule stretched into a rumor, or a confident "it's totally unregulated" that misses a real restriction sitting in plain sight in a state statute. Every species-specific legal guide below exists to replace that guesswork with the actual primary source. This hub pulls all of them together, plus the federal and state framework that sits underneath every one of them.

If you want the answer for one animal in one state, start with the **[interactive exotic pet law map](/exotic-pet-laws/map/)**. It covers 53 animals across all 50 states and DC, plus New York City, and every entry on it quotes the statute or regulation it came from with a link to the source. Pick your animal, and the states that restrict it light up.

## The Federal Layer: What It Actually Controls

Here's the part that surprises most people: **no federal law speaks expressly to the issue of private wild animal ownership**. That's not a gap in this research, it's the documented legal reality, and it comes directly from the Animal Legal & Historical Center's own framing of the field. Congress regulates exotic animals only through its enumerated powers, mainly the Commerce Clause, which means federal law reaches importation, interstate transport, international trade, and commercial activity, not whether you personally can keep an animal you already lawfully have.

What federal law does actually do:

- **The Lacey Act** works in two separate pieces that get conflated constantly. One provision (18 U.S.C. § 42) bans importing or shipping across state lines any species listed as "injurious wildlife," a front-end list currently covering close to 800 species, including certain large constrictor snakes and, as of a January 2025 rule expansion, 36 genera of salamanders linked to a fungal disease risk. The other provision (16 U.S.C. §§ 3371 et seq.) is a back-end enforcement multiplier: it turns a violation of any underlying wildlife law, state, federal, or foreign, into a separate federal crime once the animal moves in commerce.
- **The Endangered Species Act** prohibits harming, importing, exporting, or commercially trading listed species without a permit. Its captive-bred wildlife program exists for conservation breeding between registered facilities, not for private pet keeping.
- **The Animal Welfare Act** regulates dealers, breeders, and exhibitors, not private owners, and only covers warm-blooded animals. Most pet reptiles, fish, amphibians, and invertebrates fall outside it entirely.
- **CITES** regulates international trade only, permits for crossing a national border, and explicitly does not govern possession within a country.
- **The FDA's small-turtle rule** (21 CFR 1240.62) bans the commercial sale of turtles under 4 inches to curb Salmonella risk to children. It's an FDA rule under the Public Health Service Act, not a CDC rule, CDC's separate role covers importing turtles and certain other zoonotic-risk animals into the US.
- **The Big Cat Public Safety Act**, enacted in 2022, shows what federal law looks like when it does decide to act decisively: a full ban on private big cat ownership and breeding, with a one-time grandfathering window for existing owners that closed in June 2023 and is not reopening. It's not relevant to the species this site covers, but it's a useful contrast to how lightly federal law otherwise touches private exotic pet ownership.

Each of those six is worked through against its own statutory text in the [federal exotic pet law guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/), including the 2017 court ruling that ended the interstate transport ban on injurious species and the CITES appendix changes that took effect in March 2026.

## State Law: Where the Real Restrictions Actually Live

If federal law mostly steps back, states step in, and they land in roughly four categories: states with a comprehensive ban on most wild or exotic pets, states that ban a specific list of species while allowing others, states that allow ownership under a formal licensing or permit system, and a small number of states with no comprehensive ownership law at all, just import and health rules. Which category your state falls into is the single most useful thing to know before acquiring any exotic pet, and it varies by state legislative session, so treat any state-bucket summary, including the guides below, as a snapshot of the most recent comprehensive review rather than a permanently fixed list. Local city and county ordinances add yet another layer on top, and they can restrict or ban species that state law otherwise allows.

<FunFact>
Courts have almost universally upheld state exotic-pet restrictions as a valid use of state police power, the same authority states use for public health and safety regulation generally. Combined with limited wildlife-agency inspector capacity in most states, this produces a legal landscape that's simultaneously well-established in court and unevenly enforced in practice, worth keeping in mind if you're weighing a species where the letter of the law and the odds of enforcement feel very different.
</FunFact>

## Every Species-Specific Legal Guide

Every guide below was written from the statute or regulation itself, and each one links to its row on the map. Several exist to run a rumor down rather than to report a ban: the chinchilla in California and Texas, the hognose as a supposedly venomous snake, the ball python under Florida's constrictor rules, the giant millipede's imaginary 2006 import ban, and the African grey's CITES listing that sounds like a prohibition and is not. The rest report restrictions that are real and usually narrower, or stranger, than the version that circulates.

### Reptiles

- **[Ackie Monitor](/blog/ackie-monitor-legal-guide/)**: Louisiana bans two larger monitor species by name but was never written to reach this two-foot dwarf species.
- **[Argentine Tegu](/blog/argentine-tegu-legal-guide/)**: banned from new acquisition in Florida since April 2021, with a now-closed grandfathering window for prior owners.
- **[Ball Python](/blog/ball-python-legal-guide/)**: not covered by Florida's large-constrictor restrictions, legal nearly everywhere except Hawaii and New York City.
- **[Bearded Dragon](/blog/bearded-dragon-legal-guide/)**: the bearded dragon is the most commonly kept pet lizard in the country and clears 46 of 52 jurisdictions without a permit. Its home country stopped exporting live native reptiles in 1982, which is why every animal in the trade is captive-bred stock.
- **[Blue-Tongue Skink](/blog/blue-tongue-skink-legal-guide/)**: blue-tongued skinks are cleared in 46 of 52 jurisdictions, but every rule that does reach them reaches them as skinks rather than as this species. That single fact explains why their legal map differs from a gecko's in places keepers assume are identical.
- **[Boa Constrictor](/blog/boa-constrictor-legal-guide/)**: federally withdrawn from consideration in 2015, but Hawaii bans all snakes outright, New York City bans the whole family municipally, and New Jersey goes the other way and exempts the boa constrictor by name while requiring a permit for every python.
- **[Box Turtle](/blog/box-turtle-legal-guide/)**: wild collection is banned or heavily restricted in most states, and Florida caps total possession at two per person regardless of how they were acquired.
- **[Burmese Python](/blog/burmese-python-legal-guide/)**: federal injurious wildlife since 2012, but a 2017 D.C. Circuit ruling removed the interstate transport ban, and twenty-seven jurisdictions still have no rule of their own.
- **[California Kingsnake](/blog/kingsnake-legal-guide/)**: Oregon and Nevada both write their rules around what the snake looks like, not what species it is.
- **[Corn Snake](/blog/corn-snake-legal-guide/)**: Georgia's own wildlife agency bans this classic starter snake outright as a native species, captive-bred or not, and New Jersey lists the species as endangered and then names nine color morphs you may keep.
- **[Crested Gecko](/blog/crested-gecko-legal-guide/)**: crested geckos clear 45 of 52 jurisdictions without a permit, which makes the seven exceptions worth knowing precisely. The one most keepers never see coming is Massachusetts, where the permit is triggered by the species' conservation status rather than by anything it does.
- **[Garter Snake](/blog/garter-snake-legal-guide/)**: New York requires a permit even for one bought at a pet store, its own guidance says captive-bred and wild-caught are treated the same.
- **[Green Anole](/blog/green-anole-legal-guide/)**: unregulated across most of the country and restricted in the eight southeastern states it is native to. Mississippi charges a hunting license, Louisiana wrote a rule about its length.
- **[Green Iguana](/blog/green-iguana-legal-guide/)**: the green iguana is the most restricted lizard in common keeping, and the map changed recently. Florida added it to the prohibited list in 2021, which means a great deal of older advice describing Florida as the easy state for this animal is now backwards.
- **[Hognose Snake](/blog/hognose-snake-legal-guide/)**: cleared by name or by silence in most venomous-reptile laws, but New York City bans it as a rear-fanged colubrid, Hawaii bans every snake, and Kansas protects both its native hognoses. Georgia, New York State and West Virginia ban only their native hognoses, not the western.
- **[Jackson's Chameleon](/blog/jacksons-chameleon-legal-guide/)**: Jackson's chameleons clear 46 of 52 jurisdictions, and the one that bars them is the one where they are most abundant. Hawaii's feral population descends from a single 1972 release in Kaneohe, and no private keeper there may hold one.
- **[Leopard Gecko](/blog/leopard-gecko-legal-guide/)**: the leopard gecko is one of the least restricted reptiles in the country, cleared without a permit in 46 of 52 jurisdictions. Hawaii is the interesting exception, because it names this species individually rather than reaching it through a family.
- **[Milk Snake](/blog/milk-snake-legal-guide/)**: the same Georgia ban as corn snake, plus Wisconsin restricts buying one at all and Pennsylvania caps possession at one regardless of where the animal came from.
- **[Nile Monitor](/blog/nile-monitor-legal-guide/)**: the Nile monitor is the only monitor named on Florida's prohibited list, and one of very few reptiles that several states bar by name rather than by family. Nine jurisdictions ban it and eight more require a permit.
- **[Red-Eared Slider](/blog/red-eared-slider-legal-guide/)**: the well-known federal rule only bans commercial sale of small turtles, ownership restrictions actually come from state invasive-species laws, which vary and occasionally change on short notice.
- **[Red-Footed Tortoise](/blog/red-footed-tortoise-legal-guide/)**: no US jurisdiction bans the red-footed tortoise outright, which makes it unusual among the reptiles on this map. What it gets instead is conditions, and the most consequential one is a rule about shell size that has nothing to do with the species.
- **[Rosy Boa](/blog/rosy-boa-legal-guide/)**: New York City bans it under a family-wide rule written for anacondas, and California's wild population goes by a different scientific name than the one sold in stores.
- **[Russian Tortoise](/blog/russian-tortoise-legal-guide/)**: no state bans the species. The four-inch rule everyone cites is federal, restricts selling rather than owning, and carries an exception for sales not made in connection with a business.
- **[Savannah Monitor](/blog/savannah-monitor-legal-guide/)**: Louisiana bans this species by name while most states reach only the giant monitors, and Arkansas clears ten monitors without including this one.
- **[Snapping Turtle](/blog/snapping-turtle-legal-guide/)**: the one native reptile a dozen states still let you take, answered with a season and a bag limit rather than a yes or a no, and usually filed under fishing rather than wildlife.
- **[Sulcata Tortoise](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-legal-guide/)**: no state bans this species specifically, but a federal import restriction in place since 2000 shapes where captive-bred animals actually come from.
- **[Tokay Gecko](/blog/tokay-gecko-legal-guide/)**: the tokay has the worst reputation of any commonly kept gecko and the cleanest legal record. It clears 48 of 52 jurisdictions, and in New Jersey it is the one gecko that does not need a permit, because the rule carves it out by name.
- **[Veiled Chameleon](/blog/veiled-chameleon-legal-guide/)**: banned in Washington DC because the permitted-pet list has no lizard category at all, and in Hawaii because the species is already established on Maui and Oahu.

### Amphibians

- **[Axolotl](/blog/axolotl-legal-guide/)**: genuinely banned in California, New Jersey, and Maine, and reportedly in Washington DC, all driven by hybridization risk with native salamanders, not endangered-species status.
- **[Tiger Salamander](/blog/tiger-salamander-legal-guide/)**: added to the federal injurious wildlife list in January 2025, a listing USFWS's own FAQ says explicitly does not affect ownership, breeding, or interstate sale within the continental US.

### Birds

- **[African Grey Parrot](/blog/african-grey-parrot-legal-guide/)**: carries CITES Appendix I, the strictest international trade tier there is, yet a captive-breeding exemption keeps domestically bred birds completely legal to own, and no US state bans the species.
- **[Cockatoo](/blog/cockatoo-legal-guide/)**: Maine clears every parrot except the ones on the strictest international trade and endangered species lists, which happens to catch several of the cockatoos people actually keep.
- **[Quaker Parrot](/blog/quaker-parakeet-legal-guide/)**: fourteen bans, more than any other bird here, and none of them is about danger. Nebraska bans it by writing it out of a definition, and Virginia lets it through on a seamless leg band.

### Small mammals

- **[Chinchilla](/blog/chinchilla-legal-guide/)**: online claims that California or Texas restrict ownership don't survive a look at the actual statute text.
- **[Degu](/blog/degu-legal-guide/)**: the degu is the most restricted small rodent kept as a pet in the US, and the reason repeats almost word for word from state to state: the cleared-rodent list names the chinchilla, guinea pig, gerbil, hamster, mouse and rat, and stops there.
- **[Ferret](/blog/ferret-legal-guide/)**: legal almost everywhere in the US, but outright banned in California and Hawaii, plus a local ban in New York City.
- **[Flying Squirrel](/blog/flying-squirrel-legal-guide/)**: the most restricted small pet in the set, only nine of the 52 jurisdictions are a clean legal yes, and Montana prohibits the species by binomial.
- **[Gerbil](/blog/gerbil-legal-guide/)**: gerbils are legal in 50 of 52 US jurisdictions, which makes California the outlier everyone has heard about and almost nobody can cite correctly. The ban is real, and the way the regulation gets there is stranger than the ban itself.
- **[Guinea Pig](/blog/guinea-pig-legal-guide/)**: the guinea pig is the least restricted animal on this entire map, cleared in all 52 jurisdictions with only Hawaii attaching a condition. The question worth answering instead is the pairs one, and the answer is not what most people have heard.
- **[Hamster](/blog/hamster-legal-guide/)**: legal in every state but Hawaii, where the golden hamster sits on a research-and-exhibition list with no pet route. California clears both hamster groups by name and bans the gerbil in the next cage over.
- **[Hedgehog](/blog/hedgehog-legal-guide/)**: six places will not allow one, and the reasons are all different, Pennsylvania has no hedgehog law at all and bans them through an agency's reading of a definition.
- **[Prairie Dog](/blog/prairie-dog-legal-guide/)**: the federal monkeypox-era ban ended in 2008 and fifteen states kept their own, including Colorado, which prohibits the genus in the middle of the animal's native range.
- **[Rabbit](/blog/rabbit-legal-guide/)**: rabbits are unrestricted in 50 of 52 jurisdictions, so this would be a short article if two states had not written rules that appear to reach every pet rabbit in the country without ever intending to.
- **[Sugar Glider](/blog/sugar-glider-legal-guide/)**: illegal in California, Alaska, and Hawaii, and effectively unobtainable in Pennsylvania, several other commonly cited state bans turn out not to be real.

### Larger mammals

- **[Bengal Cat](/blog/bengal-cat-legal-guide/)**: the answer changes per cat, not just per state. New York clears the fifth generation, Iowa the fourth, Maryland uses a 30-pound weight test, and Minnesota's rule reaches every subsequent generation forever.
- **[Capybara](/blog/capybara-legal-guide/)**: twelve bans and thirteen permits, almost all of them from rules that regulate the order Rodentia and never mention the animal. Connecticut clears the Patagonian mara and not this.
- **[Fennec Fox](/blog/fennec-fox-legal-guide/)**: a three-pound desert fox caught by rules written about rabies vectors. Maryland bans it with the single word "fox," and Montana clears the serval while barring every canid.
- **[Serval](/blog/serval-legal-guide/)**: the most restricted animal in the set, with sixteen outright bans, because almost every state that regulates cats does it by family. Ohio bans the serval and expressly legalizes the savannah cat hybrid in the same sentence.

### Invertebrates

- **[Emperor Scorpion](/blog/emperor-scorpion-legal-guide/)**: Montana names it as noncontrolled while banning every pet tarantula, and Maine does the reverse. The only rule that follows it everywhere is a CITES export requirement.
- **[Giant African Millipede](/blog/giant-millipede-legal-guide/)**: no such thing as the "2006 import ban" that circulates online, the real rule is a USDA plant-pest import permit, with a few states adding their own conditions.
- **[Madagascar Hissing Cockroach](/blog/madagascar-hissing-cockroach-legal-guide/)**: most state wildlife law was written for animals with a backbone, and the hissing cockroach lands in the hole that leaves. Four jurisdictions ban it, one requires an agriculture permit, and three cannot be read either way.
- **[Tarantula](/blog/tarantula-legal-guide/)**: legal in most states only because their wildlife codes stop at vertebrates. Maine clears exactly three species by name, so which tarantula you own decides the answer there.

## How to Verify Any Species Yourself

The guides above cover what we found in original research on each species. For anything not listed here, or to double-check before you buy, the process is the same every time:

1. Identify the exact species by its scientific name, common names are where most confusion starts.
2. Check the federal lists that actually matter: the Endangered Species Act, the Lacey Act's injurious wildlife list, CITES appendices, and CDC import rules.
3. Go directly to your state's wildlife, fish and game, or natural resources agency website, and read the actual statute or administrative code, not a secondhand summary.
4. Search your city and county ordinances separately, municipal codes and local animal control departments frequently add restrictions state law doesn't mention.
5. Contact the responsible agency in writing for your specific species and situation, and ask for current forms and facility standards if a permit applies.
6. For breeding, selling, or exhibiting rather than simple pet ownership, also check USDA APHIS licensing requirements.
7. For high-value or high-risk situations, an attorney experienced in animal or wildlife law is worth the consultation fee before you buy, not after.

None of this is legal advice, and neither is any guide it links to. Laws change, sometimes with little public notice, so treat every one of them as a well-sourced starting point and confirm directly with the issuing agency before you act.
