# How Much Does a Leopard Gecko Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Leopard geckos are one of the more budget-friendly reptiles to keep, but "budget-friendly" still means a real setup, not a shortcut version.

## The Gecko Itself

A standard or wild-type leopard gecko from a pet store runs $20 to $45. Reputable breeders often charge $50 to $150 even for basic morphs, which sounds backwards until you factor in health guarantees and proper care history. Popular morphs land around $75 to $300, and rare designer morphs can run $500 to $3,500 or more. If you're buying online, add $35 to $100 for live shipping.

Whatever the price, look the animal over before paying: clear, clean eyes, a good appetite, a bright and alert manner, and a thick tail, since the tail is where a leopard gecko stores its fat and a thin one is the first sign of wasting disease. Ask a breeder whether the animal has had a fecal test. Cryptosporidium, the parasite behind "stick tail," was found in 51% of geckos screened at one Thai breeding facility, it has no cure, and apparently healthy carriers spread it. That is why the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) keeps a new gecko alone for 3 to 6 months, and why the first vet visit in the table below includes a fecal test.

Highly recommended, not counted in the total: a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076GXJFJG?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Solarmeter Model 6.5R Reptile UV Index Meter">UV index meter</AffiliateLink>, about $240 to $265. It is the only way to check that the UVB tube sits at the right height and to know when the tube needs replacing; UV cards do not measure UVB.

The thermostat isn't optional: it's what keeps the heat mat from overheating and burning your gecko. The UVB fixture, a low-output tube, is optional but genuinely beneficial. Don't be fooled by the 2.5% on the tube: the percentage is the share of its light that is UVB, not how strong it is, and Arcadia's own chart puts this 24-inch, 14-watt tube at a higher UV index than the short 12-inch 7% kit at the same distance (1.0 against 0.65 at 12 inches). A T5 tube's wattage is not its length: a 24-watt tube is about 22 inches long, a 39-watt tube about 34 inches and a 54-watt tube about 46 inches, so go by the length in inches when you match it to two-thirds of the enclosure. Fully equipped, the gear comes to $315 to $760 before the gecko, and the first vet exam and fecal test bring the total to $465 to $975.

## Vet Costs to Plan For

An annual wellness exam runs $85 to $105. That's the routine cost. What catches people off guard is that sick or emergency visits run $100 to $800 or more, and if diagnostics or surgery get involved, it climbs from there. A leopard gecko should see a vet once a year even when it looks healthy, so the exam is a fixed yearly line, not an optional one. Keeping a small emergency fund set aside is worth more peace of mind than it costs.

## The Lifetime Number

The monthly figures look small because the timeline is long. A leopard gecko lives 10 to 20 years with proper care, and some reach 25 to 30. At $360 to $1,020 a year in running costs, 10 to 20 years adds up to $3,600 to $20,400 before a single sick visit. Budget for the gecko reaching its twenties: a hatchling bought today may outlast the enclosure, the heat mat, and several UVB tubes.

## The Bottom Line

Leopard geckos are one of the more affordable reptiles to keep long-term: low monthly costs, a long lifespan, and minimal equipment turnover once the initial setup is done. The upfront cost is where most of the money goes, and it's worth spending properly on the thermostat and enclosure size rather than cutting corners there.

The leopard gecko is one of the [five reptiles that make the most sense for beginners](/blog/five-beginner-reptiles-overview/), alongside the bearded dragon, ball python, crested gecko, and corn snake, and like each of them it is a commitment of a decade or more.
