# Common Koi Health Issues (Water Quality Is the Root of Almost Everything)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Across koi keeping resources, one theme repeats constantly: poor water quality is behind most health problems. Elevated ammonia or nitrite, unstable pH, low oxygen, overcrowding, and sudden changes all stress fish and open the door to the issues below. Our [Koi pond setup guide](/blog/koi-tank-setup-guide/) covers the filtration side of prevention, and our full [Koi care guide](/guides/koi/) covers everything else.

## Parasites

Ich (white spot), Costia, Trichodina, flukes, anchor worm, and fish lice are the common culprits. Watch for flashing or rubbing against pond surfaces, excess mucus, clamped fins, lethargy, and in some cases visible worms or lice. New fish or plants introduced without quarantine are a common source, and stress makes existing parasite populations flare up.

Many cases are manageable at home with salt treatments or targeted antiparasitics, potassium permanganate, praziquantel, and dimilin are commonly used for specific parasites, but only once water quality is corrected and the issue is caught early. Confirm diagnosis where possible rather than guessing.

## Bacterial Infections

Commonly caused by Aeromonas and related bacteria, showing up as ulcers, fin or mouth rot, and columnaris. Look for redness, sores, frayed fins, and visible tissue damage alongside lethargy. These are usually secondary, following stress, injury, or an existing parasite problem combined with poor water. Early cases often respond to improved conditions plus antibiotics, ideally vet guided for valuable fish, while advanced ulcers can become a genuine emergency.

## Environmental Toxicity and Water Quality Crises

Ammonia burns, nitrite poisoning, oxygen depletion, and chlorine exposure from an untreated tap water change can kill quickly. Signs include gasping, flashing, lethargy, red streaking, clamped fins, and a fish isolating itself from the group. Treat this as an emergency: large partial water changes with properly dechlorinated water, added aeration, and immediate testing with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">water test kit</AffiliateLink>. This category is almost always directly caused by inadequate filtration, overfeeding, overstocking, or simply skipping routine water tests.

## Fungal Infections

Saprolegnia and similar fungi cause cotton-like growths, typically secondary to an existing injury or period of stress. Usually manageable once the underlying cause is addressed.

## Viral Disease

Koi Herpesvirus (KHV) is the most serious concern, alongside carp pox and Spring Viremia. KHV causes gill necrosis, excess mucus, lethargy, and skin lesions, and outbreaks can carry mortality rates of 80 to 100%.

<FunFact>
KHV is temperature dependent and has no reliable cure, and strict quarantine, often 4 to 6 weeks or longer, sometimes at specific temperatures, is the critical prevention step. What surprises a lot of new keepers is that even fish who survive an outbreak can remain lifelong carriers, which is exactly why quarantine gets treated as non-negotiable rather than a formality in serious koi keeping circles.
</FunFact>

This is emergency and culling level serious, and it's a big part of why quarantine matters so much before introducing any new fish, covered in more detail in our [Koi handling guide](/blog/koi-handling-guide/).

## Emergencies vs. Manageable at Home

Gasping combined with high ammonia, rapid widespread mortality, severe open ulcers, or suspected KHV are all urgent, get water quality corrected immediately and involve a vet if one is available. Mild flashing or early stage parasites with otherwise good water parameters are often manageable at home with close monitoring.

For valuable fish or any unclear diagnosis, always lean toward professional guidance, though many keepers rely on experienced koi specific resources for routine issues since aquatic vets are genuinely limited in availability. ## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Inadequate filtration, overstocking, or overfeeding leads to chronic ammonia and nitrite stress, which opens the door to both parasites and bacterial ulcers.
