# How Much Does a Bearded Dragon Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The dragon itself is the cheap part.

A standard morph runs $40 to $100 at most pet stores. The equipment around it is where the budget goes, and it isn't optional. Bearded dragons need a specific setup to stay healthy, and cutting corners here is how expensive vet bills happen later.

A named morph costs more. Juveniles with one or two visible traits, such as a hypo (lighter colors), a leatherback (smooth scales), a translucent or a citrus or red color line, are around $125 to $500, and the patternless zeros and witblits around $200 to $750. Rare lines such as paradox and the deepest reds run $1,000 to $2,500. The extra money buys color and scale texture, and the care and setup are the same. The one thing that changes is the lighting for hypo and translucent dragons, which burn more easily under UVB, so their basking UV index stays at 3.0 or lower.

## Where the Dragon Comes From

Most bearded dragons come from breeders or pet stores, since the species breeds well in captivity and is easy to find. A young, captive-raised dragon makes the better pet. An older, wild-caught animal is harder to tame, more likely to carry intestinal parasites, and often stressed by captivity, so the cheapest adult on offer can end up the most expensive one.

Wherever it comes from, check the animal before paying:

- **Alert and active**, head up and watching you.
- **A round, plump tail base** just behind the back legs. A thin tail base, loose skin or a skinny body is a warning sign.
- **Bright, open eyes**, not sunken ones.
- **A bright pink mouth with clear saliva.** Cloudy mucus, cottage-cheese material, or red or bruised gums point to infection.

Ask the seller about a health guarantee, and book the reptile vet for the first 48 hours: that first exam and fecal test run $120 to $245 on top of the setup figure. If another reptile already lives in the house, the new dragon also needs a separate room and its own tools, and the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) sets out that setup and the three to six month clock.

It is the only way to check that the UVB tube sits at the right height and to know when the tube needs replacing; UV cards do not measure UVB.

The enclosure is the adult minimum, not a "starter size" you'll outgrow. Buying it from day one saves you from buying twice. The UVB fixture isn't a nice-to-have either: bearded dragons need strong, specific UVB or they develop metabolic bone disease, which is both common and preventable. A T5 tube's wattage is not its length: a 24-watt tube is about 22 inches long, a 39-watt tube about 34 inches and a 54-watt tube about 46 inches, so go by the length in inches when you match it to two-thirds of the enclosure.

The thermostat is one of the bigger lines here, and it earns it. It regulates your heat source so it can't overheat and burn your dragon or start a fire, so don't skip it to save money. For thermometers, a digital probe is the minimum, but an infrared temperature gun is worth the extra cost since it reads the basking surface itself, where a probe only reads the air.

Substrate costs next to nothing: paper towels work fine and take loose substrate out of the impaction risk, which matters more than it sounds like it should.

### Going Bioactive

Experienced keepers sometimes trade the paper towel for a living floor: a deep arid soil mix, plants, and a cleanup crew of isopods and springtails that breaks down waste. A [bioactive build](/blog/bioactive-setups-bearded-dragons/) keeps the same lighting and heat, so the extra cost is the floor itself, priced here with a DIY soil mix of organic topsoil and play sand (bagged arid mixes cost more). The raw volume overstates it: in my own dragon's 4 by 2 foot enclosure, two bags of topsoil were enough, because the hides, the climbing branches and the basking platform take up floor the mix never has to fill. It is a bigger commitment than paper towel, and an optional one.

The feeder line is an adult's. A juvenile eats roughly 80% insects until about 18 months old, so expect the feeder bill to run higher than this range until the diet flips toward greens.

The UVB bulb needs replacing every 6 to 12 months, since bulbs stop producing usable UVB well before they burn out, so replacing on a schedule matters even if the bulb still lights up.

<FunFact>
A UVB bulb can look completely normal, still glowing, still lighting up the tank, for months after it's stopped producing usable UV output. The visible light and the UV output degrade on two different timelines, which is exactly why "replace every 6 to 12 months" is a calendar rule, not a "replace when it looks dim" rule.
</FunFact>

## The Cost People Don't Budget For

A single [impaction surgery](/blog/bearded-dragon-health-issues-guide/) runs $800 to $2,500. Metabolic bone disease treatment runs $200 to $500 or more. These are exactly the conditions that correct husbandry prevents, which is why spending properly on the thermostat and UVB upfront is cheaper in the long run than spending less now and gambling on a vet bill later.

If you're budgeting for a bearded dragon, plan for %%setup:bearded-dragon%% to get set up right, then %%monthly:bearded-dragon%% a month after that, plus an emergency fund of a few hundred dollars set aside before you need it.

## Spend on the Enclosure and UVB First

The dragon is the cheapest line in this budget, so plan around the equipment instead. Buy the adult enclosure, the UVB fixture and the thermostat at full spec from day one, and the monthly bills stay at feeders, greens and power. Cutting those three to save money is how the vet bills above get started.
