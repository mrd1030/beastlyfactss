# How Much Does a Pet Flying Squirrel Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Flying squirrels are moderately priced to buy, but the first real decision with this species isn't financial, it's legal, and it's a different kind of legal question than most exotic pets on this site raise. For the full care picture beyond budgeting, see our complete [Flying Squirrel care guide](/guides/flying-squirrel/), or our companion guides on [feeding](/blog/flying-squirrel-feeding-guide/), [handling](/blog/flying-squirrel-handling-guide/), [health issues](/blog/flying-squirrel-health-issues-guide/), and [cage setup](/blog/flying-squirrel-tank-setup-guide/).

## Check Your State's Wildlife Laws First

Flying squirrels are native wildlife across most of the eastern United States, which puts them in a different legal category than an imported exotic like the [sugar glider](/guides/sugar-glider/). Some states regulate native species like this one under wildlife and game law rather than standard exotic-pet rules, and the specifics genuinely vary by state, in a way that isn't settled or consistent enough to summarize here. Legal status varies by state since this is native wildlife across much of its range, so check your state wildlife agency before you commit to buying one, not after.

This is a real difference from how this site's other exotic-mammal cost guides open. Our [sugar glider cost guide](/blog/sugar-glider-cost-guide/) and [hedgehog cost guide](/blog/hedgehog-cost-guide/) both point to specific state-by-state bans because both species are non-native, so their legal status is settled exotic-pet law. A flying squirrel's legal status is genuinely more variable and tied up in wildlife regulation instead, which is exactly why it needs a direct check with your own state rather than a general rule of thumb.

## The Squirrel: Roughly $200 to $450, and Realistically Two

Current listings from breeders and exotic-pet sellers run from around $200 for a single baby up to $450 or more depending on age, breeder reputation, and demand. Buying young, ideally around 6 to 8 weeks old, matters more here than with most small pets, since a flying squirrel's window for bonding closely with a keeper narrows the older it gets.

<FunFact>
Flying squirrels and sugar gliders glide the exact same way, a furred membrane stretched from wrist to ankle, and evolved that trick completely independently. One is a true rodent, the other a marsupial, and their common ancestor didn't have anything like a gliding membrane. Researchers who mapped the genetics behind marsupial gliders found that gliding membranes evolved convergently across several unrelated mammal groups, including rodents, bats, and colugos, each landing on a similar structure through a different genetic path. It's one of the more striking cases of convergent evolution in mammals, two animals that look and move almost identically without being close relatives at all.
</FunFact>

Here's the detail that changes your total cost immediately: flying squirrels are intensely social, denning together in groups through the winter in the wild, and a solitary pet is a genuinely stressed one. Budget for a same-sex pair or small group from the start, effectively doubling the purchase price, the same math that applies to sugar gliders.

## Vet Costs

A routine exotic wellness exam runs roughly $75 to $150 at a small-mammal-experienced clinic, in line with published pricing at specialty exotic practices. One thing that differs from sugar gliders here: neutering isn't the routine recommendation for flying squirrels that it is for gliders. Fewer exotic vets have experience with flying squirrel surgery specifically, so most keepers manage reproduction and behavior through same-sex housing instead. Budget extra for the unexpected, a diet-related emergency like metabolic bone disease needs immediate attention, not a wait-and-see approach, see our [health issues guide](/blog/flying-squirrel-health-issues-guide/) for the specifics.

## Lifespan

3 to 6 years is typical in the wild, university and wildlife sources vary slightly within that range, but captive individuals with consistent care average closer to 10 years, and well-documented pets have lived past 19. That's a meaningfully longer commitment than most people expect from an animal this small, and combined with the pair requirement and the state-by-state legal question, it's worth treating as a considered decision rather than an impulse buy.
