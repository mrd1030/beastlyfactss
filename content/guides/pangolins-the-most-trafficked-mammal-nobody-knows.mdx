Ask someone to name an endangered animal and you will get tiger, rhino, elephant. Ask them to name the most heavily trafficked wild mammal on the planet and most people go quiet.

The answer is an animal that looks like a walking pinecone, eats nothing but ants and termites, and has no teeth at all.

It is the pangolin. Before the grim part, it is worth understanding how genuinely strange this animal is, because the biology is the reason it is worth saving.

## The Only Mammal Wearing Keratin Armor

Pangolins are the only mammals whose bodies are entirely covered in scales. Those scales are keratin, the same protein in your fingernails and hair.

The name gives away the defense. "Pangolin" comes from the Malay word penggulung, meaning roller. When something threatens it, the animal tucks its head under its tail and curls into a tight ball, hiding the one part of it with no armor, the soft scaleless underside. The University of Michigan's Animal Diversity Web notes that a balled-up pangolin will sometimes roll down a steep slope to get away.

There is a genetic hint that the armor really is the point. When researchers including the Smithsonian Conservation Biology Institute sequenced pangolin genomes for a 2016 paper in *Genome Research*, they found that a gene tied to the skin's ability to fight disease does not function in pangolins. The interpretation offered was that pangolins leaned on physical armor instead.

<FunFact>
The same genome work turned up pseudogenes, broken copies of genes that once worked, which help explain why pangolins have no teeth. The instructions are still written in the genome. They simply stopped being read.
</FunFact>

## A Tongue That Starts Near the Pelvis

This is the part that is hard to picture. A pangolin's tongue is not rooted in its mouth.

It arises deep inside the animal's chest, anchored near the last pair of ribs and the pelvis, then runs forward through a sheath before emerging past the jaws. Animal Diversity Web describes the tongue and its associated muscles as longer than the animal's head and body combined.

Sources differ on how much of that length actually protrudes, so treat any single figure with caution. IFAW puts the working tongue at around 40 centimetres (roughly 16 inches), which is still a startling amount of tongue for an animal that can be smaller than a house cat.

Coat it in saliva sticky enough to hold struggling ants, and you have a tool built for one job: pushing deep into a termite mound and coming back out loaded.

## No Teeth, So the Stomach Does the Chewing

Pangolins have no teeth. Not reduced teeth, not a couple of molars. None.

That leaves a problem, because something still has to break up thousands of hard-bodied insects. The answer sits further down the line. The pyloric end of a pangolin's stomach is thickened and muscular, with keratinous spines projecting into its interior, and it usually contains small stones the animal has swallowed.

The whole arrangement works like a bird's gizzard. Insects go in whole, the muscular wall contracts, and the spines and grit grind the meal apart. The pangolin has effectively outsourced chewing to its digestive tract.

<FunFact>
One adult pangolin can eat more than 70 million insects in a year. Their digging also turns over organic matter and aerates soil, and the burrows they abandon become shelter for other animals. Losing pangolins means losing a lot of free pest control.
</FunFact>

## Eight Species, Every One of Them Threatened

There are eight pangolin species, four in Asia and four in Africa. According to the IUCN SSC Pangolin Specialist Group, not one is doing well.

That is an entire order of mammals, Pholidota, sitting somewhere on the threatened list. Worth noting: several popular pages still describe all eight species as critically endangered. That is not what the current assessments say, and the real picture above is bad enough without inflating it.

## The Trafficking Numbers, and Why They Move Around

Here is where you have to be careful, because the figures shift depending on what is being counted and across which years.

The IUCN SSC Pangolin Specialist Group states that the equivalent of more than 895,000 pangolins were trafficked globally between 2000 and 2019, and that at least 400,000 are hunted and eaten locally in Central Africa each year.

An IUCN release in August 2025 took a different slice. Looking at seizures between 2016 and 2024, it found confiscations involving more than an estimated half a million pangolins across 75 countries and 178 trade routes. Scales made up 99 percent of the confiscated parts.

A 2018 study in *Conservation Letters* led by Daniel Ingram went at it from the hunting end, estimating that 0.4 to 2.7 million pangolins are hunted annually in Central African forests, with the number taken rising by roughly 150 percent over four decades.

Those are not four competing versions of one number. They measure different things: documented international trafficking, customs seizures, and local hunting pressure. Seizure data in particular only ever counts what got caught, which makes it a floor rather than a total.

What all of them agree on is direction.

## What Has Actually Changed

All eight species were moved from CITES Appendix II to Appendix I at the 2016 conference of the parties, which established an international trade ban for wild-caught pangolins traded commercially.

In June 2025, the US Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing seven pangolin species as endangered under the Endangered Species Act, describing pangolins in its announcement as "the world's most heavily trafficked mammal." That action is still recorded as a proposed rule on the agency's own page, not a final one.

In March 2025, China released a new edition of its pharmacopoeia, effective from 1 October 2025, which dropped raw pangolin scales along with 13 traditional formulas containing pangolin. Conservationists called it a real step and then immediately named the limits: a pharmacopoeia is a medical standards document rather than a production ban, companies holding existing certificates can still manufacture delisted formulas, and an annual quota of one metric ton of scales for the domestic market remains in place.

## The Part Worth Remembering

The demand driving all of this rests on a claim that does not survive contact with basic chemistry. Pangolin scales are keratin. So are your fingernails.

An animal that grinds its dinner with swallowed stones, hunts with a tongue rooted near its hips, and eats 70 million insects a year is being pulled out of the wild at industrial scale for a substance you could obtain by chewing your own thumbnail.

The pattern should be familiar. As with [sharks](/blog/how-dangerous-are-sharks-really/), the real story is not the animal being dangerous. And for a mammal every bit as biologically odd as [the platypus](/blog/the-platypus-natures-most-wonderfully-bizarre-mammal/), the pangolin's obscurity has turned out to be the most dangerous thing about it.
