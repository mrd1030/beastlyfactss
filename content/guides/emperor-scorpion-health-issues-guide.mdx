# Common Emperor Scorpion Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Exotic vets experienced with invertebrates are uncommon, so this list leans heavily on prevention rather than treatment. Emperor scorpions are genuinely hardy when humidity and substrate depth are right, and most problems trace directly back to one of those two things being off.

## Dehydration

The single most frequently cited issue in emperor scorpion care sheets, and it tracks directly back to where this species evolved. Animal Diversity Web (University of Michigan) describes emperor scorpions as native to "hot and humid forests," not the dry habitat most people picture when they hear "scorpion." Watch for lethargy, a shriveled appearance, sunken features, and reduced activity. Humidity that's too low, inadequate misting, or overly dry substrate are the usual causes. Caught early, this is manageable by increasing humidity and providing a shallow water source, but advanced cases can be fatal. ## Failed or Incomplete Molts

Scorpions are at their most vulnerable during and immediately after molting, and this is genuinely life-threatening if it goes wrong. A 2024 study in the peer-reviewed journal *Insects* (Guo et al.), on a different scorpion species, *Mesobuthus martensii*, but describing biology that applies broadly across scorpions, found that "the new exoskeleton is too soft to provide protection against predators and conspecifics" immediately after a molt. Watch for difficulty shedding the old exoskeleton, stuck pieces of shed, and lethargy around molt time. Incorrect humidity, whether too low or fluctuating, poor nutrition, and disturbance during the vulnerable pre- and post-molt window all raise risk. Remove live prey and avoid handling entirely during this period, an insect left in the enclosure can injure a molting scorpion that's unable to defend itself normally. The University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine documents this same principle in tarantulas, a related pet arachnid: leaving several live prey insects in an enclosure at once "may lead to injuries from the hungry prey insects biting" an animal that can't fight back normally, which is exactly the risk during a scorpion's molt. Incomplete molts often need experienced intervention and are frequently fatal without it.

<FunFact>
A molting scorpion is effectively defenseless. Research on scorpion molting describes the freshly molted exoskeleton as too soft to protect against predators or even other scorpions, which is exactly why keepers pull live feeder insects from the enclosure before a molt, a cricket or roach left behind can seriously injure or kill a scorpion that's temporarily unable to fight back.
</FunFact>

## Mites and Other Parasites

Look for visible mites on the body or in the substrate, excessive grooming behavior, and lethargy. These typically arrive via substrate, feeder insects, or contaminated decor. ## Injuries From Falls, Pinching, or Cage-Mate Aggression

Visible wounds, limping, or missing appendages point here. Improper handling, a fall from height, or housing incompatible individuals together are the common causes. Severity varies widely, minor injuries often heal on their own, while serious ones can lead to infection or death. The University of Kentucky's Department of Entomology calls this species' tolerance for cage mates "very unique," since "most predatory arthropods will kill each other when housed together," which is exactly why crowding or too few hides is the fastest way to turn that tolerance into a real injury. ## Fungal or Bacterial Issues

Less common, but linked to substrate that's chronically wet rather than properly damp, or poor ventilation that lets mold take hold.

## Emergencies vs. Manageable at Home

A stuck molt, severe dehydration, open wounds showing signs of infection, or a complete refusal to eat for an extended period after a molt are all high-concern situations. Mild dehydration, minor mites, and early humidity-related lethargy are generally manageable once husbandry is corrected. Notably, the sting itself is rarely what sends an emperor scorpion to the vet, the Merck Veterinary Manual's toxicology guidance finds severe toxicity unlikely from most of the scorpion species it covers, with stings producing localized pain rather than a medical emergency, and it's husbandry failures, not stings, that drive the list above. Veterinary options for invertebrates remain genuinely limited, which is exactly why prevention through correct humidity and substrate depth is the primary strategy here, not a backup plan.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Low or fluctuating humidity leads to dehydration and failed molts, the two most serious issues on this list. Substrate that's too shallow prevents proper burrowing, adding stress and humidity instability on top of that. Leaving live prey in the enclosure during a molt risks real injury or death to a scorpion that can't defend itself normally. And substrate that's too wet, rather than properly damp, invites mold and secondary infection.

[Tarantulas](/blog/tarantula-health-issues-guide/) sit in the same position, with dehydration and molting complications at the top of their list too, for the same underlying reasons. Obesity from overfeeding is a preventable risk of its own.
