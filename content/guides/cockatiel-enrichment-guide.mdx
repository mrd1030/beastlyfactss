# Cockatiel Enrichment: Two Studies, Five Years Apart

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Parrot enrichment research has mostly gone to large and endangered species, which left one of the most-kept birds in the world with almost nothing. That changed recently. For everything else about the species, see our complete [Cockatiel care guide](/guides/cockatiel/).

<KeyTakeaway>
A 2025 study concludes that environmental enrichment improves behavior and welfare in captive cockatiels, particularly by reducing feather-damaging behaviors. A 2020 paper looked at enrichment and cockatiel behavior in aviaries. Two studies is not many, and it is two more than this species had.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Actually Says

The 2020 paper examined the effects of environmental enrichment on the behavior of cockatiels in aviaries. The 2025 paper concludes that **environmental enrichment improves behavior and welfare in captive cockatiels**, with reduced **feather-damaging behavior** singled out.

The broader psittacine picture supports both. Foraging enrichment has documented welfare benefits in parrots: birds use it for significant parts of the day, it reduces oral repetitive behaviors, and it reduces sleeping. In conventional captive environments food is offered in a regular bowl, producing foraging times of **less than an hour**, which leaves most of the day unoccupied.

<FunFact>
The gap in the literature is itself worth mentioning in a care guide. Most enrichment studies concentrate on larger or more endangered psittacines, which is why a bird kept in millions of homes was studied later than parrots almost nobody owns.
</FunFact>

## Move the Food Out of the Bowl

Start easy. A bird that has only ever been bowl-fed does not know food is inside a device, so the first stage is often food sitting visibly on top of a toy rather than hidden inside it. ## Company and Time

Cockatiels are flock birds. A single bird with excellent toys and no company is still a single bird, and the daily time commitment for a solo cockatiel is substantial. This is a case where the enrichment is partly you.

## Priority Order

1. Food delivered through foraging rather than a bowl
2. Company, whether a compatible bird or a real daily routine with you
3. Varied perch diameters and materials
4. Shreddable, destructible material, rotated
5. Bathing opportunity
6. Out-of-cage time in a safe space
7. Novel objects, last

## What Not to Do

Do not assume plucking is behavioral before an avian vet has ruled out medical causes, which our [health issues guide](/blog/cockatiel-health-issues-guide/) covers. Do not make the first foraging toy hard, since a bird that cannot solve it eats less rather than working harder. Do not leave the same toys up indefinitely and count that as enrichment. Do not use sandpaper perch covers, which damage feet. And do not treat cage size as a substitute for occupation, because a large cage with a food bowl in it still produces a bird with nothing to do.
