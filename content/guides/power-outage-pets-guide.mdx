# Power Outage With Pets: Which Animal Needs You First

<VetDisclaimer>
This article is for educational purposes only and is not veterinary advice. An animal showing signs of cold, heat or respiratory distress during an outage needs a vet or a 24-hour exotics clinic, and the numbers for those belong on paper, not only in a phone that will not charge.
</VetDisclaimer>

**A goldfish in a stopped tank is in trouble before a bearded dragon in a cooling one, and a cockatiel in a cold room is in no trouble at all until somebody lights a camp stove next to it.** The power going out does not threaten every animal in the house the same way or on the same clock. Fish lose oxygen in hours. Reptiles lose heat over hours to a day. Rabbits and guinea pigs cope with the cold and are endangered by a summer outage instead. Birds, dogs and cats are the easy ones, right up until the moment someone tries to fix the cold with fire.

The job is to work the house in the right order, and to resist the instinct that kills more pets in an outage than the weather does.

<KeyTakeaway>
The order of operations: fish first, because oxygen runs out in hours and a battery air pump buys a day. Reptiles second, because heat loss is slower but a cold reptile cannot be fed and past a day or two below its floor it needs to be somewhere with power. Small mammals third, and only in summer, because losing cooling is worse for them than losing heat. Birds, dogs and cats last, because they are fine in a cool room with a blanket. Over all of it, one rule: nothing burns indoors. No generator in the house or garage, no gas oven for heat, no charcoal, no camp stove, no kerosene heater, no candles near a cage. Carbon monoxide has no smell and no color, and a bird breathing it has no buffer.
</KeyTakeaway>

The last row is not a joke. Most pet arthropods ride out an outage without noticing, and [the invertebrate plan](/blog/invertebrate-emergency-travel-shipping-guide/) says so plainly. The animals that need you are the ones living in water and the ones living under a heat lamp.

## Fish: The Clock Is Oxygen

An aquarium has three things keeping its residents alive, and the power runs all of them: the pump that moves water and gets oxygen into it, the filter where bacteria turn ammonia into something less toxic, and the heater. They fail in that order of urgency.

### Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L is where most fish are comfortable. Below 5 mg/L they are stressed, and at 4 mg/L or lower they begin to die, large fish first, depending on species and how long it lasts. A stopped tank falls through those numbers at a rate set by how many fish are in it, how warm it is, since warm water holds less oxygen than cool, and how much surface the water has to exchange gas across. The sign to watch for is piping: fish schooling at the surface, gulping air. Fish kills from low oxygen are usually found early in the morning, after a night with no light and no photosynthesis to add any.

The plan for the first hour or two is to do nothing. Stop feeding, switch off any automatic feeder, and do not change water. From about eight hours in, run a battery-powered air pump through an air stone or sponge filter for an hour, and repeat every eight hours. A heavily stocked tank, or one where fish are already at the surface, gets air every four hours instead. A USB air pump runs off a laptop or a phone power bank. A purpose-built battery backup pump runs about 20 hours continuously, or 40 hours or more on a mode that pulses 15 seconds on and 15 off, and turning the airflow down stretches it further. That pump lives in the outage kit, because it is the one piece of equipment in this article that cannot be improvised.

An axolotl needs the same attention to oxygen and none to heat. Cold water suits it, and [warm water is what it cannot tolerate](/blog/why-axolotls-need-cold-clean-water/), so a winter outage leaves its tank exactly where it wants to be while still stopping the pump that keeps the water breathable.

### The filter

The bacteria in a filter need oxygenated water flowing over them. A hang-on-back or canister filter with the flow stopped is a sealed box of waste and dying bacteria, and the longer it sits the worse what is inside it gets. Two options, either of which beats leaving it alone. Pull the media out and drop it into the tank itself, where the water holds more oxygen than a stagnant filter body. Or unplug the filter at the wall, so that when the power returns it does not restart on its own and flush a canister full of rot into the water. A sponge filter driven by the battery air pump keeps its bacteria alive on its own.

When the filter does fail, ammonia rises. Un-ionized ammonia above 0.05 mg/L already damages gills, and 2 mg/L is lethal for many fish. Nitrite above 0.1 mg/L is toxic. Those are the numbers the test kit is for after the outage, and the reason feeding stops at the start of it: every flake that goes in is ammonia that nothing is processing.

### Temperature

Water holds heat far better than air, and a big tank with a tight lid loses it slowly. Wrap the tank in blankets, towels or a foil emergency blanket, several layers, with the lid on. Chemical heat packs taped by their two ends to the outside glass, one per 20 to 30 gallons and replaced as they fade, add warmth without touching the water. For a tank that has dropped a long way, a warm water change works: take some tank water out into a bucket, stir in a little boiling water, check it with a thermometer, and return it. Boiling water never goes into the tank directly. The target is a slow climb, because a sudden swing of even a few degrees weakens a fish's immune system and lets infections take hold, and the general rule for any temperature change is no more than 1°F an hour.

A summer outage flips the problem. Move the tank out of sunlight, take the blankets off, and run a battery fan across the surface for evaporative cooling. Floating frozen bottles is not the answer here, because they produce exactly the swing the fish cannot handle.

### When power returns

Do not feed for 24 hours. Test ammonia and nitrite, and if either reads above zero, change water, at least half the tank if ammonia is up. Clean the hang-on-back or canister filter before plugging it in, to get the dead bacteria and waste out, but rinse the media in tank water and never scrub it clean, because what survives on it is the colony that has to rebuild. The water may cloud for a day or two while it does. A koi pond is the same problem at scale: the air pump and the surface matter, the heater does not, and the fish are not fed until the filter is running again.

## Reptiles and Amphibians: Heat, Then Patience

A reptile enclosure loses heat the moment the lamp goes dark, and the full plan, with the temperature floor for each species in one table, is [the reptile emergency plan](/blog/reptile-emergency-plan-guide/). The short version runs like this. A battery or wind-up thermometer goes in the enclosure so the number is real. A blanket or thick towels go over the enclosure with a vent gap, and the enclosure, or the animal in a ventilated tub, moves to the warmest interior room, usually one with no exterior walls. A wrapped hot water bottle or a hand warmer goes beside the animal, never touching it, because a cold, sluggish reptile cannot move away from a burn. Feeding stops. Water stays available, and a plant-eater can have a little greens. Past a day or two below the floor for the species, the animal goes to a vet, a friend with power or a reptile shop.

Two things not to do: no candles, gas stoves or open flame anywhere near an enclosure, and no warming a cold, unresponsive animal in hot water. Lukewarm, and only once it is alert. A snake or a tortoise that goes slow and quiet in a cool house is not brumating on its own terms, since [brumation](/blog/snake-brumation-guide/) is a managed, gradual cool-down with an empty gut, and an outage is neither.

## Small Mammals: Summer Is the Emergency

The rabbits, guinea pigs, chinchillas and hamsters are the group most people worry about in a winter outage and the group it threatens least. Their danger line runs the other way: 75°F for a guinea pig and about 80°F for the rest is the ceiling, and a cool room is a far smaller problem than a hot one. [The small mammal temperature guide](/blog/small-mammal-temperature-heat-stress-guide/) carries the full outage plan and the heat stress first aid. The parts that matter in the first hour:

Keep feeding them. A rabbit or guinea pig is a hindgut fermenter whose gut has to keep moving, and an animal that stops eating is in more immediate trouble than one that is a few degrees cold. Stress starts [GI stasis](/blog/rabbit-gi-stasis-guide/), and an outage is stress. In a summer outage, frozen water bottles wrapped in a towel to lie against, wet towels, shade, water in more than one container, the coolest room in the house, and a battery fan. In a winter outage, no drafts, the enclosure off the floor, deep hay to burrow in, and a check that the water bottle nozzle has not frozen. The one hard floor in the group belongs to the hamster: below 41°F a Syrian hamster can slip into torpor and starve in it. And the decision to relocate, to a friend's basement or a vet with power, is made at hour two of a summer outage, because a house without air conditioning is still climbing all afternoon.

## Birds: The Danger Is What You Burn

A healthy bird in a cool room is not the emergency. Cover most of the cage with a towel or blanket, leaving a gap for air, move it to the warmest interior room, keep the food topped up because a bird burns through it fast, and for a small or visibly cold bird, short spells under a loose shirt against your own body add heat with no equipment at all. A wrapped hot water bottle near the cage, never against it, helps in a cold house. [The bird emergency plan](/blog/bird-emergency-travel-guide/) has the rest, including the sitter sheet and the carrier.

What endangers a bird is the fix people reach for. A bird's respiratory system pulls more of whatever is in the air through its body per breath than a person's does, and it has no buffer against carbon monoxide. Which brings the whole house to the one rule that matters more than any thermometer.

## The House: Nothing Burns Indoors

The CDC's carbon monoxide rules for an outage are short and absolute. Never use a generator inside a home or garage, even with the doors and windows open. Outside, it runs more than 20 feet from any window, door or vent. Never heat a house with a gas oven. Never burn charcoal indoors. Never use a portable gas camp stove indoors. Do not use portable flameless chemical heaters indoors. Nothing burns on a stove or in a fireplace that is not vented. Battery-operated carbon monoxide detectors, or ones with battery backup, go near every sleeping area, and they are the only warning anyone in the house will get, because the gas has no smell and no color. The symptoms in a person are headache, dizziness, weakness, an upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, often mistaken for flu, and an animal cannot report any of them.

Candles are the other instinct. A battery lantern does the same job with no flame near a cage, a curtain or a blanket-wrapped tank, and no combustion in a room an animal cannot leave.

## Dogs and Cats: The Easy Ones

A dog or cat in a house that has lost its heat is in the same situation as the people in it, and the ASPCA's winter rule covers both: if it is too cold for you, it is probably too cold for your pet. They share the room you are in, with a blanket and a bed off the floor, and a short-haired dog gets a sweater. They never wait in a car, which holds cold the way it holds heat and can freeze an animal to death. They eat a little more in the cold, because staying warm burns energy, and they need water that has not frozen. In a summer outage the problem is heat, and the answers are shade, water, a cool floor and the coolest room, with the car ruled out for the same reason. A dog that goes out on snow or ice goes on a leash, because a dog can lose its own scent trail in it and more dogs are lost in winter than in any other season.

## When to Leave

Managing in place works while the house is still inside the safe band for every animal in it. When it is not, and the outage has no end in sight, the plan is a friend with power, a relative's basement, an exotics vet or a boarder, and the call is made early. Ready.gov's rule for a disaster is that if officials tell you to evacuate, the pets evacuate too, and the catch is that many public shelters and hotels do not take them, so the place that will is worked out before the day. A buddy system with a neighbor who can get the animals out if you are not home covers the hour you are at work.

The kit, built before the lights go out, is two kits: a bigger one for staying put and a lighter one for leaving. In both: several days of food in a sealed container and water to match, the animal's medication, copies of its records on paper and on a phone, a carrier or tub for each animal, litter and bags for the ones that need them, a photo of you with the animal to prove it is yours, a collar and tag with a current number and the microchip registry checked, an out-of-area contact, and something familiar to reduce the stress of the move. For this house, add the outage-specific pieces: the battery air pump and spare batteries, hand warmers or a hot water bottle, two thick towels, a battery thermometer, a battery lantern, a carbon monoxide detector that does not need the mains, and the vet, the 24-hour exotics clinic and the poison lines written on the inside of the kit lid.

The outage that goes wrong is rarely the one where the house got cold. It is the one where the fish tank was left to its own devices until morning, or the one where a generator ran in the garage to keep the reptile lamp on, and the bird in the next room stopped singing first.
