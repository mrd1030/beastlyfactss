# Mouse Enclosure Setup: Why a Wire Cage Beats a Glass Tank Here

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Mouse housing runs on the opposite logic from a lot of small-mammal advice: the glass tank that's the right call for a gerbil is specifically the wrong one for a mouse.

## Enclosure Size

The minimum is concrete: 18 inches long by 18 inches wide by 10 inches high for a small group of 2 to 3 mice. There's no single evidence-based ideal size beyond that floor, published housing recommendations are mostly drawn from laboratory settings rather than companion mice specifically, so more floor space and genuine vertical climbing room, using shelves, ramps, and multiple levels, is always the safer default rather than treating the minimum as a target.

## Why a Wire Cage, Not a Glass Tank

This is the detail most worth getting right, and it runs against the instinct a lot of small-pet owners bring from other species. An aquarium is not a suitable cage for rats or mice: air circulation is inadequate, so ammonia builds up. A solid-sided enclosure, even one topped with a mesh lid, simply can't move air the way an all-wire cage can, and ammonia from urine breaking down is a genuine respiratory hazard.

<FunFact>
This is one of the few places where the advice flips between two closely housed rodents. A [gerbil enclosure](/blog/gerbil-tank-setup-guide/) is a glass tank because gerbils need deep substrate for real tunnel-digging that a wire cage can't hold. Mice need the opposite: glass aquariums are unsuitable for them because of inadequate air circulation and ammonia buildup, so a well-ventilated wire cage, not a tank, is the right call here.
</FunFact>

Look for a cage with a solid floor (wire mesh flooring risks injury to small feet) and wire sides and top for ventilation. Keep the top mesh no larger than about 1cm square, strong enough to resist an adult mouse's chewing.

## Bar Spacing: The Single Most Important Number

Floor space matters, but bar spacing is what actually determines whether a mouse stays inside the cage. The maximum safe spacing is around a quarter inch (6mm), with 5mm sometimes recommended. That's tighter than the roughly quarter-inch standard already considered strict for a dwarf hamster, worth knowing since plenty of cages marketed broadly for "small animals," including ones with spacing around three-eighths of an inch, look tight but are still wide enough for a determined mouse to squeeze through.

## Bedding: Shallower Than You'd Guess

Provide dust-free, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4voH9Ap" product="Small-pet paper bedding">paper-based bedding</AffiliateLink>, at least about 2 to 4cm deep, enough to support nesting and light digging. That's considerably shallower than the many-inches-deep bedding a hamster or gerbil needs, mice aren't the deep-tunneling species a gerbil is, though they are enthusiastic nest builders, and [how much nesting material to give](/blog/mouse-enrichment-guide/) has an actual tested number behind it. Avoid cedar and aromatic pine entirely (the oils irritate the respiratory system) and skip fluffy, cotton-wool-style nesting products, which pose a real entanglement and blockage risk.

## Temperature and Humidity

64 to 79°F with 30 to 70% relative humidity is the comfortable range, and mice have limited ability to regulate their own body temperature, so staying inside that range matters more than it might for a hardier pet. ## The Wheel and Enrichment

A solid-surfaced, axle-free exercise wheel sized for a mouse, roughly 6 to 8 inches rather than the 10 to 12 inch wheels sold for hamsters, gives a mouse a safe outlet for its energy. A wheel shouldn't be the only form of enrichment, though. Rotating items occasionally keeps things interesting, mice investigate novelty quickly.

## Nest Material and Hides: Two Different Jobs

One hideout is not enough, because a mouse needs a nest and cover for different reasons. The nest is built from loose nesting material and is how a mouse holds its body temperature, something mice are driven to do even in a warm room. A hide is cover: as a prey species, mice prefer to stay close to something they can duck under.

In a group, the hides also do a social job. Provide several shelters, each with more than one exit and big enough for a mouse to turn around in, so a mouse under pressure from a cage-mate always has a way out. Cardboard tubes count, and they double as something to climb and chew. Offer a mix of nesting materials so the mice can choose what builds best, and watch the group for the first few days after adding any new shelter, since a prized hide can set off aggression between cage-mates.
