# Mouse Enclosure Setup: Why a Wire Cage Beats a Glass Tank Here

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Mouse housing runs on the opposite logic from a lot of the small-mammal advice elsewhere on this site: the glass tank that's the right call for a gerbil is specifically the wrong one for a mouse.

For everything else about day-to-day care, see our complete [Mouse care guide](/guides/mouse/), with companion guides on [cost](/blog/mouse-cost-guide/), [handling](/blog/mouse-handling-guide/), and [health issues](/blog/mouse-health-issues-guide/).

## Enclosure Size

The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/all-other-pets/mice/providing-a-home-for-mice) sets a concrete minimum: 18 inches long by 18 inches wide by 10 inches high for a small group of 2 to 3 mice. There's no single evidence-based ideal size beyond that floor, published housing recommendations are mostly drawn from laboratory settings rather than companion mice specifically, so more floor space and genuine vertical climbing room, using shelves, ramps, and multiple levels, is always the safer default rather than treating the minimum as a target.

## Why a Wire Cage, Not a Glass Tank

This is the detail most worth getting right, and it runs against the instinct a lot of small-pet owners bring from other species. Merck is direct about it: "Aquariums are not suitable cages for rats and mice, because air circulation is inadequate and therefore ammonia builds up." A solid-sided enclosure, even one topped with a mesh lid, simply can't move air the way an all-wire cage can, and ammonia from urine breaking down is a genuine respiratory hazard covered in more detail in our [health issues guide](/blog/mouse-health-issues-guide/).

<FunFact>
This is one of the few places on this site where the advice flips between two closely housed rodents. Our [gerbil enclosure guide](/blog/gerbil-tank-setup-guide/) recommends a glass tank specifically because gerbils need deep substrate for real tunnel-digging that a wire cage can't hold. Mice need the opposite: the Merck Veterinary Manual is explicit that glass aquariums are unsuitable for them because of inadequate air circulation and ammonia buildup, so a well-ventilated wire cage, not a tank, is the right call here.
</FunFact>

Look for a cage with a solid floor (wire mesh flooring risks injury to small feet) and wire sides and top for ventilation. Merck recommends a top mesh no larger than about 1cm square, strong enough to resist an adult mouse's chewing.

## Bar Spacing: The Single Most Important Number

Floor space matters, but bar spacing is what actually determines whether a mouse stays inside the cage. Multiple welfare-focused sources put the maximum safe spacing at around a quarter inch (6mm), with some pushing that down to 5mm. That's tighter than the roughly quarter-inch standard already considered strict for a dwarf hamster, worth knowing since plenty of cages marketed broadly for "small animals," including ones with spacing around three-eighths of an inch, look tight but are still wide enough for a determined mouse to squeeze through. Our [handling guide](/blog/mouse-handling-guide/) covers exactly how capable mice are of exploiting a gap that size.

## Bedding: Shallower Than You'd Guess

Provide dust-free, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4voH9Ap" product="Small-pet paper bedding">paper-based bedding</AffiliateLink>, at least about 2 to 4cm deep according to [RSPCA](https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/rodents/mice/environment) guidance, enough to support nesting and light digging. That's considerably shallower than the many-inches-deep bedding a hamster or gerbil needs, mice aren't the deep-tunneling species a gerbil is, though they are enthusiastic nest builders and appreciate hay or shredded paper layered on top to arrange themselves. Avoid cedar and aromatic pine entirely (the oils irritate the respiratory system) and skip fluffy, cotton-wool-style nesting products, which pose a real entanglement and blockage risk.

## Temperature and Humidity

64 to 79°F with 30 to 70% relative humidity is the comfortable range, per [Merck](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/rodents/mice-and-rats-as-pets), and mice have limited ability to regulate their own body temperature, so staying inside that range matters more than it might for a hardier pet. ## The Wheel and Enrichment

A solid-surfaced, axle-free exercise wheel sized for a mouse, roughly 6 to 8 inches rather than the 10 to 12 inch wheels sold for hamsters, gives a mouse a safe outlet for its energy. The RSPCA is clear that a wheel "shouldn't be the only form of enrichment" though.
