# Discus Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and the Beef-Heart Debate

Discus have unusually small mouths for such a broad-bodied fish, and their fry start life feeding on a parent's own skin, two details that shape almost everything about how this species eats.

## Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

- **Adults:** 2 to 3 times a day, small amounts cleared within a few minutes. Discus have a short gut relative to their body size, and smaller, more frequent meals suit that better than one large feeding.
- **Fry:** Don't eat in the conventional sense for the first stretch of life. Once free-swimming, fry feed directly on mucus secreted from both parents' skin, most intensively for roughly the first two weeks, with parents gradually weaning them off it over the following two weeks as they start swimming away more often and the fry begin investigating other food sources.
- **Juveniles (post-weaning):** Transition onto finely crushed flake, micro-pellets, or newly hatched baby brine shrimp as they move off parental mucus, with feeding frequency staying higher than for adults to support faster growth.

<FunFact>
Discus fry don't just hide near their parents after hatching, they feed directly off them. Both parents secrete a nutrient-rich mucus over their skin that fry bite at for up to several minutes at a time, and a 2010 study published in the Journal of Experimental Biology found that this mucus carries immune antibodies and protein at levels that rise and fall across the feeding period, with antibody provision peaking as the fry reach the free-swimming stage, a pattern the researchers compared to colostrum and milk in mammals. It's one of the only documented cases of sustained direct parental feeding in fish, and it's genuinely real, not aquarium-hobby folklore.
</FunFact>

## What Discus Eat

Discus are carnivores with a protein-leaning diet, and the small size of their mouth relative to their body is a detail that trips up a lot of new keepers. A quality discus-formulated pellet or granule, sized appropriately rather than a generic large-cichlid pellet, works well as a staple, supplemented regularly with frozen or live bloodworms and brine shrimp. Because discus have short guts, feeding small amounts two or three times a day works better than one large daily meal.

Beef-heart mix has a long history in discus keeping and remains genuinely popular, it's lean, roughly 60% protein by dry weight, easily digestible, and produces strong growth. The catch is what happens when it's overfed or fed alone: beef heart runs high in saturated fat and has a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio far below what's recommended for a fish's diet, so relying on it as a sole staple risks fatty deposits around organs and a genuine nutritional imbalance over time. A 2018 feeding study in the journal Aquaculture and Fisheries found that blending beef heart with shrimp meat produced growth rates statistically similar to feeding beef heart alone, which supports the common hobbyist practice of treating it as one part of a rotation rather than the whole diet. Trim visible fat and connective tissue before feeding it, and keep portions modest.

## Safe Foods and Ones to Approach With Caution

Safe and beneficial: quality discus pellets or granules, frozen or live bloodworms, frozen or live brine shrimp, and a well-trimmed beef-heart mix fed as part of a rotation rather than a daily staple.

Use caution with: beef heart fed as the sole diet, given the fat content and calcium-to-phosphorus concerns above, and any raw meat or worms from an uncontrolled source, since raw feeding always carries some bacterial risk. Food sized for a typical community fish or a large cichlid is a genuine, easy-to-miss mistake, discus mouths are noticeably small for how broad-bodied the fish is, and oversized food gets ignored rather than eaten.

## Why Discus Stop Eating

1. **New-tank or new-introduction stress.** Some hesitation in the first few days after a move is common and usually resolves on its own once the fish settles in.
2. **Poor or fluctuating water quality.** Appetite loss paired with hiding points toward degraded conditions, ammonia or nitrite swings especially, rather than illness on its own.

## When to Worry

A day or two of picky eating in an otherwise normal, settled fish isn't urgent, especially right after a move or a tank change. Refusal that stretches beyond that, especially paired with hiding, clamped fins, color loss, or stringy white feces, is worth acting on: test the water first, since a parameter swing is the most common underlying cause, then consider [quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) and a closer look at the fish itself if water quality checks out fine.
