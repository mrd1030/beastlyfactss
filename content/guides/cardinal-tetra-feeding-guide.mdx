# Cardinal Tetra Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and the Rule That Prevents an Outbreak

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A wild cardinal tetra spends its day picking tiny prey off sunken leaves, roots and wood in the blackwater streams of the Rio Negro. In a tank the same habit makes feeding simple: small food in small amounts. The rule that protects the school has nothing to do with portions at all.

## Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

Cardinals eat a larger share of their body weight while they grow, then settle onto a twice-daily routine as adults.

Twice a day is the same schedule as for the closely related [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-feeding-guide/). In the wild this species grazes through the day, and a tank can't replicate that pattern exactly, but it's a useful reminder that this is a fish built to eat small amounts often rather than one large meal. Some keepers split the day into three or four smaller feedings instead. Either approach works as long as portions stay small enough to disappear quickly.

Overfeeding lets uneaten food decay and spike ammonia and nitrite, which a school [shows before a test kit does](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/). Cardinal tetras are sensitive to nitrite and need ammonia at zero, so keeping portions tight matters here: in a laboratory tolerance test, half the fish died within 96 hours at a nitrite level of 1.1 mg/L.

The eggs hatch in 24 to 36 hours and the fry swim free 3 to 4 days later. Eggs and young fry are sensitive to light, so the rearing tank stays dark, and adults will eat the eggs given the chance, which is why fry are raised on their own.

## What Cardinals Eat, in the Wild and in the Tank

Stomach contents from 80 wild cardinals along the middle Rio Negro show a predator of very small animals. Microcrustaceans, mostly cladocerans with some copepods, turned up in about half the fish, and midge larvae in almost as many; one stomach held more than 200 cladocerans. Other insects, rotifers and invertebrate eggs turned up less often. The fish takes this prey off submerged leaf litter, roots and water plants, and the algae and detritus found in many stomachs come in with it, swallowed while the fish browses for prey.

Build the tank diet on a good-quality dried food, flake, granules or a small pellet such as a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004PBB5TK?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Semi-Floating Micro Pellets, 0.77 oz">micro-pellet food</AffiliateLink>, and supplement it with live and frozen chironomid larvae (bloodworm), brine shrimp, black mosquito larvae, cyclops, daphnia, and moina. Dried food can sustain a cardinal, but ideally it also gets small live foods on a regular basis.

<KeyTakeaway>
Cardinal tetras have small mouths, similar to neon tetra's. Crush flakes into fine pieces and stick to micro or nano-sized pellets. Food that's too large gets taken and spat back out repeatedly, which functionally starves the fish even while food is visibly going into the tank, and the rejected pieces rot uneaten.
</KeyTakeaway>

Rotating in the live and frozen foods above covers nutrition, and the crustaceans among them (brine shrimp, cyclops, daphnia and moina) also carry carotenoid pigments, the ingredient that [color-enhancing foods](/blog/fish-color-and-diet-guide/) rely on. No separate vitamin or mineral supplement is needed for a properly fed community tank.

Opened dried food loses its value with time: a container kept at room temperature is thrown out 2 months after opening, and frozen food after a year.

## Foods to Avoid

- **Too much protein-rich live or frozen food.** Live and frozen fare is the supplement, not the whole menu.
- **Low-quality live foods from questionable sources.** They can introduce parasites.
- **Vegetables or fruit in quantity.** They cause bloating in a fish this small.

<FunFact source="Merck Veterinary Manual">
Never leave a dead fish in the tank or let tankmates pick at a visibly sick fish's body. Neon tetra disease, recorded across the tetra family, spreads when fish eat infected tissue, and it has no cure. Removing a dead or dying fish the moment you see it is a feeding-time habit, and the only control that exists: a dead or sick fish left in the tank is how an outbreak of an incurable disease spreads to the whole school.
</FunFact>

## Why Cardinals Stop Eating

1. **Faster tankmates.** Small mouths that lose out to faster fish. Spread food across the surface, never into one spot.
2. **Water quality.** When fish seem to be getting sick, the first thing to check is the water, and in a cardinal tank that means ammonia and nitrite before anything else.
3. **Illness.** Wild-caught cardinals are not picky eaters by the time they reach a shop. In the Amazon export chain, fish held before shipping are fed a commercial feed for omnivorous fish twice a day. A wild cardinal that still refuses food once it has settled is more likely sick or stressed than fussy.

## When to Worry

Healthy fish eat aggressively when they are fed at regular times, and that is the baseline to watch. Color is a weaker signal: a cardinal turns lighter in bright light over a pale substrate in clear water, so a paler fish on its own is not a sick one. The signs that need action:

- **The school stops rushing the food.** Sick fish often stop feeding and turn lethargic, and a careful keeper sees that change before the first fish dies.
- **One fish eating less while the rest feed.** Move it to a hospital tank, where it can be watched on its own and the cause of the [wasting](/blog/cardinal-tetra-health-issues-guide/) pinned down before any medicine goes in.

## When You Go Away

A trip away needs one of two things: a person who feeds the fish, or an automatic feeder.

For a sitter, portion out each meal ahead of time, for example in a labeled pill organizer with one compartment per feeding, so there is nothing to overfeed with. Show them the routine once, and leave your number.

For an automatic feeder, set it up and test it for several days before you leave, with a food it dispenses well, and check that it is not jamming or overfeeding.

A fish whose wild meals are the specks clinging to a drowned leaf needs little more in a tank than small food, offered often and in small amounts.
