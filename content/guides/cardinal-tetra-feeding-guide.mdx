# Cardinal Tetra Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and the Rule That Prevents an Outbreak

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cardinal tetra feeding is simple, small food in small amounts, but the rule that protects the school has nothing to do with portions at all.

## The Staple Diet

Cardinal tetras are omnivores. Build the diet on a good-quality dried food, flake, granules or small pellets, and supplement it with live and frozen chironomid larvae (bloodworm), brine shrimp, black mosquito larvae, cyclops, daphnia, and moina.

In the wild, this species isn't picky. Cardinal tetras feed almost constantly throughout the day on tiny crustaceans, insect eggs, algae, and detritus, cladocerans, copepods, and rotifers among them. A tank obviously can't replicate that grazing pattern exactly, but it's a useful reminder that this is a fish built to eat small amounts often rather than one large meal.

<KeyTakeaway>
Cardinal tetras have small mouths, similar to neon tetra's. Crush flakes into fine pieces and stick to micro or nano-sized pellets. Food that's too large gets taken and spat back out repeatedly, which functionally starves the fish even while food is visibly going into the tank.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Twice daily, offering only what the school finishes in a couple of minutes, is the practical standard most keepers use, the same schedule as for the closely related [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-feeding-guide/). In the wild this species feeds almost constantly throughout the day, so some keepers split that into three or four smaller feedings a day instead. Either approach works as long as portions stay small enough to disappear quickly.

## Foods to Avoid

Oversized flakes or pellets, excessive protein-rich live or frozen food, and any overfeeding are the main risks. Avoid low-quality live foods from questionable sources, which can introduce parasites, and don't overdo vegetables or fruit, they cause bloating in a fish this small.

<FunFact>
Never leave a dead fish in the tank or let tankmates pick at a visibly sick fish's body. Neon tetra disease, which cardinals catch too, spreads when fish eat infected tissue, and it has no cure. Removing a dead or dying fish the moment you see it is a feeding-time habit, and the only control that exists.
</FunFact>

## Supplementation

None specific is required beyond a varied diet. Rotating in the live and frozen foods above covers nutrition and coloration on its own, no separate vitamin or mineral supplement is needed for a properly fed community tank.

## The Mistakes That Matter

Food too large for their mouths means the fish can't actually eat it, it gets spat out, rots, and pollutes the water while the fish itself goes underfed. Overfeeding lets uneaten food decay and spike ammonia and nitrite, which a school [shows before a test kit does](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/). Nitrite is the compound cardinal tetras tolerate worst, so keeping portions tight matters more here than it might for a hardier community fish. And leaving a dead or sick fish in the tank is how an outbreak of an incurable disease spreads to the whole school.
