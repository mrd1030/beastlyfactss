# Cardinal Tetra Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and the Rule That Prevents an Outbreak

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cardinal tetra feeding is simple, small food in small amounts, but the rule that protects the school has nothing to do with portions at all.

## The Staple Diet

Cardinal tetras are omnivores. [Seriously Fish](https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/paracheirodon-axelrodi/) recommends building the diet around a good-quality dried food as a base, supplemented with live and frozen chironomid larvae (bloodworm), mosquito larvae, daphnia, and moina. [Practical Fishkeeping](https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/cardinal-tetra-paracheirodon-axelrodi/) lists a similar mix: flake foods, granules, and small pellets, plus live or frozen bloodworm, brine shrimp, black mosquito larvae, cyclops, and daphnia.

In the wild, this species isn't picky. [Practical Fishkeeping](https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/cardinal-tetra-paracheirodon-axelrodi/) describes cardinal tetra feeding almost constantly throughout the day on tiny crustaceans, insect eggs, algae, and detritus, cladocerans, copepods, and rotifers among them. A tank obviously can't replicate that grazing pattern exactly, but it's a useful reminder that this is a fish built to eat small amounts often rather than one large meal.

<KeyTakeaway>
Cardinal tetras have small mouths, similar to neon tetra's. Crush flakes into fine pieces and stick to micro or nano-sized pellets. Food that's too large gets taken and spat back out repeatedly, which functionally starves the fish even while food is visibly going into the tank.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Twice daily, offering only what the school finishes in a couple of minutes, is the practical standard most keepers use and matches what our [Neon tetra feeding guide](/blog/neon-tetra-feeding-guide/) recommends for that closely related species. [Practical Fishkeeping](https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/cardinal-tetra-paracheirodon-axelrodi/) notes that in the wild this species feeds almost constantly throughout the day, so some keepers split that into three or four smaller feedings a day instead. Either approach works as long as portions stay small enough to disappear quickly.

## Foods to Avoid

Oversized flakes or pellets, excessive protein-rich live or frozen food, and any overfeeding are the main risks. Avoid low-quality live foods from questionable sources, which can introduce parasites, and don't overdo vegetables or fruit, they cause bloating in a fish this small.

<FunFact>
Critically, never leave a dead fish in the tank or let tankmates scavenge a visibly sick fish's body. Neon tetra disease is named for a different species, and is caused by the microsporidian parasite Pleistophora hyphessobryconis. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/aquarium-fish/mycotic-diseases-of-fish) documents it affecting tetras broadly, along with angelfish, rasboras, barbs, and zebrafish, cardinal tetra's own family among them, rather than being confined to its namesake species. There is no cure. If a fish dies of it and its body is eaten by others, those fish contract it as well, prompt removal of dead or sick fish is the only control that actually exists.
</FunFact>

## Supplementation

None specific is required beyond a varied diet. Rotating in the live and frozen foods above covers nutrition and coloration on its own, no separate vitamin or mineral supplement is needed for a properly fed community tank.

## The Mistakes That Matter

Food too large for their mouths means the fish can't actually eat it, it gets spat out, rots, and pollutes the water while the fish itself goes underfed. Overfeeding lets uneaten food decay and spike ammonia and nitrite, and the [Acta Amazonica tolerance study](https://acta.inpa.gov.br/fasciculos/38-4/BODY/v38n4a23.html) on this species found nitrite is the parameter cardinal tetra handles worst of the four it tested, so keeping portions tight matters more here than it might for a hardier community fish. And leaving a dead or sick fish in the tank is how an outbreak of an incurable disease spreads to the whole school.
