# Ghost Shrimp Feeding: What They'll Actually Eat, Including Each Other

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ghost shrimp feeding is about as low-effort as this site covers, they'll scavenge a real share of their own food, but there's one nuance worth knowing upfront: this is an opportunistic omnivore, not a dedicated algae eater, and "opportunistic" extends to smaller or freshly molted tankmates.

For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Ghost shrimp care guide](/guides/ghost-shrimp/).

## The Staple Diet: Scavenger First, Algae Eater Second

[Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Palaemonetes_paludosus/) describes the wild diet plainly: algae, both diatoms and green algae, along with vascular plants, detritus, aquatic insects, and other benthic organic matter. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaemon_paludosus) adds a behavioral detail worth knowing if you're wondering why you don't see much daytime foraging: this species is largely nocturnal, staying hidden among vegetation by day and emerging at night to feed on plankton, including specific diatom genera like *Fragilaria* and *Navicula*.

That's a genuinely broad, opportunistic diet, and sources disagree slightly on how much of it is algae specifically. [Quality Marine](https://www.qualitymarine.com/news/ghost-shrimp-palaemonetes-sp/) is direct about it: ghost shrimp "are not known for eating algae, but will do an excellent job cleaning up the detritus around the tank." Other keepers report them grazing soft brush algae readily enough. The honest middle ground is that ghost shrimp are a genuine all-purpose detritivore and scavenger first, and an algae eater only incidentally, useful cleanup crew, but not a substitute for a dedicated algae specialist like [Amano shrimp](/guides/amano-shrimp/) if that's the specific problem you're solving for.

<FunFact>
Ghost shrimp are nocturnal foragers, more active hunting for food at night than during the day, and their near-total transparency is part of how they get away with it. It's one of the more interesting things to watch with a flashlight after dark, a translucent shrimp working its way across the substrate, picking through diatoms and detritus that a daytime glance at the tank would never reveal was even there.
</FunFact>

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

A small amount every day or two is plenty for a modestly stocked tank. Offer a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00025JZAY?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Sinking Wafers for Catfish, Loaches and Bottom Feeders, 3.88 oz">sinking wafer</AffiliateLink> broken into smaller pieces, or a few pellets, and let scavenging cover the rest. Since ghost shrimp forage continuously rather than eating one big meal, there's little benefit to a precise multi-times-a-day schedule the way a schooling fish needs, small and frequent beats large and occasional here. Remove anything still uneaten after a few hours to keep it from fouling the water.

## The Predation Nuance: Smaller Tankmates and Their Own Kind

This is the part of ghost shrimp feeding that's easy to miss if you only think of them as a peaceful cleanup crew. [The Aquarium Guide](https://theaquariumguide.com/articles/ghost-shrimp-and-cherry-shrimp) is direct about the risk to smaller shrimp species kept alongside them: "Ghost shrimp often eat smaller fish and other shrimp," and specifically that they'll "usually bully cherry and the baby shrimp." If you're weighing a [cherry shrimp](/guides/cherry-shrimp/) and ghost shrimp combination, that's a real compatibility risk to plan around, not a hypothetical one.

The same risk applies within a pure ghost shrimp colony. [BiologyInsights](https://biologyinsights.com/are-shrimp-cannibals-when-and-why-it-happens/) explains why freshly molted shrimp specifically get targeted: a shrimp that's just shed its exoskeleton is left "soft, defenseless" for "several hours or even days" while the new shell hardens, immobile, unprotected, and, to a hungry tankmate, "a rich source of protein and the calcium carbonate needed for the aggressor's own shell hardening." It's a real evolutionary logic, not random aggression.

The prevention is straightforward and doubles as good general husbandry: dense hiding cover so a molting shrimp has somewhere to disappear, and "sufficient and frequent feeding" so a well-fed colony has less motivation to target a vulnerable tankmate in the first place, exactly the diet this guide already covers. Our [tank setup guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-tank-setup-guide/) covers the hiding-spot side in more depth, and our [health issues guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-health-issues-guide/) covers molting problems from the shrimp's own side of things.

## Foods to Avoid

Excess protein-heavy feeding, low-quality live foods from unclear sources, which can introduce parasites, and simple overfeeding are the main risks. Uneaten food decays and degrades water quality quickly in a small tank, and a colony that's already scavenging effectively rarely needs much added on top.

## Supplementation

None specific is required. A varied diet, mostly self-sourced scavenging plus an occasional sinking wafer, covers nutrition without a separate vitamin or mineral product, and it includes enough calcium to support the molting process covered in our [health issues guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-health-issues-guide/).
