# How to Handle a Leopard Gecko Safely

Leopard geckos tolerate handling reasonably well once they're settled, but they're not a species that craves interaction the way a bearded dragon might. Understanding that upfront, and understanding their single biggest physical vulnerability, makes for a much better relationship with your gecko.

## Let It Settle In First

Wait one to two weeks after bringing a new gecko home before attempting to handle it, and use that time as a benchmark: don't start until it stops fleeing or hiding when you approach and is eating normally. If your gecko is young, under about 3 inches long, hold off on regular handling until it reaches roughly 6 inches. Hatchlings are fragile and easily injured.

Use the settling weeks to introduce your hand without touching. Rest a hand flat in the enclosure for a few minutes each evening, when the gecko is up and about, and let it approach, sniff, or tongue-flick your fingers on its own terms. A gecko that walks over to investigate a still hand is ready for the first pickup.

## How Often

Once settled, start with 5-minute sessions every other day and build up gradually toward 10 to 20 minutes as your gecko gets comfortable. For young geckos, cap it at a few short sessions a week. Evening sessions tend to work best, since that's closer to when they're naturally active, and not right after they've woken up. Never handle during a shed, and skip handling for about 48 hours after a large meal, since jostling a full gecko can cause regurgitation. They don't need daily handling to be a well-adjusted pet, unlike some other reptiles.

## The Correct Way to Pick One Up

Use a two-handed scoop: one hand goes under the body to support the hips and tail base, and let the gecko walk onto your hand rather than grabbing it from above. Stay low to the ground while handling, they can jump and dart unexpectedly, and a fall from height is a real injury risk for a small animal.

## Signs It's Had Enough

- Chirping or squeaking
- Screaming, more common in juveniles
- A slow side-to-side tail swish, usually with an arched back and the gecko up on its toes
- Fast retreats or attempts to hide
- Refusing food afterward

A gecko barking isn't necessarily distress, since it can just mean it's hungry, but the other signs above mean it's time to put it back.

## Reading the Tail Before You Reach In

The tail carries most of a leopard gecko's body language, and the two common movements mean different things. A slow, deliberate swish, especially with the back arched and the legs straightened, is a warning to leave it alone. Close the enclosure and try again once the posture relaxes. A rapid tail flick or rattle is excitement instead, usually at prey or, in an adult male, at a potential mate. A gecko rattling its tail at a cricket is focused on the cricket, so let it finish hunting and handle it later.

## Tail Loss: The Thing Every Leopard Gecko Owner Needs to Understand

This is the single most important thing to know about handling this species. Leopard geckos can voluntarily drop their tail, called caudal autotomy, when grabbed by it, badly frightened, handled roughly, or attacked by a cagemate.

**Never grab or lift a leopard gecko by the tail. Ever.** It can come off when grasped, sometimes with very little pressure. The tail stores fat the gecko relies on. Losing it is a physiological event with a real cost.

Scale that against the animal in your hand. An adult is only 6 to 9 inches long, males weigh 60 to 90 grams, and a female can weigh as little as 45 grams, so a gecko that drops its tail loses a large share of its reserves at once. Support the feet, body, and tail together in an open palm, and let the gecko walk from one hand to the other instead of holding it still.

<FunFact>
A dropped gecko tail can keep twitching on its own for a minute or more after it detaches. It's a leftover distraction mechanism from the wild, a wriggling tail draws a predator's attention while the rest of the gecko escapes.
</FunFact>

If a tail does drop:

- Move your gecko to a clean paper towel substrate immediately
- Keep the enclosure especially clean while it heals
- Avoid handling for 1 to 2 weeks
- Watch the stump for swelling, discharge, discoloration, or a foul smell, any of those mean it's time for a vet

The tail does regrow, usually within about 30 days, but the new one is built on cartilage instead of bone and comes back shorter, thicker, and visually different. It never looks quite the same again.

## One More Thing

Leopard geckos can carry [Salmonella](/blog/reptile-salmonella-hygiene-guide/). Wash your hands before and after every handling session, no exceptions, especially if there are kids in the house.
