# Parrotlet Feeding: Diet, Schedule, and Foods to Avoid

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A parrotlet's bowl should look a lot like a cockatiel's or a budgie's, pellets doing most of the work, fresh produce filling in the gaps, and seed treated as a limited extra rather than the foundation. A couple of details are specific to this species, worth knowing before you fill the dish.

## Feeding Approach

Parrotlets are free-choice eaters, not scheduled ones. They pick at food throughout the day rather than eating in set meals, so refresh the base pellet bowl in the morning when they're hungriest and top it up if it empties before evening, it shouldn't sit empty for long. That bowl is the baseline, and the seed allowance and some of the pellets can go into foraging toys so the bird works for part of its day. A bird this small that fails to solve a toy still has to eat, which is why the bowl stays.

Fresh vegetables and a small amount of fruit should be offered daily, finely chopped since this is a genuinely small bird. Pull anything uneaten within a couple hours to prevent spoilage. Fresh water needs to be available at all times, with dishes cleaned daily, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Ref6FW" product="Detachable Stainless Steel Feeding Dishes, 4 Pack (Okllen)">clamp-on dishes</AffiliateLink> that stay put on the cage bars make this easier to keep on top of.

## How Much a Day

No veterinary source gives a parrotlet a fixed pellet ration. The pellet bowl stays full and the bird takes what it needs. The measured part of the day is the extras: seeds and nuts come as a tiny portion, served no more than once a day.

The number that matters is what one particular bird eats, and a gram scale finds it. Weigh the food going into the cage in the morning, weigh what is left 24 hours later, and take the difference. Do that for seven days and average it. That average is the bird's daily intake, and it is the figure to plan from when seed is being swapped for pellets, so the bird is offered its full day of food throughout.

The bird goes on the scale too. On a diet change, a loss of more than 1 to 2% of body weight in a week means the switch is going too fast: put back some of the old food and slow down.

## What Parrotlets Eat

The foundation should be a formulated <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4fGIJJa" product="RoudyBush Daily Maintenance Bird Food, Mini (44 oz)">small parrot pellet</AffiliateLink>, supplemented daily with fresh vegetables and fruit. Limit seeds and nuts to about once a day rather than treating them as the base of the diet. That's a real, useful distinction from older seed-heavy advice: a little seed is fine, particularly as enrichment through a foraging toy, an all-seed diet is not, and it's linked to the same malnutrition and fatty liver risk documented across pet parrot species generally. A parrotlet raised on seed moves onto pellets by [gradual substitution](/blog/bird-pellet-conversion-guide/), weighed as it goes, because a stubborn small bird can go hungry beside a dish it does not recognize as food.

A cuttlebone or mineral block should be available at all times for calcium, and it matters even more for a hen, since a calcium deficiency is the factor most consistently linked to [egg binding](/blog/parrotlet-health-issues-guide/).

<FunFact>
A parrotlet's upper beak has small ridges that hold a seed in place while the lower beak cracks and removes the hull, before the seed is ever swallowed. That's also exactly why this species doesn't need dietary grit the way some other birds do, there's no whole hull left for a gizzard to grind down.
</FunFact>

Parrotlets don't need grit. Like other true parrots, they hull seeds with their beak before swallowing, so there's nothing left for grit to grind. It's more than just unnecessary, free access to grit can cause a genuinely dangerous crop or intestinal impaction, and shouldn't be offered unless a vet specifically recommends it for a bird having trouble digesting.

## Safe Treats and Foods to Avoid

Safe: corn, banana, orange, pear, apple (seeds removed), peas, celery, kiwi, and carrot are all foods specifically called out as fine for this species. Broccoli, leafy greens, and other cockatiel- and budgie-safe produce work well too, introduced gradually since parrotlets can be surprisingly stubborn about trying anything unfamiliar.

Toxic, not just something to limit: avocado is the single most consistently flagged toxic food across veterinary and bird-specific sources alike. Chocolate and caffeine are genuine dangers too, both can cause a dangerously elevated heart rate, and in enough quantity, seizures or death. Alcohol is toxic even in small amounts. Onion and garlic, cooked or raw, can trigger a serious anemia. Xylitol, an artificial sweetener in some sugar-free products, is dangerous as well. A few less obvious ones: excess salt and high-fat table food strain the kidneys and liver, and the seeds or pits from rose-family fruits (apple, cherry, peach) contain compounds that affect heart function, which is exactly why apple should have its seeds removed before offering it.

## Why Parrotlets Stop Eating

1. **Resistance to switching from seed to pellets.** This can take days to weeks in a bird raised on seed. Offering pellets when the bird is hungriest, and mixing them with a little seed or moist food at first, tends to help.
2. **Heavy molting.** Growing a full new set of feathers raises the demand for protein sharply and can tax the body.
3. **A new environment or handling stress.** A short adjustment period of reduced eating after a move is commonly reported.
4. **Hormonal or egg-related behavior.** A hen can be triggered into laying without a male present at all, and the physiological cost of that shows up in appetite and behavior both.
5. **General illness.** Lethargy, fluffed feathers held constantly, nasal or eye discharge, and tail-bobbing with each breath commonly accompany appetite loss in a genuinely sick bird.

## When to Worry

Parrotlets are prey animals that hide illness as a survival instinct, so any noticeable, sustained drop in eating is worth taking seriously rather than watching for a few days first. Because they're small and fast-metabolizing, a bird that's also showing lethargy, fluffed feathers, discharge, or open-mouth breathing needs a same-day or next-day call to an avian vet.
