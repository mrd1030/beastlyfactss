# Mollies Aren't Handled Either, But They Do Need a Breeding Plan

A female molly that has met a male once can keep giving birth long after he is gone, and that is what decides whether a molly tank stays the size it was set up to be. The fish themselves are watched, and a net is the only contact they need.

## What Mollies Are Like

Mollies are active fish that do best in a group with more females than males, and they cope well in hard, alkaline, stable water while weakening quickly in soft or acidic water. Sailfins may pick on smaller fish as they mature. Most common short-fin varieties run roughly 3 to 4 inches as adults, with sailfin varieties, distinguished by their males' tall, sail-like dorsal fin, reaching 4 to 6 inches.

## Sexing: Gonopodium vs. Gravid Spot

Telling males from females is straightforward. Males carry a modified anal fin called a gonopodium, a slender, rod-like structure used to fertilize females internally, in place of the fuller fan-shaped anal fin females have. Females can develop a dark "gravid spot" near the vent when carrying young, though it is harder to see on mollies than on many livebearers, especially dark ones, and not every female shows one clearly enough to rely on.

## The Behavior That Matters Most: How Mollies Breed

Mollies are livebearers, females give birth to free-swimming fry directly instead of laying eggs that need separate incubation. That alone sets them apart from most other commonly kept aquarium fish, and it comes with a real consequence worth understanding before you buy.

<FunFact>
A single mating can be enough to keep a female molly producing fry for a long stretch afterward, since females can store sperm internally and don't need a male present again for each new brood. Gestation takes about 4 weeks, and a brood averages roughly 20 to 35 fry in lab conditions, with a documented range from 10 to over 100 depending on the female's age and condition. Broods from stored sperm ran smaller in one study, about 21 fry against 35 for freshly mated females. Put a male and female together with no plan, and the tank doesn't stay the size you started with.
</FunFact>

Molly parents don't provide any care after birth, and adults will readily eat their own fry if given the chance. If you want fry to survive, [dense live planting](/blog/molly-enrichment-guide/) or a dedicated breeding tank gives them somewhere to hide until they're large enough to hold their own.

## Managing Population, Practically

Keep males and females in separate tanks if you want to avoid breeding entirely. Keep a males-only group for a colorful, breeding-free display instead, bearing in mind that male sailfin mollies can be aggressive toward each other, so an all-male group needs space and numbers rather than a trio. Male mollies squabble among themselves more than male guppies do. Let it happen and plan for it, rehoming or selling fry as they mature, or accepting that natural predation will control numbers if you're not removing fry deliberately. If you do want controlled breeding, a ratio of roughly one male to two or three females reduces how much the females get chased and stressed by repeated mating attempts.

## First-Time Keeper Mistakes

- Buying a mixed-sex group with no population plan at all, the single most common mistake with this species
- Undersizing the tank. Mollies need a genuine 20-gallon minimum for a small group, not the smaller footprint some other community fish get by on, and a growing colony needs more room fast
- Not cycling the tank before adding fish, which leads to ammonia stress right from the start. The [aquarium cycling guide](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) gives the three readings that tell you the cycle actually finished

Ammonia from an uncycled tank is one of the triggers behind the species' well-known "shimmy." A lot of the water-quality-driven stress mollies show, including the shimmy, traces back to that last mistake more than anything else.

## How Molly Breeding Compares to Guppy Breeding

Mollies and guppies are close relatives in the same livebearer family, Poeciliidae, and their breeding biology looks nearly identical on paper: both store sperm internally, both produce repeat broods from a single mating, and both can overwhelm an unprepared tank within a couple of months, as the [guppy handling guide](/blog/guppy-handling-guide/) describes for that species.

The practical difference is body size. Mollies run larger and need a bigger tank to start with, so a growing colony outgrows a small tank sooner. Broods are in the same range as a guppy's, a few dozen fry, so an unmanaged mixed-sex molly tank outgrows itself as fast as a guppy tank does, and the larger fish crowd it sooner. If you're deciding between the two species, that's a useful thing to weigh, and the [guppy cost guide](/blog/guppy-cost-guide/) prices the guppy side of that comparison.
