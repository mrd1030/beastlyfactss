# Is It Legal to Own a Rosy Boa? State-by-State Guide

The rosy boa is about as far from a public-safety concern as a constrictor gets: a slow-moving desert species that tops out around 2 to 3 feet, kept mostly because it's calm and easy to handle. That's what makes New York City's rule so surprising. NYC's health code bans "any member of the family Boidae" by name, and a rosy boa is a boid, so it reads as banned on the exact same clause that outlaws the green anaconda. For everything else about keeping one, see our full [Rosy Boa care guide](/guides/rosy-boa/), and the [Rosy Boa encyclopedia profile](/encyclopedia/animal/rosy-boa/) for the desert range that explains why this species stays so small in the first place.

### New York City's Family-Wide Ban Catches a Harmless Species

<FunFact>
New York City's health code doesn't ban rosy boas by name. It bans "any member, or hybrid offspring of the family Boidae, including, but not limited to, the common or green anaconda and yellow anaconda." Those two anacondas are only the examples the ordinance lists, not the limit of what it covers, and a rosy boa is taxonomically a boid regardless of size. So a 2 to 3 foot rosy boa reads as banned in NYC on the same clause that catches an anaconda that can run well past ten feet. It's a real instance of a blanket family-level rule sweeping in a species nobody would think to call dangerous.
</FunFact>

### New Jersey's Genus Gap

New Jersey's exempted species list at N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.4 clears a short run of exotic reptiles from the permit requirement, and it names the boa constrictor by exact genus and species, under the old binomial *Constrictor constrictor*. It does not name the family Boidae as a whole. Rosy boas are *Lichanura*, so they fall outside that exemption.

What happens next is the part worth reading, because the rosy boa is not left to a catch-all. The permit-required list at 7:25-4.3(a)3.iii takes "Family Boidae (other than Boa Constrictors)," and that parenthesis exists for exactly one reason: the state had already exempted the boa constrictor by name, so when it went to put boas on the permit list it had to cut that one animal back out. The rosy boa is in the remainder, named by family rather than left to silence.

### California's Taxonomic Split: Two Names, One Confusing Result

Rosy boa isn't on California's restricted species list, so owning a captive-bred, pet-trade rosy boa is unrestricted and needs no permit. Where it gets confusing is the state's own wild population. Genetic work in the early 2000s split the rosy boa complex into two species, and California's native boas are now generally recognized as Lichanura orcutti, officially called the "Northern three-lined boa" in state regulations, while Lichanura trivirgata, the name most commonly used in the pet trade, is restricted to the southern end of the range in extreme southern California, Baja California, and Sonora. California's own take-limit rule lists the Northern three-lined boa with a limit of two animals under a sportfishing license, and a native reptile propagation permit is required to commercially breed or sell them. None of that touches ownership of an ordinary captive-bred, pet-store rosy boa, of either taxon. It's a wild-collection and commercial-propagation rule wearing a different scientific name than the one most keepers know the species by, easy to miss if you aren't looking for it.

### Colorado Dropped Out of the Boidae Group in 2026

Colorado used to belong in the row above. Its unregulated wildlife list cleared "all non-native tropical and subtropical species of snakes in the families: Boidae," and a rosy boa was read across on the family name the same way a boa constrictor was. That changed on May 1, 2026, when Colorado Parks and Wildlife moved every reptile and amphibian provision into a new Chapter W-7 and attached a hard geographic test to the family lists: Appendix A now reaches only snakes "native only to the tropical and subtropical region," which the rule defines as the band between 26 degrees north and 26 degrees south.

The rosy boa is a Mojave and Sonoran desert animal ranging well north of that line, so it no longer clears the family listing. CPW's own species determinations file Lichanura trivirgata as regulated wildlife on exactly that ground, alongside the rubber boa, while boa constrictor, the rainbow boas, and the sand boas stay on Appendix A. Regulated wildlife in Colorado is not a permit tier for private keepers; the agency states plainly that it will not issue a license for private possession of it. It's the sharpest illustration on this page of why reading a family name off an allow-list isn't the same as reading the rule.

### Hawaii's Total Ban, and Arizona's Wild-Take Limit

Hawaii bans every species of snake statewide under its administrative rules, the entire suborder Serpentes, with no exception for a small, docile species like the rosy boa. Arizona is a different kind of restriction entirely. Rosy boas are native to the southern part of the state, and Arizona's wildlife commission treats them as a species of conservation concern, setting a wild-take and possession bag limit of 4 animals under a valid hunting license. That regime governs collecting one from the wild in Arizona specifically. It doesn't touch simply owning a captive-bred rosy boa there, which isn't on the state's restricted live wildlife list and needs no permit.

## The Real Takeaway

Rosy boas are broadly legal to own across the US, and in most states the family-wide Boidae exemptions that clear boa constrictor clear rosy boas too. The exceptions are narrow and specific, and they cut both ways on that family name: New York City's blanket Boidae ban catches the rosy boa regardless of size, while Colorado's 2026 rewrite kept its Boidae exemption but limited it to tropical species, which drops the rosy boa out of it. New Jersey's exemption is written for a different genus, and Hawaii bans every snake outright. California's wrinkle is a naming issue tied to wild collection, not an ownership restriction; a pet-trade rosy boa there is as unrestricted as anywhere else.
