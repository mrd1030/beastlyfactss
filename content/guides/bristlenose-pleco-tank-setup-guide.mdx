# Bristlenose Pleco Tank Setup: Driftwood Is the Detail That Actually Matters

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Before tank size or filtration, the decision that matters most with a bristlenose pleco is confirming it's the fish you think it is. Get that right, and the rest of the setup is simple.

## The Size Trap: Why "A Pleco" Isn't Enough Information

"Pleco" is used in the aquarium trade as a catchall for almost any sucker-mouthed armored catfish, and that looseness causes real problems. The common pleco (Hypostomus plecostomus) and its Pterygoplichthys relatives are sold at the exact same tiny size as a bristlenose, an inch or two long, but grow into 12 to 24-inch adults that need a 75-gallon-or-larger tank. A bristlenose stays 4 to 6 inches for life. At store size the two can look genuinely similar to an inexperienced buyer, and a bristlenose's namesake bristles grow mainly on males, with females carrying few or none, so don't count on the bristles alone to tell them apart. Ask the store directly for the species name rather than settling for "pleco."

This isn't a hypothetical problem. Common plecos dumped once they outgrew their tanks have established breeding, invasive populations in Texas and Florida waterways, dense enough in places that researchers from Texas A&M and Texas State universities pulled more than 400 of them from a single stretch of the San Marcos River in January 2022 alone.

<FunFact>
Wildlife managers didn't remove those 400-plus armored catfish for fun. Invasive plecos burrow into riverbanks to nest, which weakens the banks and drives erosion, and they can survive over 20 hours out of water, which is part of why populations dumped from home aquariums have proven so difficult to eliminate once established.
</FunFact>

## Tank Size

20 gallons is the practical minimum for a single adult bristlenose, and 29 gallons or more is genuinely the better long-term choice given how much waste one fish produces relative to its size. Size up again for each additional bristlenose, and favor extra floor space and driftwood surface over height, this is a fish that spends its life low in the water column rather than swimming in open water.

## Temperature and Water Parameters

74 to 80°F suits most captive-bred bristlenose, with some sources citing a somewhat wider tolerance either side of that range, a genuine sign of how adaptable this species is. A reliable <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">heater</AffiliateLink> is worth running unless your room stays consistently warm. Aim for a pH around 6.5 to 7.8, and keep ammonia and nitrite at zero through regular water changes, a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">water test kit</AffiliateLink> is the only reliable way to confirm your actual numbers rather than guess. That pH holds only as long as the water carries enough KH to buffer it, and the driftwood this fish needs releases mildly acidic tannins that pull pH down in low-KH water, so test [KH alongside pH](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before reaching for any pH adjuster.

For hardness, a bristlenose is comfortable anywhere from 1 to 15 dGH, which covers most tap water. Keep enough carbonate hardness in the water to hold its pH against the tannins from the wood.

## Driftwood: Required, Not Decorative

<AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eR0Z2l" product="Driftwood">Driftwood</AffiliateLink> belongs in every bristlenose tank from day one. This species rasps wood constantly and instinctively, and most care guides tie that grazing behavior to healthy digestion, fiber and the biofilm colonizing the wood's surface both appear to matter. Skipping it entirely is genuinely associated with digestive problems even in a fish that's otherwise being fed correctly. Buy a substantial piece rather than an ornament, or several pieces: the [evidence on wood-eating plecos](/blog/bristlenose-pleco-enrichment-guide/) is clearest in a different group of catfish entirely, and what a bristlenose reliably needs is enough surface to rasp at. Expect tannins to tint the water, which is normal and harmless.

## Preparing the Wood

Buy wood sold for aquarium use. Roots and branches collected outdoors often have not dried or cured properly and can rot in the tank, and wood sold for reptile enclosures may carry chemicals that are harmful in water.

1. **Scrub it.** Use a clean brush and plain water to clear dirt and debris. No soap or chemical cleaner: any residue will poison the tank.
2. **Soak it.** Submerge the wood in a bucket or tub for at least 1 to 2 weeks so it saturates fully. Change the water whenever it turns dark, and the wood is ready when the water stays clear for several days in a row.
3. **Boil it, if the piece fits.** One to two hours at a boil sterilizes the wood, kills algae and fungal spores, and speeds up the tannin release, which shortens the soak.
4. **Anchor it.** Large pieces can still float after a full soak. Tie them to a rock with monofilament line to hold them down.

Whatever tannin is left will keep tinting the water and lowering the pH slightly over time, which is the reason to keep an eye on KH.

Smooth sand or rounded gravel is the safer choice, sharp substrate can injure the soft underbelly and barbels as a bristlenose forages. <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DQQZ69J6?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="CaribSea Super Naturals Aquascape Sunset Gold - 10 lb - Natural Substrate for Freshwater Aquariums, Perfect for Aquascaping & Creating Vibrant Landscapes">CaribSea Super Naturals Aquascape Sunset Gold</AffiliateLink> is a rounded, aquarium-safe sand that works well here. Add caves or PVC pipe for daytime hiding, since bristlenose are nocturnal and spend most daylight hours out of sight, plan for at least one secure hiding spot per fish, more if you're keeping multiple males, since they're territorial with each other.

## Filtration: Over-Filter for a Messy Eater

A bristlenose produces more waste than its size suggests, so run a filter rated for more gallons than the tank actually holds rather than the bare minimum. An AquaClear 70, rated for a 40 to 70-gallon tank, is a reasonable choice for a 20 to 30-gallon bristlenose setup precisely because it's oversized for the space.

## Lighting

Standard community lighting is fine. This is a nocturnal species that's most active once the lights go down, and it appreciates the option to retreat into shade during brighter parts of the day rather than needing darkness constantly.
