# California Kingsnake Feeding: Prey Size, the Adult Interval, and Why One Snake Per Tank

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The California kingsnake earned its beginner reputation at the food bowl. It takes frozen-thawed rodents reliably, it rarely refuses, and it eats less often than a new keeper expects. The details that matter are the prey size, the interval as the snake grows, and one habit of the species that changes how feeding day is run.

Once a week maintains an adult kingsnake. Feeding twice a week grows a young snake faster if it will take it, and does nothing for an adult except add weight, which is why the adult interval stretches to ten to fourteen days and a mature snake gets weighed. The reptile vet clinic's instruction is to reduce feeding once the snake reaches adult size, and a kingsnake at a healthy weight has a body that tapers cleanly rather than one with a fold behind the head.

## What California Kingsnakes Eat

In the wild, a kingsnake eats anything it can overpower and swallow whole, including rattlesnakes, which is where the name comes from. In captivity the diet is rodents, usually mice, because mice are available, nutritionally complete, and the right shape.

**Size the prey to the snake's body.** The rule from both the vet clinic and the welfare charity is the same: prey about as wide as the snake at its thickest point, or slightly wider. The meal should leave a lump a little larger than the snake's normal mid-body diameter and no more. A hatchling starts on one pinky and moves up through fuzzies and hoppers to an adult mouse as it grows.

**Frozen-thawed, fully defrosted, warmed to room temperature.** A live adult mouse can inflict real wounds on a snake that does not strike cleanly, and frozen-thawed or fresh-killed prey removes that risk entirely. Thaw the mouse completely before it is offered; a cold center is refused or regurgitated.

**Offer it on tongs.** Long feeding tongs keep a hand out of strike range, since a kingsnake keys on movement and warmth at feeding time and a finger is both. Feed inside the enclosure unless the snake has learned to strike at anything that enters, in which case a separate feeding tub teaches it that the home enclosure is not where food appears.

**Variety is optional.** A chick once a month or so adds variety, and a snake on good-quality whole prey needs no supplements at all. The mouse carries its own calcium.

<KeyTakeaway>
Never feed two kingsnakes in the same enclosure, and never house two together. A kingsnake eats snakes, including its own species, and two adults that meet over a mouse can end as one adult. The [milk snake feeding rules](/blog/milk-snake-handling-guide/) carry the same warning for the same reason.
</KeyTakeaway>

## After the Meal

Wait 48 hours after feeding before handling. A snake picked up with a fresh meal inside it can regurgitate, which is unpleasant for everyone and costs the snake the meal. Until a new snake is settled with you, the safer rule is to leave it alone until the lump has digested down to normal diameter. Handling around a shed is avoided for the same reason the snake is off food then: it is stressed and its skin is vulnerable.

Water sits at the cool end, in a dish large enough for the snake to immerse itself, replaced at least daily since a kingsnake soaks and fouls it. Fill the bowl only halfway so a soaking snake does not flood the substrate; if the enclosure starts to look or feel damp, the bowl comes out and goes in for a couple of days a week instead.

## Why Kingsnakes Stop Eating

**Shedding.** A snake with clouded eyes and dull skin is in shed and is not interested. Appetite returns within a day or two of the old skin coming off.

**A cold enclosure.** Digestion needs the warm end at temperature. A kingsnake that cannot get warm will refuse rather than take a meal it cannot process.

**Too much handling, or a new home.** A snake handled daily, or one that arrived this week, is stressed and eats when the stress stops. Give a new arrival a week of quiet before the first meal is offered.

**The prey.** Too cold in the middle, too large, or a different species from the one the snake was raised on. A refuser will often take a mouse that has been warmed a little more, or one size smaller.

**A winter slowdown.** Kingsnakes from cooler parts of their range slow down as the days shorten, and an adult that eats less from late autumn while holding its weight is following the season, not sick.

## When to Worry

The vet clinic's line is a refusal running past three to four weeks, and the welfare charity's is simpler: a snake that suddenly stops feeding sees a reptile vet. Weighing the snake monthly is what turns those into a decision. A kingsnake that has skipped three meals while holding its weight in a warm enclosure is fine. One that has lost weight, or that refuses alongside wheezing, a swollen jaw, or regurgitation, is not, and the [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) covers reading the droppings that tell you which.

A kingsnake fed a mouse the width of its body every week or two, on tongs, alone, will eat that way for twenty years. The species makes feeding easy. The keeper's only job is not to make it generous.
