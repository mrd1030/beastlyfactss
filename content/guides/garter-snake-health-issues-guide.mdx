# Common Garter Snake Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

A garter snake's health issues trace back to the same two places its diet does: what it eats and how damp its enclosure runs. Get the fish and earthworm diet right and the temperature and humidity dialed in, and most of what follows stays theoretical.

## Thiamine (Vitamin B1) Deficiency

This is the health issue that makes garter snakes genuinely different from a corn snake or hognose on a vet's exam table. Thiaminase, an enzyme concentrated in certain fish, breaks down vitamin B1 into a form the body can't use, and a diet that leans too heavily on thiaminase-rich fish like goldfish or rosy red minnows, without a B1 supplement, can cause a real deficiency over time. It's specific enough to fish-eating snakes that the standard veterinary reference for exotic animal medicine calls out garter and water snakes by name as the species most likely to develop it.

**Watch for:** muscle tremors, loss of coordination, a twisted or tilted neck (torticollis), an arched, rigid posture (opisthotonos, sometimes called stargazing), and apparent blindness in advanced cases. Left uncorrected, it progresses to death. Initial treatment is injectable thiamine from a vet; the actual fix is dietary, switching to thiaminase-free fish (tilapia, salmon, silversides) and adding a B1 supplement whenever fish make up more than roughly a quarter of the diet.

## Respiratory Infection

Usually bacterial, and almost always tied to an enclosure that's running too cold, too damp, or poorly ventilated. **Watch for:** open-mouth breathing, wheezing or a gurgling sound, mucus or discharge around the nose or mouth, and lethargy. This needs a reptile vet and prescription antibiotics, not a wait-and-see approach.

## Mouth Rot (Infectious Stomatitis)

A bacterial infection of the mouth tissue, commonly linked to unsanitary enclosure conditions (including a soiled water container, worth keeping clean given how much time this species spends in and around water), minor mouth injuries, or the same temperature and humidity problems behind respiratory infection.

**Watch for:** excess drooling, redness or small hemorrhage spots on the gums, visible swelling, discharge, and reduced appetite. This needs veterinary treatment, antibiotics plus cleaning of affected tissue, rather than home care.

## Internal and External Parasites

Garter snakes carry more real parasite risk than most other pet snakes, simply because of what they eat. Earthworms, fish, and any wild-caught prey are more likely to introduce internal parasites (roundworms, tapeworms, and similar) than a diet of commercially bred frozen rodents, and this is especially true for a wild-caught snake, which may already be carrying a parasite load picked up before it was ever your pet. Mites, the more common external parasite, show up as tiny black or red specks on the scales or in the water dish.

A veterinary fecal exam is genuinely worth prioritizing for this species, for any newly acquired garter snake and periodically after that, rather than treating it as optional. ## Retained Shed (Dysecdysis)

Usually a humidity problem. Look for patches of skin, or retained eye caps, that didn't come off cleanly. A humid hide and consistent access to the soaking water this species already needs prevents most of this.

<FunFact>
Two of the most distinctive problems on this list, thiamine deficiency and a heavier parasite load than most pet snakes carry, trace back to the exact same root cause as everything that makes garter snake feeding interesting in the first place: a real, varied, fish-and-invertebrate diet instead of a bag of frozen mice. The diet that makes this species unusual is also what makes its health risks unusual.
</FunFact>

## The Pattern Worth Remembering

Diet drives more of this list than husbandry does, which is unusual for a pet snake. Thiamine deficiency and elevated parasite risk both come directly from what a garter snake eats, while respiratory infection, mouth rot, and retained shed come from the same temperature, humidity, and cleanliness fundamentals that apply to virtually every pet snake. Get the fish-and-earthworm diet right, supplement B1 when needed, and keep the enclosure appropriately warm and clean, and this is a genuinely manageable species to keep healthy.
