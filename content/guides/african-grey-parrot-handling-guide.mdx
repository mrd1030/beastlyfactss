# How to Handle an African Grey Parrot

African grey parrots are highly intelligent, sensitive, and social, and they need a genuine daily time investment, roughly 4 to 5 hours of interaction, to stay well-adjusted. This isn't a bird that thrives on occasional attention.

## Just How Intelligent Are We Talking?

Landmark peer-reviewed research on a grey named Alex documented the ability to label 50 different objects, 7 colors, and 5 shapes, count quantities up to six, and even grasp the concept of "none."

A separate, less formally studied case involving a grey named N'kisi was reported by his owner to have a contextual vocabulary in the hundreds of words. That figure comes from the owner rather than a controlled study, though, so it's worth treating with more caution than the peer-reviewed Alex research.

<FunFact>
The peer-reviewed research on Alex the African grey documented labeling of 50 objects, 7 colors, and 5 shapes, along with counting quantities up to six and grasping the concept of "none," a level of documented cognition that set this species apart in the scientific literature.
</FunFact>

## Understanding the Bite

African greys have a real reputation for biting, and it's worth understanding this isn't primarily about aggression. Biting is far more often tied to fear, a lack of trust, or overstimulation. A grey bite can break skin, and their sharp nails can scratch, but most biting incidents trace back to a specific trigger rather than the bird simply being difficult.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Pushing physical interaction before trust has genuinely been established.
- "Beak wrestling," playing roughly with the beak, which the bird reads as an invitation.
- Over-clipping nails, which can leave a grey feeling insecure and more prone to fear-biting.
- Under-providing sleep or enrichment. Greys need 10 to 12 hours of dark, quiet rest nightly.

## How This Compares to Smaller Parrots

Compared to the budgies, cockatiels, and conures already covered on this site, African greys are considerably more cognitively demanding and, notably, more prone to stress-driven feather plucking specifically. Smaller parrots are generally more forgiving of an owner's schedule and less likely to develop this particular issue.
