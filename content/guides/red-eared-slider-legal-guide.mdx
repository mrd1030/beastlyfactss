# Is It Legal to Own a Red-Eared Slider? State-by-State Guide

Red-eared sliders are the most common pet turtle in the US, and also one of the most misunderstood when it comes to the law. Most people have heard of "the turtle ban," but that federal rule doesn't actually stop anyone from owning one. What restricts red-eared sliders is a patchwork of state invasive-species laws, and one state rewrote its rules in 2026 with a filing deadline that has since passed.

<LegalDisclaimer />

## The Federal Rule Everyone Misunderstands

Since 1975, the FDA has banned the *sale* of viable turtle eggs and live turtles with a shell under 4 inches, under 21 C.F.R. § 1240.62. This was a public health measure, not a wildlife law: small turtles were linked to a significant share of salmonellosis cases in children who put them in their mouths. A 2010 industry lawsuit challenging the rule failed, and it remains in force today, unchanged. Critically, the rule regulates commercial sale and distribution, not private ownership. You can legally own a red-eared slider of any size in all 50 states under this particular law.

### Virginia Names This Turtle and Nothing Else

Virginia runs two schemes, and 4VAC15-20-50 decides which one an animal is in. A "nonnative (exotic) animal" is one "not naturally occurring in Virginia, excluding domestic and naturalized species", so the special permit table at 4VAC15-30-40 never reaches an animal Virginia already has, and native and naturalized status is fixed by a document incorporated into the rule, the department's 2024 List of Native and Naturalized Fauna of Virginia. That list carries "Red-eared Slider * Trachemys scripta elegans *", and the asterisk denotes a naturalized species, which is expressly excluded from the definition of a nonnative (exotic) animal. So the permit table was never the right rule here.

The clause that decides it is 4VAC15-360-10(A)(6): "The following species may not be taken or possessed in any number for private use: red-eared slider and all reptile and amphibian Species of Greatest Conservation Need designated in Virginia's 2015 Wildlife Action Plan." In any number is the operative phrase. This is not the chapter's one-per-address cap set to a smaller figure, it is the private-use allowance withheld, and with it withheld nothing in Virginia permits possession of what 4VAC15-20-50 calls a wild animal. The regulation names the 2015 plan and the department now publishes the list as revised for 2025, saying the 2025 rankings were built from the 2015 ones. The slider is the only animal the subdivision names by common name, ahead of the conservation-priority species it shares the sentence with, and the reason is in the sentence next to it: Virginia’s own Cumberland and yellow-bellied sliders are both on the Species of Greatest Conservation Need list. Released pets are what made this turtle naturalized in Virginia, and the rule reads as a response to that.

### Kansas Counts Them, West Virginia Cannot Decide

Kansas had been read against K.S.A. 32-1301, the dangerous regulated animal statute, which names six big cats, bears and non-native venomous snakes and settles nothing here. The pair that decides a Kansas animal is K.S.A. 32-1002(a)(2), which makes possessing any wildlife unlawful unless a law or a regulation of the secretary permits it, and the two regulations that do the permitting: K.A.R. 115-20-3 for exotic wildlife, which subsection (d) defines as species "not native or indigenous to Kansas", and K.A.R. 115-20-2 for the state's own. The pond slider is an established Kansas taxon and Kansas sits inside the natural range of the red-eared subspecies, so 115-20-2 is the rule: reptiles are takeable for personal use year-round under (b)(13), and (e) caps possession at "no more than five reptiles and no more than two individuals of any reptile species per person and/or domicile, whichever is reached first."

West Virginia is the one cell on this map where nativity is itself the question, and both answers are restrictive. WVDNR publishes its turtle checklist under a native-species heading and the red-eared slider is the last name on it, which would put the turtle inside W. Va. C.S.R. § 58-73-1.1, prohibiting possession of any native West Virginia reptile the rule does not list. Read the other way, as an introduced animal rather than a native one, it is an exotic reptile and W. Va. Code § 20-2-13 bars possessing live wildlife from without the state without an importation permit. Banned on one reading, permit on the other, and no West Virginia text separates native from established.

### Colorado's 2026 Overhaul, and the Deadline That Has Passed

<FunFact>
This one moved fast, and the window has already closed. Colorado Parks and Wildlife approved a full herpetofauna rule overhaul (Chapter W-7) in March 2026, effective May 1, 2026, which formally classifies red-eared sliders as an "Invasive Herptile" for the first time. Anyone may now take unlimited red-eared sliders from the wild, and wild-caught invasive herptiles must normally be euthanized, except sliders, which may be kept alive for the animal's life. If you already owned a red-eared slider before the rule took effect, you had to file a CPW Legacy Report by July 30, 2026 to keep it legally. That date has now passed: the filing window closed on July 30, 2026, and CPW has published no extension, so an owner who never filed should contact CPW directly instead of assuming the legacy route is still open.
</FunFact>

### Connecticut and North Carolina

Connecticut bans importing, buying, selling, or exchanging red-eared sliders outright, and separately prohibits releasing them into state waters, under Conn. Gen. Stat. Ann. § 26-78. North Carolina's rule is broader still, prohibiting import, transport, purchase, sale, and possession, with permits reserved for research and education. A grandfather clause lets owners who already had a slider before August 1, 2018 keep, transport, or export it.

### Vermont's New Law

Vermont's Act 47 (2025) prohibits importing, possessing, or selling a red-eared slider unless it was legally acquired as a pet before July 1, 2025, or is held under a commercial pet-dealer permit. A separate provision in the same act bans intentionally taking any reptile or amphibian from the wild in Vermont starting January 1, 2027.

## Everywhere Else: Generally Legal, But Never Legal to Release

Outside of the states above, red-eared sliders don't require a special permit in most of the country. California, for example, has no slider-specific ban and allows keeping one, though releasing any aquatic animal into state waters is illegal under general law, and wildlife officials note sliders compete with native western pond turtles for food and basking sites. Minnesota treats it as a "regulated invasive species," legal to possess, buy, and sell, but never to release. The pattern holds nationwide: ownership is usually fine, release is not.

## The Takeaway

If you already own a red-eared slider, the law almost never asks you to give it up, but it may ask you to do paperwork, Colorado's 2026 legacy filing being the sharpest recent example. If you're thinking about getting one, check your specific state before you buy, since restrictions here vary more by state than almost any other pet. And wherever you live, never release a pet turtle into a pond or lake; it's illegal nearly everywhere and harmful to native turtle populations.
