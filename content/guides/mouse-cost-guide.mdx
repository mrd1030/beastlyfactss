# How Much Does a Pet Mouse Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A pet mouse is one of the cheapest animals to buy, and one of the easiest to under-budget for, because two facts change the math more than the sticker price does: mice need to live in a group, not alone, and that group has a real housing complexity of its own.

## The Mouse Itself: $5 to $20, Usually Times Two or Three

Most fancy mice run $5 to $10 from a breeder and $10 to $20 at a larger pet-store chain, with adoption or rescue often landing at $0 to $10. That's cheaper than a dwarf hamster and in the same ballpark as a gerbil, but the number worth planning around is the group, not the individual. Mice are social animals, and the [RSPCA](https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/rodents/mice/company) is direct about solitary housing being stressful enough to reserve for cases where a vet has specifically advised it.

Sex is the detail that actually changes the shopping list. In the RSPCA's own words, single-sex groups of females "usually get on well together," while intact adult males housed together are prone to real fighting, especially once they can smell an unfamiliar male or a female nearby. That doesn't rule males out. Littermate groups established before weaning and never added to afterward do fine, and desexing is a genuine option for males kept together, but it does make a male group a more deliberate project than a female one. It's a meaningfully bigger factor here than it is for the closely related [rat](/guides/rat/), where this kind of same-sex grouping caution is less central.

Add those two correctly sized items to the rest of the list and a proper first setup runs roughly $100 to $210 for one mouse's worth of animal cost, plus another $5 to $40 for the second or third mouse the group actually needs.

Bedding is the line item that moves the most, and it's tied directly to odor. Male mouse urine carries a distinctly strong smell, and letting a cage go too long between cleans lets ammonia build up in a way that's a genuine respiratory risk, not just unpleasant. Other published estimates for mouse ownership run as high as $45 a month depending on group size and how much fresh produce you offer.

## Vet Costs

A routine exam at an exotics-experienced practice typically runs $35 to $75, though it's worth confirming ahead of time that a given vet actually sees mice, not every general practice does. Treating an actual illness costs more, and given how quickly a small animal like a mouse can decline, that's not a cost worth deferring on. One widely cited mouse-cost guide notes that many owners skip routine vet visits for mice entirely given the cost relative to the animal's value, a real pattern worth naming honestly, even though it isn't the same as saying it's the right call.

## Lifespan: Short, Even Next to a Rat

Pet fancy mice typically live 1.5 to 2 years. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/rodents/mice-and-rats-as-pets) puts the average pet mouse lifespan at 18 to 24 months, and mice kept under ideal, calorie-controlled conditions have occasionally lived to around 5 years, though that's a real outlier, not something to plan a budget around.

<FunFact>
A mouse's lifespan is genuinely short even next to its closest relative, the rat. Pet fancy rats commonly live 2 to 3 years, while pet fancy mice average 18 to 24 months, meaning a mouse's whole life often fits inside the first two years of a rat's. That's less time to spread a setup cost across, but it also means a mouse asks for a shorter total commitment than almost any other small mammal.
</FunFact>

## The Honest Budgeting Takeaway

A pet mouse is arguably the cheapest small mammal to house properly, not because any single item is unusually cheap, but because everything scales down with the animal. A correctly sized cage, a correctly sized wheel, and a correctly sized dish all cost less than the hamster or gerbil equivalent. The catch is that "correctly sized" is doing more work here than it does for almost any other species. A too-wide bar spacing or an oversized wheel isn't a minor mismatch, it's the difference between a secure setup and one a mouse escapes from within a week.
