# Amano Shrimp Feeding: The Hobby's Best Algae Eater, With Real Limits

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Amano shrimp do deserve the reputation as the single best true algae eater in the aquarium hobby, but that reputation gets oversold in a specific, useful-to-know way.

## What "Best Algae Eater in the Hobby" Means

Amano shrimp sit near the top of the algae-eating hierarchy for good reason: they take on hair algae and thread algae readily, and unlike most fish and snails, they'll also pick at black beard algae, a notoriously tough type that keeps a lot of other cleanup crew members away entirely. Soft, filamentous algae is squarely in their wheelhouse.

That's a genuinely strong résumé, and it's earned the species its status as the default recommendation whenever a planted tank starts growing more algae than its owner wants.

## Where the Reputation Oversells It

The gap shows up on flat, tightly adhered algae. Amano shrimp will happily eat beard algae but cannot get at the tougher types like green spot algae, the hard, adhered spots that show up on glass and slow-growing leaves. The trade name "algae shrimp" misleads in the first place: the species cannot survive on a diet of algae alone. Cyanobacteria, often called blue-green algae, isn't a true algae at all, and amano shrimp generally leave it alone, it needs to be treated and corrected like the bacterial bloom it actually is, not grazed away.

None of that makes them a bad choice, it makes them one tool rather than a complete algae-control plan on their own. A single established beard algae outbreak is still more than any one shrimp, or even a full group, can clear by itself.

## When Tank Algae Runs Low, Supplement or They'll Starve

This is the part that trips up new keepers the most. Algae is a starting food supply, not a renewable one, and a hungry group of amano shrimp works through it faster than most people expect, especially in a newer or well-maintained tank that never had much algae to begin with. If the tank is clean, supplement, full stop.

A hungry amano shrimp gives you an honest signal before it starves: it climbs the glass and decor actively searching rather than sitting still and waiting for food to arrive. Once you see that behavior, or the tank's visible algae is clearly thinning, add a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00025JZAY?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Tropical Sinking Wafers for Catfish, Loaches and Bottom Feeders, 3.88 oz">sinking wafer</AffiliateLink> or blanched zucchini, spinach, or cucumber.

## Feeding Frequency and the "Stay Hungry" Rule

Feed lightly and only every couple of days rather than heavily every day. Overfeeding supplemental food is its own mistake here: shrimp that are getting plenty of easy wafers and pellets stop bothering with algae, which defeats the point of keeping them in the first place. Offer a small amount, remove anything left after a few hours, and let the shrimp stay motivated to keep working the tank.

<FunFact>
After molting, an amano shrimp will turn around and eat its own just-shed exoskeleton over the next day or two, recovering the calcium and other minerals in it before it has a chance to decay. It is an efficient piece of recycling: the old shell is packed with exactly the minerals the shrimp needs to harden its new one. It's also a good reason not to remove a fresh molt from the tank the moment you spot it, give it a couple of days first.
</FunFact>

## Foods and Additives to Avoid

Avoid any food, medication, or fertilizer that contains copper in any form, since it's [toxic to invertebrates even in amounts too small to bother fish](/blog/amano-shrimp-health-issues-guide/). Beyond that, skip anything oversalted or heavily processed, a varied rotation of algae, wafers, and blanched vegetables covers what an amano shrimp actually needs.
