# Gerbil Enrichment: Deep Bedding Alone Does Not Stop the Digging

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Almost every gerbil care sheet tells you to provide deep bedding. The research says that is roughly half the answer.

<KeyTakeaway>
Gerbils raised with an artificial burrow, meaning **a tunnel with a chamber connected to it**, do not develop stereotypic corner digging. Gerbils offered sand to dig in still do. And cage size made no difference to the behavior at all, which rules out the explanation most keepers reach for first.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Says

Christoph Wiedenmayer studied stereotypic digging in gerbils at the University of Zurich and published the result in *Animal Behaviour* in 1997.

The findings, in order of how much they should change your setup:

**Cage size did not affect the digging.** Bigger enclosure, same behavior. Whatever is causing it, it is not simply confinement.

**The burrow is the goal.** Wild gerbils dig burrow systems of chambers connected by tunnels, and Wiedenmayer's reading is that digging is directed at producing that structure. In a cage where digging can never produce one, the behavior runs without ever completing.

**Tunnel-and-chamber dens prevented it. Sand did not, or not nearly as well.** Gerbils raised with an artificial burrow available did not develop the stereotypy. Offering a substrate to dig in was much less effective.

Later work on laboratory gerbil housing has reinforced the same direction, with opaque tubing assessed against stereotypies and refinement guidance recommending burrow structures over deep bedding alone.

<FunFact>
This is one of the cleanest examples in animal welfare of a stereotypy being explained and not just described. The behavior is not random damage from captivity, it is a normal motor pattern with no way to reach its endpoint, running on repeat.
</FunFact>

## Depth, Still

None of the above replaces bedding depth. Gerbils excavate, and a shallow layer with a tunnel sitting on top is not a burrow either.

Deep enough that a gerbil can dig down into it and have the tunnel hold, which for most setups means considerably more than a bag's worth. This is the most common corner cut in gerbil keeping and it is also the cheapest thing to fix.

## Chewing, Constantly

Open-rooted incisors that grow for life, and a gerbil with nothing to chew will chew the enclosure. <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0G41RJPZJ?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Rabbit Chew Toys, Toys for Guinea Pigs and Hamsters, Natural Chew Sticks and Balls Set">Natural chew sticks</AffiliateLink> plus cardboard, untreated wood and plain paper give both the dental outlet and something to shred.

Shredding material is nest building as much as chewing, and it is worth providing generously, never as a token.

## A Pair, Not a Single

Social animals. Same-sex pairs or small groups, ideally littermates or animals introduced young, since introducing unfamiliar adult gerbils is genuinely difficult and can go badly.

A solitary gerbil is a poorer setup and not a simpler one.

## Sand for Bathing, Which Is Different From Sand for Digging

Worth separating, because the study makes the distinction matter. A sand bath is for coat condition and is genuinely enriching. It is **not** the burrow substitute the digging research was testing against.

Offer it as a dish or house for short periods instead of leaving it in permanently, since it will end up scattered and soiled.

## Space to Run

Floor area and levels. Gerbils are active and use height when it is given to them, and a wheel with a solid running surface is a reasonable addition, since an open-rung wheel risks tail and limb injury.

Space is not the fix for stereotypic digging, per the research, and it is still worth having on its own terms.

## Priority Order

1. A tunnel connected to a chamber, buried in the substrate
2. Bedding deep enough to dig into and hold shape
3. A same-sex pair or small group
4. Constant chewing and shredding material
5. A sand bath, offered in sessions
6. Floor area, levels, and a solid-surface wheel
7. Rearranging and novelty, last

## What Not to Do

Do not assume deep bedding alone prevents stereotypic digging, since the study tested exactly that and it underperformed a burrow. Do not buy a bigger cage expecting the corner digging to stop, because cage size made no difference. Do not keep a gerbil alone. Do not use an open-rung wheel. Do not use cotton wool or fluffy commercial nesting fiber, which is an entanglement risk.
