# How to Handle a Betta Fish (Hint: You Mostly Don't)

Betta fish are not a hands-on pet in the way a guinea pig or a bearded dragon is. The honest answer to how you handle a betta is almost always: you don't, at least not with bare hands, and understanding why protects the fish more than any technique would.

## Why Bare Hands Are Off Limits

A betta's protective slime coat is a genuine barrier against infection and parasites, and bare hands strip it away on contact. Every time a betta gets touched directly, that protection takes a hit. Move the fish with a net or a cup, never scoop it up by hand.

## Bringing a New Betta Home

A betta usually comes home in the small cup or bag it was sold in, and bettas are built for that trip better than most fish. They are routinely shipped in small bags holding about 90% air and a little water, and a 2023 study by Kajimura and colleagues found them unusually tolerant of ammonia and nitrite, possibly a result of generations of that shipping. Tolerance is not immunity, and the cup water still stays out of the tank: float the sealed cup or bag for 20 to 30 minutes until the temperatures meet, then net the fish out. A gap of more than 10°F between the two waters, or a very different pH or hardness, calls for [slow drip mixing instead](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/). Add only one fish at a time, ideally through a separate [quarantine tank for a set number of weeks](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) first, and keep an eye on water parameters for a day or two after any addition.

<FunFact>
A betta's slime coat isn't just a passive layer, it's actively working against infection and parasites around the clock. That's exactly why a technique that seems harmless, briefly cupping a fish in bare hands, does real damage even when nothing looks wrong afterward.
</FunFact>

## What "Handling" Looks Like Day to Day

It comes down to tank maintenance and observation:

- Net or cup transfers only, for water changes or moving to a new tank
- Avoid tapping the glass, it startles the fish and adds stress with no benefit
- Keep the tank away from constant foot traffic or vibration if you can

## Other Fish in the Tank

The fighting fish name is earned between males. A male betta lives alone or with tankmates that are peaceful, short-finned, and not interested in his fins: corydoras catfish, mystery snails, ghost shrimp, and some small tetras or rasboras. Never a second male, and never known fin-nippers like tiger barbs, which will shred the long fins that make a betta worth buying. Females can be kept together in a group called a sorority, and that group needs a tank of at least 10 gallons with cover.

Peaceful tankmates also need a bigger tank than a lone betta. Plan on at least 10 to 20 gallons for peaceful nano fish, and a group of six standard corydoras needs a 20-gallon long on its own count. Ghost shrimp suit a betta tank only at 10 gallons or more, with plenty of hiding places and shrimp that have passed about 1.5 inches, because smaller or juvenile shrimp get eaten. A fast school such as zebra danios stresses a slow, long-finned betta even without real aggression.

When a tankmate goes in, the betta's reaction is the thing to watch. Flared gill covers and spread fins aimed at a newcomer are a display. A display that turns into chasing and torn fins, on either fish, means the pairing has failed and one of them needs another home.

## Males, Females and Bubble Nests

Males and females only become distinguishable at about 3 to 4 months old. Breeders rear young bettas together until then, and the males must be separated at that point, because a male spreads his fins and flares his gill covers almost at once when he meets another male.

A bubble nest is a raft of bubbles a male builds at the surface. It lets eggs develop in water that is warm, low in pH and low in dissolved oxygen, relatively free of predators. A male will build one with no female in the tank, and he keeps adding bubbles when the nest needs them, so a nest on its own is not a sign of breeding and not a reason to worry.

Breeding is a managed routine, not something that happens by accident in a pet tank. A single male and female go into a container with floating vegetation or a small piece of foam where he builds the nest. Spawning typically takes 1 to 3 days, with about 300 eggs released per spawning. The male wraps his body around the female, fertilizes the eggs as she releases them, catches them in his mouth as they fall and places them in the nest, then tends them. The female is removed when spawning is finished. The fry are removed to their own tank once they have absorbed their yolk sacs, about 3 days after hatching, and start on infusoria and brine shrimp nauplii.

## Reading Your Betta Instead of Touching It

Since you're not going to be handling this fish, learning to read its condition from a distance is the real skill here.

**Stress signs:** lethargy, fins clamped tightly against the body, faded color, loss of appetite, hiding, and rapid gill movement. Almost all of these trace back to poor water quality or a temperature that's drifted out of range, not to anything you did or didn't do by hand.

**A healthy betta** shows good, vibrant color, intact and flowing fins, normal swimming activity, and eager interest in food.

## The Takeaway

If you're looking for a pet you can pick up and interact with physically, a betta isn't going to give you that. It's a different kind of pet. The real relationship with a betta is built through good water quality and careful observation, not touch.
