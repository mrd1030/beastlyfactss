# How to Handle a Red-Footed Tortoise

Red-footed tortoises are food-motivated and often more personable than drier-climate tortoises, but the same general handling caution applies here as with any tortoise: this is an observation pet, not a lap pet.

## What to Expect

Many keepers describe red-footed tortoises as more food-motivated and personable than a Russian or sulcata tortoise, readily approaching for a meal and appearing to recognize their keeper over time. That food-motivated reputation is genuine, but it doesn't change the basic rule for tortoises generally: frequent, purposeless handling causes real stress. Treat physical interaction as occasional and purposeful, feeding time, a health check, moving the tortoise for cleaning, and never routine.

## General Handling Caution: Shell and Limb Injury Risk

This applies to any tortoise, and it's worth taking seriously with a species that can reach a substantial adult size. Shell trauma from being dropped is one of the most commonly documented causes of shell injury in pet tortoises, alongside dog bites and blunt trauma from other animals, according to [veterinary guidance on managing tortoise shell injuries](https://www.vettimes.com/clinical/exotics/managing-tortoise-shell-injuries-cpdreptiles). The shell's dermal bone is sensitive tissue, a fracture causes real pain, and healing is slow even with treatment, basic granulation can take around 12 weeks and full bone regeneration can run one to two years. When you do pick a red-footed tortoise up, keep it low to the ground, support the shell fully from underneath with both hands, and never flip it onto its back. Adults can weigh up to 20 pounds, well short of a sulcata's bulk, but heavy enough that a drop is a real, not theoretical, risk.

<FunFact>
Male red-footed tortoises make a clucking sound during courtship, genuinely similar to a chicken, one of the more unexpected behaviors documented in a group of animals most people assume are silent.
</FunFact>

## Growth and the Decades-Long Housing Commitment

Hatchlings emerge at just 1.5 to 2 inches and 35 to 50 grams, small enough to sit in a spoon. Growth from there is steady, never explosive: roughly 3 to 4 inches by the first birthday, 7 to 9 inches and several pounds by age 2, and 9 to 11 inches by age 5, according to a [growth chart compiled by Hepper](https://articles.hepper.com/red-footed-tortoise-size-weight-growth-chart/). Most individuals reach close to full adult size, 11 to 16 inches, within 5 to 10 years, continuing to fill out gradually for a while after that. Combined with a lifespan commonly cited at 50 to 70 years or more, sometimes reportedly approaching 90, that means the enclosure you build should be sized for the adult this tortoise will become, not the hatchling in front of you today.

## Substrate and Why It Matters Here

This is a species that spends much of its time rooting through substrate, pushing through leaf litter, and digging shallow scrapes to rest in, and you'll be observing and interacting with it in that environment constantly. A red-footed tortoise comes from humid tropical forest and forest edge, unlike the arid steppe a Russian tortoise calls home or the desert fringe a sulcata does, and a dry, sandy, arid-style substrate is a genuine mismatch for it. Beyond the direct health risk, the wrong substrate flattens out the natural digging and foraging behavior that makes this tortoise rewarding to watch in the first place.

## How This Compares to Russian and Sulcata Tortoises

Handling difficulty here sits exactly where size does, between the Russian tortoise and the sulcata. A Russian tortoise stays light enough that a fall is dangerous but rarely catastrophic, see our [Russian Tortoise handling guide](/blog/russian-tortoise-handling-guide/) for that smaller-scale version of the same caution. A sulcata becomes genuinely too heavy to lift within its first several years, our [Sulcata Tortoise handling guide](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-handling-guide/) covers what that transition looks like. A red-footed tortoise stays liftable with two hands for its entire life, but its adult weight, up to 20 pounds, still demands the same low-to-the-ground, two-handed support that matters for any tortoise.
