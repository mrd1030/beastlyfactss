# Feather Dust, Air Quality, and Bird Fancier's Lung

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most bird care advice on this site is about keeping the bird healthy. This one runs the other direction. Cockatoos, African grey parrots, and cockatiels all produce a fine, continuous keratin dust as a normal part of how their feathers work, and in a closed-up room that dust settles on every surface, gets breathed in by everyone living there, and, in a documented but uncommon number of long-term owners, causes a real lung disease in the person, not the bird. It's called bird fancier's lung, and it's the kind of thing almost nobody looks up before bringing one of these species home, because nothing about a healthy, dusty parrot looks like a medical problem.

This article covers both ends of that dust: what it actually is and which birds make it, the human condition it's linked to and why it goes undiagnosed for a long time, the practical steps that cut real exposure without getting rid of the bird, and the fact that the same dust is also how a serious viral disease spreads between birds. If you already have a cockatoo, African grey, or cockatiel, this is worth reading regardless of how long you've had them. If you're considering one, it's worth reading first.

## What Powder Down Actually Is

Most feathers replace themselves once a year in an ordinary molt and otherwise just sit there. Powder down is different. It's a specialized type of feather that grows continuously, and instead of shedding on a yearly cycle, the tips of its barbules break down on an ongoing basis into a fine keratin powder that the bird spreads through its plumage during preening, giving the rest of its feathers a dry, waterproof coating. The Merck Veterinary Manual's guidance for bird owners names African grey parrots and cockatoos specifically as species that "normally produce this substance on their feathers," and its manual for veterinary professionals separately identifies cockatiels and cockatoos as birds that "produce large amounts of powder down."

Practically, that means these three species, cockatoo, African grey, and cockatiel, are shedding a visible white or grey film onto perches, cage bars, nearby furniture, and the air itself every single day, not just during a seasonal molt. A healthy bird producing powder down isn't sick, and a clean-looking layer of dust on a shelf near the cage is completely normal for these species rather than a sign anything is wrong. It's also not something you can stop. The powder is doing real work protecting the bird's feathers, and species that make it are going to keep making it for as long as they're healthy.

<KeyTakeaway>
A budgie, a lovebird, or most finches don't produce anywhere near this volume of dust. If daily feather dust is a genuine concern for your household, it's specifically a cockatoo, African grey, or cockatiel consideration, not a blanket bird-ownership one.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Bird Fancier's Lung: When the Dust Becomes a Human Illness

Here's the part that gets skipped almost everywhere else. Prolonged exposure to avian proteins carried in that dust, and in dried droppings, can trigger a real immune-mediated lung disease in people. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) describes it under the umbrella term hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and states plainly that it's "also called bird fancier's lung," caused by breathing in substances that include "bird droppings, feathers, and animal furs." The NHLBI lists having pet birds in the home, not only working with them professionally, as a factor that "can raise your risk," alongside occupational exposure in people who "breed animals or birds," and notes the condition tends to be diagnosed in people between 50 and 70 years old.

A 2025 case report published in the medical journal Cureus, authored by Louay Kila and colleagues, describes bird fancier's lung as a subtype of hypersensitivity pneumonitis "resulting from the repeated inhalation of avian proteins found in bird droppings, feathers, and serum." It's worth being clear about what that case documents: the patient in it was exposed through pet pigeons, not a parrot, so it's evidence for the same underlying mechanism rather than a claim specific to cockatoos or African greys. The NHLBI's own material is the source that generalizes the risk to pet birds in an ordinary home.

Symptoms, per the NHLBI, include shortness of breath, cough, and a flu-like illness with fever, chills, and muscle or joint pain, with persistent bronchitis and weight loss showing up over a longer course. Diagnosis involves a physical exam, lung function tests, blood tests, and sometimes a precipitin test that checks for antibodies to the specific protein involved. None of that happens in a bird owner's living room. It happens with a doctor, and it usually only happens once someone thinks to ask about exposure in the first place.

<KeyTakeaway>
The single most useful thing in this whole article: if you develop a cough, breathlessness, or a flu-like illness that lingers or keeps coming back, tell your doctor you keep a parrot. The Cureus case report specifically flags how easily this gets missed, describing delayed diagnosis tied to symptoms that overlap with far more common conditions, and both sources agree that reducing the exposure is a real part of how the condition is managed, not just something to try after other treatment fails. This is exactly the detail that never comes up unless the patient volunteers it.
</KeyTakeaway>

Left unaddressed, this isn't a minor inconvenience. The NHLBI states that without proper management, hypersensitivity pneumonitis "can cause permanent lung damage" and progress toward pulmonary hypertension or heart failure. The Cureus case report is more specific about the mechanism behind that damage, noting that "early recognition and intervention" matter because untreated cases can progress to chronic disease "which may result in irreversible pulmonary fibrosis," permanent scarring of lung tissue that doesn't reverse once it's set in. Management in that case combined "reduction of exposure to avian proteins, and the use of corticosteroid therapy," and the patient was specifically "advised to remove the pigeons from his household." That's the pattern worth remembering: a doctor manages the disease, but removing or sharply cutting the exposure is part of the actual treatment, not a separate lifestyle suggestion on the side.

None of this means bird fancier's lung is common, or that most owners of powder-down species should expect to develop it. It means the exposure is real, the mechanism is documented in the peer-reviewed medical literature, and the fix that matters most, cutting the dust down and telling a doctor about the bird, costs nothing and works regardless of how likely any individual owner is to actually develop symptoms.

## The Control Hierarchy: Cutting Exposure Without Giving Up the Bird

None of the following requires rehoming a healthy, well-loved bird. It requires treating the dust the way you'd treat any other airborne particulate you can't eliminate at the source: filter the air, reduce what gets airborne in the first place, and stop redistributing what's already settled.

## The Same Dust Spreads Disease Between Birds, Too

Feather dust isn't only a human air-quality issue. It's also the transmission route for psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD), the viral disease that attacks feather and beak growth in parrots. The Merck Veterinary Manual states that the virus "spreads through direct contact with infected birds and through feather dust, dander, and dried droppings," which is exactly the same dust this article has been talking about the whole time, just moving bird to bird instead of bird to person. That overlap is part of why a HEPA purifier and good general hygiene around a powder-down bird's cage pull double duty: they're cutting down the same airborne material that matters for both risks at once. ## Buy This Information Before the Bird, Not After

This is genuinely more useful before you bring a cockatoo, African grey, or cockatiel home than after. It doesn't change the recommendation that any of these species can be an excellent, long-lived companion bird, and most owners of powder-down species never develop bird fancier's lung. What it does is give you an honest, medically sourced answer to a question that rarely comes up during the excitement of choosing a bird: what does daily life with this species' dust actually look like, and what does it cost to manage well. Our [choosing a pet bird guide](/blog/choosing-a-pet-bird-guide/) is the right starting point for weighing a powder-down species against a lower-dust one like a budgie or lovebird, our [cockatoo cost guide](/blog/cockatoo-cost-guide/) prices out ownership more broadly, and our [African grey parrot health issues guide](/blog/african-grey-parrot-health-issues-guide/) covers the rest of what to watch for medically once the bird is home.

And if you already share your home with one of these species and develop a cough or breathlessness that doesn't behave like an ordinary cold, the single sentence that changes how quickly you get an accurate diagnosis is the one so few owners think to say out loud: tell your doctor you keep a parrot.
