# Bird Dust: What It Is, Which Parrots Make It, and When It's Harmful

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Bird dust is the fine white keratin powder that cockatoos, African grey parrots, and cockatiels shed every day from a special kind of feather called powder down. In a closed-up room it settles on every surface and gets breathed in by everyone living there, and in a documented but uncommon number of long-term owners it causes a lung disease in the person: bird fancier's lung. Few people look it up before bringing one of these species home, because nothing about a healthy, dusty parrot looks like a medical problem.

## What Powder Down Is

Most feathers replace themselves once a year in an ordinary molt and otherwise just sit there. Powder down is different. It's a specialized type of feather that grows continuously, and instead of shedding on a yearly cycle, the tips of its barbules break down on an ongoing basis into a fine keratin powder that the bird spreads through its plumage during preening, giving the rest of its feathers a dry, waterproof coating. African grey parrots and cockatoos normally carry this powder on their feathers, and cockatiels and cockatoos produce it in large amounts.

Practically, these three species shed a visible white or gray film onto perches, cage bars, nearby furniture, and the air itself every day of the year. A healthy bird producing powder down isn't sick, and a layer of dust on a shelf near the cage is normal for these species. It's also not something you can stop: the powder protects the bird's feathers, and a healthy bird keeps making it.

<KeyTakeaway>
A budgie, a lovebird, or most finches don't produce anywhere near this volume of dust. Heavy daily feather dust is a cockatoo, African grey, and cockatiel consideration.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Bird Fancier's Lung: When the Dust Becomes a Human Illness

Prolonged exposure to avian proteins carried in that dust, and in dried droppings, can trigger a real immune-mediated lung disease in people. The disease is hypersensitivity pneumonitis, in this form also called bird fancier's lung, caused by breathing in substances that include bird droppings, feathers, and animal fur. Having pet birds in the home raises the risk, not only working with them professionally or breeding them, and the condition tends to be diagnosed in people between 50 and 70 years old.

A 2025 case report in the medical journal Cureus, by Louay Kila and colleagues, describes bird fancier's lung as a subtype of hypersensitivity pneumonitis "resulting from the repeated inhalation of avian proteins found in bird droppings, feathers, and serum." The patient in that case was exposed through pet pigeons, so it documents the same mechanism through a different bird; the risk itself extends to pet birds in an ordinary home.

Symptoms include shortness of breath, cough, and a flu-like illness with fever, chills, and muscle or joint pain, with persistent bronchitis and weight loss showing up over a longer course. Diagnosis involves a physical exam, lung function tests, blood tests, and sometimes a precipitin test that checks for antibodies to the specific protein involved. None of that happens in a bird owner's living room. It happens with a doctor, and it usually only happens once someone thinks to ask about exposure in the first place.

<KeyTakeaway>
If you develop a cough, breathlessness, or a flu-like illness that lingers or keeps coming back, tell your doctor you keep a parrot. Delayed diagnosis is a recognized problem with this condition, because its symptoms overlap with far more common ones, and reducing the exposure is part of how it is treated. It is a detail that rarely comes up unless the patient volunteers it.
</KeyTakeaway>

Without proper management, hypersensitivity pneumonitis can cause permanent lung damage and progress toward pulmonary hypertension or heart failure. "Early recognition and intervention" matter because untreated cases can progress to chronic disease "which may result in irreversible pulmonary fibrosis," permanent scarring of lung tissue. Management in the pigeon case combined "reduction of exposure to avian proteins, and the use of corticosteroid therapy," and the patient was "advised to remove the pigeons from his household." A doctor manages the disease, and removing or sharply cutting the exposure is part of the treatment.

Bird fancier's lung is uncommon, and most owners of powder-down species never develop it. The exposure is real and the mechanism is documented in the peer-reviewed medical literature, and the two steps that matter most, cutting the dust down and telling a doctor about the bird, cost nothing.

Every step below works with a healthy, well-loved bird still in the home. Treat the dust the way you'd treat any other airborne particulate you can't eliminate at the source: filter the air, reduce what gets airborne in the first place, and stop redistributing what's already settled.

## The Same Dust Spreads Disease Between Birds, Too

Feather dust isn't only a human air-quality issue. It's also the transmission route for psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD), the viral disease that attacks feather and beak growth in parrots. The virus spreads through direct contact with infected birds and through feather dust, dander, and dried droppings, the same dust that reaches people, moving from bird to bird. That overlap is part of why a HEPA purifier and good general hygiene around a powder-down bird's cage pull double duty: they're cutting down the same airborne material that matters for both risks at once. PBFD's signs, from stunted feathers to a flaky beak, are part of the wider picture of [feather loss in pet birds](/blog/bird-feather-loss-and-molt-guide/), and cockatoos and African greys carry the most risk.

Airborne danger also runs the other way: fumes and chemicals in the same room can harm the bird itself, one of the [household hazards for pet birds](/blog/bird-household-hazards-guide/) to plan for when setting up a powder-down species' room.

## Before a Powder-Down Parrot Comes Home

A cockatoo, African grey, or cockatiel can be an excellent, long-lived companion, and most of their owners never develop bird fancier's lung. The dust is part of the bird, so the time to plan for it is before one arrives, especially in a household where someone already manages asthma, allergies, or another lung condition, and the [choosing a pet bird guide](/blog/choosing-a-pet-bird-guide/) weighs these species against lower-dust ones like a budgie or lovebird. Once the bird is home, a purifier, a damp cloth, and regular baths keep the load down, and a lingering cough is the moment to tell your doctor about the parrot.
