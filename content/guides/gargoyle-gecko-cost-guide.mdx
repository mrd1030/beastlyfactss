# How Much Does a Gargoyle Gecko Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Gargoyle geckos sit at an interesting price point: affordable for a common reticulated animal, but morphs and proven breeding lines can push the number into four figures.

## The Animal Itself: %%animal:gargoyle-gecko%%

Common reticulated or striped animals start as low as $50, with most typical pet-trade geckos landing in the $100 to $300 range. Color and pattern are the real price drivers, not sex or personality. Reticulated gray, black, and white, the original wild-type look, stays cheapest. Striped, red-striped, and orange-striped animals run higher, and saturated "super red" or selectively bred white and black lines can reach $500 to $1,000 or more, especially from proven breeding lines.

Every gargoyle on the market should be captive-bred. Once among the rarest lizards in captivity, the species is now bred in large numbers and is standard fare in the pet trade, so an animal offered as wild-caught, or with no breeder history at all, is a reason to walk away. The health checks worth making before paying for any reptile are in the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/).

Every row before the vet exam adds up to $335 to $630, and the first exam brings it to %%setup:gargoyle-gecko%%. Add a common reticulated or striped gecko and a typical first setup runs roughly $435 to $1,080; a premium morph instead pushes it to roughly $885 to $1,780. A T5 tube's wattage is not its length: a 24-watt tube is about 22 inches long, a 39-watt tube about 34 inches and a 54-watt tube about 46 inches, so go by the length in inches when you match it to two-thirds of the enclosure. Some keepers swap the coconut fiber and loose decor for a bioactive starter kit, which bundles substrate and hardscape together. The choice changes the running cost: loose natural substrate without a clean-up crew is spot cleaned daily and replaced monthly, while a bioactive build keeps its substrate and lets springtails and isopods break the waste down. Most of the hard gear only needs replacing once every few years. The running cost stays below a desert species' for one reason: the [basking spot this one wants is around 82 to 85°F](/blog/gargoyle-gecko-tank-setup-guide/), so the bulb doing the work is a low-wattage one.

Diet is the biggest steady cost on that list. One 8-ounce bag of powdered gecko diet lasts a single adult about two months and runs $20 to $30, so six bags a year, plus occasional live insects and calcium and D3 top-ups once the first supply runs out. A complete powdered diet already carries its vitamins, so a gargoyle on one needs no separate multivitamin. Most keepers land in the $15 to $30 monthly range for food and supplements, with two replacement UVB tubes a year, about $40 to $45, and vet visits showing up as periodic costs rather than every-month ones. Electricity for a low-wattage heat bulb on a thermostat and a UVB tube on a timer adds about $25 to $45 a year.

<FunFact>
Gargoyle geckos commonly live 15 to 20 years, with some keepers reporting animals still breeding well past 20. That's double the commitment many people associate with a small pet lizard, and it's worth weighing as much as the sticker price when deciding between a $50 gecko and a $1,000 one.
</FunFact>

## Vet Costs

A routine exotic exam commonly runs $50 to $150. A common-illness visit with diagnostics runs $150 to $600, and advanced care, imaging or surgery, can reach $800 to $2,500 or more. There's no gargoyle-specific fee data out there, these are general exotic reptile vet pricing benchmarks.

## The Long-Game Cost

15 to 20 years is typical for this species, with some individuals still breeding past 20. That's the real cost-of-ownership number to sit with, this isn't a short-term pet, and the setup you buy now should be one you're comfortable maintaining for decades.
