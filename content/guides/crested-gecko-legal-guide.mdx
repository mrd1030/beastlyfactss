# Is It Legal to Own a Crested Gecko? State-by-State Guide

The crested gecko was thought extinct until it turned up again in New Caledonia in 1994, which is the single most useful fact for understanding its legal position. It arrived in the pet trade decades after most state exotic-animal lists were written, so the lists that restrict it rarely mention it by name. They reach it through a family, an infraorder, or a rule about what happens to an animal nobody wrote down.

## The Massachusetts Rule Catches This Gecko and Not Its Neighbors

Massachusetts exempts "All Geckos (Gekkonidae)" from its permit requirement, which reads like a clean pass until the sentence finishes. The clearance is expressly subject to 321 CMR 9.01(3), and that subsection pulls back anything categorically non-exempt, which includes species carrying a rare conservation assessment.

The crested gecko is assessed as vulnerable by the IUCN. That is enough to put it back inside the permit requirement. MassWildlife's own plain-language summary of the rule phrases the exemption as all geckos except rare species and the Big Bend gecko, which is the same reading arrived at from the other direction.

This is the detail worth carrying away, because it splits a group that keepers usually treat as interchangeable. A [leopard gecko](/blog/leopard-gecko-legal-guide/) is assessed least concern and walks through the same exemption untouched. Two geckos on the same shelf in the same shop, one needing a permit in Massachusetts and one not, on the strength of an international conservation rating neither shop nor keeper is likely to check.

## Hawaii Bans by Silence

Hawaii runs its rule inverted. Under § 4-71-6.5, introduction of a non-domestic animal is permitted only for animals appearing on the conditionally approved list or the restricted list. Anything on neither is barred, and the absence is the prohibition rather than a gap in the drafting.

The crested gecko appears on no Hawaii list, under its current name Correlophus ciliatus or under the older Rhacodactylus ciliatus that most older sources use. Its relative Rhacodactylus leachianus is on the restricted list, which shows the geckos were worked through individually and this one simply was not reached. The likely reason is chronological: when the lists were drawn, this species was believed extinct and had no trade to regulate.

The practical effect is the same as a named ban. There is no route for a private keeper.

## New Jersey Has a Taxonomy Problem That Changes Nothing

New Jersey's permit list names "Geckos - Family Gekkonidae other than Tokay Gecko". Under current taxonomy the crested gecko sits in Diplodactylidae, not Gekkonidae, so whether that entry reaches it depends on reading Gekkonidae in the older broad sense that covered all geckos.

The answer comes out the same either way. If the entry reaches the species, the permit requirement comes from 7:25-4.3. If it does not, there is no clearance elsewhere in the chapter that would let the animal through without one. Keepers occasionally raise the family split hoping it is a loophole. It is not.

## West Virginia Regulates the Shipment, Not the Species

West Virginia never names the crested gecko, and no species list reaches it. A crested gecko is a reptile and a New Caledonian species, so it is wildlife from without the state on any route into West Virginia. That puts it under [W. Va. Code § 20-2-13](/exotic-pet-laws/state/west-virginia/#crested-gecko), which bars bringing live wildlife in from out of state, or keeping it there, without an importation permit from the director. The state's definition of wildlife covers reptiles with no captive-bred exception, so buying from a breeder does not change the answer.

The permit exists on paper only. The Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for it, and no rule puts the section into practice outside aquaculture. If you keep one in West Virginia or are moving there with one, ask the DNR in writing before the animal crosses the state line and keep the reply with its purchase records.
