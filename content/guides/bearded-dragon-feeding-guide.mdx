# Bearded Dragon Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why They Stop Eating

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A bearded dragon's diet changes as it grows up, and not only in amount: what belongs on the plate shifts too. Here's how that shift works, and how to read a dragon that's gone off food.

## Feeding Schedule by Age

- **Hatchlings (under about 1 month):** 2 to 3 times daily, mostly flightless fruit flies and gut-loaded pinhead crickets.
- **1 to 4 months:** Twice daily, insects plus vegetables introduced.
- **Juveniles (roughly 4 to 18 months):** Insects once or twice daily, plus a fresh salad every day (every other day at the least), so the greens habit is in place before the insects taper off.
- **Adults (18+ months):** A salad daily and insects a few times a week, since growth has stopped and metabolism has slowed.

As an ectotherm, a dragon can only digest its food when it's warm enough. Feed only when basking temperatures are in the correct range (the [enclosure setup guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-tank-setup-guide/) has the targets).

## What Bearded Dragons Eat

The ratio flips with age: juveniles eat roughly 80% insects and 20% greens, and adults flip to about 80% greens and 20% insects. That isn't an arbitrary husbandry rule, since wild bearded dragons make the same shift as they grow.

**Insects:** calcium-dusted crickets, dubia roaches, black soldier fly larvae, and silkworms as regular staples. Hornworms are occasional: mostly water, good for hydration or tempting a picky eater, weak as a staple. Mealworms and superworms are an occasional food from about a month old, never daily. Superworms in particular are high in fat, and both have hard exoskeletons that can contribute to tooth wear. Waxworms are treat-only. Insects should be no larger than the space between the dragon's eyes to avoid choking, impaction, and tooth damage. Never feed wild-caught insects or fireflies.

**Gut-loading and dusting matter as much as the insect choice itself.** Gut-load feeder insects on a calcium-rich diet for a day or two before offering them, then dust with <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000UJPHL8?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Zoo Med Reptile Calcium without Vitamin D3, 8 oz">plain calcium powder</AffiliateLink>, near-daily for juveniles and 2 to 3 times a week for adults, with calcium plus D3 a couple of times a week and a multivitamin once or twice a week. Dusting doesn't replace gut-loading: a dragon needs both. UVB lighting is essential too, because without it a dragon can't use the calcium it's getting. **Vegetables and greens** should make up the majority of plant matter: collard greens, mustard greens, turnip greens, dandelion greens, watercress, bell peppers, green beans, and squash are good staples. Carrots are occasional: a pinch for color and variety, not a daily base. Feed spinach, Swiss chard, and beet greens sparingly, because they contain oxalates that bind calcium, and rotate in cabbage and kale carefully too. Skip iceberg lettuce and celery. They aren't toxic, just nutritionally empty. Fruit is a minor treat: aim for 5% of the diet or less, since it's high in sugar, and never let it pass 10%. Larger adult females may occasionally get a pinky mouse, mainly during reproductive cycles, not as routine food.

## Foods to Avoid

Avocado contains persin, a compound with documented toxicity in birds and a range of mammals and no established safety margin in reptiles. Also keep off the menu: rhubarb (especially the leaves), onion, garlic, leeks, chives, and any wild-caught insects. Fireflies in particular are potentially lethal even in small amounts. Dog food, cat food, raw meat, rabbit food, and eggs are all wrong for this species even though they sometimes get suggested online, since none of them is nutritionally balanced for it. Citrus causes digestive irritation, and insects noticeably wider than the space between the dragon's eyes are a real choking and impaction risk.

## Why Bearded Dragons Stop Eating

1. **[Brumation](/blog/bearded-dragon-brumation-guide/).** A seasonal dormancy most captive dragons can enter, typically starting between 1 and 3 years old, commonly lasting 1 to 3 months and most often about 2. Appetite often spikes first, then drops until the dragon mostly rests in its hide.
2. **Shedding.** Makes dragons itchy and uncomfortable, commonly paired with a temporary appetite drop and more lethargy around the shed.
3. **Gravidity.** Female dragons can produce infertile egg clutches without a male present. Many eat voraciously during early follicle development, then go off food substantially in the 1 to 2 weeks before laying.
4. **Stress.** A new environment, an enclosure that's too small, cohabitation with another dragon, or overhandling can suppress appetite.
5. **A basking temperature that's too low.** As an ectotherm, a dragon needs correct heat to produce stomach acid and digestive enzymes. Too cold, and appetite wanes while digestion slows or stalls, which can also contribute to impaction.
6. **Impaction.** A blockage from oversized insects, loose substrate ingestion, or slowed cold-temperature digestion, often paired with reduced or absent stool and straining.
7. **Illness, parasites, or metabolic bone disease.** Usually accompanied by other symptoms like lethargy, abnormal stool, or visible weakness or deformity.
8. **Picky phases.** Dragons can simply go off a food they're bored with. Worth ruling out before assuming illness.

## When to Worry

An adult that skips food for a day or two is normal. An adult refusing food for 1 to 2 weeks outside brumation, a shed or a gravid cycle is the point to call a vet. Babies and juveniles have far less room because they're growing so fast, so don't let one go more than about 2 to 3 days without food.

During confirmed brumation, not eating for 1 to 3 months is typical, most often about 2, but consult a vet if it runs longer than 4 months, the dragon loses 10% or more of its starting body weight, or appetite doesn't come back once it's up and moving around consistently. Regardless of day count, seek care for lethargy, stopped or abnormal stool, a swollen abdomen, visible straining, sunken eyes, unexplained weight loss, hind-limb weakness, or a persistent black-beard stress display. After brumation ends, appetite should return within 2 to 3 weeks. If it doesn't, that's worth a vet visit too.

A cricket is mostly a container for whatever it ate last, which is why [what the feeders eat](/blog/gut-loading-feeder-insects-guide/) matters as much as the dust on them.

## Match the Plate to the Age

The plate follows the age: mostly insects for a growing dragon and mostly greens for an adult, with every feeder gut-loaded and dusted. None of it counts without warm basking temperatures and a working UVB tube overhead. When an adult skips a few days, check for brumation, a shed or an egg cycle before you worry, and call the vet at two weeks, or sooner if other signs come with it.
