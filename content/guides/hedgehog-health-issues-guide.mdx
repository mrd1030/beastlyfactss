# Common Hedgehog Health Issues (And Why Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome Isn't as Common as You've Heard)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Hedgehogs face a mix of genetic, husbandry-related, and age-related conditions, and one of them, a specific neurological disease, is worth understanding clearly since it's often misrepresented in how commonly it actually occurs. For everything else about day-to-day care, see our full [Hedgehog care guide](/guides/hedgehog/).

## Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome (WHS)

A progressive, currently incurable neurodegenerative disease, genetic in origin. It typically begins with hind-limb wobbling or difficulty fully curling into a ball, and progresses to full paralysis, usually over 9 to 15 months.

**How common is it, really?** Older figures suggested roughly 10% of captive hedgehogs are affected, but a more recent 20-year veterinary study across multiple US institutions found a considerably lower confirmed rate.

<FunFact>
The often-repeated figure for Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome is that roughly 10% of captive hedgehogs are affected. A more recent 20-year retrospective study across multiple US veterinary institutions found a considerably lower confirmed rate, closer to 3%, with the average age of onset around 3.3 years, older than earlier estimates suggested. WHS is real and genuinely serious when it happens, but it's less common than the older, more widely repeated number implies.
</FunFact>

Always see a vet for any wobbling or coordination changes, since several other conditions, cold-related sluggishness, minor strokes, and tumors, can look similar and are far more treatable. There's no cure for confirmed WHS, only supportive care, and it can't currently be prevented beyond avoiding breeding from affected lines.

## Obesity and Fatty Liver

From overfeeding, particularly free-choice cat food, combined with insufficient exercise. Watch for a hedgehog that can't fully curl into a ball anymore or struggles to walk or use its wheel.

## Mites

Crusting or flaking skin and quill loss point here, most commonly introduced by a new hedgehog that wasn't properly quarantined. Quarantining any new arrival for at least two weeks is the real prevention. Treatable, but needs a vet, the standard treatment (selamectin) is prescription.

## Ringworm

Scaly patches near the base of the quills. See a vet, and take precautions handling your hedgehog in the meantime, this is zoonotic and can spread to people.

## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, nasal discharge, and lethargy, often triggered by an enclosure that's running too cold. Always see a vet.

## The Temperature Connection Worth Taking Seriously

A cold enclosure doesn't just risk respiratory infection, it risks something more immediately dangerous: hedgehogs kept below roughly 72°F can attempt to hibernate, and this can be fatal.
