# Common Hedgehog Health Issues (And Why Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome Isn't as Common as You've Heard)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Hedgehogs face a mix of genetic, husbandry-related, and age-related conditions, and one of them, a specific neurological disease, is worth understanding clearly since it's often misrepresented in how commonly it occurs.

## Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome (WHS)

A progressive, currently incurable neurodegenerative disease, genetic in origin. It typically begins with hind-limb wobbling or difficulty fully curling into a ball, and progresses to full paralysis, usually over 9 to 15 months.

**How common is it?** Older figures suggested roughly 10% of captive hedgehogs are affected, but a more recent 20-year veterinary study across multiple US institutions found a considerably lower confirmed rate.

<FunFact>
The often-repeated figure for Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome is that roughly 10% of captive hedgehogs are affected. A more recent 20-year retrospective study across multiple US veterinary institutions found a considerably lower confirmed rate, closer to 3%, with the average age of onset around 3.3 years, older than earlier estimates suggested. WHS is real and genuinely serious when it happens, but it's less common than the older, more widely repeated number implies.
</FunFact>

Always see a vet for any wobbling or coordination changes, since several other conditions, cold-related sluggishness, minor strokes, and tumors, can look similar and are far more treatable. There's no cure for confirmed WHS, only supportive care, and it can't currently be prevented beyond avoiding breeding from affected lines.

## Obesity and Fatty Liver

From overfeeding, particularly free-choice cat food, combined with insufficient exercise. Watch for a hedgehog that can't fully curl into a ball anymore or struggles to walk or use its wheel.

Portion size isn't the only diet mistake worth avoiding here. Hedgehogs are lactose intolerant, and a well-meaning saucer of milk sets off [the bloating and diarrhea dairy causes a hedgehog](/blog/hedgehogs-are-lactose-intolerant-despite-what-cartoons-show/).

## Mites

Crusting or flaking skin and quill loss point here, most commonly introduced by a new hedgehog that wasn't properly quarantined. Quarantining any new arrival for at least two weeks is the real prevention. Treatable, but needs a vet, the standard treatment (selamectin) is prescription.

## Ringworm

Scaly patches near the base of the quills. See a vet, and take precautions handling your hedgehog in the meantime, this is zoonotic and can spread to people.

## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, nasal discharge, and lethargy, often triggered by an enclosure that's running too cold. Always see a vet, and line that vet up before anything goes wrong: hedgehogs are exotic patients in most general practices, so ask a clinic whether it treats and anesthetizes them, the same [screening any small mammal owner should do](/blog/small-mammal-vet-visits-and-travel-guide/).

## Cancer, Uterine Disease and the Spay Decision

Cancer turns up in hedgehogs from about three years old, most often in the mouth, stomach or intestines. Its signs are the vague ones, weight loss, a poor appetite and lethargy, which is one more reason an older hedgehog that eats less deserves a vet visit. Squamous cell carcinoma of the mouth is among the most common, showing as a foul smell from the mouth, swelling around it, or weight loss.

Females carry a second, specific risk: uterine disease, including uterine tumors, is reported at a high rate in pet hedgehogs, and vaginal bleeding is the sign to watch for. Spaying (ovariohysterectomy) is the treatment, and it is also the prevention. Hedgehogs live alone, so a spay is almost never needed for contraception. Because uterine disease is so common, vets advise that a preventive spay be strongly considered for a pet female. The operation is the same one done in other mammals, and hedgehogs tend to worry at surgical wounds, so vets close the skin with buried stitches. Raise it at the first checkup, while she is young and healthy. Males housed alone have no equivalent need for castration.

## The Temperature Connection Worth Taking Seriously

A cold enclosure doesn't just risk respiratory infection, it risks something more immediately dangerous: hedgehogs kept below roughly 72°F can attempt to hibernate, and this can be fatal. The [working range is 75 to 85°F inside a wider 72 to 90°F](/blog/hedgehog-tank-setup-guide/), held by a lightless ceramic emitter on a thermostat in nearly every home. This is arguably the single most important husbandry fact for this species, and it's worth treating as a hard requirement rather than a general guideline.
