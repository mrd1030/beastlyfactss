# How to Handle a White's Tree Frog

White's tree frogs are unusually handleable for an amphibian, docile, slow-moving, and known for their calm, almost smiling expression. That said, correct technique matters more here than it does with most pets, because the risk runs in a direction people don't always expect. It's not really about the frog hurting you: it's about you accidentally hurting the frog.

## Why Technique Matters So Much

Amphibian skin is permeable and absorbs whatever it comes into contact with, including oils, lotions, soap residue, and chemicals from your hands. This isn't a minor sensitivity, it's a genuine route for harm, which is why the standard advice here is unusually specific.

## The Correct Technique

Wash your hands thoroughly with plain water only, no soap, immediately before handling, and make sure no residue from lotion, hand sanitizer, or soap remains. Alternatively, wear powder-free nitrile gloves lightly wetted with dechlorinated water. If you've recently used lotion, cleaning chemicals, or had a manicure, gloves are the safer option regardless of how well you've washed. Keep sessions short, roughly 5 to 15 minutes, and don't handle more than about twice a week. Wash your hands again afterward with soap and running water, away from the frog: even a healthy amphibian can carry Salmonella in its gut. Children younger than 5 should not handle the frog or touch its enclosure at all, since they are the most likely to become seriously ill.

<FunFact>
A White's tree frog absorbs moisture and oxygen directly through its skin, the same permeability that makes it so sensitive to lotion and soap residue. Plain dechlorinated water is essentially the only thing that's ever truly safe to touch one with.
</FunFact>

## What to Expect

Most White's tree frogs genuinely tolerate handling well once they're used to it, more so than the great majority of frog and toad species kept as pets. They're sedentary by nature, don't expect a lot of active movement during a session, this is a calm, low-drama handling experience by amphibian standards.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Handling with lotioned or chemical-exposed hands, by far the most common and most damaging mistake
- Handling too frequently, which adds unnecessary stress even to a tolerant species
- Using tap water straight from the faucet on hands or gloves without [dechlorinating](/blog/amphibian-quarantine-and-water-guide/) it first

## Keeping Two, and What Happens If They Breed

More than one White's tree frog can share an enclosure as long as they are close in size, since a large one will eat a much smaller one. A male and female kept together can also breed. Males call through the year, and breeding in captivity follows the wet season the frogs get in the wild: keepers who want eggs trigger it by imitating heavy summer rain. The male grips the female from behind in amplexus, which can last for days, and she lays 150 to 300 eggs in still water. Hatching starts 28 to 36 hours after laying.

The tadpoles are the long part. In good conditions some finish metamorphosis in 2 to 3 weeks, most are out of the water within 2 months, and a few stay tadpoles for up to a year. The froglets then take about two years to reach breeding age. A keeper who wants company for a frog without that project keeps two of the same sex, and since juveniles are hard to sex, that usually means buying adults whose throats show which is which.

## How They Compare to Other Tree Frogs

White's tree frogs are often confused with smaller, slimmer species like American green tree frogs, but they're notably larger, more robust, and considerably hardier. Among tree frogs generally, they stand out as one of the few genuinely suited to occasional, careful handling rather than strict observation-only care.

## The Exception That Proves the Rule

This is worth contrasting directly with the Pacman frog. Pacman frogs are the opposite case entirely, an ambush predator that strikes reflexively at anything finger-shaped and should never be handled by hand, fed only with tongs. If White's tree frog is the exception that proves handleable amphibians exist, [Pacman frog](/blog/pacman-frog-handling-guide/) is the reason the "don't handle amphibians" rule exists in the first place. Same broad category of pet, almost opposite handling reality.
