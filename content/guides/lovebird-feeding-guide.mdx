# Lovebird Feeding: Calcium, Vitamin A, and the Hen That Keeps Laying

<VetDisclaimer />

A single female lovebird, with no mate and no encouragement, will often lay clutch after clutch. Each egg costs her calcium twice over, once for the shell and again for the muscle work of passing it, and where that calcium comes from is decided months earlier at the food dish.

## The Proportions

A formulated pellet should be about 75 to 80% of what a lovebird eats, with vegetables and a little fruit filling the remaining 20 to 25%.

The portion is the part most keepers overshoot. Fill the food dish a quarter full once a day and let the bird finish it, and treat a thumbnail-sized piece of any fresh food as a dinner-plate serving for a lovebird.

One consequence of hitting that number: a bird eating that much formulated food does not need a general vitamin supplement stacked on top. The pellets are the supplement. Adding more is not extra insurance, and some nutrients cause problems in excess.

Seed fails for the same reasons it fails in any parrot, short on vitamins, minerals and protein, and made worse by a bird eating selectively from the mix instead of across it.

## The Two This Species Runs Into

For lovebirds, the deficiencies that arrive first are vitamin A and calcium.

Vitamin A deficiency is the quieter of the two and it shows up through the respiratory tract and the skin, in a bird whose diet has been light on the dark leafy greens and orange vegetables that carry the precursors. It also raises the odds of a hen becoming egg bound, which links it straight to the second problem.

Calcium is the one with a clear mechanism, and chronic egg laying is the reason.

## What Laying Costs a Hen

Lone female lovebirds sit in a small group of species, alongside cockatiels, budgerigars and eclectus, that commonly become chronic layers. A hand-raised bird bonded to a person does this more readily than a wild-caught one, so it is a companion bird problem specifically.

Every egg draws down calcium. Some goes into the shell, and some goes to the muscle contractions and nerve signaling needed to move the egg through and out. A hen doing that repeatedly is spending a reserve she has to rebuild between clutches.

On an all-seed diet she cannot rebuild it fast enough. Seed is poor on calcium and heavy on phosphorus, which is the worst combination for the job, and the deficit accumulates instead of resetting.

The result when it goes far enough is a bird that is weak and lethargic, with bones brittle enough to fracture during ordinary handling, prone to egg binding, and in severe cases at risk of seizures and death. That is what the diet is for. Not a glossier bird: whether a hen has the calcium to survive her own laying.

Dark leafy greens are a genuine dietary calcium source and belong in the daily vegetables for that reason. A hen who is actively laying is one of the few clear cases for supplementing calcium directly, which is worth arranging with a vet instead of guessing at.

## Why Dusting Seed Does Nothing

This one catches people who are trying to do the right thing.

A lovebird hulls a seed and eats the kernel inside, discarding the shell. Powder sprinkled over a seed mix rides out with the husks. The bird eats around it entirely, and the keeper reasonably believes the supplement is being taken.

If a powdered supplement is in use, during a pellet conversion for instance, put it on moist food where it will stay put and be eaten.

Grit fails on the same logic. A bird that shells its own seed has no grinding job for grit to do, and eaten in quantity it can cause a serious gastrointestinal obstruction.

## Foods to Rule Out

Avocado and onion are the flat exclusions and should never be offered to a bird.

Beyond those, the ordinary parrot list applies: chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and salty processed food.

## Water

Fresh, clean water at all times, with the dish washed daily. Lovebirds bathe enthusiastically in whatever water is available, which is good for them and means the drinking dish often needs changing more than once a day.

## Getting There From Seed

If the bird is currently on seed, the conversion is its own procedure with a real failure mode, since a bird that does not recognize pellets as food can decline to eat rather than switch.
