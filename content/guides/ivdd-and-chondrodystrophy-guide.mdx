# IVDD: Why Short Legs and Bad Backs Come From the Same Gene

<VetDisclaimer />

**IVDD stands for intervertebral disc disease.** To make sense of it you need to know what the disc is.

Think of the spine as a string of pearls: bony vertebrae separated by discs. Each disc has a gel-like center called the **nucleus pulposus** sitting inside a tough outer ring of fibrous cartilage called the **annulus fibrosus**. Together they let the spine bend, flex and turn while cushioning the vertebrae against shock. In a young animal the nucleus is up to 88% water.

When a disc degenerates or is damaged it can bulge like a jelly-filled donut, or rupture outright, pressing on the spinal cord or the nerve roots branching off it. The reason this is so serious is anatomical: **the spinal cord runs through a tunnel of bone**, so displaced disc material has nowhere to go except into the cord.

Lifetime prevalence across all dogs is about 3.5%. Roughly 65% of cases involve the thoracolumbar spine, the back, and about 18% the neck alone.

In chondrodystrophic breeds the disc degenerates within the first few months of life, and extrusion can happen as early as one to two years of age with acute, severe signs. In large breeds the same tissue matures slowly instead, and problems appear after about five years as gradual wobbliness and weakness.

<KeyTakeaway>
Worth knowing that this tidy split is being questioned. Recent histopathology finds features of chondroid metaplasia in both breed groups, challenging the traditional Type I versus Type II distinction. There are also newer categories outside Hansen's framework entirely, such as acute non-compressive nucleus pulposus extrusion, which happens during exercise in relatively young dogs with healthy discs.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Gene That Does Both Things

**Chondrodystrophy** means altered endochondral ossification producing shortened long bones: the short-legged, long-backed silhouette. Cornell abbreviates it CDDY. In dogs it traces to a **retrogene**, an extra reinserted copy of a gene, in this case fibroblast growth factor 4.

Dogs carry two FGF4 retrogenes, one on chromosome 18 and one on chromosome 12. The chromosome 12 copy is the consequential one.

That combination of inheritance patterns is the cruel part. One copy gives only modestly shortened legs, but because the disc effect is **dominant**, one copy is enough for the full disc risk. A dog does not have to look like a Dachshund to have a Dachshund's spine.

The effect on the disc itself shows up plainly in calcification rates: 84.8% of dogs with two copies had calcified discs, against 63.8% with one copy and 18.5% with none. Dogs carrying the retrogene also came to surgery roughly 27 to 31 months younger than those without.

<FunFact>
These mutations are ancient. FGF4 has been retrotransposed twice in dogs within the last three to four thousand years, and there are descriptions of short-legged dogs going back over four thousand years. The genetics of the Dachshund's back predate the breed by millennia.
</FunFact>

Carrier frequency is close to fixed in some breeds. For the chromosome 12 retrogene: Beagle 1.00, Dachshund 0.99, French Bulldog 0.94, Pembroke Welsh Corgi 0.92, Basset Hound 0.83. Cornell's affected list also includes Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, Cocker Spaniels, Miniature Poodles and Pekingese.

Disc calcification in Dachshunds peaks between 24 and 27 months of age, which is precisely why the radiographic screening scheme used by breeders images dogs at 24 to 30 months. Dogs scoring 2 or fewer calcified discs are suitable for breeding, 3 to 4 should be bred judiciously, and 5 or more should be excluded.

## Severity, and the One Test That Matters

Clinicians grade thoracolumbar injury on the Modified Frankel Scale, six categories from normal to paraplegic without sensation. Be aware that different authors number it in opposite directions, so the descriptions matter more than the numbers.

**Deep pain perception**, usually written DPP, is the pivotal finding. It is tested by pinching the bone of a toe or the tail and watching for a genuine behavioral response, a bark or a turn of the head. A reflex flexion of the limb must not be mistaken for one. In a paraplegic dog, whether DPP is present is the most important prognostic finding there is.

Speed matters even where the eventual outcome does not. For dogs paraplegic with intact deep pain, mean time to walking again was 15 days after surgery versus 84 days with conservative treatment.

The worst outcome, **progressive myelomalacia**, is a spreading softening of the cord that develops in 5 to 10% of dogs that are paraplegic with lost deep pain. Affected dogs develop flaccid paralysis that ascends until breathing fails. Delay beyond 12 hours is associated with increased risk, which supports decompressing severely injured dogs within 12 to 24 hours. Notably, there is **no** evidence supporting the widely repeated 48-hour cutoff beyond which recovery is supposedly impossible.

## Treatment

Dogs with pain and mild to moderate deficits often recover with two to three weeks of cage rest. A short course of prednisone can relieve pain, but there is no evidence that steroids or any other drug improves neurological recovery.

One safety point deserves emphasis: **giving pain relief without enforcing cage rest is contraindicated**, because a comfortable dog moves more and may extrude more disc material. Signs recur after conservative treatment in 30 to 40% of cases.

Surgery is a ventral slot for the neck, or hemilaminectomy for the back. Prophylactic fenestration of commonly affected disc spaces reduces recurrence: one randomized trial of 207 dogs found 7.5% recurrence with multi-site fenestration against 18% with single-site.

## What the Evidence Does Not Support

This is the section most articles skip, and it matters more than the rest.

**Restricting exercise appears to be backwards.** Dachshunds exercising more than an hour a day had *reduced* odds of IVDD (OR 0.50), while those exercising under 30 minutes had *increased* odds (OR 2.49). One review concludes plainly that the common clinical recommendation to restrict activity in these breeds "might be counter-productive."

**Banning stairs and furniture has no support as prevention.** Dogs not allowed on furniture had higher odds, and dogs doing no daily stairs had higher odds than those doing a full flight. This is very likely reverse causation, owners restricting dogs after a diagnosis, so it is not evidence that stairs protect. But it is not evidence for the ban either.

**Glucosamine and chondroitin showed higher odds of IVDD**, again almost certainly because owners start them after diagnosis. Either way there is no evidence they prevent it.

**Routine physiotherapy has not been shown to improve locomotor outcome** in mild to moderate cases, across two prospective trials.

Two things that *are* supported: **delaying neutering**, particularly in females, where neutered females carried a relative risk of 1.81 and those neutered under 12 months 2.12; and **buying from tested parents**, since genetic testing for the retrogene exists and breeders can plan matings around it.

The blunt summary from VCA is worth quoting directly: there is no way to prevent intervertebral disc disease.
