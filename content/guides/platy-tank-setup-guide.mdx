# Platy Tank Setup: A Real 10 Gallons, Hard Water, and a Plan for Fry

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Platies are genuinely one of the easier fish to house well. A real 10 gallons, water on the harder and more alkaline side than most tropical fish prefer, and a filter that won't shred fry covers most of what this species needs. This covers the full setup, for what it all costs, see our [Platy cost guide](/blog/platy-cost-guide/), or the full [Platy care guide](/guides/platy/) for everything else.

## Tank Size

10 gallons is a realistic starting point for a small group of about five platies. [Seriously Fish lists an absolute minimum footprint of 24x12x12 inches](https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/xiphophorus-maculatus/), which works out to roughly 15 gallons, while [Aquarium Co-Op recommends 10 to 20 gallons](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/platy-care-guide) depending on how many fish you're keeping. Step up to a full 20 gallons for a group of six or more, or if you're letting the group breed, since a growing colony of fry adds to the bioload faster than a small starting group suggests. Go long and rectangular rather than tall, and use a lid, platies are capable jumpers.

## Temperature

Roughly 68 to 82°F works well, with the mid-to-upper 70s°F the most commonly cited target across sources. Stability matters more than hitting an exact number, avoid swings larger than about 2°F in a 24-hour period, since temperature instability is one of the more commonly cited triggers behind the livebearer "shimmy" covered in our [Platy health issues guide](/blog/platy-health-issues-guide/). Variatus-line platies tolerate the cooler end of that range better than most tropical fish, but a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">heater</AffiliateLink> is still the safer default unless your room stays consistently warm year-round.

## Water Chemistry

Platies do best with a pH of roughly 7.0 to 8.2, though sources report tolerance as wide as 6.8 to 8.5. They prefer medium-hard to hard water, roughly 10 to 30 dGH, and genuinely won't thrive in very soft, acidic conditions the way some other tropical fish will.

<FunFact>
Platies evolved in warm springs, ditches, and slow-moving canals across their native range in Mexico and Central America, water that tends to run mineral-rich rather than soft. That history is a big part of why this species tolerates, and even prefers, harder tap water that would need active softening for many other popular aquarium fish.
</FunFact>

If your tap water runs soft, a mineral supplement, crushed coral or a commercial buffering product, can bring it into range without much extra effort.

## Filtration

A gentle sponge filter works well for most platy tanks. It provides enough biological filtration for a modest group while staying safe for fry, an important detail if you're not actively preventing breeding, since a stronger power filter can pull small fry into its intake. For a larger or intentionally non-breeding group, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000260FUW?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="AquaClear 70 Power Filter (40-70 gal)">hang-on-back filter</AffiliateLink> handles a bigger bioload comfortably. Either way, platies don't need the aggressive turnover a larger or messier fish would require.

<AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">A sponge filter</AffiliateLink> is the simpler, fry-safer default for most first-time setups.

## Substrate

Fine gravel or sand both work well. Avoid sharp or coarse gravel, which can damage fins, and darker substrate tends to make platy coloring stand out more in the tank.

## Lighting

A basic LED light on an 8 to 10 hour daily cycle is sufficient. It isn't strictly necessary for the fish's health, but it supports live plants and makes for better viewing.

## The Two Non-Obvious Requirements

Cycle the tank before adding fish. Building the beneficial bacteria colony that processes waste takes 2 to 4 weeks with a fishless cycle, or somewhat less with a bottled bacteria product. Skipping this step is one of the most common beginner mistakes in the hobby generally, and it's a fast route to the ammonia-driven stress that shows up as shimmy and other issues covered in our [Platy health issues guide](/blog/platy-health-issues-guide/).

Always use a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3Rg5gTT" product="Water conditioner">water conditioner</AffiliateLink> on tap water before it goes in the tank, dechlorinating and neutralizing chloramine and heavy metals that are otherwise harmful to fish. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4p1T7yd" product="Water test kit">water test kit</AffiliateLink> lets you confirm the cycle actually finished and catch drift afterward.

Dense live plants aren't just decoration in a platy tank either, they're a real population-control lever. Platy parents don't look after their fry, and adults will eat them given the chance. If you're allowing your platies to breed, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4grZYPq" product="Live terrarium plants">dense live plants</AffiliateLink> give fry somewhere to hide from adults, meaningfully improving survival odds. If you'd rather your population stay naturally controlled, skipping dense cover lets normal predation keep numbers in check instead, the same lever our [Guppy tank setup guide](/blog/guppy-tank-setup-guide/) and [Molly tank setup guide](/blog/molly-tank-setup-guide/) cover for those two close relatives.
