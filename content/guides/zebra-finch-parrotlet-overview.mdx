# Two Small Birds, Two Completely Different Pets

A zebra finch and a parrotlet both turn up on "easy starter bird" lists, both stay small, and both get bought by first-time bird owners for similar reasons. Past that, they don't have much in common. One is a true finch that can't talk and does best left mostly to its own kind. The other is a true parrot, the smallest one commonly kept as a pet, that can learn a few words and bond hard enough to one person that handling actually matters.

## Zebra Finch: A True Finch, Not a Small Parrot

Zebra finches are one of the most popular pet birds in the world. They're a true finch, not a parrot, and their care reflects that, no talking, no hand-taming the way a parrot bonds with an owner, and a strong instinct to live in a flock rather than pair off with a person. Wild zebra finches come from the arid interior of Australia, traveling in flocks of dozens to over a hundred birds while foraging grass seed around waterholes. Males are easy to spot, a barred black-and-white throat, a solid black breast band, chestnut flanks spotted with white, and an orange cheek patch, while females are a plain grey with none of it. They're also the most studied songbird in neuroscience, prized for how precisely males learn their song from a tutor as chicks.

## Parrotlet: The Smallest True Parrot, Biggest Personality

The Pacific parrotlet is the most popular of nine parrotlet species and, at well under six inches, the smallest true parrot commonly kept as a pet, smaller even than a budgie. Size is about the only small thing about it, parrotlets are famously bold and feisty, often described as a large parrot's attitude packed into a tiny body. Unlike the zebra finch, a parrotlet can learn to say a handful of words, and when it's hand-raised and handled consistently, it bonds intensely to one person, though a parrotlet that isn't well socialized can turn sharp and nippy, well out of proportion to its size. Wild parrotlets live in the dry forests and scrubland of coastal Ecuador and Peru, traveling in flocks of 40 to 50 birds, a long way from the one-on-one bond a hand-raised pet parrotlet tends to form instead.

<FunFact>
A zebra finch and a parrotlet are often shopped for in the same breath, small, inexpensive-looking, easy to house, but they're built for opposite kinds of homes. The zebra finch is a genuine flock animal, happiest with its own kind and never really meant to be a one-on-one companion the way a parrot is. The parrotlet is the opposite, a true parrot in a tiny body that can learn a few words and will bond hard to a single person if it's raised that way, nipping included. Get the finch for the sound of a small flock going about its day, get the parrotlet only if a parrot's kind of relationship is actually what you're after.
</FunFact>
