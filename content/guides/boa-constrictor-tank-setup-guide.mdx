# Boa Constrictor Enclosure Setup: Sizing for the Adult It Will Become

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Boa constrictor housing scales up significantly as the snake grows, and building for the adult size from a reasonably early point saves you from repeated upgrades.

<Figure src="/assets/images/boa-constrictor-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a boa constrictor enclosure showing the guarded heat emitter, warm and cool-side hides, and tall vertical space for this semi-arboreal species" caption="Sized for the adult it will become, both floor space and height matter here since boas are semi-arboreal." shareable />

## Enclosure Size

A useful working rule: enclosure length plus width should equal or exceed the snake's length, and height should be at least half the snake's length. In practice, adults typically need somewhere around 6 to 8 feet long, 2 to 3 feet wide, and 3 to 4 feet tall, roughly 10 square feet of floor space at minimum. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H3WT574V?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Reptile Habitats 6x2x2 (180 Gallon) PVC Enclosure V3">6ft+ adult PVC enclosure</AffiliateLink> is a solid way to build for that final size from the start rather than upgrading twice. Boas are semi-arboreal, so unlike a purely ground-dwelling snake, both floor space and height genuinely matter here. House boas individually, never together.

<FunFact>
A boa constrictor's enclosure needs both floor space and height in a way that few other pet snakes do. Because boas are semi-arboreal, the rule of thumb isn't just length plus width matching the snake's length, height has to reach at least half that length too. A purely ground-dwelling snake of the same length can get away with a much shorter enclosure.
</FunFact>

## Temperature

Basking area 88 to 92°F, occasionally up to 95°F, ambient warm side 80 to 85°F, cool side 75 to 80°F, nighttime 70 to 80°F. A heat source is required, and it needs to be genuinely inaccessible to direct contact, no exposed heating elements a snake this size and strength could reach. ## Humidity

60 to 70%, higher during shedding cycles. Achieve this with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eKyN0W" product="Large soak-able water dish">large water bowl</AffiliateLink>, regular misting, moisture-retentive substrate, and a dedicated humid hide. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07MTPKDG5?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Exo Terra LED Thermometer and Hygrometer Bundle">thermometer and hygrometer</AffiliateLink> keeps this confirmed rather than guessed at, since both temperature and humidity in an enclosure this large can vary a lot between the warm end and the cool end.

## Substrate

Coconut husk or fiber, cypress mulch, or a reptile-specific soil mix all work well. A cypress mulch or coconut fiber substrate covers most setups, though a true bioactive setup needs a proper soil-based mix rather than relying on cypress or coco husk alone. Whatever you choose, avoid letting it stay persistently wet, that's a direct path to scale rot.

## Lighting

Not strictly required, but increasingly recommended: a low level of UVB alongside a consistent day and night cycle offers real benefits even though boas have historically been kept without it. A boa is a Ferguson Zone 2 animal, the same partial-sun group as corn snakes and ball pythons in the [UVB zone breakdown](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/), so the target is a basking UV index of 2.0 to 3.0. A 5 or 6% T5 HO tube gets there mounted 10 to 14 inches above the basking spot through a mesh top, or 14 to 18 inches with no mesh in the way, and it is replaced every 12 months.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Two Hides, Not One

Provide a hide on the warm side and a separate hide on the cool side, two snug hides sized to fit a coiled boa work well for this. This lets your boa thermoregulate, moving between temperature zones as needed, while still having secure cover available wherever it settles. A front-opening enclosure with good ventilation is strongly preferred over a top-opening tank, both for your own ease of access with an animal this size and for the snake's air quality.

## Locks and Latches

Most snake escapes come down to an enclosure that was never properly secured, and an adult boa loose in a house is a hard animal to find and a heavy one to recover. A front-opening enclosure needs a lock or latch on its doors, used every time they close. If a young boa starts out in a glass aquarium, the lid needs at least four lid clamps, and more on a larger tank. Never use tape to hold a lid or door shut: snakes that touch the sticky side get injured by it.

This is a noticeably bigger build than a [ball python](/blog/ball-python-tank-setup-guide/) or [hognose snake](/blog/hognose-snake-tank-setup-guide/) needs, worth knowing if you're coming to boas from either of those species.
