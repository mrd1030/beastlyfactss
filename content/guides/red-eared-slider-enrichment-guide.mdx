# Red-Eared Slider Enrichment: Escape Behavior Is the Measurement

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most reptile enrichment studies measure something abstract. This one measured a turtle trying to get out of its tank, which is a signal every slider keeper has already seen.

<KeyTakeaway>
Colored objects placed in the enclosure reduced escape behavior and increased activity in captive pond sliders. They did not work the same way for every individual, which is the part worth carrying: the study says test enrichment on your turtle rather than assume the group result applies to it.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Says

A study of captive pond sliders, *Trachemys scripta*, offered colored objects as enrichment and tracked behavioral budgets. **Escape behavior fell** and **activity levels rose before feeding**. The turtles were **more interested in yellow and red fish-scented objects** than in green or white ones.

The caveat the authors made a point of: the **direction of change in escape behavior differed between individuals**. A group average concealed turtles that responded differently, and they argued explicitly for assessing new enrichment per animal, not only across a group.

There is also a [chelonian enrichment study](/blog/red-footed-tortoise-enrichment-guide/) worth knowing about: Case, Lewbart and Doerr tested an enriched environment in eastern box turtles, which remains one of the few controlled enrichment experiments in the group.

<FunFact>
Color preference in that study was tangled up with scent, since the objects were fish-scented. A turtle going for a yellow object is not necessarily choosing yellow. That kind of confound is normal in a small enrichment study and it is a reason to read the result as a lead and not a rule.
</FunFact>

## Why Color Works on a Slider

The colored-object result fits the animal. Sliders hunt by sight, and their eyes register wavelengths from ultraviolet through red, a wider band than human eyes cover. Their own communication is visual and tactile too: eye rolling, displays, vibrations and touch, including the male's courtship display of fluttering his long foreclaws near a female's eyes. An animal built around color and movement is the one you would expect to respond to a red or yellow object that smells of fish.

## The Tank Is the Baseline, Not the Enrichment

None of the objects below do anything for a turtle whose basic housing is short. Wild sliders spend a large share of the day basking on rocks, logs, banks and floating debris, so a platform they can climb onto, dry off on and leave at will is where the day's thermal choices happen. If the turtle is working the front glass constantly, check the tank against those basics before you buy a colored object.

## Feeding as Work, Matched to Age

Sliders forage opportunistically, in the water and on land, and what they forage for changes as they grow. Juveniles are mostly carnivorous, taking insects and other small prey. Adults shift to omnivory, with gut bacteria that let them digest plants, and eat mostly aquatic plants in the wild.

So the enrichment follows the age. For a juvenile, the hunt is the point: live insects or small feeder prey scattered through the water. For an adult, make grazing take time: floating aquatic plants and greens left to be picked at, food sunk under structure or frozen into ice blocks that release slowly. <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00OV0WIPA?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Mazuri Nutritionally Complete Aquatic Turtle Food, Freshwater Formula, 12 oz">Aquatic turtle pellets</AffiliateLink> are a reasonable base at any age, but a meal that lasts five seconds is not enrichment.

## Novel Objects, Tested Per Turtle

This is where the study lands most directly. Introduce objects, watch what your animal actually does, and drop the ones it ignores. Yellow and red are the reasonable starting colors based on the data, and scent seems to be doing part of the work.

Then check the escape behavior. That is the outcome the researchers moved, and it is one you can see from across the room.

## Priority Order

1. A tank that meets the basics: adult water volume, depth with structure, filtration, a dry basking platform
2. Foraging matched to age: live prey for juveniles, plants to graze for adults
3. Novel objects in red and yellow, assessed on your individual turtle
4. Rearranging the layout, occasionally

## What Not to Do

Do not house an adult in the tank you bought for the hatchling, which is the single failure this species is defined by. Do not treat constant glass-working as a personality trait. Do not run a basking site that never gets properly dry. Do not skip UVB for a species whose shell depends on it. Do not release an unwanted slider outdoors, since they are among the most damaging invasive reptiles worldwide.
