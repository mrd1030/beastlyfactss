# Bearded Dragon Safe Foods List: Staple, Occasional, and Never Foods

A bearded dragon's plate is four separate categories stacked on top of each other: feeder insects, leafy greens and vegetables, fruit, and a short but important never-feed list. Getting the ratio right matters as much as picking safe items, since even a "safe" food fed as the daily base instead of an occasional extra can throw off calcium balance over months. This covers what belongs in the salad bowl and the feeder tub, tier by tier, with the reasoning behind each call.

## Why the Tiers Exist

Every food on this list gets sorted by two things: how much calcium it has relative to phosphorus, and whether it carries a compound that interferes with calcium absorption or is outright toxic. Bearded dragons need a diet where calcium consistently outweighs phosphorus, because a reptile that's short on usable calcium is at risk of metabolic bone disease, a serious and largely preventable condition covered in our [health issues guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-health-issues-guide/). Two compounds show up repeatedly in the "occasional" and "rare" reasoning:

- **Oxalates** bind calcium in the gut, making it unavailable to the dragon even if the food itself is calcium-rich. Spinach and beet greens are the classic example, high in calcium on paper, but the oxalate cancels much of it out.
- **Goitrogens**, found in cruciferous vegetables like kale, cabbage, and broccoli, can interfere with thyroid function in large regular amounts. An occasional serving isn't a problem; a daily one is.

Staples are built around the foods that dodge both issues. Everything else gets layered in for variety without becoming the backbone of the diet.

The feeding schedule by age, and why a dragon might refuse food altogether, is in the [Bearded Dragon feeding guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-feeding-guide/). Everything else is in the complete [Bearded Dragon care guide](/guides/bearded-dragon/).

## Feeder Insects

Feeder insects should always be captive-bred, never wild-caught, and never wider than the space between your dragon's eyes. That sizing rule matters more than which species you pick: an oversized insect is a choking and impaction risk regardless of tier, and in juveniles undersized prey isn't just about digestion, hind-leg weakness has been linked to oversized feeders in young dragons still building bone density.

### Gut-Loading and Dusting Aren't Interchangeable

Gut-load feeder insects on a calcium-rich diet, leafy greens, carrot, squash, or a commercial gut-load, for 24 to 48 hours before offering them (NC State's veterinary hospital gives a wider window, up to 72 hours). What the insect last ate is what your dragon actually absorbs, so a well-fed cricket and a starved one aren't nutritionally equal even if they're the same size. Our [gut-loading feeder insects guide](/blog/gut-loading-feeder-insects-guide/) covers exactly what to feed feeders and for how long.

Dusting is a separate step on top of gut-loading, not a substitute for it. Plain calcium powder (no D3, phosphorus-free) is the everyday dust, applied at most feedings for juveniles and a few times a week for adults. Calcium with added D3 swaps in a couple times a week as a backup to UVB, never at every feeding. Under correct UVB lighting a dragon makes its own vitamin D3, and stacking supplemental D3 on top of that risks vitamin D toxicity, which calcifies soft tissue and is harder to treat than a calcium shortfall would have been. UVB strength and distance are the other half of that equation, and the [complete UVB lighting guide](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) sets both by bulb type.

Check the label on any calcium or gut-load product before buying. A plain calcium carbonate powder with no added vitamins is what you want for the daily dust, while a D3-inclusive formula should be reserved for the once- or twice-weekly dose described above. Our [bearded dragon shopping list](/blog/bearded-dragon-shopping-list/) has specific product picks for both if you're stocking up for the first time.

Wash everything, chop to bite size, and replace the salad daily rather than leaving it to wilt. If greens keep getting ignored, offer them before insects in the morning with a little grated squash or a berry mixed in. Juveniles commonly skip salad entirely until around 6 months old; keep offering it anyway so the habit is already there once insects taper off.

## Fruit and the Never-Feed List

Fruit is a treat, not a food group. It should make up only about 5 to 10% of the plants fed to a bearded dragon, offered once or twice a week at most, well behind greens and vegetables in daily volume.

A handful of "human food" questions come up often enough to answer directly. Commercial pellets or salad toppers and a pinch of bee pollen are fine as occasional variety, never the main diet. Pinky mice are not a routine food; they show up occasionally for larger adult females, usually during reproductive cycles, and only when a reptile vet suggests it for a specific reason (more on that cycle in the [eggs and egg binding guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-eggs-and-egg-binding-guide/)). Eggs, meat, chicken, and fish are the wrong protein and fat balance for this species. Dog, cat, and rabbit food are nutritionally wrong and hard on the kidneys. Bread, pasta, dairy, chips, and anything processed offer no benefit, and reptiles can't digest lactose at all.

Put together, the full never-feed list reads: avocado, rhubarb, onion, garlic, leek, chives, citrus, raw potato and nightshade leaves and stems, mushrooms, fireflies, any wild-caught insect, ladybugs, boxelder bugs, stink bugs, monarch caterpillars, any insect wider than the space between the eyes, and eggs, meat, dairy, pet food, or processed food. Houseplants count too during supervised free-roam time outside the enclosure: pothos, philodendron, ivy, lilies, daffodils, and oleander are all toxic.

## Building a Daily Salad

A workable routine, once a dragon is old enough to be eating salad regularly:

1. Pick two or three staple greens and chop them fine (collards, mustard greens, and dandelion greens is a reliable rotation).
2. Grate one staple vegetable on top, winter squash is the easiest year-round choice.
3. Add a pinch from the occasional tier for variety, a little shredded carrot or a few chopped green beans.
4. Dust the whole thing with plain calcium powder on any day without insects.
5. Rotate which staple greens you're using week to week rather than repeating the exact same three every day, it keeps nutrient intake broader and keeps a dragon from getting bored with one flavor.

<FunFact>
  Bearded dragons are omnivores in the wild too. Wild adults skew heavily toward plants, insects, and occasional small vertebrates, which is exactly the shift this list mirrors as a dragon grows from a mostly-insect hatchling into a mostly-plant adult.
</FunFact>

## The Short Version

Most of this list isn't about memorizing individual foods, it's about defaulting to the staple tier for daily meals and treating everything else, insects included, as seasoning. When in doubt on a new food, check where the phosphorus and oxalate content lands relative to calcium, and when in doubt on portion size, go smaller.
