# Quaker Parakeet Cage Setup

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A Quaker's cage needs to support an intelligent, assertive, chew-happy bird with a strong instinct to build, and getting the size, bar spacing, and enrichment right matters more than it might for a quieter species.

## Cage Size

A reasonable minimum is 24 by 24 by 36 inches, though a wider <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4ymrwMJ" product="Prevue Hendryx Wrought Iron Flight Cage (5 ft, rolling)">flight-style cage</AffiliateLink> in the 30 to 36 inch range is a better real-world target for this active bird. Use 1/2 to 5/8 inch bar spacing, don't exceed 3/4 inch, and choose heavy-gauge bars, because this species chews hard.

## Temperature and Room Air

Normal comfortable room temperature works well. Avoid drafts and temperature extremes, and never leave a Quaker outdoors once temperatures climb toward 90°F. Normal household humidity is fine, with nothing to add.

## The Cage Floor

Plain cage liner or newspaper is all this species needs on the cage floor. Loose particulate bedding is unnecessary, and paper shows the droppings, which are worth reading every day.

## Cleaning, With the Bird Out

The daily routine is short: empty and wash every food and water dish with soap and water, rinse, and refill, and change the paper. Uneaten fresh food comes out after a couple of hours, sooner in a warm room, before it spoils. A full scrub of the cage, perches and toys adds a disinfectant, the right contact time and a thorough rinse, and which products are safe around a bird and which fumes are not is laid out in the [bird household hazards guide](/blog/bird-household-hazards-guide/).

The Quaker-specific part is timing. Quakers can be strongly protective of their cage, and a hand reaching in to change dishes or scrape the floor is exactly when a territorial bird bites. Clean while the bird is out on a play stand or in another room, never with it sitting inside watching. A second set of dishes makes the daily swap quicker: clean bowls go in, dirty ones come out to be washed.

Chewed wood, frayed rope and cracked plastic toys get checked during the clean and replaced. Heavy metal toxicity is on the list of common Quaker health problems, and a bird that chews its cage this hard will find zinc or lead in galvanized wire, old paint or cheap hardware if it is there.

## Lighting

Full-spectrum lighting is optional rather than required, and most pet birds do fine without any. If you run a standard full-spectrum bulb, up to about 4 hours a day is the conservative figure, and there is no controlled study behind bird lighting recommendations to sharpen that with. Provide a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4fbLrVy" product="Penn-Plax E-Cuttlebone, Omega 3 & Honey Enriched (2 pack)">cuttlebone</AffiliateLink> as an additional calcium source.

<FunFact>
A Quaker only about eleven inches long uses every inch of a cage this size, stocky, busy, and territorial in a way that makes floor space and bar strength matter more than the bird's own dimensions would suggest.
</FunFact>

## Diet Basics

Pellets are the base, and the figure to hold to is a minimum of 70 percent of the diet. Fruits, vegetables and greens make up the rest, no more than 20 to 40 percent of daily intake, with fruit kept to the smaller end of that because of its water and sugar content. Seed belongs in the diet only as a very small part of it, never as the whole thing: a seed-based diet is what drives the fatty liver disease this species is prone to.

Dark leafy greens, broccoli, carrot, squash, peppers and sweet potato all work. A small amount of lean cooked meat, fish, cooked egg or a little cheese is fine occasionally. Pull fresh food after a couple of hours, sooner in a warm room, before it spoils. Avocado and onion are potentially toxic and never go in, and neither do chocolate, caffeine in any form, or alcohol.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: Manage the Nest-Building Instinct Deliberately

This is the detail that separates a well-adjusted pet Quaker from a hormonal, territorial one. The Quaker is the only parrot that builds its own nest, and it needs an outlet for that: plenty of shreddable wood and <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4wMu5Wu" product="Hanging Sola Ball Chew/Forage Toys, 2 Pack (BBjinronjy)">foraging toys</AffiliateLink> to weave and tear. **A pet Quaker never gets a nest box.** A nest site, together with a long day length, is on the standard list of [reproductive triggers](/blog/bird-photoperiod-sleep-guide/), and in this species it brings on breeding-style hormonal aggression.

Offer varied perch diameters to support foot health, and plan for several hours of supervised out-of-cage time daily. This is too intelligent and social a bird to thrive on cage time alone.
