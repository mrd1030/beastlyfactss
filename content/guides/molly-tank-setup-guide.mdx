# Molly Tank Setup: A Real 20 Gallons, Sturdy Filtration, and the Salt Question

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Mollies need more room and sturdier filtration than a guppy from day one, and they raise a question guppies never do: whether the water needs a little aquarium salt in it.

## Tank Size

20 gallons is the real minimum for a small group, a step up from the 10-gallon tank that works fine for guppies. Mollies simply run bigger as adults, and a colony that starts breeding adds to the bioload fast. A 30-gallon tank is the cautious choice for sailfin varieties or any growing group. Go long and rectangular rather than tall, and use a lid, mollies are capable jumpers.

## Temperature

Short-finned mollies are comfortable at 72 to 82°F, and sailfins are listed at 70 to 79°F, so a sailfin tank sits in the lower half of that span. Stability matters more than hitting an exact number, so avoid swings larger than about 2°F in a 24-hour period. Temperature swings add to the stress behind the molly "shimmy." ## Water Chemistry

Mollies do best with a pH of 7.5 to 8.5 and hard water, roughly 15 to 30 dGH. In soft or acidic water they weaken fairly quickly, often showing shimmy, fungus or clamped fins. If your tap water runs soft, a mineral supplement, crushed coral or a commercial buffering product, can bring it into range, and the [freshwater pH, GH and KH guide](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) explains why KH is the parameter doing the work there and why dosing pH-down against a hard buffer crashes tanks.

In a mixed livebearer tank, the molly sets the water. Platies, guppies and swordtails all tolerate the harder, more alkaline end of their own ranges, so a shared tank run at about pH 7.5 to 8.2 in hard water suits all four. Soft-water fish such as neon and cardinal tetras are the compromise that fails, because they want the opposite chemistry. Good company is other livebearers, some rainbowfish, catfish such as corydoras, and barbs, danios, loaches and tetras that tolerate hard water and come close to a molly's size. Avoid slow, long-finned fish the mollies may nip, and shrimp small enough to eat.

## The Salt Question, Answered Honestly

This is the detail that separates molly care from guppy care, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a repeated hobbyist talking point. Mollies are a brackish-tolerant, euryhaline species: sailfin mollies tightly regulate their internal salt balance up to roughly 65 parts per thousand, nearly twice seawater strength of about 35, though their oxygen use climbs as salinity rises. That's real biology, not an old wives' tale.

<FunFact>
"Euryhaline" means a species can tolerate a wide range of salinities. The sailfin molly is especially tied to brackish habitats, yet it lives perfectly well in fresh water as long as that water is not soft or acidic. Mollies are one of the more striking examples of it among common aquarium fish, and wild populations turn up in everything from coastal saltwater lagoons to freshwater roadside ditches, sometimes just a short distance apart.
</FunFact>

Whether a healthy freshwater display tank needs salt added as standard practice is the contested part. Some keepers add a small maintenance dose. Others, including longtime livebearer specialists, argue mollies "adapt to" salinity rather than requiring it. What they do need is hard, alkaline, mineral-rich water, and large, frequent water changes and good live plant cover handle the problems salt is usually reached for, without salt's downside: it can inhibit live plant growth. Salt can also harm snails at higher concentrations. Salt does have a targeted use against ich, a bath of 4 to 5 grams per liter (about 15 to 19 grams per gallon) for 7 to 10 days, which mollies tolerate, but that is a treatment, not a standing requirement for every molly tank.

If you do decide to add salt to a freshwater tank, use plain aquarium salt, which is sodium chloride made for the job, rather than a marine salt mix formulated for saltwater tanks. In a freshwater tank, never use table salt, which contains iodine and anti-caking agents that aren't meant for fish.

## Filtration

Mollies need more real filtration capacity than guppies. They're bigger fish producing more waste, and a breeding colony compounds that quickly. For a standard or growing group, a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000260FUW?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="AquaClear 70 Power Filter (40-70 gal)">hang-on-back filter</AffiliateLink> or a stronger <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQAk5K" product="Canister filter (Fluval 107, up to 30 gal)">canister filter</AffiliateLink> handles the bioload comfortably. The general rule is a filter that can turn over the full tank volume at least four times an hour.

If fry survival is the priority instead, for example a dedicated breeding or nursery tank, a gentle <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">sponge filter</AffiliateLink> is the better call, since it won't pull small fry into strong current the way a power filter can. The default for a display group is the hang-on-back, sized to [turn the tank over](/blog/aquarium-filtration-guide/) at least four times an hour. Switch to a sponge filter only in a tank that exists for the fry.

## Substrate

Fine gravel or sand both work well. Avoid sharp or coarse gravel, which can damage fins, especially on longer-finned sailfin and lyretail varieties.

## Lighting

A basic LED light on an 8 to 10 hour daily cycle is sufficient. It isn't strictly necessary for the fish's health, but it supports live plants and makes for better viewing.

## The Two Non-Obvious Requirements

Cycle the tank before adding fish. Building the beneficial bacteria colony that processes waste takes 4 to 6 weeks with a fishless cycle, or somewhat less with a bottled bacteria product. Skipping this step is a fast route to the ammonia-driven stress that can show up as shimmy. It is one of the most common beginner mistakes in the hobby generally.

Always use a water conditioner on tap water before it goes in the tank, dechlorinating and neutralizing chloramine and heavy metals that are otherwise harmful to fish. Chlorine in untreated tap water adds to the stress behind shimmy. A water test kit lets you confirm the cycle actually finished and catch drift afterward.

Dense live plants aren't just decoration in a molly tank either, they're a real population-control lever. Molly parents don't look after their fry, and adults will eat them given the chance. If you're allowing your mollies to breed, dense live plants give fry somewhere to hide from adults, meaningfully improving survival. If you'd rather your population stay naturally controlled, skipping dense cover lets normal predation keep numbers in check instead, the same lever our [Guppy tank setup guide](/blog/guppy-tank-setup-guide/) covers for that closely related species.
