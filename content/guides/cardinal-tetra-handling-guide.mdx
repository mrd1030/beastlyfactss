# Cardinal Tetras Aren't Handled Either, and School Size Is the Rule That Counts

Cardinal tetras are an observation pet. Nobody picks one up, and the two handling questions that decide how the fish does are how many share the tank and how it gets from the shop bag into the water. The second question has more parts than it looks, because this soft-water fish is unusually sensitive to cold, and a bag that has traveled a long way is a hazard of its own.

## How Many to Bring Home: Ten

Cardinal tetras are obligate schooling fish: they need a group to feel secure and behave normally. Plan for a group of ten, with eight as the bare minimum, the same figures as for neon tetras. Ten in a mixed-sex group is where natural schooling settles in, and a group that's too small leaves the fish exposed, so they turn pale, hide constantly, and are more vulnerable to illness.

### What a Properly Schooled Group Looks Like

Confident, brightly colored fish swimming together in a loose group, working the mid-water of the tank instead of hiding in corners. It is one of the more rewarding things to watch in a community aquarium, and it only happens reliably once the school is big enough.

## Netting

A net, or a cup, is the only thing that ever touches a cardinal, and its one essential job comes at the end of the trip home: lifting the fish out of the bag and into the tank. The bag water stays behind and goes down the drain. It has spent the journey collecting the fishes' waste, and none of it belongs in the aquarium.

## The Trip Home

A sealed bag changes in three ways on the road, and each one has a fix.

| What changes | Why it matters for cardinals | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature falls | In laboratory testing, deaths rose once water dropped below about 67°F (19.6°C), and a bag that sat in a cold car or a winter shipping box can arrive well under that | Check the bag against the tank before deciding how long to take |
| Ammonia rises | Fish waste builds up in the bag for the whole trip | Plan the shortest trip the route allows |
| pH falls | Carbon dioxide from the fishes' breathing accumulates in the water | Expect the bag to read more acidic than the shop tank did |

Cardinals bought online travel the longest. Before ordering, check that the seller offers a dead-on-arrival guarantee, photograph any problem before the bag is opened, and contact the seller promptly. The insulation and oxygen choices for a long journey are the same for every fish, and [moving fish on a longer trip](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/) sets them out.

## Acclimation: Float, Drip, or Straight In

The routine is the one every freshwater fish gets, and what is particular to cardinals is which of its exceptions they tend to hit. New arrivals stay in fairly dark conditions. The standard routine is to float the bag so its water comes to tank temperature, then add tank water every few minutes until the bag holds more aquarium water than shop water. The drip method feeds tank water into the fish's container one to two drips a second and is meant for sensitive animals, and it is done when the two waters measure about the same in dissolved solids.

| Method | How long | When it fits a cardinal |
|---|---|---|
| Floating and mixing | 30 to 60 minutes | Shop and tank water close in hardness and pH |
| Drip acclimation | A couple of hours | Soft shop water going into hard tap water |
| Temperature match, then straight in | Only as long as the temperatures take to even out | After long shipping, when ammonia has built up in the bag |

The pH figure often quoted for cardinals needs care. In laboratory tolerance testing, every cardinal survived 96 hours at any pH from 4.0 to 8.5, but the researchers brought the water to each pH gradually, adding acid or alkali at one-hour intervals. That shows how wide a range the fish can live in once it gets there slowly. It says nothing about an abrupt change from bag water to tank water.

Hardness is the other mismatch to check. Cardinals are kept in soft water, 1 to 12 dGH and toward the bottom of that range for wild fish.

Long shipping is the exception to going slow. By the end of a long trip the bag holds built-up ammonia at a depressed pH, and as tank water goes in and the pH rises, more of that ammonia shifts into its toxic form. A slow mix stretches that exposure out. The alternative to netting shipped fish straight in is an ammonia detoxifier added to the bag before mixing.

Wild-caught cardinals, still a large share of the fish sold, cope worse with deteriorating water than commercially bred ones. That makes a quiet, dim tank and a stable first few weeks more important than the transfer itself.

## Telling Males From Females

Sexing a cardinal comes down to body shape, and only once the fish are mature. A sexually mature female is noticeably rounder through the body and a little larger than a male, and the gap widens when she is full of eggs. A male stays slimmer, and in breeding condition he shows his best color. Young fish at the store are hard to sex, and a mixed school does not need to be: the difference matters only to a keeper setting up a breeding pair in a separate spawning tank.

<FunFact source="Linhares et al. (2018), Boletim do Instituto de Pesca">
That vivid red-and-blue stripe depends on the light. In a Brazilian lab test, cardinal tetras under intense light grew pale in the blue and black stripes as their pigment cells thinned, while fish kept at 250 lux or less looked more vivid. Dim, tannin-stained water is the lighting this species is built for.
</FunFact>

## Fish Tank Granuloma: Gloves Over Cuts

Floating bags, netting fish and pouring away bag water all put a keeper's hands in aquarium water. The CDC lists mycobacteriosis, known as fish tank granuloma or swimming pool granuloma, among the diseases pet fish can spread to people, and it reaches them through broken skin. Children under 5, adults 65 or older and anyone with a weakened immune system carry the higher risk.

Prevention comes down to a few habits:

- **Gloves for tank work.** Wear them whenever you clean or care for the aquarium, and above all when there is a cut or wound on your hand.
- **Wash scratches at once.** Then get medical care if a wound turns red, painful, warm or swollen.
- **Wash hands before and after.** Every time hands go into the tank.
- **Keep young children out of it.** Children under 5 should not clean the tank or handle its equipment.
- **Dump tank water away from the kitchen.** A bathtub, a laundry sink or outdoors, and disinfect the sink afterward.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Buying just a handful of cardinal tetras and assuming that's enough.
- Adding them to a tank that hasn't finished [cycling](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/). Cardinals are sensitive to nitrite, which an incompletely cycled tank produces, and ammonia must read zero.
- Mixing them with fin-nipping tankmates, which disrupts normal schooling behavior even in an otherwise adequate group.

## How to Tell Cardinal Tetra and Neon Tetra Apart

The two fish get confused constantly, and the reliable tell is the red stripe. On a cardinal tetra it runs the full length of the body, from the gills all the way to the tail. On a [neon tetra](/blog/neon-tetra-handling-guide/), the red only covers roughly the back half, with the front half showing just the blue stripe. Cardinals also run slightly larger and tolerate noticeably warmer water, useful to know if you're stocking a warmer community tank where straight neon tetra would struggle.
