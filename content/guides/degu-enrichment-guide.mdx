# Degu Enrichment: The Dust Bath Is a Message Board

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most care sheets file the dust bath under grooming. The research on this species says it is doing something considerably more interesting.

<KeyTakeaway>
Degus dust bathe **at a higher rate on soil a familiar degu has already used**, and dust bathing plays a role in male-to-male communication. In the wild, colony bathing sites are marked with urine and anal gland secretions. The dust tray is a shared scent board and not a shower.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Says

Degus get more research attention than most exotic pets because they are used as a model for diabetes and Alzheimer's disease. Two threads matter to a keeper.

**Dust bathing is communication.** Work on *Octodon degus* found dust bathing is **involved in communication during male-to-male interactions**, though not female-to-female, and that **both sexes dust bathe at a higher rate on soil previously used by a familiar conspecific**. Wild colony dust baths are scent-marked.

**They are colonial and need company.** Colony management guidance for research degus is explicit that housing has to reflect their group-living ecology, and that in the absence of social interaction and physical stimulation animals may become aggressive or self-mutilate. At least two together is the baseline.

<FunFact>
The familiarity effect is the strange part. Degus do not simply prefer clean or dirty soil. They respond to soil carrying a specific animal's signature. A freshly sterilized tray every time is, from the degu's point of view, wiping the board.
</FunFact>

## Social Housing, Non-Negotiable

The starting point, never an enhancement. Degus are colonial, and a single degu is missing the thing the species is built around.

Two minimum, same-sex or neutered, and ideally littermates or animals introduced young. Introductions of unfamiliar adults need care. Isolation is linked to aggression and self-mutilation in this species, which is a stronger statement than the equivalent for most rodents.

## The Dust Bath, Offered Properly

Given what it is doing, offer it in sessions to the group rather than as a permanent fixture. A shallow tray of <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vgxhss" product="Chinchilla dust (Oxbow Poof)">chinchilla bathing dust</AffiliateLink> two or three times a week, about twenty minutes at a time, is the schedule, and more is not better: overuse of dust baths can cause health problems.

Take the tray out after each session. Left in, it becomes a toilet and gets contaminated with urine and droppings. Between sessions, sift out droppings and top the dust up instead of replacing all of it every time, since the research suggests the scent history is part of what makes it worth using.

## Height and Levels

Active, agile, diurnal climbers. A tall cage with multiple solid levels, ramps, ledges and branches gives them far more usable space than floor area alone.

Solid shelves, not wire, since [wire flooring causes foot problems](/blog/degu-health-issues-guide/). Fill the height. Buying it and leaving it open wastes it.

## A Wheel, Solid and Large

Degus run, and a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4gmXgL1" product="Solid exercise wheel (chinchilla)">solid-surface exercise wheel</AffiliateLink> gets used heavily. Solid surface and large diameter both matter: an open-rung wheel is a tail and limb injury waiting to happen, and a small one forces an arched spine.

This is one of the few pieces of equipment for this species that reliably earns its space.

## Chewing, Which They Do to Everything

Open-rooted teeth and a serious appetite for destruction. <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F6V5ZZR6?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="PINVNBY 9 Pcs Lava Blocks for Chinchillas, Hamster Chew Stones">Mineral and lava chew blocks</AffiliateLink>, untreated wood, cardboard and apple or willow branches all get worked through.

Assume anything plastic in the cage will be chewed eventually, and choose accordingly.

## Foraging Without Sugar

The constraint that makes degus different. They are highly prone to diabetes and cannot handle sugar, so fruit and most commercial treats are out.

Build foraging on safe material instead: scatter their pellets and dried herbs through hay and substrate, hide portions in cardboard, paper parcels and a <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DPKP3JBX?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hamiledyi 3 Pack Guinea Pig Foraging Toys, wooden enrichment puzzle">wooden foraging puzzle</AffiliateLink>, and let them work for the ordinary diet rather than for treats.

## Time Out of the Cage

Degus need a safe exercise area outside the cage as well as the space inside it, and every minute of that time is supervised. A degu chews structurally, so the room is prepared before the door opens: electrical cords unplugged and taken out of reach, houseplants removed in case any are toxic, and anything chewable you value taken out or blocked off.

## Priority Order

1. At least two degus, and preferably more
2. A tall cage with solid multi-level structure
3. Dust baths in sessions, shared, not sterilized every time
4. A large solid-surface wheel
5. Constant chewing material
6. Sugar-free scatter and puzzle foraging
7. Rearrangement and novelty, last

## What Not to Do

Do not keep a degu alone. Do not treat the dust bath as purely hygiene, which is the framing the research complicates. Do not leave the bath in permanently. Do not use an open-rung wheel or wire shelving. Do not feed fruit, sugary treats or standard rodent mixes to a diabetes-prone species. And do not grab one by the tail, since degus can shed the tail sheath and it does not grow back.
