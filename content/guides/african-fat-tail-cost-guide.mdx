# How Much Does an African Fat-Tailed Gecko Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

African fat-tailed geckos cost more than most beginner geckos, and morphs push that number considerably higher.

## The Animal Itself: $75 to $600, Averaging $100 to $200 for Normals

Normal, wild-type patterned animals are the cheapest, commonly around $100. Morphs change the price substantially: mid-range combinations like Amelanistic or single-gene Oreo animals often run $200 to $400, while rare or high-end combos, Whiteout, Zulu, or stacked multi-gene animals, can reach $600 to $1,000 or more, occasionally higher for truly rare lines.

Every row before the vet exam adds up to $485 to $835, and the first exam and fecal test bring it to $585 to $1,050, not including the animal. Without the optional UVB and its timer, the setup is $375 to $680. Don't be fooled by the 2.5% on the UVB tube: the percentage is the share of its light that is UVB, not how strong it is, and this longer 14-watt tube gives more UV at the same distance than a short 7% kit. Food and supplements come to $10 to $25 a month, mostly feeders, with about $5 a month in supplement top-ups once the first supply runs out. Electricity for a heat mat and optional UVB is modest and sits on top of the table.

<FunFact>
The "fat tail" is more than a name. This species stores fat in its tail and can draw on it during lean periods, which is part of why a healthy fat-tail's tail should always look plump rather than thin.
</FunFact>

## Vet Costs

A routine exam commonly runs $35 to $105, plus a fecal test around $65 to $110. Illness or emergency visits with diagnostics typically run $150 to $300 or higher, with surgical intervention, for example egg-binding, costing more still.

Annual wellness exams are the recommended baseline for this species, covering body condition, hydration and nutrition. The exam plus a fecal test puts the yearly check at $100 to $215 before anything is wrong.

## Why the Hygrometer and Scale Are on the List

Both are cheap, and both catch the two problems this species runs into most. The hygrometer tells you whether the humid hide is holding 70 to 80% or has quietly dried out, which is the difference between clean sheds and skin stuck on the toes. The scale matters because adults weigh only 40 to 90 grams, averaging about 50: a loss of 5 grams is a tenth of the animal and invisible by eye until the tail has already thinned. A kitchen scale that reads in single grams does the job.

## One Gecko, One Setup

Budget for one animal per enclosure. Both sexes are territorial and two males should never share a tank, so a second fat-tail usually means a second full setup at another $485 to $835. A female kept alone can still produce eggs, so her setup also needs a laying box of moist, diggable substrate, which costs little compared with an egg-binding surgery.

## What It Adds Up To

Feeders and supplements at $10 to $25 a month come to $120 to $300 a year. Add the $100 to $215 wellness exam and fecal, and two UVB tubes a year at $50 to $55 if you run one, and a healthy adult runs $270 to $570 a year, before electricity or any sick visit. Over 15 years that is about $4,050 to $8,550 on top of the animal and the first setup. The UVB tube is a recurring line because output drops long before the light looks dim: [UVB lamps are replaced every 6 to 12 months](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/), by the maker's rated life, and this budget assumes a change every 6 months.

## Lifespan: A Long Commitment

15 to 20 years is the commonly cited range, and the best-documented figure, the maximum recorded captive lifespan, is just over 16 years. Either way, it is a long-term commitment, well beyond many other beginner reptiles.

Among the other easygoing geckos, the [gargoyle](/blog/gargoyle-gecko-cost-guide/) heats with a low-wattage bulb instead of a mat, and the [mourning gecko](/blog/mourning-gecko-cost-guide/) skips the feeder bill.
