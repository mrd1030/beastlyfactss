Every summer, a week of television turns the shark into a monster. Ominous music, slow-motion breaches, a fin cutting toward a swimmer. It makes for great TV. It also leaves a lot of people genuinely nervous about stepping into the ocean.

So here is the question worth asking: how dangerous are sharks, actually? Not in the trailer, in the data.

The answer is well documented, and it is not close.

## The 2025 Numbers

The International Shark Attack File, maintained by the Florida Museum of Natural History, is the closest thing there is to a global ledger of shark bites. Researchers there investigate every reported incident and sort out which ones were genuinely unprovoked.

For 2025, they investigated 105 alleged shark-human interactions worldwide. Of those, 65 were confirmed as unprovoked bites, and 29 were classed as provoked, meaning the person initiated contact in some way, such as trying to touch, feed, or unhook the animal.

Nine of the 65 unprovoked bites were fatal.

That is the whole planet, for a whole year.

<FunFact>
The 2025 total of 65 unprovoked bites was actually **below** the recent 10-year average of 72. The nine deaths were above the 10-year average of six, which sounds alarming until you notice how small both numbers are. When the annual figure is single digits, one unusual year moves the average a lot.
</FunFact>

## Where Bites Actually Happen

The geography is lopsided in a way that surprises most people.

The United States recorded the most bites but only one death. Australia recorded fewer bites but five deaths, which is 56% of the global fatality count from 32% of the incidents.

Florida on its own accounted for 11 bites and no deaths. That single state sees more shark bites than most countries, and the overwhelming majority are quick, exploratory, and survivable.

Surfers accounted for 32% of bites in 2025. That is not because sharks prefer surfers. It is because surfers spend hours at a time in shallow, churned-up water at dawn and dusk, splashing on the surface, in exactly the conditions where a shark's visibility is worst.

## The Comparison Nobody Puts in the Trailer

Here is the part that reframes everything.

In the same year that sharks bit 65 people and killed nine, humans killed sharks on an industrial scale. A study led by Boris Worm, covering global fishing data, found annual shark mortality climbing from about 76 million in 2012 to more than 80 million by 2017. Once sharks that were not identified to species are accounted for, the estimate reaches roughly 100 million a year.

Put those side by side and the ratio is difficult to hold in your head. Nine human deaths. Tens of millions of shark deaths.

The trend is going the wrong way, too. That same research found shark mortality rose even as regulations against finning increased roughly tenfold over the study period. More rules did not translate into fewer dead sharks.

## What That Is Doing to Shark Populations

The fishing pressure has left a mark you can measure.

A 2021 study in *Nature*, led by Nathan Pacoureau, tracked oceanic sharks and rays over half a century. Global abundance fell **71%** between 1970 and 2018, driven by an 18-fold increase in relative fishing pressure.

Three quarters of the species in that group are now threatened with extinction.

These are not obscure animals. The list includes species most people can name, and they are disappearing from the open ocean while the public conversation is still mostly about whether it is safe to go swimming.

<FunFact>
Sharks have been around for roughly 450 million years and survived all five mass extinctions. They predate trees. They predate Saturn's rings. The thing that finally put a dent in them, in about fifty years, was industrial fishing. ## So Should You Worry?

A useful way to think about risk is not "could this happen" but "how often does it happen, given how many chances it gets."

Millions of people enter the ocean every single day. Against that, 65 bites worldwide in a year is an extraordinarily thin slice. The Florida Museum researchers make the point directly: drowning is the third leading cause of death from unintentional injury worldwide, with over 4,000 deaths a year in the United States alone, while shark bites remain, in their words, extremely low.

If you want to lower your odds further, the practical advice is unglamorous and works:

- Swim near lifeguards, and stay out of the water at dawn and dusk when sharks feed and visibility drops
- Avoid murky water, river mouths, and areas where people are fishing or bait is in the water
- Skip shiny jewellery, which can flash like fish scales
- Do not swim alone, and get out if you see a lot of baitfish or diving seabirds

Notice that none of these are about sharks being aggressive. They are about not accidentally looking like food in conditions where a shark cannot see well.

## The Real Story

Shark Week is genuinely good at one thing: it makes people care about sharks. The footage is spectacular and the animals deserve the attention.

But the framing has a cost. A shark bite is rare enough that every single one worldwide gets individually investigated and catalogued by researchers. Meanwhile the actual emergency, tens of millions of sharks killed a year and a 71% collapse in oceanic populations, does not get a soundtrack.

The sharks are not the danger in this story. They are the ones in trouble.
