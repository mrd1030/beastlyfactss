# Ghost Shrimp Enrichment: A Night Forager From the Weed Beds

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A wild ghost shrimp spends its day pressed close to the bottom of a pond or slow stream, inside a dense bed of submerged plants, and comes out at night to pick algae, plant matter and small insect larvae off whatever it can reach. A tank that gives it those three things, cover, a night shift, and surfaces worth foraging, delivers most of what enrichment means for this animal.

<KeyTakeaway>
Ghost shrimp are nocturnal, bottom-dwelling grazers of thick vegetation. Plant heavily, leave most surfaces unscrubbed, and feed after the lights go out.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Where It Lives

*Palaemon paludosus* is native to the ponds, lakes and streams of the Atlantic coastal plain, from New Jersey to Florida. It lives in some kind of aquatic cover and is most abundant in dense beds of submerged vegetation; where there is little or no vegetation it is uncommon. In summer it crowds into semi-aquatic grasses and floating mats of water hyacinth.

The tank version of that habitat is heavy planting. ## A Night Animal

Ghost shrimp are more active at night than during the day. By day they stay close to the bottom, which cuts their risk from predators that hunt by sight, and they do their foraging after dark, when movement is less likely to draw an attack.

Two things follow in a tank. A shrimp that hides through the day is behaving normally, not sulking or sick. And the best time to feed, and to watch, is after the lights go off. A pinch of food dropped at lights-out, viewed by the glow from the room, shows the shrimp doing what it does in the wild: walking the floor and working every surface with its small front claws.

## What It Forages On

The wild diet is dominated by algae, mostly diatoms and green algae, with living plants, detritus, and aquatic insects making up the rest. The insects are larvae: mayfly nymphs, and the larvae of midges, mosquitoes and other small flies. In the lab the shrimp have been seen feeding on aquatic weeds.

- **Leave the film.** The brown and green film on glass, rock, wood and leaves is the staple, so scrub the front glass for viewing and leave the back and sides to grow.
- **Scatter, do not pile.** Food spread thinly across the floor and plants gets searched for. A single pile gets eaten in one spot.
- **Add the insect part.** An occasional frozen bloodworm, which is a midge larva, matches the insect share of the wild diet.
- **Expect some grazing on soft plants.** A shrimp that eats aquatic weeds in the lab will pick at a soft-leaved plant in a tank.

The body helps here. The gut shows through the transparent shell, bright green after a meal of algae, so a keeper can see which shrimp has fed and on what.

<FunFact>
A male ghost shrimp only recognizes a female as a mate by touching her shell. She is receptive just after she molts, while the new shell is still soft, and mating happens within about seven hours of the molt.
</FunFact>

## Molting and Mating

Molting is when cover matters most. A freshly molted shrimp is soft until the new shell hardens, and in the wild that is also the moment a female mates, so the thickest planting in the tank is where a molting shrimp goes to wait it out. Stable hardness is what gets a shrimp through a molt cleanly, and [the shrimp molting guide](/blog/shrimp-molting-guide/) covers the mineral side and what a failed molt looks like.

A female carries her fertilized eggs under her abdomen for about 12 to 14 days at 79 to 82°F, and longer in cooler water. The young hatch as larvae and pass through three larval stages before they look like shrimp. A berried female tucked into the plants is one of the more rewarding things a ghost shrimp tank shows, and the larvae need a tank without fish or a filter intake that can take them.

## Company

The species is solitary. It does not school or need a group, and groups are common mainly because the shrimp are cheap. A group works when the planting and grazing surface give each shrimp its own spot to hide by day. Other animals in the tank are the bigger question: a betta eats a small ghost shrimp, and [the pairing is only safe once the shrimp passes about 1.5 inches](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/). Against the other common cleanup shrimp, ghost shrimp are [the hardy generalists that eat almost anything](/blog/cherry-amano-ghost-shrimp-overview/), which is why a varied, grazeable tank suits them better than a single food.

## Why It Is Worth the Effort

No enrichment study exists for this species. The relevant evidence is the 2021 review of decapod sentience by Birch, Burn, Schnell, Browning and Crump, commissioned for the UK government. The authors applied an eight-criterion framework to over 300 scientific publications and concluded that decapod crustaceans are probably sentient, and the UK then extended animal welfare law to cover them, shrimp included. An animal sold by the dozen as live food still spends its nights doing what its wild relatives do, and a planted, unscrubbed tank is where it gets to.
