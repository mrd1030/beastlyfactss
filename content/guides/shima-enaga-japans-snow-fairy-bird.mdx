The first time most people see a shima enaga, they assume it's CGI, a plush toy, or a very good Photoshop job. It's none of those things. It's a real wild bird, and the "too cute to be real" reaction is exactly why it's become one of Japan's most photographed animals.

## Only on Hokkaido

The shima enaga (Aegithalos caudatus japonicus) is a subspecies of the long-tailed tit, found exclusively on Hokkaido, Japan's northernmost main island. It doesn't occur naturally anywhere else. Long-tailed tits on mainland Japan keep a dark eye-stripe running across the face, the same marking juvenile shima enagas are born with. Somewhere in the process of growing up, Hokkaido's birds lose it entirely, ending up with a face that's completely, uniformly white.

That white face is the single detail that separates a shima enaga from every other long-tailed tit subspecies at a glance, and it's the reason the nickname stuck: yuki no yousei, "snow fairy."

<KeyTakeaway>
The white face isn't decorative camouflage dreamed up for a viral photo. It's a genuine taxonomic marker. Mainland Japanese long-tailed tit subspecies keep a dark stripe through the eye their whole lives; only the Hokkaido population loses it as adults.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Puff Is Not Just for Show

Photos of shima enagas almost always show the same shape: a nearly perfect white sphere with a long tail stuck on the back like an afterthought. That shape is real, but it's not permanent. It's the bird fluffing its feathers to trap a layer of air against its skin, the same basic insulation trick used by birds fluffing up on any cold morning, just taken to an unusually photogenic extreme by an unusually small bird in an unusually cold place.

In warmer weather, or simply when the bird isn't cold, the feathers sit flatter and the shima enaga looks noticeably slimmer, more like an ordinary small songbird and less like a snowball. The roundness is weather-dependent behavior, not a fixed body shape.

<FunFact>
Hokkaido winters are genuinely severe, and a bird this small loses heat fast. Fluffing up doesn't just look cozy, it's a real thermoregulation strategy: trapped air is a better insulator than compressed feathers, so a puffed-up shima enaga is measurably better at holding onto body heat than a sleek one.
</FunFact>

## What's Under All That Fluff

Strip away the fluffed feathers and the shima enaga is a tiny bird, roughly 14 centimeters from beak to tail tip, and close to half of that length is tail. The body itself, the part doing the "snowball" impression, is barely bigger than a golf ball, and the whole animal weighs around 8 grams. Two US quarters outweigh it.

The name enaga (柄長) refers to that tail specifically. It comes from hishaku, a long-handled ladle traditionally used in Japan, a reference to the silhouette of a small round body trailing a long, thin handle. Shima simply means "island," marking this as the island (Hokkaido) population of the wider enaga species.

Despite the round, soft appearance, a shima enaga is an active insectivore, not a seed-eater. Its diet is almost entirely arthropods, with a particular preference for the eggs and larvae of moths and butterflies, foraged from bark and branches even in the middle of a Hokkaido winter.

## A Nest Built to Stretch

Long-tailed tits as a species, shima enaga included, build one of the more remarkable nests in the bird world. It's an enclosed, dome-shaped structure of moss bound together with spider silk, shingled on the outside with crusty grey lichen so it reads as just another lump on a branch. Both parents build it, and a finished nest takes up to three weeks.

The spider silk isn't only glue. It gives the whole nest elasticity, so the structure bulges outward as the chicks inside grow larger instead of forcing the parents to rebuild or abandon it partway through raising a brood. The interior gets lined with up to 1,500 small feathers for insulation and cushioning, gathered one at a time across those three weeks. For a bird that weighs 8 grams, that is an enormous amount of carrying.

<KeyTakeaway>
The elasticity is the genuinely clever part. Most nests are a fixed size the chicks eventually outgrow. A long-tailed tit's spider-silk-bound nest stretches with its occupants instead, which is a real structural engineering solution to a problem most birds just don't solve.
</KeyTakeaway>

Outside the breeding season, shima enagas don't go it alone. They travel in flocks, typically family groups made up of parents and the previous season's offspring, moving and foraging together through the winter that gives the species its nickname in the first place.

Those family ties do real work in spring. Long-tailed tits are cooperative breeders: a pair whose own nest fails late in the season often stops trying again and instead starts feeding the chicks of a relative nearby, and in a Sheffield study population followed since 1994, about half of all broods are raised with one to three such helpers on the payroll. Failure at a nest does not end a bird's breeding season so much as redirect it toward the nearest set of nephews.

Learning to find a bird this small usually comes down to the contact calls a flock keeps up as it moves, which is the same skill that [carries most of a birder's identifications in the field](/blog/ultimate-birdwatching-guide-best-places-times-tips-2026/).

The completely white face belongs to adults, and juveniles carry dark markings they later lose, one of several things the [shima enaga Beastfile](/beastlypedia/shima-enaga/) separates out.
