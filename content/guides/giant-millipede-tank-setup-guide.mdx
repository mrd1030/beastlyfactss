# Giant Millipede Enclosure Setup

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Giant millipede care comes down almost entirely to one thing done well: deep, correctly moist organic substrate. Get that right and this is a genuinely low-maintenance pet to keep long-term.

<Figure src="/assets/images/giant-millipede-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a giant millipede enclosure showing deep organic substrate, high humidity, and a secure tight-fitting lid" caption="Care comes down almost entirely to one thing done well: deep, correctly moist organic substrate." shareable />

## Enclosure Size

A minimum of 10 to 15 gallons for one adult, with many keepers recommending something closer to a 36x18x18 inch enclosure, roughly a 40-gallon equivalent, or a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eSbKl2" product="20 gallon long tank">20 gallon long tank</AffiliateLink>. Floor space matters more than height for this ground-dwelling species. A secure, tightly fitting lid is essential, millipedes are capable climbers and genuine escape artists. More than one adult is fine and normal, since this is a communal species that will cluster together even in a large enclosure, as long as space, hides and food are all abundant. What limits a group is crowding, which brings stress and competition for resources.

## Temperature

72 to 80°F suits this species well, and normal room temperature is often sufficient without any additional heat source. Keep the enclosure away from direct sun and drafts. A low-wattage heat mat is only worth adding if your space runs consistently cold.

## Humidity

70 to 80%, achieved by keeping roughly a third of the substrate genuinely moist, misting once or twice daily with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vPKMjX" product="Fine mist spray bottle">fine mist spray bottle</AffiliateLink>, and ensuring good cross-ventilation to prevent mold and mite problems. Monitor with a <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3QHrM81" product="Digital thermometer/hygrometer combo">digital hygrometer</AffiliateLink> rather than guessing.

## Substrate: The Single Most Important Element of This Setup

This deserves real attention, since substrate for this species is both habitat and food source at once, not just decoration. Provide 4 to 6 inches of pesticide-free organic topsoil, such as this <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B086R7Z52J?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Josh's Frogs Milli Mix Millipede and Isopod Substrate, 2 Quarts">Milli Mix millipede and isopod substrate</AffiliateLink>, mixed with coconut fiber, along with decaying hardwood and hardwood leaf litter, oak and beech both work well. Avoid softwoods like pine and cedar entirely, their resins are toxic to this species, and never use any substrate material that's been treated with pesticides. Add a calcium source, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4fbLrVy" product="Penn-Plax E-Cuttlebone, Omega 3 & Honey Enriched (2 pack)">cuttlebone</AffiliateLink> or crushed oyster shell, directly into the enclosure. Skip it and the exoskeleton goes [soft and the molts start failing](/blog/giant-millipede-health-issues-guide/).

## Diet Basics

The substrate is most of the diet. Decaying hardwood and pesticide-free hardwood leaf litter, oak, beech and maple, is the staple food itself, and the floor it sits on at the same time.

Fresh produce is a supplement on top of that: cucumber, carrot, sweet potato and leafy greens, plus small amounts of fruit such as apple, banana, melon or mango, offered every two to three days and taken back out before it molds. Calcium stays in permanently instead of going in on a schedule, a cuttlebone or powdered calcium carbonate left on the substrate for the animal to gnaw when it needs it. So is water, in a small shallow dish with a few pebbles dropped in so nothing drowns in it.

## Lighting

None required. This species actively avoids bright light. If you're running live plants in a bioactive setup, a low-output 6500K plant light is the only lighting worth adding.

## The Non-Obvious Requirement: No Cleanup Crew in a Breeding Tank

If you're building a bioactive enclosure, isopods and springtails can go in with the millipede. Bugs in Cyberspace's care sheet has millipedes doing well housed with cockroaches, isopods and other humid-habitat bugs, and the one open question is eggs: there is some concern the tankmates eat millipede eggs, which the same sheet calls unproven. Keep a cleanup crew in a display tank, and leave it out of a tank you are breeding in.
