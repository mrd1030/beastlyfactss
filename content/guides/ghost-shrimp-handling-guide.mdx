# Ghost Shrimp Aren't Handled by Hand, But Acclimation Is Where Most Losses Actually Happen

Ghost shrimp aren't a hands-on pet, and there's no real technique to learn for picking one up the way there is with a hermit crab. The handling skill that matters here happens before the shrimp ever touches your hand: getting it from the bag into the tank without shocking it, and giving it the quarantine that feeder-sourced stock usually never received in the first place.

## Netting and Transfer

Use a soft aquarium net rather than your hands whenever you need to move a ghost shrimp, and keep it in water as much as possible during the move. Their exoskeleton is thin and their legs and antennae are delicate, a firm grip or a dry transfer is a genuine injury risk in a way it wouldn't be for most fish. Keep transfers brief and purposeful, never repeated or prolonged.

The full care picture for the species is in our complete [Ghost shrimp care guide](/guides/ghost-shrimp/).

## Why Drip Acclimation Matters More for Shrimp Than for Fish

Dropping a bagged fish straight into new water, sometimes called "plop and drop," is already a rough introduction. For shrimp, the same move is a genuinely bigger risk. According to [BucePlant](https://buceplant.com/blogs/aquascaping-guides-and-tips/7-easy-steps-to-drip-acclimate-shrimp-and-fish), shrimp and snails are among the most sensitive aquarium animals to sudden water chemistry changes, because unlike fish, they can't quickly regulate their own internal water balance, and their permeable exoskeleton means osmotic stress hits them faster and harder.

The fix is a slow drip acclimation: run an airline tube from the tank into a container holding the shrimp and its bag water at roughly 1 to 2 drops per second, and let the volume double before moving the shrimp into the tank with a net, a process that typically takes 1 to 2 hours. It's a bigger time investment than "plop and drop," but for an animal this sensitive to sudden change, it's the difference between a smooth introduction and unnecessary, avoidable stress right at the start.

<FunFact>
The reason shrimp need such a gradual introduction comes down to their exoskeleton doing double duty as a semi-permeable membrane. A fish's scales and skin regulate water and salt exchange fairly tightly, but a shrimp's thinner shell lets water move across it more freely, which is efficient for everyday osmoregulation in stable water but leaves almost no buffer against a sudden shift. Drip acclimation isn't an overcautious ritual, it's directly addressing the one structural feature that makes shrimp more fragile than fish in exactly this one situation.
</FunFact>

## Why Feeder-Sourced Stock Needs Extra Care

This is the detail that makes ghost shrimp different from a shrimp bred specifically for the hobby, and it traces straight back to what our [cost guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-cost-guide/) covers about how cheap this species really is. [Quality Marine](https://www.qualitymarine.com/news/ghost-shrimp-palaemonetes-sp/) is upfront that most ghost shrimp move through the trade as live feed rather than as a display animal, and a feeder tank is run for volume and turnover, not individual animal welfare.

That distinction has real consequences. Writing about feeder animals generally, [AquariumStoreDepot](https://aquariumstoredepot.com/blogs/news/feeder-fish) puts it bluntly: "These fish are not pets. They receive no disease screening, no quarantine period, and minimal care," and high turnover in a crowded tank means "sick fish are constantly cycling through." The same dynamic applies to shrimp sold the same way. The standard precaution is a 2 to 4 week quarantine in a separate, simple setup before adding any new shrimp to an established tank, long enough for anything the shrimp was carrying to show itself before it can spread.

## Signs of Stress

- Lethargy or unusual stillness
- Staying hidden far more than normal, beyond the first day or two of settling in
- Refusing food
- A curled or clamped-looking posture
- Rapid, unexplained losses within a small group, a stronger signal than a single individual struggling

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Skipping acclimation entirely and dropping shrimp straight into new water
- Buying from a bargain feeder tank without asking whether the stock is meant for pet sale
- Skipping quarantine before adding new shrimp to an established tank
- Mixing ghost shrimp with tankmates sized wrong in either direction, a large enough fish will eat them, and they'll opportunistically eat smaller shrimp themselves, a risk our [feeding guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-feeding-guide/) covers directly

That last point matters most in a [betta fish](/guides/betta-fish/) tank, a common pairing where the size balance genuinely determines the outcome.
