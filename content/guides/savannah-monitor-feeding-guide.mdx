# Savannah Monitor Feeding: Insects as the Staple, Rodents as the Exception, and the Liver That Pays for the Difference

<AffiliateDisclosure />

The savannah monitor is marketed as a big lizard that eats mice, and that single idea is behind more early deaths in the species than any enclosure mistake. In the wild it is a scavenger of insects and small prey across a seasonal cycle of plenty and scarcity. In captivity it is fed rich food year-round with a fraction of the movement, and the result is the fatty liver that heads the veterinary list. Here is the diet that avoids it.

No published source puts a number on the portion, so body condition does the job the number would. A savannah monitor should be lean, with a visible waist behind the ribs and a tail that tapers. A rounded, heavy body shape means the diet is too rich or too frequent, whatever the count of insects says, and the [body condition guide](/blog/pet-obesity-body-condition-guide/) covers reading that on an animal you see every day.

## What Savannah Monitors Eat

**Insects, as the staple.** Large crickets, dubia and other roaches, locusts and grasshoppers, superworms and king mealworms, silkworms, and crayfish, all gut-loaded, with cooked egg white as another low-fat option. In the wild these monitors are scavengers that cover long distances searching for small prey, and the insect diet is what that prey mostly is. A dubia colony is the practical answer to feeding a lizard that eats this many insects.

**Gut-load them.** The insect's last meal is the monitor's nutrition, and the [gut-loading guide](/blog/gut-loading-feeder-insects-guide/) covers what to feed the feeders and for how long.

**Rodents, occasionally.** Mice, rats, chicks and freshwater fish may be offered, but only occasionally, to reduce the risk of obesity. The veterinary sheet's list of the species' important medical conditions has obesity with the words "rodent-based diet" beside it, and hepatic lipidosis directly above. A monitor that gets a mouse a week is being fed a treat as a diet.

**Calcium, on the insects.** Dust the non-breeding adult's diet with a calcium carbonate or gluconate supplement once weekly, using a product low in or free of phosphorus, at a minimum calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of 2:1. The powder follows the lighting: plain calcium under a working UVB bulb, calcium with D3 without one. Avoid products high in vitamin D, which reach toxicity in this species. A general vitamin and mineral supplement goes on once weekly too. The [UVB lighting guide](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) covers why a monitor under the wrong bulb gets metabolic bone disease on a dusted diet, since that disease comes from a low calcium-to-phosphorus ratio or missing D3 in the food, and from a lack of UVB.

<KeyTakeaway>
Lean, and moving. The reptile manual's summary of obesity in captive reptiles is restricted exercise, no seasonal rest where one belongs, and too many calories, together, and a savannah monitor in a small enclosure on rodents has all three. The enclosure is half the diet.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Foods to Avoid

Processed meats, dog or cat food, and excessive rodents. Fatty whole prey fed often. Goldfish as feeders, which carry an enzyme that destroys thiamine. Any vitamin product high in vitamin D. Wild insects from sprayed ground. And feeding by hand: a savannah monitor strikes at movement and warmth at feeding time, and the tongs are the line between a feeding and a bite.

## Why Savannah Monitors Stop Eating

**It cannot get warm.** A basking surface that is not at temperature stops digestion, and a monitor that cannot digest stops eating. The temperature gun on the basking spot is the first check.

**It is new, or wild-caught.** Most savannah monitors in the trade are wild-caught or farmed, and a new arrival is stressed and often parasitized. The veterinary sheet lists parasitism among the causes of starvation in the species, and the [reptile quarantine guide](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) covers the fecal screen that finds it.

**It is following the season.** Wild savannah monitors are active and hunting for much of the year and far less active through the dry periods. A captive adult that slows down and eats less in the cool months while holding its weight is on that clock.

**It is in shed.** A few days off food.

**It is full.** An adult fed three times a week is not hungry between meals, and a keeper who reads that as a fast and adds food is building the fatty liver.

## When to Worry

A monitor that skips meals in a warm enclosure while holding its weight is fine. One that refuses with weight loss, a swollen abdomen, lethargy, or a soft jaw is the vet visit, and so is one that is losing weight while eating, which is the parasite picture. The [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) covers the dry, gritty urate that says the animal is dehydrated and the diet is too rich in protein.

A savannah monitor on gut-loaded insects three times a week, in an enclosure big enough to walk, lives fifteen to twenty years. The same lizard on a mouse a week in a tank lives half that. The difference is entirely the plate.
