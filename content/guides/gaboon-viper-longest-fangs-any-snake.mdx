Most venomous snakes are known for speed or aggression. The Gaboon viper's reputation is built on the opposite trait: patience. It carries the longest fangs and the largest venom yield of any snake alive, and it will spend days lying completely still rather than use either one unless it absolutely has to.

## The Longest Fangs of Any Snake

A Gaboon viper's fangs can reach 5.5 centimetres (a little over 2 inches), longer than any other snake's, folded back flat against the roof of its mouth until a strike swings them forward. Its venom glands are enormous to match: a single bite can deliver among the largest venom doses of any snake on record, and even an average-sized bite from an average specimen is considered a serious medical emergency.

<KeyTakeaway>
This isn't an exaggerated internet fact. Multiple herpetological sources independently confirm the same record: no other venomous snake has fangs as long as the Gaboon viper's.
</KeyTakeaway>

Despite that, bites are genuinely rare. The snake's range sits mostly within rainforest, and it almost never goes looking for a confrontation.

Venom delivery this specialised is unusual even among venomous animals. [The slow loris, the only venomous primate, has no fangs built for the job at all](/blog/slow-loris-the-only-venomous-primate/) and instead has to improvise a venom system from an arm gland and its own saliva.

## Unaggressive by Nature, But Not Harmless

Handlers and herpetologists have described the Gaboon viper as one of the most tolerant vipers in the world, rarely hissing or striking without serious provocation, a reputation for placid behaviour that's unusual among vipers of any size. None of that makes it safe. When a Gaboon viper does strike, it's among the fastest-striking snakes alive, fast enough that handling one at all is discouraged even by people experienced with venomous reptiles.

<FunFact>
Almost every recorded Gaboon viper bite happens because someone stepped on one they never saw coming, not because the snake went hunting for a fight. Its camouflage, a mosaic of brown, cream and purple-grey blotches, dissolves so completely into dead leaf litter that fully exposed specimens can go unnoticed in plain sight.
</FunFact>

## It Doesn't Let Go

Most vipers strike, inject venom, and release, then track the prey down once the venom takes effect. The Gaboon viper does something different: it hangs on to what it bites with its long fangs instead of letting go, a behaviour distinct enough from other African vipers like the puff adder that researchers cite it specifically. Holding on rather than releasing also lets the snake deliver more of its venom into a single bite rather than losing its grip partway through.

For an ambush hunter that spends most of its life motionless in leaf litter, holding on means prey that's already been struck has far less chance of stumbling off into cover before the venom does its work.

Not every dangerous snake relies on venom at all. [Constrictors like the boa and green anaconda skip it entirely](/blog/constriction-does-not-crush-or-suffocate/), collapsing a prey animal's blood pressure through squeezing alone.

## Straight-Line Movement

Most snakes move by throwing their body into S-shaped curves and pushing off the ground at each bend. A Gaboon viper is heavy enough, some individuals top 8 kilograms, that this approach works poorly for it. Instead its usual gait is rectilinear: a slow, straight-ahead "walk" driven by its belly scales, with almost no side-to-side motion at all. It will still writhe briefly if alarmed, but only for a short distance before returning to its usual straight, unhurried crawl.

<KeyTakeaway>
Every trait here points the same direction. The camouflage, the stillness, the straight-line crawl, and the reluctance to strike unless provoked are all part of the same strategy: stay unnoticed for as long as possible, and make the one moment that matters count.
</KeyTakeaway>
