# Black Skirt Tetras Nip Fins, So the School and the Tankmates Come First

The black skirt tetra (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi), also sold as the black widow tetra, is a round-bodied tetra with a dark, flared rear half, and it grows to 2 to 2.5 inches in a home tank, 3 inches at the most. No one holds one. The handling it gets is a net, a bag and an hour of mixing water, and the choice that shapes its life in the tank comes earlier: which fish it shares the water with.

## A Lively Fish With a Nipping Habit

Black skirts are generally peaceful and busy, and they rank among the better tetras for a general community tank. They also carry a reputation as fin nippers, and it is earned. The fish most at risk are slow swimmers and anything with long, trailing fins.

The fix is the school. In a group of at least 12, the squabbling stays among the black skirts themselves, and contained inside the school it causes no harm. A group of three or four has nowhere to spend that energy except on the neighbors, which is how a small purchase turns into a tank of ragged fins.

Black skirts nip fins: keep a school of 12 and skip slow or long-finned tankmates.

## Tankmates That Work

Fish that are quick, compact and short-finned get along with a full school of black skirts:

- Most livebearers.
- Danios and rasboras.
- Other tetras.
- Peaceful bottom dwellers such as Corydoras and the smaller plecos.
- Sturdier dwarf cichlids, the kribensis among them.

## Tankmates to Leave Out

Anything slow with flowing fins is on the wrong side of the rule, and a betta is the clearest example. The fish whose whole appeal is its fins should never share a tank with a known nipper, which is why the [betta's own tankmate list](/blog/betta-fish-handling-guide/) rules out fin nippers by name. The same reasoning applies to any fancy long-finned fish.

Long fins on the black skirt itself raise the same problem. The long-fin black skirt, the long-fin white skirt and the long-fin GloFish Skirt Tetra all grow the trailing fins that nippers aim for, so a tank holding them is a tank to keep free of other nippers. Before any new species goes in, the [fins against nippers check](/blog/aquarium-stocking-and-tankmates-guide/) sits alongside temperature, water chemistry and mouth size in the stocking decision.

## How Many to Bring Home

Twelve, sized for a 20-gallon long tank with a 30 x 12 inch (75 x 30 cm) base, and the [black skirt tetra tank setup guide](/blog/black-skirt-tetra-tank-setup-guide/) covers why the length of that base matters for an active fish. A dozen fish at once is a real load of new waste, so the tank they go into has completed its [nitrogen cycle](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) and kept running steadily afterward.

## What to Check in the Shop Tank

Every black skirt in the trade comes from captive breeding, and that history is why the species is so easygoing about water and food. The plain black skirt, the pale white skirt, the long-fin forms and the GloFish Skirt Tetra are all the same species with the same care, so choosing between them is a matter of looks and of the tankmate rule above.

What to look for before pointing at a fish:

- **Movement.** A healthy black skirt is active and keeps with the group.
- **Fins.** They should be held open. GloFish lists droopy or flat fins as a warning that the water is wrong.
- **The whole tank.** Pass on any tank where a fish carries salt-grain [white spots](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/), ragged fins or sits apart from the others, since every fish in it shares that water. The early signs that rule a fish out are laid out in [what a sick fish looks like](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/).
- **Dye.** Leave "painted" or "berry" tetras on the shelf. They are white skirts that have been dipped in or injected with dye, a stressful process that often shortens their lives.

## Netting and Bagging

The net is the one tool that touches the fish, at the shop and again at home. After each transfer, look over the net, the bag and the floor, because a fish can stay caught in the mesh or jump out unseen.

For the trip, the bag should hold one part water to three parts air. The air carries the oxygen supply, so a bag that looks half empty is correctly packed. Set it in a dark box, or wrap it in a towel or newspaper, for the ride home. A drive longer than a short local trip needs the insulation and oxygen planning that go into [moving fish over a longer distance](/blog/aquarium-power-outage-and-transport-guide/).

## Acclimation, Step by Step

1. **Lights off.** New arrivals settle better in a fairly dim tank than under full lighting.
2. **Mix in tank water a little at a time.** Every few minutes, add a small amount of aquarium water to the bag. Carry on until the bag holds more tank water than shop water, which takes 30 to 60 minutes.
3. **Move the fish with the net.** The bag water stays out of the tank, because pouring the whole bag in is poor practice.
4. **Count before the bag goes.** Recheck the bag, the net and the floor for any fish left behind.

GloFish gives a shorter routine for its own fluorescent skirt tetras, 25 minutes in all, which starts with the sealed bag floating in the aquarium for 15 minutes. For fish that arrive by mail, it adds one step before anything else: take out any heat pack or cold pack in the box. The longer mix above gives the fish more time when shop water and tank water differ.

Captive-bred black skirts take a wide range of water, and they are kept at a pH of 6.0 to 7.5 and a hardness of 5 to 19 dGH. A tank inside those ranges suits them, and testing the [pH and hardness](/blog/freshwater-ph-gh-kh-guide/) before the trip shows whether it is. A quarantine tank is a sound first stop for any new fish, wherever it came from, and the same acclimation routine applies when the bag goes into [quarantine](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) first.

## Telling Males From Females

Males are noticeably slimmer than females and a little smaller. Their dorsal and anal fins also end in a sharper point, while a female's fins are rounder and her body deeper. A mixed school needs no sexing, and the difference matters only to a keeper picking out a breeding group.

<FunFact>
A black skirt's color is not fixed. The black can fade or deepen with the fish's mood, and the intense black of a young fish slowly fades as it grows older.
</FunFact>
