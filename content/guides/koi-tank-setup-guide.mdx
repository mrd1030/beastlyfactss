# Koi Pond Setup: Built for Waste Volume, Not Just Water Volume

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Koi husbandry is really water management at scale. Get the volume, filtration, and depth right, and koi are genuinely hardy, low-drama fish. This covers the full pond setup, for what it all costs, see our [Koi cost guide](/blog/koi-cost-guide/), or the full [Koi care guide](/guides/koi/) for everything else.

## Size

Roughly 250 gallons per adult koi, or about 10 gallons per inch of body length. In practice, 1,000 gallons is the frequently cited minimum practical pond volume, and it only comfortably supports a few fish, larger is strongly preferred for water stability and healthy growth. Juveniles can start in something smaller, even a 29-gallon tank temporarily, but they outgrow that fast.

Indoor aquariums can technically house koi, but matching the volume and industrial-grade filtration an adult needs indoors is expensive and impractical for most keepers. Outdoor ponds are the standard setup for adult koi for good reason. Red-eared sliders follow a similar arc, sold small into tanks that quickly become inadequate, our [Red Eared Slider tank setup guide](/blog/red-eared-slider-tank-setup-guide/) covers that same pattern in a very different animal.

## Depth

Minimum 3 feet, ideally 3 to 5 feet or more.

<FunFact>
Pond depth for koi isn't really about the fish's size at all, it's about surviving the seasons. Depth matters for temperature stability, protection from predators, and survival through winter in climates that freeze. A pond that's too shallow can freeze solid or overheat, both genuinely dangerous for the fish, which is why "how deep" ends up mattering more than "how wide" in a lot of pond designs.
</FunFact>

## Temperature

Koi are hardy coldwater fish, comfortable in a 64 to 75°F range, some sources extend this to 59 to 77°F. They tolerate wider extremes than that range suggests, but rapid swings, more than about 2°F a day, are the real risk, not the absolute temperature. No heater is needed in a temperate outdoor pond, a de-icer keeps a section ice-free for gas exchange in genuinely cold climates. Feeding and metabolism drop sharply as water cools, which is why koi eat far less in winter, covered in more detail in our [Koi cost guide](/blog/koi-cost-guide/).

## Substrate

Smooth-sided pebbles or gravel in a shallow layer are acceptable per some care guides, but many experienced keepers prefer bare-bottom ponds, they're easier to clean and avoid anaerobic pockets or pump damage. Avoid sharp or jagged material, which can injure mouths and gills during foraging or damage the pond liner, and skip very fine material that clogs equipment.

## Lighting

No UVB lighting is required, koi don't rely on it for vitamin D the way many reptiles do. Natural daylight is preferred for outdoor ponds, with some shade to limit algae growth and temperature spikes. A UV clarifier or sterilizer is common and genuinely useful, but it's a water-treatment device for controlling algae and pathogens, not lighting for the fish themselves.

Koi are heavy waste producers, and this is where most beginner setups fall short. ## Other Non-Obvious Requirements

- **Predator protection:** herons, raccoons, and similar predators are real threats. An <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076KQSFDZ?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="AlpineReach Koi Pond Netting Kit 15 x 20 ft">appropriately sized netting kit</AffiliateLink> and adequate depth both matter here.
- **Easy maintenance access:** you'll be netting and cleaning regularly, design for it.
- **A quarantine system:** essential for any new arrival before it joins the main pond, covered in more detail in our [Koi handling guide](/blog/koi-handling-guide/).
- **Careful plant selection:** koi eat many plants, choose varieties that can handle it.
- **Winter preparation:** de-icers, reduced feeding schedules, and depth all matter more as temperatures drop in cold climates.
