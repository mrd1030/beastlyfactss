# Common Milk Snake Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Milk snakes are hardy, and most of what shows up on this list traces back to enclosure moisture and temperature, both fully within an owner's control.

## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, nasal or oral discharge, and open-mouth breathing point here. From temperatures running too cool, humidity that's off, or poor ventilation, and the [numbers those three should be holding](/blog/milk-snake-tank-setup-guide/) are worth checking before anything else. Always see a vet, this needs antibiotics and won't clear up on its own.

## Scale Rot (Blister Disease)

Red, inflamed skin and fluid-filled blisters, typically on the belly, from <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vNgpe1" product="Cypress mulch substrate">substrate</AffiliateLink> kept too wet or dirty. This is well documented by veterinary sources as a real risk that can progress to septicemia and become fatal if left untreated. Always see a vet, and address the underlying substrate and cleaning routine at the same time, treatment alone doesn't fix a setup that's still too damp.

## Mites and Parasites

Tiny black or red specks around the eyes and scales point to mites, while restlessness and excessive soaking can be signs of either. External mites are treatable with a reptile-safe product and a deep enclosure clean. Internal parasites need a fecal exam and a vet's guidance. A new snake is the usual way mites get in, which is why it spends 3 to 6 months in [quarantine](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) on bare paper towel, where a fallen mite shows, before it shares a room with any other reptile.

## Retained Shed (Dysecdysis)

From low humidity or dehydration. Manageable at home with a properly maintained <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4bpnvNm" product="Sphagnum moss (for moist hide)">humid hide</AffiliateLink> and correct overall humidity. A shed that comes off in pieces rather than one length is the first sign the enclosure has drifted dry, and the [reptile shedding guide](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/) covers the soak-and-loosen routine for stuck shed. One handling note worth remembering here: avoid handling a snake mid-shed, when its skin is more fragile and easily damaged.

## Regurgitation, and When It Is Not the Meal

A single regurgitated meal usually traces to prey that was too large or handling too soon after feeding, and the fix is the next meal's size and timing. Repeated regurgitation is different. *Cryptosporidium serpentis*, a stomach parasite of snakes, causes regurgitation of mucus-coated prey a few days after eating, steady weight loss, and a firm swelling at mid-body where the stomach wall thickens. Diagnosis takes a vet: acid-fast staining of feces or of the coating on regurgitated prey, or a stomach biopsy. No treatment reliably clears it, which is why an infected snake stays isolated for life. It spreads by the fecal-oral route, and ordinary 3% bleach does not kill the oocysts; 5% ammonia is the disinfectant used against it. This is also the strongest argument for quarantining a new milk snake before it shares a room, tools, or a sink with another snake.

## Mouth Rot and Obesity

Less common but both reported in this species. Mouth rot, infectious stomatitis, needs a vet. Obesity is manageable through appropriate feeding frequency and portion size relative to your snake's actual size and activity level.

<FunFact>
Three of the conditions on this list trace back to just one variable, moisture. Substrate that's too wet causes scale rot, air that's too dry causes retained shed, and the two sit at opposite ends of the same dial. Getting the humidity and substrate moisture right isn't just one husbandry task among many for milk snakes, it's close to the central skill of keeping this species healthy long-term.
</FunFact>

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Substrate kept too wet is the direct cause of scale rot, one of the more serious conditions on this list. Low humidity, the opposite problem, causes retained shed. And incorrect temperature or ventilation contributes to respiratory infection. Getting the moisture balance right, not too wet, not too dry, is really the central skill in keeping this species healthy long-term, and regurgitation, the other common call, traces to prey too large or handling too soon, which the feeding guide sizes and times.
