# Common Milk Snake Health Issues

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Milk snakes are hardy, and most of what shows up on this list traces back to enclosure moisture and temperature, both fully within an owner's control.

## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, nasal or oral discharge, and open-mouth breathing point here. The usual causes are temperatures running too cool, humidity that's off, or poor ventilation, so the [numbers those three should be holding](/blog/milk-snake-tank-setup-guide/) are worth checking before anything else. Always see a vet. This needs antibiotics and won't clear up on its own.

## Scale Rot (Blister Disease)

Red, inflamed skin and fluid-filled blisters, typically on the belly, from substrate kept too wet or dirty. Untreated, it can progress to septicemia and kill. It needs a vet, and the underlying substrate and cleaning routine get fixed at the same time. Treatment alone doesn't fix a setup that's still too damp, so the soggy bedding comes out and fresh <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vNgpe1" product="Cypress mulch substrate">substrate</AffiliateLink> goes in.

## Mites and Parasites

Tiny black or red specks around the eyes and scales point to mites, while restlessness and excessive soaking can be signs of either mites or internal parasites. External mites are treatable with a reptile-safe product and a deep enclosure clean. Internal parasites need a fecal exam and a vet's guidance. A new snake is the usual way mites get in, which is why it spends 3 to 6 months in [quarantine](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/) on bare paper towel, where a fallen mite shows, before it shares a room with any other reptile.

## Retained Shed (Dysecdysis)

Low humidity or dehydration causes it, and it is manageable at home with a properly maintained <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4bpnvNm" product="Sphagnum moss (for moist hide)">humid hide</AffiliateLink> and correct overall humidity. A shed that comes off in pieces rather than one length is the first sign the enclosure has drifted dry. A piece of stuck shed is [soaked loose before it is removed](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/), never pulled off dry. Avoid handling a snake mid-shed, when its skin is more fragile and easily damaged.

## Regurgitation, and When It Is Not the Meal

A single regurgitated meal usually traces to prey that was too large or handling too soon after feeding, and the fix is the next meal's size and timing. Repeated regurgitation is different. *Cryptosporidium serpentis*, a stomach parasite of snakes, causes regurgitation of mucus-coated prey a few days after eating, steady weight loss, and a firm swelling at mid-body where the stomach wall thickens. Diagnosis takes a vet: acid-fast staining of feces or of the coating on regurgitated prey, or a stomach biopsy. No treatment reliably clears it, which is why an infected snake stays isolated for life. It spreads by the fecal-oral route, and ordinary 3% bleach does not kill the oocysts; 5% ammonia is the disinfectant used against it. This is also the strongest argument for quarantining a new milk snake before it shares a room, tools, or a sink with another snake.

## Mouth Rot and Obesity

Less common but both reported in this species. Mouth rot, infectious stomatitis, needs a vet. Obesity is manageable through appropriate feeding frequency and portion size relative to the snake's actual size and activity level.

## A New Snake Waits Its Turn

Most of this list is fixed in the enclosure, but *Cryptosporidium* is the one problem a keeper cannot undo once it arrives. A new milk snake spends its first 3 to 6 months apart, sharing no room, tools or sink with another snake, and that wait is what keeps an incurable parasite out of a collection.
