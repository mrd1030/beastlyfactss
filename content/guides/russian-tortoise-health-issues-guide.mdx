# Common Russian Tortoise Health Issues and What Causes Them

Russian tortoises are hardy, and most health problems trace back to a handful of husbandry basics, temperature, humidity, and diet, that are within an owner's control.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

This is among the most common conditions in pet tortoises. Watch for a soft or deformed shell and weak limbs. Caused by inadequate UVB or insufficient dietary calcium. ## Respiratory Infection

Wheezing, nasal discharge, open-mouth breathing, and lethargy. Caused by temperatures running chronically low, excess humidity, or drafts. Always see a vet immediately, this genuinely does not resolve on its own and needs antibiotics. Raise temperature and ensure hydration while arranging care.

<FunFact>
Pyramiding gets written up across the hobby as a solved problem with one cause, but it is not completely understood. What is consistent is the timing: it is set during the growing years, and once those raised, cone-shaped scutes form, they are permanent.
</FunFact>

## Shell Rot

Soft, discolored, or foul-smelling patches on the shell, a bacterial or fungal infection that typically enters through a cut or scrape, often worsened by an overly damp enclosure. See a vet, this can progress to septicemia, a genuine life-threatening emergency, if left untreated.

## Pyramiding

Abnormal, raised growth of the shell scutes into cone shapes rather than a smooth carapace. The condition is not completely understood, but it occurs commonly in young tortoises kept at the wrong temperature and humidity, or fed an imbalance of calcium, phosphorus, or protein.

That uncertainty is real rather than a hedge, and the research does not converge on one cause. Our [sulcata tortoise health issues guide](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-health-issues-guide/) covers the two studies that disagree most usefully, one pointing at low humidity during growth and the other at growth rate itself. What holds across all of it is that the growing years are when it is decided, and that it is not reversible afterward.

## Parasites

Roundworms and protozoa are fairly common, particularly in wild-caught animals. A fecal test is worth doing for any newly acquired tortoise, whether or not symptoms are present.

## Hexamita parva (Kidney and Urinary Tract Infection)

Hexamita parva is a flagellate protozoan, not a worm, and it targets the kidneys and urinary tract rather than the gut. Russian tortoises and other Testudo species are notably affected. A 2018 study of pet tortoises in Germany found the organism invading the renal tubules, and described chronic hexamitosis in chelonians as capable of causing severe tubular-interstitial nephritis, tubular necrosis, and gout, with infections occasionally proving fatal.

Watch for abnormally watery, jelly-like urine alongside soft droppings, both signs the kidneys are already under strain. As the infection progresses it causes real kidney damage, and by the time a tortoise looks visibly unwell the disease is often well established. Diagnosis is by examining a fresh sample of urine or feces under a microscope, since the parasite is motile and easiest to spot before it dies off in an older sample. The [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) shows what normal and abnormal urates look like side by side.

The parasite spreads when a tortoise ingests cysts shed by another infected animal, so it moves easily through a shared enclosure or water dish. An affected tortoise needs to be isolated from other tortoises immediately, and the same German study recommends a fecal screen for any tortoise before it joins a new group or enclosure. Our [chelonian herpesvirus and quarantine guide](/blog/chelonian-herpesvirus-quarantine-guide/) covers the isolation basics for any new or sick arrival.

Metronidazole is the drug typically used against Hexamita. A reptile veterinarian should set the exact dose and course for your tortoise rather than treating at home from a general figure, since dosing accounts for the individual animal's weight, kidney function, and how advanced the infection is.

<KeyTakeaway>
Watery or jelly-like urine in a Russian tortoise is worth a vet visit on its own. Isolate the tortoise from any others while you wait for results.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Bladder Stones (Urolithiasis)

Bladder stones, also called uroliths, are common in tortoises and are made almost entirely of urate salts, the same waste compound a tortoise's kidneys are meant to flush out. A 2021 survey of 101 tortoises treated at three Japanese veterinary hospitals found Russian tortoises accounted for 8.9% of urolithiasis cases, behind only African spurred and Indian star tortoises, and every analyzed stone was pure ammonium acid urate.

Chronic dehydration is the mechanism most often pointed to. Reptile kidneys cannot concentrate urine the way mammal kidneys do, so when a tortoise is not drinking enough, is kept too hot, or has no reliable water source, more water gets pulled back out of the bladder and colon to compensate. That further concentrates and supersaturates the urate already sitting in the bladder, encouraging it to crystallize into a stone. Worth noting, the same Japanese survey could not confirm a link between diet and stone formation in its own cases, several tortoises with appropriate diets still developed calculi, so dehydration and individual physiology likely matter more than food alone. Our [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) walks through how to tell if a tortoise is staying properly hydrated.

Straining and reduced appetite were the most common signs in that survey, present in roughly half and just under half of cases respectively, but some tortoises showed no outward symptoms at all until the stone was already sizable. Diagnosis is typically by X-ray. Treatment is surgical removal, and the survey found a vent approach, going in through the cloaca rather than cutting into the plastron, succeeded in the large majority of surgical cases and was less invasive than opening the shell.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD. Cold, damp, or drafty conditions cause respiratory infection, the more urgent condition on this list. Temperature, humidity and diet through the growing years decide whether pyramiding happens. And an unscreened new tortoise brings parasite risk into your collection.
