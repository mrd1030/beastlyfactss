# Common Ball Python Health Issues and What Causes Them

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ball pythons are generally hardy snakes, but they're also known for being sensitive to husbandry mistakes in specific, predictable ways. Most of what shows up on this list traces back to temperature, humidity, or hygiene, which means most of it is preventable.

## Respiratory Infection

This is the signature ball python illness, the one every keeper should be able to recognize. It's caused by temperatures that run too low, incorrect humidity, a dirty enclosure, or ongoing stress, and vets typically see a seasonal spike each winter as enclosures cool down along with the weather outside.

**Watch for:** wheezing, open-mouth breathing, visible mucus or bubbles around the mouth or nostrils, nasal discharge, lethargy, and sometimes gaping.

This needs vet-prescribed antibiotics, not a wait-and-see approach. Left untreated, it can progress to pneumonia and become fatal. Both of those are numbers a [correct setup](/blog/ball-python-tank-setup-guide/) holds without you intervening.

## Scale Rot (Infectious Dermatitis)

Caused by damp, dirty substrate, excess moisture, or general poor hygiene in the enclosure. Look for discolored belly scales (brown, reddish, or yellow), blisters or sores, scales that feel soft or damaged, and a foul odor.

Move your snake to a clean, dry enclosure immediately and correct the humidity level. Mild cases respond to antiseptic or antibiotic treatment, but anything spreading or blistering needs a vet.

## Mouth Rot (Infectious Stomatitis)

Often shows up secondary to stress, injury, or ongoing poor husbandry. Signs include pinpoint hemorrhages on the gums, thick excess mucus or pus, cheesy-looking buildup in the mouth, a sour smell, visible swelling, open-mouth breathing, and refusing food. This needs veterinary treatment, it doesn't resolve on its own.

## Mites

Look for tiny red or black specks on the scales or clustered around the eyes, along with excessive soaking or rubbing against enclosure surfaces and a drop in appetite. Mites require both treating the snake and deep-cleaning the entire enclosure, since they can persist in the environment even after the snake looks clear.

## Internal Parasites

Any newly acquired ball python should get a fecal exam before you assume it's healthy, even if it looks fine and is eating well. Parasites often cause no visible symptoms for months, and by the time signs show up the burden can already be significant. This matters even more for wild-caught and imported animals, which routinely carry a much heavier parasite load than captive-bred snakes, a result of the environments they came from and the stress of collection and shipping. Because captive-bred and imported animals get mixed together at shows and in the pet trade, you can't assume a snake is parasite-free just because a seller says it's captive-bred.

A standard fecal exam commonly turns up protozoa, nematodes, and, in more serious cases, Cryptosporidium serpentis. Most protozoa found in reptile stool are harmless commensals, but some, like Entamoeba invadens, are not and can cause serious illness in snakes. Nematodes such as Strongyloides and Rhabdias show up often and can range from a minor issue to a source of intestinal obstruction or secondary pneumonia depending on species and load. Cryptosporidium is the one that worries vets most. It causes chronic thickening of the stomach lining and repeated regurgitation after meals, has no reliably effective treatment, and can be fatal.

**Watch for:** weight loss in a snake that's still eating normally, regurgitation of recently eaten meals, and abnormal stool, watery, mucus-heavy, or containing visible worms. Checking stool and urates on a regular basis is worth doing on its own. Our [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) walks through what normal looks like and what a change might mean.

Treatment depends entirely on what the fecal test finds. Your vet will choose the right dewormer or antiprotozoal and dose based on the specific parasite identified, this isn't something to guess at or treat with an over-the-counter product.

## Shedding Problems (Dysecdysis)

Caused by humidity that's too low. Look for flaky, patchy shed that comes off in pieces rather than one clean piece, and check carefully for retained eye caps after any shed that looked incomplete. Prevent this by keeping ambient humidity at 55 to 65%, <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4vPKMjX" product="Fine mist spray bottle">raising it to 70 to 80% during the shed cycle itself</AffiliateLink>, and <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4bpnvNm" product="Sphagnum moss (for moist hide)">providing a humid hide year-round</AffiliateLink>.

If skin is still stuck once the humidity is right, a humidity chamber is the gentler fix for this species: a lidded tub with a warm, damp towel inside, kept warm and covered so it is dark, with the snake in it for no more than an hour. Most of the remaining skin comes away within a day, and the chamber can be repeated. A short, supervised soak is the other option, and the [snake shedding guide](/blog/snake-shedding-humidity-myth-guide/) gives its depth and time. Never pull at skin that has not loosened.

<FunFact>
Retained eye caps, where the old clear scale over the eye fails to come off during a shed, can be one of the easiest health problems to miss since the snake's vision and behavior often look completely normal. It's exactly why every shed is worth a quick visual check rather than just confirming the snake looks fine overall.
</FunFact>

## Dehydration

Yellow, orange or gritty urates are usually the first sign. Past that, look for wrinkled or loose skin outside a shed, skin that stays tented when gently pinched instead of springing back, sunken eyes or dented eye caps, and sheds that keep coming off in pieces. A snake heading into a shed can look wrinkled too, so judge the skin once the eyes have cleared.

The usual causes are a snake not drinking enough and humidity running too low. Fix the water bowl and the humidity first; a shallow, supervised soak at enclosure temperature can get a mildly dehydrated snake drinking. A snake that is sunken-eyed and lethargic needs a vet, which can give fluids by injection or stomach tube.

A common, entirely preventable injury caused by heat sources running without a thermostat. ## Inclusion Body Disease (IBD)

IBD is a viral disease that shows up as neurological symptoms, most recognizably "stargazing," where the snake holds its head up and back at an odd angle as if staring at the sky. Other signs include tremors, abnormal tongue flicking, disorientation, loss of the righting reflex, meaning the snake can't right itself when flipped over, and in advanced cases, seizures.

The disease is caused by reptarenaviruses and primarily affects boas and pythons, the two groups that between them include boa constrictors and ball pythons. Boas can carry the virus for months or years with few outward signs, while pythons tend to develop severe, faster-progressing disease once symptoms appear. It spreads through body fluids, including bites during breeding, fight wounds, and snake mites carrying infected blood between animals. That last route is why [a real quarantine is measured in months](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/), since a carrier can look completely healthy for the whole window.

<KeyTakeaway>
There is no cure for IBD. Diagnosis requires PCR testing and biopsy, and once neurological signs appear, most vets discuss euthanasia rather than treatment. This is exactly why quarantining any new snake for months, away from your existing collection, isn't optional. It's the only real defense against IBD reaching animals you already own.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Prolapse

A prolapse is when internal tissue pushes out through the vent and becomes visible from outside the body. What prolapses can vary, most often it's the cloaca itself, but it can also be the hemipenes in males or a section of the colon. This is always an emergency, not a wait-and-see situation.

If you see prolapsed tissue, keep it clean and moist with a damp paper towel or clean cloth, and get to a vet immediately. Exposed tissue dries out and swells quickly, and the longer it stays out, the harder it is to save. Do not try to push it back in yourself.

Prolapse isn't a standalone disease, it's a symptom of something else going wrong. Straining from constipation or impaction, dehydration, internal parasites, egg binding, or bladder stones can all cause a snake to push hard enough that tissue prolapses. Because of that, a vet visit for prolapse isn't just about the visible tissue, it's about finding and fixing whatever caused the snake to strain in the first place. Without addressing the underlying cause, prolapse tends to recur.

## Females: Follicles, Eggs and Egg Binding

A female does not need a male to cycle. Males can breed from about 16 to 18 months and females from about 27 to 31 months, and a female's readiness follows weight as much as age: breeding females mature at around 1,500 g, at 2 to 2.5 years. Her ovaries carry small follicles all year. In the breeding season they grow, and without fertilization they are normally reabsorbed. A young female around 800 to 1,000 g often stops eating for weeks while follicles develop, and the answer is smaller meals offered less often and a weekly weigh-in, with a vet if she starts losing weight fast.

**When she does ovulate,** a firm swelling appears in the rear third of the body and stays for several days. She sheds once more before laying, and the eggs come about 30 days after that pre-lay shed, or roughly 35 to 62 days after ovulation. A typical clutch is about six eggs, and a female kept alone can lay infertile ones.

**Egg binding (dystocia)** is eggs she cannot pass, and it can kill her. A healthy female carrying eggs often refuses food but stays bright, alert and active. A female with dystocia usually stops eating, turns weak and may look swollen, and untreated cases slide into severe lethargy until she stops responding. Reptiles can hold eggs for weeks or months past the normal laying time, which makes the change easy to miss, so any female who turns weak or lethargic around laying time, or who is past it and losing condition, sees a reptile vet promptly. Most dystocia cases vets see are single females that never met a male. Follicles that neither ovulate nor reabsorb, called follicular stasis, are a separate problem and a common reason for surgery too.

The usual causes are a setup running too hot or too cold, humidity off, nowhere suitable to lay, dehydration, and poor condition. Treatment ranges from fluids, calcium and hormone injections to surgery, and for a pet female a vet may recommend removing the ovaries and oviducts so it cannot happen again.

## When to See a Vet

- Any wheezing, open-mouth breathing, or visible mucus, this one is urgent
- Belly scale discoloration that's spreading or blistering
- Signs of mouth rot
- A prolapse (keep the area moist and get to a vet as soon as possible)
- Visible mites
- A female who turns weak, swollen or lethargic around laying time, or is past it and losing condition
- Sunken eyes and lethargy together, the sign of serious dehydration

## A Note on Appetite Refusal

Ball pythons are famous for going on hunger strikes, and a healthy-looking snake in good body condition that refuses a meal or two isn't automatically a health emergency. If husbandry checks out (correct temperature, correct humidity, no other symptoms), it's often reasonable to offer food again in a couple of weeks before assuming something is wrong. Work through the usual causes in this order, the enclosure first: temperature or humidity off, a shed underway, stress from a new home or too much handling, breeding season from October to March (males especially), and the seasonal slowdown of cooler months. If refusal continues alongside weight loss or any other symptom from this list, that's when it's time to get a vet involved.
