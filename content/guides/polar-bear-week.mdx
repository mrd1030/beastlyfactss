A polar bear has black skin under a coat that looks white, and the hairs that make up that coat are close to transparent when they lie flat. For a couple of decades a tidy story linked those three facts together, and it ended up in physics textbooks before anyone thought to measure it.

## The Fiber Optic Story

The idea grew out of a 1970s finding by researchers in Guelph and Oslo that polar bear pelts reflect very little ultraviolet light. A few decades later a team from the Boston area proposed an explanation: the hairs were collecting ultraviolet light and channeling it down their length to the black skin, which absorbed it as heat. The bear, on this account, wore a coat of solar collectors.

The team did not test it. The idea traveled anyway, into the Christian Science Monitor, the New York Times and Time magazine, and into at least one physics textbook, which is where Daniel Koon, a physicist at St. Lawrence University, first came across it.

Koon and a student, Reid Hutchins, took hairs from a male polar bear at a zoo in Rochester, New York, and measured how much light traveled along a typical inch-long hair. Less than 0.001 percent of red light made it from one end to the other. Less than a trillionth of the violet light did. Ultraviolet, the light the whole theory depended on, did even worse.

Koon published the result in Applied Optics in 1998 under the title "Is polar bear hair fiber optic?", reporting attenuation of 2 to 8 decibels per millimeter in the visible range. A hair that loses that much light over a few millimeters is absorbing it, and it cannot be delivering any to the skin. The likely culprit is keratin, the protein hair is made of, which researchers at Penn State had already suggested was behind the pelt's ultraviolet absorption, and Koon agrees.

Koon's own verdict on why the story lasted is the useful part: people want to believe in the fantastic, and the fantastic is often accepted without being tested.

## A Week Timed to Freeze-Up

Polar Bear Week is the first week of November, November 1st to 7th in 2026. Polar Bears International created it and sets the dates each year, and it timed the week to the annual gathering of polar bears near Churchill, Manitoba, on the western shore of Hudson Bay.

The bears are there because they are waiting. Hudson Bay loses its ice entirely in summer, and a polar bear hunts seals from the ice. Through the ice-free months the bears come ashore and live mostly on stored fat. In the fall they gather near Churchill to wait for the ice to return, and once it does they go back out to hunt.

That ice-free stretch has grown by three to four weeks since the 1980s, and every extra week is drawn from the same fat reserve. The trend is not confined to Hudson Bay. Harry Stern and Kristin Laidre examined sea ice in all 19 polar bear subpopulation regions from 1979 to 2014 and found earlier spring retreat and later fall advance in every one of them, by roughly 3 to 9 days per decade at each end, with the number of ice-covered days falling by 7 to 19 days per decade.

The same timing governs the narwhal, which [follows the ice into fjords as it retreats and back offshore as it returns](/blog/narwhal-facts-the-real-unicorn-of-the-sea/), and at the far end of the planet it decides whether an [emperor penguin colony on fast ice](/beastlypedia/emperor-penguin/) gets a breeding season at all. Polar bears and penguins never meet. The ice problem is shared.

## What the Hudson Bay Count Shows

The Western Hudson Bay population, the Churchill bears, has been studied by Environment and Climate Change Canada since the 1980s and surveyed from the air by the Government of Nunavut every five years. The estimates ran 949 bears in 2011, 842 in 2016 and 618 in 2021. The population was already considered to be in decline after the 2016 count, and the 2021 figure, released in late 2022, read as a steep further drop.

The picture has since grown more complicated. The neighboring Southern Hudson Bay population was surveyed at the same time, and taken together the two came to 1,622 bears in 2016 and 1,621 in 2021, which the Government of Nunavut describes as stable across the pair. A movement study by Environment and Climate Change Canada helps explain the 2021 drop in Western Hudson Bay: bears moving south into the neighboring area, likely drawn by ice that lingered there near the time of the survey.

So the 618 is a real count of the bears that were in Western Hudson Bay that summer, and it is far less clear evidence of bears dying than it first appeared. What it does show is how tightly the animals track ice: a pocket of late ice in one part of the bay was enough to account for most of a 224-bear fall in another, in a single season.

Both halves of the polar bear story went the same way. A tidy explanation for the fur reached textbooks before anyone measured a hair, by the same route that left International Sloth Day with one year's date [copied until it looked verified](/blog/international-sloth-day/). One survey then read as a collapse before anyone tracked where the bears had gone. In both cases the measurement was less dramatic than the story and more useful, and the number that matters for the Churchill bears is still the one Polar Bear Week is timed to: the day the bay freezes.
