# Common Guppy Health Issues (Stress, Water Quality, and Quarantine)

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Most guppy illness traces back to stress, poor water quality, overcrowding, or skipped quarantine, and each of those is something a keeper controls.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white dots like grains of salt on the skin and fins, often preceded by flashing (rubbing against tank decor), extra mucus, weakness, rapid breathing, lethargy, and loss of appetite. Ich is a parasite that arrives with new fish or shared equipment and spreads quickly from fish to fish, and outbreaks tend to follow stress and rapid temperature changes. Treat by raising the tank gradually to around 82°F, the top of the guppy's range, alongside a commercial ich medication. The warmth shortens the parasite's life cycle, and the medication does the killing: in warm water it goes in daily, for about 3 to 7 treatments depending on the product.

Guppies live in fresh water and in some brackish water in the wild, and their salinity range is wide. That is why a salt bath of 4 to 5 parts per thousand for 7 to 10 days is an option for them against ich in a small tank, a method that only suits fish that can handle the extra salt. Guppies are among them, so a salt course, dissolved first and raised gradually, is a sound alternative to medication for this species.

<FunFact>
Guppies turn up in almost every kind of water across their range, from highly turbid ponds, canals and ditches at low elevations to pristine mountain streams at high elevations.
</FunFact>

## Velvet

Small golden or rust-colored spots or a fine dusty coating on the body, along with clamped fins and scratching against decor. Treat with a copper-based medication, though a fish vet may use chloroquine instead, and plan a recheck after 7 to 10 days. This is contagious between fish, so quarantine affected individuals if possible. Velvet is caused by a parasite, not by the water itself. Raising the temperature slightly and dimming the tank lights during treatment is common practice.

## Fin and Tail Rot

Ragged, frayed, or discolored fin edges can mean a bacterial infection, with poor water quality, ammonia buildup, stress, or an untreated injury often behind it. Improving water quality alongside an antibacterial treatment often clears a case caught early. Many bacterial infections respond to antibiotics, but lab testing is needed to know which drug will work, so a fish vet is the route for a case that does not improve once the water is corrected. Left unaddressed, it can progress and become systemic.

## Dropsy

Bloating combined with scales that raise into a pinecone-like pattern. Dropsy is a sign, not a single disease. A bacterial infection with Aeromonas is one cause, and it may respond to antibiotics once lab testing picks the right one. Other causes, such as kidney disease from certain parasites, have no practical treatment, so the outlook depends on what is behind the swelling. By the time the pinecone pattern is visibly obvious, the outlook is often poor.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems, floating, sinking, or swimming at odd angles, which can follow overfeeding or constipation. Often manageable at home with a short fast and a return to normal, appropriately sized feeding afterward.

## Camallanus: Red Threads at the Vent

A cluster of blood-red threads hanging from a guppy's vent is Camallanus, a parasitic roundworm that infects guppies and other aquarium fish. An infected fish passes larvae in its waste. Tiny crustaceans such as cyclops and daphnia eat them, and a guppy that eats those crustaceans picks up the worm. The larvae can also infect fish directly, and the parasite can keep reproducing in a tank with no crustaceans for several months, so water changes alone do not clear it. The worms are about ¼ to ½ inch long. A few worms are enough to do serious harm in a fish this small. Shared plants, gravel and nets carry it between tanks too.

Treatment is a dewormer, either levamisole or fenbendazole, dosed to the whole tank following the label or a vet's directions. Treat every fish in the tank, since the ones without visible worms may still carry them. A 25% water change a day later helps, and the drug does not reach the larvae, so the tank gets a second dose two weeks after the first.

## When Treatment Stops Working

A fish still wasting after a full course of treatment, or one with pinecone-stage swelling, calls for a fish vet, because only some causes of dropsy respond to treatment. The professional directories that list [vets who treat fish](/blog/spotting-a-sick-fish-guide/) cover North America and beyond. When nothing is left to try, a clove oil overdose is an accepted euthanasia method for fish, though it is used extra-label, and the fish stays in the solution for at least 10 minutes after the gills stop moving. Putting a live fish in the freezer is not humane. Flushing a live guppy is not humane either. Use a small container of the guppy's own tank water for the clove oil.
