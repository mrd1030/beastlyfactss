# Is It Legal to Own a Bearded Dragon? State-by-State Guide

The bearded dragon is the default answer when someone asks which lizard a beginner should start with, and the legal map reflects that. Forty-six jurisdictions place no restriction on it at all. The interesting part is not where it is restricted, which is a short and predictable list, but the fact that the country it comes from will never send another one.

## Maine Names It, Which Changes What Applies

Most of Maine's reptile clearances work at family or genus level, and each carries an asterisk stripping out anything the IUCN rates endangered, critically endangered or extinct in the wild. The bearded dragon is not cleared that way. Pogona vitticeps appears on the Unrestricted Species List by species.

That distinction has a practical effect: a species-level entry carries no asterisk, so no conservation-status filter is ever applied to it. Compare the [crested gecko](/blog/crested-gecko-legal-guide/), which Maine clears at infraorder rank and where the asterisk becomes the thing worth checking.

What does apply is the heading above the whole Reptilia section: captive bred origin only. For this species that is close to a formality, since captive bred is the only kind there is, but it is the condition that makes Maine conditional rather than clear.

## The Two Absolutes Are Not About This Animal

Hawaii names the inland bearded dragon on Part A of its Restricted Animal List. Part A is headed for research and exhibition, so the listing does not create a permit a keeper could apply for. It creates a route for institutions and closes the one for households.

The District of Columbia never mentions the species. Section 8-1808(j)(1) bars possession of wildlife with seven exceptions, and the reptile exception at (E) covers non-venomous snakes, fish and turtles. No lizard appears anywhere in the seven, which is what bars the bearded dragon, the green iguana and the tokay gecko in a single stroke, none of them individually considered.

New Jersey's permit is likewise structural. The state bars possession of any exotic or nongame species without a permit and then names the handful that may be kept without one. The bearded dragon is not among them, so the permit requirement arrives by default rather than by decision.

## Australia Closed the Door in 1982

Australia banned the commercial export of all live native vertebrates in 1982, under the Wildlife Protection (Regulation of Exports and Imports) Act, and the prohibition carried through into the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 that replaced it. Live native reptiles have not been legally exportable for commercial purposes in over forty years.

The consequence for a buyer is straightforward: the American trade is entirely captive-bred, and there is no lawful wild-caught supply to worry about. That removes a category of concern that still follows the [tokay gecko](/blog/tokay-gecko-legal-guide/) and a good deal of the imported invertebrate trade, where wild collection remains ordinary.

No US state rule turns on any of this. It is the background to why the animal in the shop is what it is.

## West Virginia Regulates the Shipment, Not the Species

West Virginia never named the bearded dragon, and the rule that reaches it is not on any species list. W. Va. Code § 20-2-13 says that "no person shall transport into or have in his possession within this state any live wildlife or viable eggs thereof from without the state, except as authorized by an importation permit issued by the director." Wildlife is defined in § 20-1-2 as wild birds, wild animals, game and fur-bearing animals, fish, reptiles, amphibians, mollusks and crustaceans, and the reptile and amphibian clause carries no nativity qualifier and no captive-bred exception. A bearded dragon shipped in from a breeder is therefore live wildlife from without the state.

The argument that an out-of-state animal sits outside the chapter is closed before it can be made: § 20-2-4(b) says wildlife lawfully taken outside of this state is subject to the same laws and rules as wildlife taken within it. What is missing is the other half. No rule in Title 58 implements § 20-2-13 outside aquaculture, the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for an importation permit, and the only penalty the section spells out is written for coyotes. The requirement is clear on the page and invisible in practice, which is the honest thing to tell someone moving to Huntington with a tank in the car. Neither of West Virginia’s two species lists touches it: the dangerous wild animal rule at W. Va. C.S.R. § 74-1-3 is a mammal list plus the Komodo dragon, and the reptile rule at § 58-73 reaches native species only.

## What to Check Before Buying

An adult bearded dragon needs a four foot enclosure and lives eight to twelve years, so the question is where you will be, not only where you are.
