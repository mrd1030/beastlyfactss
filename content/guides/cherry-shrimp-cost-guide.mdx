# How Much Does Cherry Shrimp Ownership Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Cherry shrimp are cheap to buy, but the budgeting question isn't the price tag on the shrimp itself, it's how fast a starting group of ten turns into a colony of fifty without you doing anything on purpose.

## The Shrimp: Roughly $4 to $8 Each, Depending on Grade

Cherry shrimp are graded by how solid and opaque their red coloring is, and price climbs with grade. At [Flip Aquatics](https://flipaquatics.com/products/red-cherry-shrimp), a 10-pack of standard Red Cherry Shrimp runs $49.99 (about $5 a shrimp) and a 20-pack runs $94.99 (about $4.75 a shrimp). Their higher-grade [Painted Fire Red Shrimp](https://flipaquatics.com/products/painted-fire-red-shrimp) cost more, a 5-pack at $39.99 and a 10-pack at $74.99, roughly $7.50 to $8 a shrimp. [Aquatic Arts](https://aquaticarts.com/collections/shrimp-combo-boxes/products/bloody-mary-shrimp-neocaridina-davidi-bredby-aquatic-arts) prices their top-grade Bloody Mary shrimp from $10.99 for a single animal down to about $6.15 each in a 20-pack.

That's a real range worth knowing before you shop: standard grades cluster closer to $4 to $5 a shrimp, and the deepest-colored grades run $6 to $8. Buying in a small group rather than one or two shrimp lowers the per-shrimp price at every retailer we checked, and it also gives a young colony a healthier starting gene pool. Local fish stores and bulk marketplace listings can undercut these mail-order prices, since specialty retailers are also charging for live-arrival guarantees and, in [Flip Aquatics's](https://flipaquatics.com/products/red-cherry-shrimp) case, a 30-day in-house quarantine before anything ships.

Grading itself has no regulated industry standard. Every source agrees the least colorful, most translucent shrimp sit at the bottom (often just called "regular" or Sakura grade) and that opacity climbs from there, but which of Fire Red, Painted Fire Red, or Bloody Mary counts as the very top grade genuinely varies by seller. Every grade is the same animal, *Neocaridina davidi*, with identical care needs regardless of price tag, and the same is true of the separate blue, yellow, orange, chocolate, black, and clear-banded "Rili" color lines the species has also been bred into. If you'd rather skip the color premium entirely, [ghost shrimp](/guides/ghost-shrimp/) are a related but far cheaper option, though they're less colorful and, as our health issues guide touches on, not always a safe tankmate for cherry shrimp specifically.

Aquariadise names [5 gallons as a workable minimum tank size](https://www.aquariadise.com/caresheet-cherry-shrimp/), and Aquarium Co-Op notes cherry shrimp can technically survive in a 2-gallon nano tank, though they recommend [10 gallons or larger for a healthy breeding colony](https://www.aquariumcoop.com/blogs/aquarium/cherry-shrimp-care).

Add substrate and basic decor, and most complete first-time setups land around $80 to $150. Skip the heater if your room stays reliably above the high 60s, more on that below.

Prices last checked September 2026 at LiveAquaria and PetSmart. Retail prices move; treat the table as a snapshot, not a quote.

Food and conditioner are genuinely cheap for a colony this size, a single container of sinking wafers lasts a small group most of a year. The one recurring cost that table leaves out is a [mineral or GH-boosting supplement](/blog/cherry-shrimp-tank-setup-guide/), typically a few dollars every few months, that keeps the water hard enough for shrimp to build a solid new shell after every molt. The test kit is a purchase you'll rely on repeatedly rather than a one-off, since cherry shrimp tolerate very little ammonia or nitrite.

## Vet Costs

Not applicable in the traditional sense. There's essentially no practical veterinary care available for aquarium invertebrates this small, so husbandry and prevention do all the work here, not treatment after the fact.

## Lifespan

Up to 2 years under ideal, stable conditions, according to [Aquariadise](https://www.aquariadise.com/caresheet-cherry-shrimp/), though they note shrimp in a typical home aquarium often average closer to just the first year. Budget for equipment that keeps water parameters stable for that whole span rather than just the initial setup, since parameter swings are a bigger threat to this species than to most fish.

<FunFact>
A cherry shrimp colony that grows faster than you expected isn't just a fun surprise, it's also why you should never dump extras in a local pond, creek, or drainage ditch. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's [2025 ecological risk screening](https://www.fws.gov/media/ecological-risk-screening-summary-cherry-shrimp-neocaridina-davidi-high-risk) rates *Neocaridina davidi* a High overall invasion risk for the contiguous United States, with a strong climate match in the Great Lakes region, peninsular Florida, the southern Great Plains, and parts of the Rocky Mountains. In Europe, the agency notes established wild populations are currently limited to thermally polluted waterways, warm water discharge sites where a tropical-leaning shrimp can survive a cold climate year-round. A thriving colony is exactly the point of keeping this species, just find a local fish store or online forum to rehome extras rather than releasing any.
</FunFact>

## Why Cherry Shrimp Stay Cheap to Keep

Low individual price, low equipment cost, and low ongoing spend all stack in cherry shrimp's favor, and the fact that a starting colony breeds itself into a bigger one means you're rarely buying replacement stock the way you might with a fish that doesn't reproduce in a home tank. That's a genuine point of contrast with [amano shrimp](/guides/amano-shrimp/), a similarly priced invertebrate whose larvae need brackish water to survive, so an amano colony never grows on its own the way a cherry shrimp colony does.
