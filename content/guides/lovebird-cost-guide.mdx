# How Much Does a Lovebird Really Cost?

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Lovebirds are one of the more affordable parrots to buy, and the name suggests something gentler than what you get. This is a small bird with a real capacity for territorial behavior, which matters for cost planning as much as temperament.

## The Bird Itself: %%animal:lovebird%%

Common peach-faced or Fischer's lovebirds run $50 to $150. Rare color mutations, lutino, blue, pied, and combinations, run $200 to $400 or more, with exceptional individuals reaching $1,000. Adoption typically runs $20 to $100.

Hand-raised birds cost more than parent-raised ones, and they're generally the better choice if you want a tame, interactive pet. A parent-raised bird can still make a fine companion, but expect more work getting there.

Avoid the $30 to $50 range entirely, cheap cages rust, and a rusting cage introduces a genuine zinc toxicity risk, a real veterinary emergency, not a minor concern.

That "breathable cage cover" line item deserves an honest note. Most nighttime products marketed for pet birds as "sleep tents" are actually enclosed pouch or hut-shaped bird tents, and for a lovebird specifically, that shape is a real problem, not a style preference. A plain, breathable cage cover for nighttime darkness serves the same purpose without the risk. Skip tunnel-shaped, nest-like "happy hut" products marketed as bird tents, they're a documented trigger for hormonal, territorial behavior and chronic egg-laying in this species.

## Vet Costs

A routine avian checkup runs %%vet:lovebird%%, recommended once or twice a year, and annual care can add up to a couple hundred dollars total. Emergencies are the real budgeting risk here: egg-binding or an injury can run into the thousands. An emergency fund is worth having for this species.

## Lifespan

10 to 15 years typically, up to 20 with excellent care. This is a multi-decade relationship for a bird often sold as a casual, low-commitment pet, worth sitting with before you buy.

## The Cost Decision That Matters Most: One Bird or Two

This is where lovebird budgeting gets interesting, and it isn't about money at all. A single, hand-raised lovebird bonds to you and becomes tame and interactive, but needs real daily time investment: interaction and time out of the cage every day, not just on weekends. A bonded pair bonds to each other instead, largely ignoring you, entertaining to watch but rarely tame or reliable to handle.

<FunFact>
The decision to buy one lovebird or two isn't a minor preference, it changes the entire pet relationship. One hand-raised bird can end up riding on your shoulder and stepping up on command. A bonded pair will bond to each other so completely that you become little more than the source of food, closer to keeping a pair of finches than a tame parrot.
</FunFact>

If you can't commit meaningful daily time, two birds that keep each other company is honestly the more humane choice, even though it changes what kind of pet relationship you'll actually have.
