# Green Iguana Feeding: Building the Salad and the Protein Mistake

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

A green iguana is a folivore. Wild animals work through leaves, blossoms and fruit, and they carry enlarged ceca adapted for hindgut fermentation. Everything below follows from that one anatomical fact.

## How Much of the Bowl Is Greens

Dark leafy greens are the majority of the diet, and the two veterinary sources that state a proportion do not fully agree on the rest. VCA Animal Hospitals gives 80 to 90% dark green leafy vegetables with less than 20% of the diet as fruit. LafeberVet says dark, leafy greens should make up the majority of the adult diet and caps fruit at no more than 5%, naming papaya, melon and banana as the nutrient dense choices.

Both bounds are worth knowing, because the gap between 5% and 20% fruit is the difference between a treat and a habit. The lower figure is the safer target for an animal whose common problems are calcium related.

## The Greens to Build On

The calcium-rich greens are collard, beet, mustard and turnip greens, alfalfa hay, bok choy, kale, parsley, Swiss chard, romaine, kohlrabi, escarole, chicory and dandelion. For the vegetable share, squash, zucchini, sweet potato, broccoli, peas and carrots.

Rotate across the week instead of settling on one green. A veterinary diet history for this species specifically watches for nutrients diluted by too much added fruit, so variety in the greens matters more than variety in the sweet things.

Some of that salad does not have to be bought. The [safe weeds and wild plants list](/blog/herbivorous-reptile-safe-plants-guide/) covers what is safe to pick for a herbivorous reptile.

## Oxalates and Goitrogens

Two separate problems get flattened into one warning on most care sheets, and they are not the same.

Oxalates bind calcium. Swiss chard, spinach and beet greens are themselves rich in it, and should still never be the only greens on offer, because the same plants block the uptake of dietary calcium and trace minerals. The point arrives from the other direction too, in the standing advice to favor low-oxalate vegetables to reduce the likelihood of kidney stones.

Goitrogens affect the thyroid. Cabbage, kale and mustard greens carry them, and in quantity they can push the thyroid toward goiter, leaving the gland enlarged and working poorly.

Note that both lists include greens the same page recommends. Kale and mustard greens appear on the calcium-rich list and in the goitrogen warning; chard, spinach and beet greens appear as calcium sources and as oxalate risks. The instruction that follows is not to ban them but to keep them off the base of the salad and rotate them in.

Iceberg lettuce is a different case. It carries little of anything, so it displaces food that would have done some work.

## Animal Protein

The gut described at the top does not run on it. Diets high in protein may predispose reptiles to uric acid accumulation, which can cause gout: urates deposited on visceral and articular surfaces, with the suspected causes being inappropriate dietary nitrogen and dehydration. For an animal that excretes uric acid instead of urea, a protein load and a hydration problem push in the same direction.

Sources are not unanimous on an absolute ban. VCA allows foods high in animal protein at under 5% of an adult iguana's diet, and up to 10% for young growing animals. That tolerance is real and worth stating plainly.

The recommendation here is still to leave animal protein out. A plant diet supplies protein on its own, and the protein sources a vet looks for in a mixed salad are romaine and legumes, nothing from an animal. Nothing an iguana needs arrives only in an insect, so the upside of feeding one is nothing and the downside is a renal system doing work it was not built for. The [health issues guide](/blog/green-iguana-health-issues-guide/) covers what that damage looks like once it is done, and why it is usually found late.

## Calcium and Vitamins

Supplementation is scheduled by age, and the two sources differ slightly in how heavy the adult schedule is.

VCA gives young iguanas a reptile-specific calcium powder, calcium carbonate or gluconate, every other day, about 4 to 5 times per week, alongside a multivitamin containing vitamin D twice a week. Adults drop to a calcium supplement lightly sprinkled on food once to twice per week, with a vitamin D multivitamin twice a month.

LafeberVet dusts a non-breeding adult once weekly instead, and asks that the supplement be low in phosphorus, at a minimum 2:1 calcium to phosphorus ratio. Target dietary calcium for these lizards sits at roughly 1.5 to 2.0% against phosphorus near 0.8%, with a maximum tolerance around 2.5%.

A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D82DPB1D?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Repashy SuperVeggie, 6 oz Jar">plant-eater supplement</AffiliateLink> handles the mineral side, but supplements do not work in isolation. Dietary vitamin D3 and [UVB exposure](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) are the two halves of the same calcium problem, and an iguana that gets the powder and no usable UVB still ends up short.

Over-supplementing is its own hazard, not insurance: hypervitaminosis D often causes severe kidney damage, which is why the diet has to be right instead of merely generous.

## How Much, and How Often

The sources are thinner here than on composition. The general reptile figure is 1 to 4% of body weight daily on a dry-matter basis, with herbivore pellets at 25 to 50% of the diet, which is a ceiling on how much of the bowl a commercial product should be doing.

The veterinary literature is blunt that commercial diets alone are inadequate for this species, and treats a diet history full of them as a warning sign. Fresh greens do the work; a pellet fills a gap in it.
