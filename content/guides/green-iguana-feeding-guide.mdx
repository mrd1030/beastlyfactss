# Green Iguana Feeding: Building the Salad and the Protein Mistake

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

A green iguana is a folivore. Wild animals work through leaves, blossoms and fruit, and they carry enlarged ceca adapted for hindgut fermentation, which is the 1996 Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians summary of what the gut is actually built to process. Everything below follows from that one anatomical fact.

## How Much of the Bowl Is Greens

Dark leafy greens are the majority of the diet, and the two veterinary sources that state a proportion do not fully agree on the rest. VCA Animal Hospitals gives 80 to 90% dark green leafy vegetables with less than 20% of the diet as fruit. LafeberVet says dark, leafy greens should make up the majority of the adult diet and caps fruit at no more than 5%, naming papaya, melon and banana as the nutrient dense choices.

Both bounds are worth knowing, because the gap between 5% and 20% fruit is the difference between a treat and a habit. The lower figure is the safer target for an animal whose common problems are calcium related.

## The Greens to Build On

VCA's calcium-rich list runs collard greens, beet greens, mustard greens, turnip greens, alfalfa hay, bok choy, kale, parsley, Swiss chard, romaine, kohlrabi, escarole and dandelion. LafeberVet's overlaps closely and adds chicory, then lists squash, zucchini, sweet potato, broccoli, peas and carrots as the vegetable share.

Rotate across the week rather than settling on one green. The 1996 ARAV proceedings on green iguana nutrition flags the dilution of nutrients from excessive additions of fruit to commercial diets as a specific thing to watch in a diet history, so variety in the greens matters more than variety in the sweet things.

Some of that salad does not have to be bought. The [safe weeds and wild plants list](/blog/herbivorous-reptile-safe-plants-guide/) covers what is safe to pick for a herbivorous reptile.

## Oxalates and Goitrogens

Two separate problems get flattened into one warning on most care sheets, and they are not the same.

Oxalates bind calcium. VCA's wording is that Swiss chard, spinach and beet greens are a great source of calcium but should not be fed exclusively, because these plants prevent absorption of dietary calcium and trace minerals. The MSD Veterinary Manual makes the same point from the other direction, recommending vegetables with a low oxalate content to decrease the likelihood of kidney stone formation.

Goitrogens affect the thyroid. VCA notes that cabbage, kale and mustard greens contain goitrogens, chemicals that can lead to goiter, or hypothyroidism, in which the thyroid gland becomes enlarged and functions improperly.

Note that both lists include greens the same sources recommend. Kale and mustard greens appear on VCA's calcium-rich list and in its goitrogen warning; chard, spinach and beet greens appear as calcium sources and as oxalate risks. The instruction that follows is not to ban them but to keep them off the base of the salad and rotate them in.

Iceberg lettuce is a different case. It carries little of anything, so it displaces food that would have done some work.

## Animal Protein

The gut described at the top does not run on it. The MSD Veterinary Manual states that diets high in protein may predispose reptiles to uric acid accumulation, which can cause gout, and the ARAV proceedings describe gout as the deposition of urates on visceral and articular surfaces, with etiopathologic theories including inappropriate dietary nitrogen levels and dehydration. For an animal that excretes uric acid rather than urea, a protein load and a hydration problem push in the same direction.

Sources are not unanimous on an absolute ban. VCA allows foods that include large amounts of animal-based protein at less than 5% of an adult iguana's total diet, and up to 10% for young growing iguanas. That tolerance is real and worth stating rather than hiding.

The recommendation here is still to leave animal protein out. A plant diet supplies protein on its own, and the ARAV paper's guidance to look for protein sources in a mixed salad names romaine and legumes rather than anything from an animal. Nothing an iguana needs arrives only in an insect, so the upside of feeding one is nothing and the downside is a renal system doing work it was not built for. The [health issues guide](/blog/green-iguana-health-issues-guide/) covers what that damage looks like once it is done, and why it is usually found late.

## Calcium and Vitamins

Supplementation is scheduled by age, and the two sources differ slightly in how heavy the adult schedule is.

VCA gives young iguanas a reptile-specific calcium powder, calcium carbonate or gluconate, every other day, about 4 to 5 times per week, alongside a multivitamin containing vitamin D twice a week. Adults drop to a calcium supplement lightly sprinkled on food once to twice per week, with a vitamin D multivitamin twice a month.

LafeberVet dusts the non-breeding adult's diet with calcium carbonate or calcium gluconate once weekly, and asks that the supplement be devoid of or low in phosphorus, with a minimum calcium to phosphorus ratio of 2:1. The ARAV proceedings put target dietary calcium for these lizards at roughly 1.5 to 2.0%, with phosphorus near 0.8%, and note a maximum tolerance around 2.5% calcium.

Dietary vitamin D3 and [UVB exposure](/blog/uvb-lighting-complete-guide/) are the two halves of the same calcium problem, and an iguana that gets the powder and no usable UVB still ends up short.

Over-supplementing is its own hazard rather than insurance. VCA notes that hypervitaminosis D often causes severe kidney damage, so a proper diet is essential.

## How Much, and How Often

The sources are thinner here than on composition. The MSD Veterinary Manual gives a general reptile figure of 1 to 4% of body weight daily on a dry-matter basis, and puts herbivorous reptile pellets at 25 to 50% of herbivorous reptile diets, which is a ceiling on how much of the bowl a commercial product should be doing.

The ARAV paper is blunt that commercial diets alone are inadequate for this species, and treats a diet history full of them as a warning sign rather than a shortcut. Fresh greens do the work; a pellet fills a gap in it.
