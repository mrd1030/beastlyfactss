The tiger is the largest cat alive and one of the most recognizable animals on the planet. Almost everyone can draw one.

The most recent global assessment puts the wild population between about 3,700 and 5,600 animals, cubs excluded, of which fewer than 3,200 are mature breeding adults. That is the total across thirteen countries, for the entire species. A modest town holds more people than there are wild tigers on Earth.

## What Was Lost

A century ago the estimate was around 100,000. Tigers ranged from eastern Turkey across Central Asia, through the Indian subcontinent, into China, Korea and the Russian Far East, and south through Indonesia. They occupied an enormous span of habitat, from Siberian snow forest to tropical mangrove.

They now occupy about 7 percent of that historic range. What remains is fragmented: separate populations in separate patches, often too far apart for animals to move between them, which is a genetic problem on top of a numbers problem.

Three subspecies are already gone. The Bali tiger was extinct by the 1940s, the Caspian tiger by the 1970s, and the Javan tiger by the 1980s. The South China tiger has not been reliably confirmed in the wild for decades and is generally treated as functionally extinct in the wild, though captive animals remain.

## The Part Almost Nobody Expects

Here is the fact that reliably surprises people. There are likely more tigers in captivity in the United States than in the wild anywhere on Earth.

Estimates for the US captive population have ranged from around 5,000 upward, though the honest position is that nobody has a firm number, and that is itself the point. Accredited zoos account for only a few hundred. The rest sit with private owners, roadside attractions, and unaccredited facilities, under a patchwork of state rules that historically ranged from strict licensing to essentially nothing.

The Big Cat Public Safety Act, signed in 2022, restricted private ownership and direct public contact such as cub petting, which has tightened things considerably. But an animal that is genuinely scarce in the wild and simultaneously abundant in private hands is an odd situation, and the captive population contributes nothing to wild recovery: most of it is of unknown or mixed subspecies ancestry and is not part of any managed breeding program.

<KeyTakeaway>
Between 3,700 and 5,600 tigers remain in the wild across thirteen countries, fewer than 3,200 of them breeding adults. The number held privately in the United States alone has been estimated as comparable, and almost none of it is of known subspecies or usable for conservation.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Stripes Go Deeper Than the Fur

The pattern is individual. No two tigers carry the same arrangement, close enough to a fingerprint that camera trap surveys identify animals by their flanks alone. A good deal of what we know about remaining population sizes comes from exactly that, which is why a number this specific is possible for an animal this scarce and this hard to find.

Unlike lions, tigers are solitary. Adults hold large individual territories and come together mainly to mate. That makes them harder to count, and it means each animal needs a substantial area of habitat to itself, which is the core of why fragmentation hurts them so specifically.

## It Is Not Only Bad News

The trajectory has genuinely turned in places. India holds the majority of the world's wild tigers and counted 3,167 in 2022, up from 2,967 four years earlier. Nepal went from 121 in 2009 to 355 in 2022, closer to a tripling than a doubling, and is the only one of the thirteen range countries to have met the doubling target they all set in 2010. Bhutan and Russia have also recorded increases.

The gains are real and they are reversible, and they depend on the two things that are hardest to sustain: continuous anti-poaching effort, and habitat that stays connected rather than being cut into pieces.
