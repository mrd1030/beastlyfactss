# Molly Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why Vegetables Matter

Mollies eat more like grazers than most other livebearers, and skipping the vegetable side of that diet is the single most common molly-specific feeding mistake. For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Molly care guide](/guides/molly/), or our companion guides on [cost](/blog/molly-cost-guide/), [breeding and handling](/blog/molly-handling-guide/), [health issues](/blog/molly-health-issues-guide/), and [tank setup](/blog/molly-tank-setup-guide/).

## Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

- **Fry (newborn to several weeks):** The highest feeding frequency of any stage. Because mollies are livebearers, fry are born larger and more developed than the fry of egg-laying species, so many can go straight onto crushed flake or newly-hatched baby brine shrimp rather than needing infusoria first. Feed small amounts at least 3 to 5 times a day, tiny stomachs need frequent refueling to keep pace with early growth.
- **Juveniles:** More frequent, higher-protein feeding than adults, tapering toward adult portions as they approach full size. This stage is less precisely documented across sources than fry or adult feeding, treat exact numbers as approximate.
- **Adults:** Once or twice a day is the most commonly cited range. The portion rule matters more than the exact frequency: feed only what's fully cleared within a minute or two.

## What Mollies Actually Eat

Mollies are omnivores, but they lean more herbivorous than most other commonly kept livebearers. In the wild, their diet consists primarily of algae and plant material, supplemented by aquatic invertebrates like mosquito larvae. That balance should carry over into the tank: a high-quality flake or pellet formulated for tropical community fish as the base, with real vegetable content layered on top rather than treated as optional.

<FunFact>
Pelleted food generally holds its nutritional value better than flake, which can lose nutrients as it breaks down and disperses in water. For a species that needs consistent vegetable and algae intake, a quality pellet or algae wafer is often a more reliable staple than flake alone.
</FunFact>

Rotate in blanched vegetables two to three times a week: zucchini, spinach, cucumber, lettuce, and peas all work well, cut small and left in the tank only long enough to be grazed on. Protein-rich foods, bloodworms, brine shrimp, and similar live, frozen, or freeze-dried options, should stay a supplement rather than a staple, some sources recommend limiting bloodworms specifically to around three feedings a week alongside the regular diet.

## Safe Treats and Foods to Avoid

Safe: quality flake or pellet with real vegetable content, algae wafers, blanched zucchini, spinach, cucumber, lettuce, and peas, and occasional bloodworms or brine shrimp as a protein supplement.

Avoid feeding an all-protein diet with no vegetable or algae component at all, this is the single most repeated molly-specific mistake across care sources, and it's tied directly to digestive and bloating problems over time. Beyond that, the general aquarium-keeping rules apply: skip spoiled or expired food, and don't refeed frozen food that's already been thawed and left sitting out, that encourages bacterial growth in the tank.

## Why Mollies Stop Eating

1. **Stress.** New-tank relocation, aggressive tankmates, and an imbalanced male-to-female ratio are common triggers. A ratio of roughly one male to two or three females reduces the chasing and harassment that comes with too many males pursuing too few females.
2. **Poor or unstable water quality.** Ammonia or nitrite spikes and incorrect pH are common causes of both appetite loss and lethargy in freshwater aquarium fish generally, worth testing for directly rather than waiting out.
3. **Cold water.** Mollies are a tropical species, and cold water slows metabolism directly, which shows up as lethargy and reduced appetite along with a weakened immune response.
4. **Impending birth.** A female close to giving birth may eat less in the day or so beforehand, from reduced stomach capacity and a shift toward finding a place to deliver. Appetite typically returns quickly afterward.
5. **Illness.** Ich, velvet, and other conditions covered in our [Molly health issues guide](/blog/molly-health-issues-guide/) are usually accompanied by other visible signs, not appetite loss alone.

What's worth acting on regardless of exact duration: appetite loss combined with lethargy, hiding, clamped or discolored fins, visible spots, or unusual swimming, including the side-to-side "shimmy" this species is known for. Any of those together are a signal to check water parameters immediately rather than wait it out.

Overfeeding is a bigger practical risk than any of the above. It drives constipation and swim bladder problems directly, and uneaten food breaking down in the tank is a common, avoidable driver of the water-quality decline behind most other molly health issues.
