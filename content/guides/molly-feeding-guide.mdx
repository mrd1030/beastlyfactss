# Molly Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why Vegetables Matter

Mollies get a large part of their diet from plants and algae, so a menu with no plant food at all leaves out most of what they naturally eat. Skipping the vegetable side of that diet is the molly-specific feeding mistake to avoid.

## Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

- **Fry (newborn to several weeks) and juveniles:** The highest feeding frequency of any stage, since tiny stomachs need frequent refueling to keep pace with early growth: at least 3 to 5 times a day for fry, in small amounts. Because mollies are livebearers, fry are born larger and more developed than the fry of egg-laying species, so they accept newly hatched baby brine shrimp or powdered flake from birth and can go straight onto crushed flake rather than needing infusoria first. Juveniles get more frequent, higher-protein feeding than adults, tapering toward adult portions as they approach full size, though this stage is less precisely documented across sources than fry or adult feeding, so treat exact numbers as approximate. Young fish take a larger ration for their body weight than adults, up to about 5% of body weight against about 3% for an adult.
- **Adults:** At least once a day. Once or twice a day is a common range. The portion rule matters more than the exact frequency: feed only what's fully cleared within a minute or two.

## What Mollies Eat

The sailfin molly is almost entirely a plant eater in the wild, grazing algae and other plant material and taking aquatic invertebrates such as mosquito larvae on the side. The short-finned molly is an omnivore that feeds on worms, small crustaceans and insects as well as plants, but it still needs a large share of plant food. That balance should carry over into the tank: a high-quality flake or pellet formulated for tropical community fish as the base, with plant food layered on top rather than treated as optional.

<FunFact>
Sailfin males that get too little greenstuff may not develop their full sail-like dorsal fin. Pellets tend to hold together in the water longer than flake, which is lighter and breaks down and disperses faster. For a species that needs consistent vegetable and algae intake, a quality pellet or algae wafer is often a more reliable staple than flake alone.
</FunFact>

Spirulina flake and pellets make good staples, and blanched vegetables are popular additions: rotate in lettuce, cooked spinach, zucchini, cucumber, and peas two to three times a week, cut small and left in the tank only long enough to be grazed on. Protein-rich foods, bloodworms, brine shrimp, and similar live, frozen, or freeze-dried options, should stay a supplement rather than a staple, with bloodworms once or twice a week at most.

## Safe Treats and Foods to Avoid

Safe: quality flake or pellet with real plant content, spirulina flake, blanched lettuce and cooked spinach, and occasional bloodworms or brine shrimp as a protein supplement. Algae wafers and blanched zucchini, spinach, cucumber, lettuce, and peas are safe too.

Avoid feeding an all-protein diet with no vegetable or algae component at all. Over time it can lead to digestive and bloating problems. Beyond that, the general aquarium-keeping rules apply: skip spoiled or expired food, and don't refeed frozen food that's already been thawed and left sitting out, that encourages bacterial growth in the tank.

## Why Mollies Stop Eating

1. **Stress.** New-tank relocation, aggressive tankmates, and an imbalanced male-to-female ratio are common triggers. A ratio of roughly one male to two or three females reduces the chasing and harassment that comes with too many males pursuing too few females.
2. **Poor or unstable water quality.** Ammonia or nitrite spikes and incorrect pH are common causes of both appetite loss and lethargy in freshwater aquarium fish generally, worth testing for directly rather than waiting out.
3. **Cold water.** Mollies are a tropical species, and cold water slows metabolism directly, which shows up as lethargy and reduced appetite along with a weakened immune response.
4. **Impending birth.** A female close to giving birth may eat less in the day or so beforehand, from reduced stomach capacity and a shift toward finding a place to deliver. Appetite typically returns quickly afterward.
5. **Illness.** Ich, velvet, and similar conditions are usually accompanied by other visible signs, not appetite loss alone.

What's worth acting on regardless of exact duration: appetite loss combined with lethargy, hiding, clamped or discolored fins, visible spots, or unusual swimming, including the side-to-side "shimmy" this species is known for. Any of those together are a signal to check water parameters immediately rather than wait it out.

Overfeeding is the easiest risk to avoid: uneaten food breaking down in the tank is a common driver of the water-quality decline behind [most other molly health issues](/blog/molly-health-issues-guide/), the shimmy among them. Steady filtration and a regular water-change habit keep that from compounding. Overfeeding is a bigger practical risk than any of the above, and it can lead to constipation and swim bladder problems. Mollies also need a more plant-heavy diet than many fish, so plant food belongs in the weekly rotation.
