# Three Geckos, Three Very Different Pets

"Gecko" sounds like one kind of animal, but these three barely share a care sheet. One needs to stay cool, one needs belly heat. One you'll never touch, one you'll hold daily. One digs, the other two climb. If you've been picturing all geckos as roughly the same pet, this trio is the clearest way to see how wrong that assumption is.

## Gargoyle Gecko: The One Where Heat Is the Enemy

Most reptile care revolves around keeping things warm enough. Gargoyle geckos flip that: room temperature is correct, and anything much above 82°F starts to cause real problems. They're calmer as adults than as skittish juveniles, and unlike their cousin the crested gecko, a gargoyle's bite can actually break skin, though its dropped tail, also unlike a crested gecko's, grows back.

The cool-running care makes sense once the origin is known. Gargoyle geckos are native to New Caledonia, a group of islands between Australia and Fiji, and they stay a modest 8 to 10 inches from snout to tail. They are also a long commitment for a small lizard, averaging 15 to 20 years with good care. The [crested gecko](/blog/crested-gecko-tank-setup-guide/) comes from the same islands, which is why the two share a cool, humid, climbing setup more than either shares with the geckos below.

## Mourning Gecko: The Pet You'll Never Hold

Every mourning gecko is female. They reproduce without a male at all, which is why they're commonly sold and kept as small self-multiplying colonies rather than single animals. They're also tiny, fast, and genuinely not a handling species, this is a display animal you watch through the glass of a lushly planted vivarium, not one you take out.

The reproduction trick has a name, parthenogenesis, and it means one mourning gecko can found a colony with no male involved, so a keeper needs a plan for the eggs from the start. The animals themselves are tiny, just 3.5 to 4 inches long with half of that in a fairly thick tail, and they eat an omnivorous diet of nectar, fruit, fruit flies and other small insects in the wild. Small does not mean short-lived: mourning geckos can live as long as 10 years.

## African Fat-Tailed Gecko: The Digger

Close cousin of the leopard gecko and just as handleable, calm enough that it's often recommended alongside leos as a great starter reptile. The real difference is underfoot: fat-tails are burrowers, ground-dwelling and needing depth to dig into, plus more humidity and a moist hide that a leopard gecko doesn't require.

Fat-tails are nocturnal ground dwellers from the desert areas of West Africa, and they grow from about 2 inches at hatching to around 9 as adults. The humidity difference is modest but real: the enclosure runs slightly more humid than a leopard gecko's, with misting a few times a week, and at least one hide kept moist with damp paper towel or moss to help with shedding. A dropped tail grows back, though the regrown tail looks different from the original. With proper care they live 15 to 20 years in captivity.

<FunFact>
Look at the three temperature ranges side by side and the differences get almost comedic: a gargoyle gecko can start struggling above 82°F, while an African fat-tailed gecko's warm hide runs 88 to 92°F, a full ten-plus degrees hotter, on purpose. Same word, "gecko," pointing at two animals with opposite thermal comfort zones.
</FunFact>

## The Common Thread

There isn't really one, and that's the point. These three prove "gecko" is a category, not a care plan, and the [leopard gecko](/guides/leopard-gecko/), the beginner gecko most people start with, proves it again from a different direction with a heat mat and a dish of insects. Read the specific guide for whichever one you're considering, the assumptions that work for one will actively hurt another.
