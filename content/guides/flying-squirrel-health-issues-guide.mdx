# Common Flying Squirrel Health Issues (Diet Drives Almost Everything)

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Flying squirrel health comes down to diet more than almost any other factor, the same as their unrelated look-alike, the sugar glider. Get it right and this is a genuinely resilient exotic pet. Get it wrong, and problems can appear with real suddenness. ## Metabolic Bone Disease

This is the most common serious health issue in captive flying squirrels, and it's almost entirely diet-driven. It's caused by a calcium-poor, phosphorus-heavy diet, typically one built too heavily around nuts, seeds, and acorns without calcium and vitamin D3 correction to balance it out. The target calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is roughly 2:1.

Watch for lethargy, reduced appetite, and reluctance to climb or move normally early on, progressing toward hind-limb weakness, tremors, and in advanced cases, fractures, paralysis, and seizures.

<FunFact>
Metabolic bone disease in flying squirrels can progress gradually, following a fairly recognizable path from reduced appetite to hind-end weakness to paralysis, or it can show up with real suddenness, a squirrel that seemed fine appearing paralyzed within a short window. There's a real difference from sugar gliders worth knowing too: because flying squirrels can synthesize some vitamin D through skin exposure to light and not just from food, keepers are generally advised to provide regular access to natural or full-spectrum light alongside dietary calcium, not diet correction alone.
</FunFact>

Always see an exotic vet at the first sign of reduced appetite, lethargy, or coordination changes, don't wait to see if it resolves on its own. Caught early, this is treatable with corrected calcium, vitamin D3, appropriate light exposure, and an overall diet fix under veterinary guidance. ## Dental Disease

This is a genuine point of difference from the sugar glider content on this site, since gliders are marsupials and don't share it. Flying squirrels are true rodents, and like all rodents, their incisors are rootless and grow continuously throughout life. Inadequate wear can lead to overgrown or misaligned incisors, a recognized issue across pet rodents generally, so appropriate gnawing opportunities and a watchful eye on tooth length and alignment are worth building into routine care, even though it's a smaller risk here than diet-driven bone disease.

## Obesity and Malnutrition

This comes from a diet too heavy in nuts, seeds, and sugary fruit relative to how much a caged animal actually moves compared to a wild forager covering real distance every night. It's manageable at home with diet correction, cutting back the treat-heavy items and leaning on the balanced staple instead, but it's worth a vet visit to confirm you're making the right adjustment rather than guessing.

## Illness Is Often Hidden Until It's Advanced

Like most small pet rodents, flying squirrels are prey animals by instinct, and they tend not to show obvious signs of pain or distress until a condition is fairly advanced. A squirrel that's simply a little quieter or eating a little less can already be dealing with something real. That's exactly why any change in appetite, activity level, or coordination deserves prompt exotic veterinary attention rather than a wait-and-see approach, the same standard covered in our [handling guide](/blog/flying-squirrel-handling-guide/) for behavior changes generally.
