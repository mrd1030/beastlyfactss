# Four Fish, Four Completely Different Difficulty Levels

Zebra danio, bristlenose pleco, discus, and cardinal tetra rarely get compared side by side, and that's kind of the point of putting them here together. Between them they cover four different tank roles, a hardy beginner schooling fish, a low-maintenance algae-eating catfish, a genuinely demanding centerpiece cichlid, and a small, vivid schooling tetra, and four different experience levels to match. Three of these four are squarely beginner fish. The fourth, discus, is not, and nothing here is going to pretend otherwise.

## Zebra Danio: A Fish Famous Outside the Fish Tank

Zebra danios are fast-swimming, egg-scattering schoolers from the streams and monsoon-flooded rice paddies of South Asia, a genuine contrast to livebearers like guppies and mollies. A spawning pair releases eggs into open water or fine-leaved plants, builds no nest, and provides no parental care afterward. Hardy and beginner-friendly at 1.5 to 2.5 inches, this fish is also, under its other common name zebrafish, the most widely used non-mammalian vertebrate model organism in developmental biology and genetics research. Its embryos develop transparently outside the mother's body, and roughly 70 percent of human genes have a working counterpart in its genome, which is why it's been a mainstay of research labs since the 1990s.

## Bristlenose Pleco: The Pleco That Doesn't Outgrow the Tank

"Pleco" is a label that covers dozens of very different species, and the classic cheap juvenile sold as a tiny algae-eater is usually a common pleco or a related Pterygoplichthys species that grows into a real 12 to 24 inch, 75-plus-gallon adult, a well-documented size surprise that drives constant rehoming and has left escaped or released plecos established as an invasive species in Texas and Florida waterways. The bristlenose is the pleco actually worth buying: it stays a manageable 4 to 6 inches for life. Mature males grow branching, tentacle-like bristles across the snout, used to signal sex and spar with rivals, while females stay smooth or grow only a few short ones. Algae alone isn't a complete diet for an adult of either type. Our [bristlenose pleco feeding guide](/blog/bristlenose-pleco-feeding-guide/) covers what else needs to be on the menu.

## Discus: The Genuine Outlier Here

Discus is the one fish in this roundup that doesn't belong in a beginner's first tank, and it's worth saying plainly rather than softening it. This tall, laterally compressed Amazonian cichlid comes from slow-moving, soft, acidic blackwater, and keeping it well means replicating that rather than a standard community tank setup. Unlike most cichlids, which pair off and defend territory, discus are genuinely social and do best in a group, prized in the hobby as the self-styled "king of the aquarium" for their vivid, selectively bred color patterns. Parental care is unusual too: both parents feed newly hatched fry a nutrient-rich mucus secreted from their own skin, nicknamed "discus milk." Get the water right and this is also the longest-lived fish in this group, 8 to 10 years in captivity and up to 15.

## Cardinal Tetra: Often Mistaken for a Neon Tetra

Cardinal tetras get confused with neon tetras constantly, and the difference is visible once you know what to look for: a cardinal's iridescent red stripe runs the full length of its body, while a neon's stops at the halfway point and leaves the front half silver-blue. Both are small schooling fish from Amazon blackwater, but cardinal tetras come specifically from the Amazon and Orinoco basins, and large numbers are still wild-caught on the Rio Negro every year through community-based fisheries. That trade is often argued to pay for keeping the forest standing, a conservation case that is contested rather than settled. At 2 inches and rated beginner to intermediate, it's a step up in fussiness from a zebra danio but nowhere near discus territory.

<FunFact>
A cardinal tetra and a neon tetra get mixed up constantly, and the two are close enough in size, shape, and coloring that the confusion is understandable. The tell is the red stripe. On a cardinal tetra, the iridescent red runs the entire length of the body, underneath the blue stripe from nose to tail. On a neon tetra, that same red only covers the rear half, leaving the front of the body silver-blue with no red at all. Neither fish is rare or hard to find, but knowing which one is actually swimming in a tank matters for stocking. A cardinal grows slightly larger and is rated a notch harder to keep than the neon it's so often mistaken for.
</FunFact>
