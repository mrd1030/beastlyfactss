# How to Handle a Rabbit Without Hurting It

Rabbits are more physically fragile than most first-time owners expect. They have delicate spines and powerful hind legs, and the combination means an unsupported or panicked rabbit can genuinely injure itself trying to escape your grip. Getting handling right matters more here than with almost any other common pet.

## Why Support Matters So Much

A rabbit that struggles against an unsupported hold can fracture its own lumbar spine, the strength of its hind legs works against it in exactly the wrong way if its body isn't fully supported. This isn't a minor handling preference. It's the single most important thing to get right with this species.

## The Correct Way to Pick One Up

Support the chest with one hand (positioned just behind the front legs) and the hindquarters with the other, simultaneously. Lift with both regions supported at once, hold the rabbit close against your body rather than out at arm's length, and keep sessions low to the ground. If the rabbit struggles, lower it back down immediately instead of fighting to keep hold.

Never lift a rabbit by the ears. Never rely on the scruff alone to support its weight. A rabbit needs body support underneath it, and a neck grip is not that.

One useful technique vets use: the "football hold," tucking the rabbit's head into the crook of your elbow with its body supported along your forearm. It's a genuinely low-stress way to carry a rabbit securely.

## Skip "Trancing"

You may see advice about flipping a rabbit onto its back to calm it, sometimes called trancing. Don't do this. Research measuring rabbits in this position found significant increases in respiration, heart rate, and stress hormone levels, this is a fear response the animal can't escape, not relaxation, regardless of how still the rabbit goes.

Repetition makes it worse, not better. The more often a rabbit is laid on its back, the faster it goes still, and that is a rabbit that has learned what is coming and panics sooner, not one that has come to accept it. Afterward, rabbits that have been turned over tend to hide more and explore less. The one legitimate use is clinical: a vet may use the position briefly for an X-ray or a look at the teeth when it avoids sedation.

<FunFact>
A "tranced" rabbit lying perfectly still on its back looks calm to the untrained eye, which is exactly why the myth persisted for so long. Measuring actual heart rate and stress hormones told a very different story, the stillness is closer to freezing in fear than relaxing.
</FunFact>

## Building Trust

Most rabbits are calmest with [all four feet on the ground](/blog/rabbit-enrichment-guide/), and many never come to love being picked up even once they trust you completely, that's normal. Build trust through floor-level interaction: sit or lie down at the rabbit's level, hand-feed treats, and let the rabbit choose to approach you instead of reaching in to grab it. Over time, this earns you a rabbit that's relaxed around you even if actual pick-ups stay infrequent.

## Nail Trims, Grooming and Health Checks

Most of the handling a rabbit needs is not cuddling but maintenance, and it all happens with four feet on a stable surface, never with the rabbit on its back. Nails need trimming every one to two months, and a rabbit's hind nails can badly scratch an unprotected arm, so long sleeves help. The trim itself, including where the quick sits and how to stop a bleed, is laid out in the [small mammal grooming guide](/blog/small-mammal-grooming-nails-molting-guide/).

Vets use a hold worth copying for a quick look at eyes, ears or teeth: one hand behind the rabbit so it cannot back up, the other lightly covering its eyes and steadying the head so it does not bolt forward. It is the lowest-stress restraint there is.

Reward-based training pays off here too. A rabbit taught to come when called, and to walk into its carrier for a treat, is one that rarely has to be scooped up at all, which makes a trip to the vet in a [secure carrier](/blog/small-mammal-vet-visits-and-travel-guide/) far less of an ordeal for both of you.

## Bonding a Second Rabbit

Rabbits live best in bonded pairs, but two strangers put in one hutch can fight hard enough to injure each other. The introduction runs in stages:

1. **Neuter both first.** A male can stay fertile for up to six weeks after neutering, and hormones take weeks to settle, so wait about four weeks after surgery before the first meeting.
2. **Vaccinate and quarantine.** The newcomer gets its RHDV2 vaccination and two weeks in a separate space before it shares air or bedding with the resident.
3. **Side by side.** Two enclosures next to each other with a barrier, so the rabbits can see and smell each other and each can hide. Expect about a week of unrest.
4. **Neutral ground.** Short, supervised meetings in an area neither rabbit has lived in, lengthening day by day.
5. **Moving in.** Once they spend one to two hours together every day without trouble, they can share a living space.

Lying together and grooming each other mean it is working. Mounting each other's heads, biting, or chasing that does not stop means separating them immediately and starting again from side by side. A bond is failing when one or both rabbits lose weight or develop new behavior problems over the following weeks; those two can live next to each other but should not share space.

## Signs of Stress

- Thumping a hind foot
- Tense, rigid body posture
- Wide eyes
- Fast, shallow breathing
- Teeth grinding, a pain signal, not contentment, and one of the early signs of [GI stasis](/blog/rabbit-gi-stasis-guide/)
- Freezing in place
- Bolting or kicking

If you see these, set the rabbit down in one smooth motion, straight down and never dropped, and give it space. Those are acute, in-the-moment signals, but when freezing or constant hiding becomes a rabbit's normal state rather than a reaction to being picked up, you're looking at something longer-running, and [what anxious pet behavior is really telling you](/blog/when-your-pet-wont-stop-pacing-or-hiding-what-anxious-pet-behavior-is-really-telling-you/) covers how to read and unwind it.
