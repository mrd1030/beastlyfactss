# Common Green Anole Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />

Green anoles are small enough that health problems can progress fast, which makes recognizing the early signs important with this species.

## Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

Caused by insufficient UVB or calcium. Watch for weakness, trembling, a soft or swollen jaw, and difficulty moving normally. Always see a vet, and act quickly, a lizard this small has little margin before a developing problem becomes serious.

## Respiratory Infection

Open-mouth breathing, discharge from the eyes or nostrils, and lethargy. Caused by an enclosure that's running too cold or substrate that's staying too wet. Always see a vet, this needs antibiotic treatment.

## Incomplete or Stuck Shed

Green anoles shed in pieces rather than all at once, and low humidity can leave patches stuck, especially around the toes and tail, where retained shed can constrict circulation. Manageable at home by correcting humidity and gently assisting a stuck shed once it's been softened with extra misting.

## Dehydration

Green anoles drink water droplets off leaves and surfaces rather than reliably using a water bowl, which makes daily misting genuinely essential, not optional. Watch for sunken eyes, wrinkled-looking skin, and lethargy. Manageable at home if caught early, increase misting frequency right away. See a vet if your anole doesn't respond or shows real lethargy.

<FunFact>
A green anole rarely drinks from a bowl the way many reptiles do. It mostly drinks water droplets off leaves and surfaces, which is exactly why daily misting is essential rather than optional for this species.
</FunFact>

## Eggs, With or Without a Male

An adult female green anole can produce eggs whether or not she has ever met a male; the eggs are simply infertile. In the wild, the breeding season runs roughly April through August, and a female lays one egg, occasionally two, about every two weeks through it, six to nine eggs in a year. She buries each one in moist soil or leaf litter. Females also store sperm, which has been found seven months after mating, so a female separated from a male can still lay fertile eggs long afterward.

That rhythm is what the enclosure has to support. Give her a patch of loose, slightly damp substrate or a planted pot deep enough to dig into, keep calcium dusting and UVB on schedule through spring and summer, and check the floor for eggs.

Egg binding, or dystocia, is when a formed egg cannot be passed. Single females with no male contact are the most common reptile dystocia cases vets see, and the usual causes are the ones a keeper controls: no suitable spot to lay, low calcium, missing UVB, temperatures or humidity off, dehydration. Signs are a female who stops eating, turns weak, and has a visibly swollen belly, often while restlessly digging or straining. Untreated, it progresses to severe lethargy, so a gravid female who goes off food and turns weak is a same-day vet visit.

## Parasites

More common in wild-caught animals specifically. A vet fecal exam is worth doing for any new anole, whether or not you see obvious symptoms.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Weak UVB and calcium cause MBD, arguably the most dangerous issue on this list given how quickly it can progress in such a small animal; the feeding guide has the every-feeding dusting that closes the calcium half. A cold or overly wet enclosure causes respiratory infection. Low humidity causes stuck shed. And insufficient misting causes dehydration. Nearly everything here connects directly back to [the setup](/blog/green-anole-tank-setup-guide/), which is where the bulb, the UVI target, the humidity band and the temperatures all live. This is a species where getting the basics right matters more than it might seem to for something this small.

A lizard this small has little margin for any of these, which is why the green anole ends up more fragile than a [four-foot tegu that looks far harder to keep](/blog/greenanole-tegu-firebelliedtoad-tigersalamander-overview/).
