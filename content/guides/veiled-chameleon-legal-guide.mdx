Veiled chameleons are legal in forty-eight states. Two places say no, and the interesting thing about both is that neither of them decided anything about chameleons. In one, the species lost by being a lizard. In the other, it lost by not being on a list.

That is worth understanding before you assume a species is safe because no rule names it. Two of the strictest jurisdictions in the country work by silence, and the animals they catch are the ones nobody thought to write down.

## Washington DC Banned Every Lizard by Accident of Drafting

D.C. Code § 8-1808(j) says no person may keep "any living member of the animal kingdom" as a household pet except for seven categories. The reptile category reads, in full:

> Non-venomous snakes, fish, and turtles

That is the whole reptile allowance. Snakes are in, turtles are in, and lizards are simply absent. Not restricted, not permitted with conditions, not mentioned. A bearded dragon, a leopard gecko and a veiled chameleon are all in exactly the same position in the District, which is to say outside the list and therefore prohibited.

This is the same drafting style that catches hedgehogs in DC, where the mammal category is "Domesticated rodents and rabbits" and a hedgehog is not a rodent. Closed lists produce results nobody argued for, and they are almost never corrected, because correcting them requires someone to notice and then to care.

## Hawaii Already Has Them, Which Is the Point

Hawaii runs the strictest system in the country and runs it backwards. Under HAR § 4-71-6:

> The introduction into Hawaii of live animals or live non-domestic animals as defined in this chapter at any stage of development is prohibited except for those animals on the lists

Three lists exist. The prohibited list names species that may not come in at all. The conditionally approved list clears animals for import subject to conditions, and it is generous in places, the whole genus *Testudo* is on it. The restricted list clears animals for research and exhibition only.

*Chamaeleo calyptratus* is on none of them. The only chameleons Hawaii names anywhere are *Chamaeleo chamaeleon* and *Furcifer oustaleti*, both on the restricted list, so neither is a pet either.

The reason this matters more in Hawaii than elsewhere is that the veiled chameleon is not a hypothetical risk there. Escaped and released animals have established breeding populations on Maui and Oahu, exactly the outcome the default rule exists to prevent, which makes it vanishingly unlikely the species gets added to an approved list.

<FunFact>
Two states rewrote their lizard laws recently and left the veiled chameleon alone both times. Florida's February 2021 rule added sixteen high-risk reptiles to its prohibited list, including the green iguana and all four tegus of the genera *Salvator* and *Tupinambis*. Louisiana went further in January 2025, prohibiting the green iguana, the Nile monitor, the savannah monitor and every tegu. Neither list touches Chamaeleonidae. The animals that get banned are the ones that survive a hurricane season loose in a subtropical state, and a chameleon that needs a misting schedule is not on that list.
</FunFact>

## New Jersey Is the Permit, Again

New Jersey's exotic pet framework is a closed exemption list plus a catch-all. N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.4 sets out the species that need no permit, and 7:25-4.5 requires one for any other exotic species not exempted.

The veiled chameleon is not exempted, so a personal possession permit from Fish and Wildlife applies before the animal arrives. Same answer as the Russian tortoise, the hedgehog and the sugar glider. New Jersey is consistent about this in a way most states are not.

## Where the Rule Says Yes Out Loud

Most states reach the veiled chameleon by not mentioning it, which is fine but tells you nothing. A few clear it explicitly, and those are the ones worth quoting if you ever need to show someone.

Rhode Island's regulations say "Old World Chameleons (Chameleonidae) may be kept without an exotic animal possession permit," clearing the family in one line. Montana names Chamaeleonidae among the lizard families on its statutory noncontrolled list, which matters because Montana otherwise bars possessing any exotic wildlife the commission has not classified. Alaska clears "Any nonvenomous reptile" as a class, which is unusually broad for a state that bans sugar gliders outright.

Indiana gets there differently. Its wild animal permit rule reaches only venomous reptiles and crocodilians over five feet, and states in terms that a permit is required only for animals "referenced in this rule." A chameleon is referenced nowhere, so no permit exists to need.

## The Real Takeaway

Washington DC and Hawaii are the two places you cannot keep one, and neither ban is going to be argued away, because neither is written about chameleons. New Jersey wants a permit. The other forty-eight states have nothing to say.

The thing actually worth checking is your city. Municipal exotic animal ordinances are written locally, often decades ago, and reptile clauses in them tend to be broad. That is the layer that catches people whose state law is perfectly clear.
