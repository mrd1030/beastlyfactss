# Goldfish Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and Why They Stop Eating

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Goldfish don't have a true stomach, which changes how feeding works for them and why overfeeding is such an outsized risk. Here's a real feeding routine, and how to read a goldfish that's gone off food.

## Feeding Schedule by Life Stage

- **Fry:** Don't feed for the first couple of days after hatching, they're still absorbing their yolk sac. Once free-swimming, offer infusoria or baby brine shrimp nauplii, 3 to 6 times a day for the first month, tapering to about 3 times a day through roughly the first 4 months.
- **Juveniles:** 2 to 3 small feedings a day, reflecting higher caloric needs for growth.
- **Adults:** Sources genuinely diverge. One veterinary charity's own goldfish page recommends once a day, while its general fish page says 2 to 3 times daily, and other sources describe 1 to 2 times daily as standard. There's no single settled number, treat "1 to 2 small meals a day" as the safe range, and consider splitting into two feedings since goldfish have no stomach to hold a large single meal.

Portion size matters more than the exact frequency. Feed only what's eaten in about 30 seconds to 2 minutes, and remove anything left uneaten.

**Pond and outdoor goldfish** need to scale feeding down with water temperature. A normal diet is fine above roughly 50°F. Between about 39 and 50°F, switch to a reduced, more digestible wheat-germ-based formula fed less often. Below that, stop feeding entirely, the fish enters a low-metabolism dormancy and uneaten food just fouls the water.

## What Goldfish Eat

Goldfish are omnivores. A quality <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0006G7OEI?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Hikari Gold Fancy Goldfish Pellets, 11 lb Mini">sinking pellet formulated specifically for goldfish</AffiliateLink> should be the base diet, not tropical fish flake, the nutrient profiles differ. Flakes work, especially for smaller or younger fish, but lose a meaningful share of certain vitamins within about 30 seconds of hitting water, making pellets the more nutritionally stable staple. Sinking pellets are also preferred for fancy, round-bodied varieties, since gulping floating food at the surface makes them prone to swallowing air.

Supplement 2 to 3 times a week with blanched or softened vegetables (shelled peas, spinach, kale, lettuce, zucchini, cucumber), plus high-protein treats in moderation, bloodworms, brine shrimp, and daphnia, and algae wafers. There's no gut-loading or calcium dusting involved the way there is for reptile or invertebrate feeders, nutritional balance here comes from genuine diet variety instead.

## Safe Treats and Foods to Avoid

Safe: shelled peas (mashed or whole, often used to relieve constipation), spinach, kale, lettuce, zucchini, cucumber, bloodworms, brine shrimp, daphnia, algae wafers, and the occasional small bit of orange, watermelon, or squash. Peas are a genuinely useful short-term fix for constipation, but shouldn't become a long-term staple.

Never feed: bread and other refined human starches, raw meat, cheese, and onions. Keep avocado and chocolate away from any pet, though the toxicity behind both is documented in birds and mammals rather than in fish. Citrus is fine as the rare treat noted above but not as a routine food. Tropical fish flake isn't toxic but is nutritionally mismatched to a goldfish's needs, skip it as a primary food.

## Safe Foods by Tier

Not every safe food belongs in the same rotation. A goldfish's diet works best as layers, a staple it eats every day, supplements that show up a couple of times a week, and treats that stay occasional. The never list isn't just a set of arbitrary bans either, each entry has a specific reason behind it.

## Pellet vs. Gel vs. Flake: Which Is Best

All three formats sit on the same pet store shelf, but they aren't interchangeable, and treating them as equal choices is where a lot of feeding mistakes start.

Flake food is thin and light, which is exactly why it's easy to overfeed, a pinch looks like almost nothing, so it's easy to add more before realizing how much surface area you've actually covered. Flakes also lose quality fast once a container is open. Ascorbic acid, a nutrient goldfish need for normal growth, is highly volatile, and university extension guidance on preparing fish feeds recommends buying food in small amounts, ideally about once a month, storing it in a cool, dry place, and discarding it after roughly three months regardless.

Pellets are cooked and more nutritionally uniform per particle than flakes, and for fancy, round-bodied goldfish specifically, sinking pellets are generally the better call. Fancy goldfish have a swim bladder connected directly to the digestive tract, so air swallowed while chasing floating food at the surface can end up in the swim bladder and contribute to buoyancy problems. Veterinary guidance on swim bladder disorders in fish points to switching to a sinking or neutrally buoyant diet as a way to cut down on that excess air intake.

Gel food is the newer option, mixed at home or bought pre-set and cut into pieces. It holds together in water far better than flake, losing meaningfully less protein and fat into the tank rather than into the fish, which also means less of it fouling the water uneaten. It's a reasonable pick for picky eaters or for working medication into food. It hasn't replaced sinking pellets as the default recommendation, though. For most tanks, a quality sinking pellet is still the simplest, best-established staple, with gel and blanched vegetables filling in around it.

## Blanching Vegetables the Right Way

Leafy and fibrous vegetables, peas, spinach, kale, zucchini, cucumber, are genuinely good goldfish food, but not straight out of the fridge. University extension guidance on preparing fish feeds specifically points to fresh leafy or cooked green vegetables rather than raw ones, and a quick blanch, a minute or two in boiling water, then cooled, is the easiest way to get there at home.

Blanching softens the tough structure of raw vegetables enough for a goldfish, which has no teeth built for grinding fibrous plant matter, to break pieces apart and eat them instead of mouthing a chunk and spitting it back out. Cut the softened pieces small, and only offer what will realistically be eaten within a few minutes.

Whatever your goldfish leaves behind, pull it out of the tank. Uneaten vegetable matter breaks down and fouls the water quickly, and overfeeding of any kind, vegetables included, is one of the more common drivers of the [ammonia spikes](/blog/aquarium-cycling-guide/) that stall a tank.

## Why Goldfish Stop Eating

1. **Water quality problems.** Ammonia, nitrite, or nitrate spikes, especially in a newer tank still cycling, are the single most commonly cited reason a goldfish stops eating.
2. **Overfeeding, constipation, or swim bladder trouble.** Excess or low-fiber food causes gas buildup that presses on the swim bladder, showing up as appetite loss alongside abnormal floating or sinking.
3. **Stress from environment or tank mates.** A new tank, incompatible tank mates, bullying, or an undersized enclosure are repeatedly named triggers, with appetite loss often the first visible sign.
4. **Disease, parasites, or infection.** Ich, internal or external parasites, and fungal infections often show up as reduced appetite before more visible symptoms appear.
5. **Low water temperature.** Below roughly 50°F, outdoor pond goldfish slow into a natural, low-metabolism dormancy and eat little to nothing until spring. Normal for pond fish, not illness.
6. **Breeding season.** As temperatures rise in late spring, goldfish preparing to spawn, especially chasing males, may temporarily go off food. A normal, hormone-driven shift.
7. **Diet monotony.** An unbalanced, overly repetitive diet, or too much treat food crowding out regular pellets, can reduce interest in eating.

## When to Worry

Short gaps of up to about 3 to 4 days are fine without any special arrangement for a healthy adult in an established tank. Beyond about 5 days, effects on health may start to show, and beyond a week to 10 days, arranging a feeder is a good idea. Outright survivability is commonly cited around 1 to 2 weeks thanks to a slow cold-water metabolism, but that's an upper survival bound, not something to aim for.

The trigger to seek help is 24 to 48 hours of refusal combined with any of: pronounced lethargy, pineconing scales (a classic dropsy warning sign), clamped fins, floating sideways or sinking abnormally, gasping at the surface, visible wounds or white spots, cotton-like fungal growths, or a gaunt, visibly thinning body. That combination, not the fasting alone, is what actually warrants an aquatic vet.

A goldfish that goes off food in a tank that was always too small is telling you about the tank, and [the tank size and bowl myth](/blog/goldfish-tank-size-bowl-myth/) is where that starts.
