# Lovebird Cage Setup: What Your Bird Actually Needs

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Lovebird housing has a few specific safety details that matter more than they might seem to at first glance, bar spacing especially. Get those right, along with size and material, and the rest of the setup is fairly straightforward. For costs and ongoing supplies, see our [Lovebird cost guide](/blog/lovebird-cost-guide/), or the complete [Lovebird care guide](/guides/lovebird/) for everything else.

## Cage Size

Minimum 18 inches long by 18 inches wide by 24 inches tall for a single bird, or 24 by 18 by 24 inches for a pair. Bigger is always better, and horizontal, longer cages suit this species well since lovebirds fly side to side rather than climbing vertically. A wide <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4wuT808" product="Large bird flight cage">flight-style cage</AffiliateLink> is worth the extra footprint over a tall, narrow one.

## Bar Spacing: Genuinely a Safety Issue

Keep bar spacing between three-eighths and five-eighths of an inch, with half an inch being ideal. This isn't a minor preference, wider spacing lets a lovebird trap its head between bars, which can be fatal. Skip round cages entirely, they don't provide safe corners and can cause disorientation given how this species positions its eyes.

## Materials

Powder-coated or stainless steel only. Avoid homemade cages, cages with wood components, or galvanized wire, all carry a real risk of zinc or other toxic exposure if chewed, and lovebirds chew constantly. Our [Lovebird cost guide](/blog/lovebird-cost-guide/) covers why cutting corners on cage quality (the cheap, rust-prone end of the price range) is a false economy for exactly this reason.

## Temperature

Normal household range, 65 to 80°F, works well. No special heating or cooling needed.

## Substrate and Lining

A paper cage liner is the simplest, most hygienic option, changed regularly. Avoid substrates that can grow mold in a humid environment.

<FunFact>
Unlike some other pet bird species, lovebirds don't need grit added to their cage. They shell their own seeds before eating, so grit isn't just an unnecessary extra here, it isn't part of how this species actually processes food at all.
</FunFact>

## Lighting

Indoor birds benefit from supplemental UV exposure to properly synthesize vitamin D3 for calcium absorption, since window glass filters out the UV wavelengths that matter. An avian-specific UVB lamp is worth adding if your bird doesn't get regular safe outdoor time. Keeping a consistent day and night cycle, with genuine darkness at night, also matters for reasons beyond sleep, our [Lovebird health issues guide](/blog/lovebird-health-issues-guide/) covers how a stable photoperiod helps prevent chronic egg-laying and egg-binding.

## The One Thing to Deliberately Leave Out

Don't provide a nest box or happy hut to a pet lovebird unless you're actually breeding. This belongs in the setup conversation too: adding nest-like furnishings is one of the most common and most avoidable triggers for hormonal aggression and chronic egg-laying in this species. That includes the enclosed, tunnel-shaped bird tents sold for nighttime use, if you want a "sleep tent" line item covered, a plain breathable cage cover does the job without the hormonal downside.
