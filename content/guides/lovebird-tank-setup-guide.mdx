# Lovebird Cage Setup: What Your Bird Actually Needs

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Lovebird housing has a few specific safety details that matter more than they might seem to at first glance, bar spacing especially. Get those right, along with size and material, and the rest of the setup is fairly straightforward.

<Figure src="/assets/images/lovebird-tank-setup-guide-1.jpg" alt="Labeled diagram of a lovebird cage showing a bonded pair, safe bar spacing, and varied perches and chew toys" caption="A few specific safety details, bar spacing especially, matter more than they might seem to at first glance." shareable />

## Cage Size

Minimum 18 inches long by 18 inches wide by 24 inches tall for a single bird, or 24 by 18 by 24 inches for a pair. Bigger is always better, and horizontal, longer cages suit this species well since lovebirds fly side to side rather than climbing vertically. A wide <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4wuT808" product="Large bird flight cage">flight-style cage</AffiliateLink> is worth the extra footprint over a tall, narrow one.

## Bar Spacing: A Safety Issue

Keep bar spacing between three-eighths and five-eighths of an inch, with half an inch being ideal. This isn't a minor preference, wider spacing lets a lovebird trap its head between bars, which can be fatal. Skip round cages entirely: they give the bird no corner to retreat into.

## Materials

Powder-coated or stainless steel only. Avoid homemade cages, cages with wood components, or galvanized wire, all carry a real risk of zinc or other toxic exposure if chewed, and lovebirds chew constantly.

## Temperature

Normal household range, 65 to 80°F, works well. No special heating or cooling needed.

## Substrate and Lining

A paper cage liner is the simplest, most hygienic option. Line the tray under a removable metal grate, so droppings fall through and the bird never stands in them. Avoid substrates that can grow mold in a humid environment.

<FunFact>
Unlike some other pet bird species, lovebirds don't need grit added to their cage. They shell their own seeds before eating, so grit isn't just an unnecessary extra here, it isn't part of how this species actually processes food at all.
</FunFact>

## Lighting

Indoor birds benefit from supplemental UV exposure to properly synthesize vitamin D3 for calcium absorption, since window glass filters out the UV wavelengths that matter. An avian-specific UVB lamp is worth adding if your bird doesn't get regular safe outdoor time. Keeping a consistent day and night cycle, with genuine darkness at night, also matters for reasons beyond sleep: a stable photoperiod helps prevent [chronic egg-laying](/blog/bird-chronic-egg-laying-guide/) and egg-binding.

## The One Thing to Deliberately Leave Out

Don't provide a nest box or happy hut to a pet lovebird unless you're actually breeding. This belongs in the setup conversation too: adding nest-like furnishings is one of the most common and most avoidable triggers for hormonal aggression and chronic egg-laying in this species. That includes the enclosed, tunnel-shaped bird tents sold for nighttime use, if you want a "sleep tent" line item covered, a plain breathable cage cover does the job without the hormonal downside.

## Cleaning Routine

**Daily:** take out soiled paper and discarded food, and wash the food and water bowls. A lovebird bathes in any water it can reach, so the drinking dish needs changing whenever it has been used as a bath, which is often more than once a day. Changing the paper each day also gives a daily look at the droppings.

**Weekly:** the full clean. Move the lovebird to a travel cage in another room, so it is in a separate air space while anything is sprayed or scrubbed. Strip out the paper, perches and toys, and wash and disinfect the cage with a bird habitat cleaner or a 3% bleach solution. Rinse everything until no trace of the cleaner, or its smell, is left, and let it all dry completely before the bird goes back in. A pair soils the cage faster, so the liner is changed more often than weekly when two birds share it.

**As needed:** perches and toys get washed and completely dried whenever they are soiled. The weekly clean is also the time to check the chew toys: a lovebird works through them fast, and a toy chewed down to a loose string or an exposed metal clip comes out.

Only cleaning products formulated for use around pets belong near the cage, because a bird's airways react to aerosolized fumes. Dilutions, ventilation and the products that must never be mixed are set out in the [bird household hazards guide](/blog/bird-household-hazards-guide/).
