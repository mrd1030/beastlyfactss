# Four Pets, Four Things Nobody Mentions Before You Buy

Every pet has a detail that matters more than the sales pitch, something the low price tag, the cute reputation, or the simple appearance doesn't tell you. Three of these four hide a real complication. One hides a genuine perk.

## Savannah Monitor: Cheap Because It's Overfed, Not Because It's Easy

A low purchase price and a "beginner monitor" reputation hide the real story: this species is notoriously overfed in captivity, and fatty liver disease kills far more pet savannah monitors young than anything genetically wrong with them. What nobody mentions upfront: an adult runs anywhere from 2.5 to more than 4.5 feet, more animal than the price suggests.

The weight problem is made in captivity, and nothing in the animal's genes sets it up. Wild savannah monitors eat mostly insects through the wet season, with some vertebrate prey, and cover long distances foraging. In a tank, the same lizard gets rodents and fatty foods year round and a fraction of the exercise, and that overfeeding is what leads to severe obesity and organ disease, fatty liver above all. The fix is on the keeper's side: gut-loaded insects as the staple, rodents only occasionally, and an adult enclosure near 6 by 3 by 6 feet so there is room to move.

## Russian Tortoise: The Good Surprise

Here's the one piece of good news in this batch. Unlike most tortoises, this species can live its entire multi-decade life indoors, no eventual outdoor enclosure required. If you want the tortoise-keeping experience without ever needing a yard, this is the species nobody tells you about.

Indoors does not mean small, though. A 50 gallon container is the floor for one tortoise, and an enclosure of at least 4 by 2 feet is the better target, with enough substrate to dig in, because Russian tortoises are excellent burrowers. Outdoor time is still the ideal when the weather allows, with fencing buried deep, since they dig under it, and they come inside once temperatures drop below 40°F.

## Cockatoo: The Surrender Statistics the Pet Store Won't Bring Up

A cuddly baby cockatoo doesn't advertise what one behavior consultant reports directly: cockatoo owners make up over half her practice, and rescues consistently name this species their most surrendered bird. Separation anxiety, extreme screaming, and feather-destructive behavior are the norm here, not the exception, and nobody mentions that at the point of sale. The lifespan turns that into a lifetime problem: cockatoos live up to 70 years or more as pets, and between the noise, the behavior and the decades, many are rehomed, some of them several times. Adopting from a rescue is a common recommendation for exactly that reason.

## Stick Insect: Cheap, Gentle, and Legally Complicated

The animal itself is about as low-stakes as pets get, a few dollars, minimal care, a genuinely social species you can keep in groups. What rarely gets mentioned: most commonly sold stick insects are technically regulated under a federal permit system that hobbyists can't realistically satisfy. Worth knowing before you buy. The care itself is simple: almost all stick insects eat washed bramble leaves, which can be found through the winter, and a few species take privet or ivy. The one hard requirement is height, since stick insects molt hanging from above, so a cage needs to be at least 30 cm (about 12 inches) tall and up to 100 cm for the longest species.

<FunFact>
This batch holds one of the widest lifespan spreads in the pet trade: a stick insect's life is measured in about a year, a Russian tortoise can live 40 plus years, entirely indoors if you want, and a cockatoo can live 70 years or more. Three animals in the same roundup, three completely different scales of commitment.
</FunFact>

## The Common Thread

Three of these four hide a real cost, financial, behavioral, or legal, behind an easy first impression. The fourth hides a genuine advantage behind an assumption that all tortoises need a yard eventually. Either way, the detail that actually matters is never the one on the price tag.
