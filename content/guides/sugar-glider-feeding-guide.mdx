# Sugar Glider Feeding: Schedule, Diet, and the Calcium Ratio That Prevents Paralysis

A sugar glider's wild diet of eucalyptus sap, nectar, pollen, and insects is genuinely hard to replicate in captivity, and getting the calcium-to-phosphorus balance wrong is the leading cause of illness in this species. For the rest of their care needs, see our complete [Sugar Glider care guide](/guides/sugar-glider/).

## The Staple Diet

Wild sugar gliders eat eucalyptus and acacia sap and gum, nectar, pollen, manna, honeydew, and insects and spiders, with fruit only a minor part. VCA's balanced framework for captivity is roughly one-third nutritionally balanced <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08B36DP17?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Exotic Nutrition Glider Complete Staple Diet">pelleted kibble</AffiliateLink>, one-third a nectar or sap-based mixture, and one-third a small number of insects offered every other day, a calcium-based multivitamin, and a variety of fresh vegetables and fruits.

<KeyTakeaway>
Established, vet-endorsed formulated diets include Leadbeater's mixture, Bourbon's Modified Leadbeater's (BML), High Protein Wombaroo (HPW), Critter Love Complete, and Exotic Nutrition diets. VCA specifically names Exotic Nutrition and Wombaroo as high-quality formulated options rather than a homemade mix assembled from guesswork.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Feeding Frequency and Portions

Feed roughly 15 to 20% of body weight daily, offered in the evening since gliders are nocturnal. Kibble can stay available at all times, while the fresh, nectar, insect, and produce components are given daily, with insects every other day. Keep fruit to a small portion, gliders will preferentially eat sweets over balanced food given the chance. Adult males typically weigh 100 to 160 grams and adult females 80 to 130 grams, per VCA.

## Foods to Avoid

Chocolate and dairy, canned fruit for its excess sodium and preservatives, and any pesticide-treated produce. Avoid foods high in oxalates that impair calcium absorption, including raspberries, strawberries, blackberries, spinach, carrots, beets, pears, lettuce, figs, and collard greens. Skip yogurt drops, peanut butter, pasta, rice, and human candy entirely, and limit high-phosphorus, low-calcium foods like beef, most seeds, and nuts. Raw corn is fine only very occasionally, it's very sweet.

## Supplementation: The Part That Actually Matters Most

This is genuinely the core of keeping this species healthy.

<KeyTakeaway>
A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08DP3H5N9?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Exotic Nutrition Sugar Glider Vitamin Combo Pack">calcium and vitamin D3 multivitamin made specifically for sugar gliders</AffiliateLink>, not a reptile vitamin, is essential because the typical fruit-and-insect diet runs phosphorus-rich and calcium-poor. Dust or gut-load insects with calcium before feeding, and sprinkle the multivitamin on food. Some breeder guidance calls for daily dusting, while other veterinary sources recommend a vitamin A/D3 supplement on insects and produce twice weekly. The target dietary calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is roughly 2:1, with some sources citing 1.5:1 to 2:1.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The Mistakes That Actually Matter

A fruit-only, nectar-syrup, or insect-heavy diet, low in calcium and high in phosphorus, causes nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, better known as metabolic bone disease. Per the Merck Veterinary Manual, gliders maintained mainly on fruit with few gut-loaded insects or protein sources are very susceptible, and the condition can progress to posterior weakness, hindlimb paralysis, muscle tremors, pathological fractures, and seizures. The body leaches calcium from the bones to keep blood calcium stable, which is exactly what weakens them.

<FunFact>
Feeding too much fruit or sugary treats like yogurt drops is the second major risk, driving obesity, hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver), and dental disease. And an unbalanced homemade or pellet-only diet causes the same underlying deficiencies over time, a diet plan is worth building with an exotic vet rather than assembling from guesswork.
</FunFact>
