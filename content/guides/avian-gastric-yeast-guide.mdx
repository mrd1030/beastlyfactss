# Avian Gastric Yeast (Megabacteriosis): The Silent Weight-Loss Disease

<VetDisclaimer />

A budgie, cockatiel, or lovebird with avian gastric yeast can keep eating on schedule, keep its feathers smooth, and still be losing ground by the gram. That gap between how a bird looks and what its body weight is actually doing is the whole problem with this disease. It used to be called megabacteriosis, back when researchers assumed the culprit was an unusually large bacterium. It's now known to be a yeast, Macrorhabdus ornithogaster, and most avian vets call it avian gastric yeast, or AGY, instead.

## What This Organism Actually Does

Macrorhabdus ornithogaster colonizes the junction between the proventriculus (a bird's true, acid-producing stomach) and the ventriculus (the muscular gizzard that grinds food). It's a large, rod-shaped organism, easily visible under a microscope once a vet knows to look for it, and it interferes with digestion by disrupting the normal acid environment a bird needs to break down food.

The Merck Veterinary Manual names budgerigars, parrotlets, lovebirds, cockatiels, and finches as the small pet birds most often affected. Budgerigars carry the longest history with the disease. It's where the organism was first identified, in Australian budgies in the early 1990s, and the term "going light," a chronic wasting pattern aviculturists used to describe sick budgies long before the yeast itself was understood, comes from decades of watching this exact presentation. Canaries show up repeatedly in older avian veterinary case material too, including specific research on how the disease shifts a canary's proventricular pH from a normal 0.7 to 2.4 up to 7.0 to 7.3 in severely affected birds. Zebra finches fall under the finches on that list. That said, the record isn't a simple budgie-only story: a 2023 case series from Iran examined 100 parrots referred with digestive signs, 37 of them cockatiels, 21 budgerigars, 21 grey parrots and 21 of four other species. Microscopy found the yeast in 9 of the cockatiels, 5 of the budgies and 3 of the greys, and in none of the others. That is a small, sick, single-clinic sample, not a prevalence figure, but it puts cockatiels ahead of budgies in the one group that was counted. If you keep any of these species, this is a real risk on your radar, not just a budgie problem. Our [budgie health issues guide](/blog/budgie-health-issues-guide/) already lists AGY among the conditions worth knowing, and it's a similar story for [cockatiels](/blog/cockatiel-health-issues-guide/) and [lovebirds](/blog/lovebird-health-issues-guide/).

## Why "Going Light" Is So Easy to Miss

The clinical signs reported for avian gastric yeast include ongoing weight loss, regurgitation, lethargy, diarrhea, and undigested seed passed whole in the droppings, all changes that build slowly rather than announcing themselves. The genuinely dangerous part is appetite. Documented case findings describe affected birds showing normal appetite or even increased appetite (one clinical study specifically notes polyphagia) right alongside the weight loss, because the bird is still eating, sometimes eating more, while digesting less of what it swallows. A bird that finishes its seed dish every day can still be quietly losing body mass underneath its feathers, and feathers hide weight loss extremely well on a two- or three-ounce bird.

<KeyTakeaway>
The only reliable way to catch this early is a gram scale and a habit. Weigh your bird at the same time of day, ideally before its first meal, and write the number down. A slow downward trend over a couple of weeks is the signal, long before a bird looks visibly thin.
</KeyTakeaway>

Whole or partially digested seed showing up in soft, watery droppings is another concrete flag, since it means food is passing through a gut that isn't breaking it down properly. In more advanced cases, droppings may show digested blood, and an uncommon acute form has been documented in budgerigars in otherwise good condition that collapse and die within 12 to 24 hours, usually from bleeding inside the proventriculus. That acute pattern is rare, but it's the reason a bird with any of these symptoms plus sudden weakness needs a same-day vet visit rather than a wait-and-see approach.

## One Negative Fecal Test Doesn't Clear a Bird

Diagnosis starts with a fresh dropping sample examined under a microscope, and vets look for the organism directly, sometimes backed by a modified Wright's stain or a Gram stain, with PCR testing available at some laboratories. The complication is shedding. Macrorhabdus doesn't come out in every dropping at a steady rate, birds can shed low numbers intermittently even while infected, so Merck Veterinary Manual is explicit that a negative fecal exam does not exclude infection. A vet working up a bird with suspicious symptoms typically wants droppings checked more than once, sometimes over consecutive days, before ruling the organism out.

Mortality is genuinely hard to pin to a single number, and any source that hands you one flat figure is oversimplifying. Merck puts it at 10 to 80 percent of affected birds, a wide range that depends on the individual bird's condition and which strain of the yeast is involved. Some birds carry the organism with no symptoms at all for long stretches, while others, especially once symptomatic, decline fast.

## Treatment: What the Actual Protocol Looks Like

This is the part worth getting exactly right, and the part where guessing is genuinely dangerous. The regimen most consistently cited by avian veterinary references is oral amphotericin B. Merck Veterinary Manual lists it specifically as 100 mg/kg, given by mouth, twice a day, for 30 days, and calls that the protocol with the highest treatment success rate, with one important caveat built into the same sentence: treatment failures are common, especially when the course runs shorter than that. A vet-run clinical resource from Melbourne Bird Vet, an Australian avian-only practice, describes the same shape independently, a minimum of 30 days of twice-daily oral medication, with some birds needing repeated or ongoing treatment beyond that.

That duration matters because it's the single most common way this treatment fails in practice: stopping early because the bird looks and acts better. Oral amphotericin B is dosed unusually high compared to other routes specifically because it barely absorbs from a healthy digestive tract, which is also why an oral course can run at doses that would be unsafe given by injection. None of that changes the fact that getting an accurate current body weight, sourcing a compounded oral formulation (this drug is not something you'll find on a pharmacy shelf in bird-appropriate form), and deciding how long to continue treatment, especially if a bird is a slow responder, is exam-room work. Your avian vet will determine the actual dose and duration for your bird. Some vets use voriconazole instead, anecdotally reported effective at 10 mg/kg twice a day, and flocks or aviaries are sometimes managed by acidifying or medicating drinking water rather than dosing birds one at a time. Whichever route your vet chooses, expect at least one recheck fecal exam during treatment and another after it ends, since a bird can look and act recovered while still shedding the organism.

## Reducing the Risk

Avian gastric yeast tends to show up alongside stress and immune suppression, whether that's a concurrent illness, poor husbandry, overcrowding, or the strain of breeding and molting. Quarantine and fecal-check any new bird before it joins an existing pair or flock, since carriers can look entirely healthy while shedding intermittently. Keep cages and food dishes genuinely clean, not just tidy, since the organism spreads through fecal contamination of shared surfaces and food. If you keep [parrotlets](/blog/parrotlet-health-issues-guide/), [canaries](/blog/canary-health-issues-guide/), or [zebra finches](/blog/zebra-finch-health-issues-guide/), the same weigh-in habit and the same low threshold for a vet visit apply just as much as they do for budgies and cockatiels. None of this is species-specific advice, it's the same baseline hygiene and observation that catches most avian illness early, applied consistently.
