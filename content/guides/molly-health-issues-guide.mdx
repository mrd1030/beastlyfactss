# Common Molly Health Issues (Starting With the "Shimmy")

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Molly shimmy is the one condition specific to this species, and water quality sits underneath almost everything else on this list too.

## Molly Shimmy (The Condition Specific to This Species)

Shimmy is a distinctive side-to-side, almost snake-like rocking motion as the fish swims. It isn't a disease in itself so much as a sign of stress. Mollies kept in soft or acidic water weaken fairly quickly, and shimmying, fungus and clamped fins are frequent signs of it. Even a small amount of ammonia can cause clamping and shimmying, and crowded or poorly filtered conditions in shops and home tanks add to it.

<FunFact>
Fancy mollies specifically carry an extra risk factor here. Most mollies on sale are mass-produced in the Far East, and osmotic shock from moving fish out of salted farm water into home tanks is one of the suspected causes of shimmy, independent of anything the new owner did wrong.
</FunFact>

Treatment starts with correcting the environment, not medicating the fish. Test for ammonia, check and correct pH toward 7.5 to 8.5, and address mineral hardness if your water runs soft, since a mineral supplement like crushed coral or a commercial buffering product can help. Isolating the affected fish in a [bare hospital tank with its own net and siphon hose](/blog/fish-quarantine-and-treatment-guide/) while you sort out the cause is standard practice.

## Does a Molly Tank Need Aquarium Salt?

This is worth answering honestly rather than repeating the standard hobbyist line. Mollies come from fresh, brackish and coastal water, and salt in modest doses doesn't harm them. That part isn't folklore.

Whether a healthy freshwater tank needs salt added at all is the open question. Some keepers recommend a small standing dose. Ted Coletti, director of the American Livebearer Association, holds that mollies "adapt to," rather than "demand," salinity, and livebearer specialist Dr. Joanne Norton argues that salt lets a keeper carry on poor tank-management habits that stay harmful even when the fish survive them. Large, frequent water changes, hard water and good plant cover address the same problems without the downside that salt can inhibit live plant growth. Treat continuous salt dosing as one legitimate tool rather than a mandatory ingredient, and prioritize water changes and stable parameters first. The much stronger salt bath used against ich is covered below.

If you add salt to a freshwater tank, use plain aquarium salt, which is sodium chloride made for the job. Marine salt mixes are formulated for saltwater tanks and belong in a deliberately brackish molly setup instead.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt across the body and fins, caused by the protozoan parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. The parasite burrows into skin and gills before dropping off to reproduce, and a single organism can produce up to 1,024 daughter parasites in one generation, which is what makes an outbreak escalate quickly. Affected fish are lethargic with clamped fins and visible white dots. Warm the tank slowly to about 82°F, the top of the molly's range, with an air stone running: heat speeds the parasite's cycle, and warm water holds less oxygen, but it does not kill the parasite. The medication does that. At 75 to 79°F the life cycle runs in about 3 to 6 days, so chemical treatments are repeated daily, 3 to 5 times in all, as set out in the [ich treatment guide](/blog/aquarium-ich-treatment-guide/). The alternatives are a salt bath of 4 to 5 grams per liter (about 15 to 19 grams per gallon) for 7 to 10 days, which mollies tolerate easily, or formalin or malachite green, and the water quality issue that let it take hold needs correcting too. Copper sulfate is the last pick: never use it without first testing alkalinity, and not at all if alkalinity is below 50 mg/L.

## Velvet

A dusty coating caused by the protozoan parasite *Piscinoodinium* (also called *Oodinium*) in freshwater tanks. Veterinary references list copper sulfate and chloroquine (for non-food fish) as treatments, and the same alkalinity caution applies to copper here as with ich.

## Fin and Tail Rot

Ragged, frayed, or discolored fin edges, a bacterial problem that poor water quality sets up. Correcting the water quality is the first step.

## Dropsy

Fluid buildup in the abdomen that makes the scales stand out in a raised, pinecone-like pattern, commonly tied to *Aeromonas*, the most common bacterial infection of freshwater aquarium fish. Stress, poor water quality and overcrowding make it more likely.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems: floating, sinking, or swimming at odd angles. The balloon form of molly, bred with a rounded body, can have swim bladder and digestive problems built into its shape, and may die early because of it.

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Stress, poor or unstable water quality, overcrowding, and skipping quarantine are the common thread behind nearly every condition on this list, not just shimmy.
