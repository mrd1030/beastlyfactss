# Common Molly Health Issues (Starting With the "Shimmy")

## Molly Shimmy (The Condition Specific to This Species)

Shimmy is a distinctive side-to-side, almost snake-like rocking motion as the fish swims, and livebearers, mollies, platies, swordtails, and guppies, are especially prone to it. It isn't a disease in itself so much as a sign the fish has lost some nerve and muscle control. The most commonly cited causes are low water temperature, low pH or mineral hardness, unstable or poor general water quality, and chlorine exposure from untreated tap water.

<FunFact>
Fancy mollies specifically carry an extra risk factor here. Much of the fancy-molly supply is bred overseas in harder, more mineral-rich, brackish-leaning water, then shipped and sold into home tanks running softer, more neutral freshwater. That mismatch alone can be enough to trigger shimmy in an otherwise healthy-looking fish, independent of anything the new owner did wrong.
</FunFact>

Treatment starts with correcting the environment, not medicating the fish. Raise the temperature toward the 76 to 80°F range, check and correct pH toward 7.0 to 8.0, and address mineral hardness if your water runs very soft, a mineral supplement like crushed coral or a commercial buffering product can help. Isolating the affected fish in a hospital tank while you sort out the cause is standard practice.

## Does a Molly Tank Actually Need Aquarium Salt?

This is worth answering honestly rather than repeating the standard hobbyist line. Mollies are a genuinely brackish-tolerant species, peer-reviewed research on sailfin mollies shows they can tightly regulate their internal salt and water balance up to roughly 65 parts per thousand, well past typical seawater, without major physiological stress. That part isn't folklore.

Where sources genuinely diverge is whether a healthy freshwater tank needs salt added at all. Some hobbyist and retail sources recommend a small standing dose, and salt does show up as part of shimmy treatment specifically, one commonly cited hospital-tank dose is 1 tablespoon of aquarium salt per 5 gallons of water. But an experienced counterargument, laid out by livebearer specialist Dr. Joanne Norton, holds that mollies "adapt to," rather than "demand," salinity, and that sufficiently large, frequent water changes and good plant cover fix the same problems salt is usually reached for, without the downsides. Those downsides are real: salt can inhibit live plant growth and can harm snails at higher concentrations. Treat continuous salt dosing as one legitimate tool rather than a mandatory ingredient, and prioritize water changes and stable parameters first.

If you do use salt, it needs to be the right kind. Aquarium salt is plain, additive-free sodium chloride. Marine or reef salt mixes carry buffers and trace minerals built for full saltwater tanks and aren't a substitute, and table salt's iodine and anti-caking agents aren't meant for aquarium use either.

## Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots resembling grains of salt across the body and fins, caused by the protozoan parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. The parasite burrows into skin and gills before dropping off to reproduce, and a single organism can produce hundreds of new ones, which is what makes an outbreak escalate quickly. Treatment is copper sulfate or formalin-based medication, available through a vet or pet store, alongside correcting whatever water quality issue let it take hold.

## Velvet

A fine, gold or rust-colored dusty coating on the body, caused by the protozoan parasite *Piscinoodinium* (also called *Oodinium*) in freshwater tanks. Symptoms include appetite loss, lethargy, and scratching against decor, and death rates can run high if it isn't caught early. It's contagious between fish, so isolate affected individuals where possible.

## Fin and Tail Rot

Ragged, frayed, or discolored fin edges, bacterial in origin and usually tied to poor water quality, ammonia buildup, or an untreated injury. Improving water quality alongside an antibacterial treatment resolves most cases caught early.

## Dropsy

Fluid buildup in the abdomen that makes the scales stand out in a raised, pinecone-like pattern. It's typically the visible endpoint of a systemic *Aeromonas* bacterial infection, and it's the hardest condition on this list to treat successfully, often carrying a poor outlook by the time it's obvious.

## Swim Bladder Disorder

Buoyancy problems, floating, sinking, or swimming at odd angles, usually from overfeeding or constipation. Often manageable at home with a short fast and a return to normal, appropriately portioned feeding afterward.
