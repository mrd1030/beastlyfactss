# Milk Snake Feeding: Prey at 1.5 Times the Body Width, and Why Hatchlings Eat Snakes

<AffiliateDisclosure />

A milk snake is a constrictor that swallows its prey whole, and feeding one well is a matter of two sizing rules, an interval that lengthens with age, and one fact about the species that decides how many snakes go in a tank. Here is all of it.

The interval stretches as the snake grows because an adult milk snake needs far less food for its size than a hatchling building a body. A snake that seems to be getting fat gets a longer interval or a smaller feeder, not a skipped meal here and there.

## What Milk Snakes Eat

**Whole prey, because nothing else is complete.** A milk snake is a carnivore that needs a diet of whole animals to get the nutrition its body requires, and a rodent carries its own calcium, so a snake on good-quality whole prey needs no supplement. Frozen-thawed mice are the staple, fully defrosted and warmed to room temperature, offered on tongs. Frozen-thawed is safer for the snake than live and the more humane choice, and a live adult mouse bites.

**Two sizing rules.** Prey is no more than one and a half times the width of the snake at its widest point, or roughly ten percent of its body weight. On a healthy snake both rules give the same mouse, and the weight rule is the one that catches an overfed adult, since a fat snake is wider than it should be and the width rule follows it up.

**Variety, within reason.** Wild milk snakes eat rodents such as voles, mice and rats as adults, and also birds, bird eggs, lizards, snake eggs and other snakes. A captive snake does well on mice for life, and the occasional chick or a different rodent adds variety without changing the schedule.

<KeyTakeaway>
Milk snakes eat snakes. Wild adults take other snakes including venomous ones, coral snakes and rattlesnakes among them, and hatchlings feed mainly on other young snakes. A pet milk snake will eat a cagemate, so it is one snake per enclosure, and never two fed in the same space. The [California kingsnake](/blog/california-kingsnake-feeding-guide/) carries the same rule for the same reason.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Water and the Bowl

A large bowl of fresh, clean water, big enough for the snake to curl up inside for a soak, is always available. Milk snakes soak before a shed and sometimes for no reason a keeper can see, and a bowl that gets fouled is changed the same day. The bowl is the humidity for this species too, and a dry enclosure with a small bowl produces the stuck sheds the health guide describes.

## After the Meal

Leave the snake alone for 48 hours after it eats. A milk snake picked up with a fresh meal inside it can regurgitate, which costs it the meal and stresses it, and a snake that regurgitates twice in a row needs a longer rest and a smaller prey item before the next attempt. Handling around a shed is avoided for a different reason: the skin is vulnerable and the snake is irritable.

## Why Milk Snakes Stop Eating

**Shed.** Clouded eyes and dull skin mean a shed within the week, and most snakes refuse until it is done.

**A cold enclosure.** A snake that cannot warm up cannot digest and will not take a meal it cannot process. The warm end is the first check.

**A new home, or too much handling.** A milk snake that arrived this week, or one handled every day, is stressed, and a milk snake is a flightier animal than a corn snake to begin with. A week of quiet fixes most of it.

**The prey.** Too large, too cold in the middle, or a species the snake was not raised on. A refuser will often take a mouse warmed a little longer, or one size down.

**Winter.** An adult from a temperate population slows down as the days shorten and eats less through winter while holding its weight. That is the season, not an illness, and a snake that is cooled for a deliberate winter rest is fasted first so nothing sits in a cold gut.

## When to Worry

A skipped meal or two in a warm enclosure, with the snake's weight holding, is nothing. A refusal running past a month, or any refusal with weight loss, wheezing, a swollen jaw, or repeated regurgitation, is the point to see a reptile vet. The [reptile stool and urates guide](/blog/reptile-stool-urates-hydration-guide/) covers what a snake's droppings say about hydration and digestion, and a milk snake produces enough of them to read.

One thawed mouse, sized to the body, every week or two, alone. A milk snake fed that way is a bright, easy animal that will eat for twenty years, and the discipline it asks for is the same as its cousins': not generosity, and never a cagemate.
