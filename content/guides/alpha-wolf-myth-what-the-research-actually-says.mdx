The alpha wolf is one of the most successful ideas in popular biology. It turns up in dog training advice, business books, gym culture, and nearly every nature documentary made before about 2010. The picture is always the same: a pack locked in permanent competition, with one animal on top because it fought its way there.

It is wrong. And the person who did the most to correct it is the same person who did the most to spread it.

## The Study Everything Was Built On

Between 1934 and 1942, a behaviourist named Rudolph Schenkel watched wolves at the Basle Zoological Garden in Switzerland. He published his findings in 1947, and that paper became the foundational text on wolf social behaviour for the next fifty years.

The problem was the wolves. Schenkel described up to ten animals kept together on a floor space of roughly 10 metres by 20 metres. They were unrelated to one another, collected and put in the same enclosure, because researchers at the time assumed that was roughly how wild packs formed anyway: unattached wolves assembling as winter came on.

Put ten unrelated adults of almost any social species in a pen that size and they will sort themselves out by force. That is what these wolves did, and Schenkel recorded it carefully. Rank, rivalry, a top male and a top female. The observations were accurate. The setting had manufactured the behaviour.

## How It Reached Everybody Else

In 1970, a young biologist named L. David Mech published *The Wolf: The Ecology and Behavior of an Endangered Species*. It was the first serious synthesis of wolf science since 1944, it leaned heavily on Schenkel, and it sold. Writing in 2008, Mech put it at more than 120,000 copies in circulation, and noted that most other general wolf books had drawn on his.

That is how a description of ten zoo animals in a 200 square metre pen became the default mental model for an entire species.

## What Wild Wolves Actually Do

From 1986 to 1998, Mech spent thirteen summers on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic with a pack he had habituated to his presence, sometimes watching from a metre away. Living far from human persecution, those wolves behaved normally around him.

What he found was a family. A breeding pair and their offspring from the previous one to three years. The young wolves leave: some as early as nine months, most between one and two years, and few stay past three. The parents are the only long-term members of the group.

Leadership was not won by anyone. The parents led because the pups followed them, the same way pups follow parents in most species. Across all thirteen summers, Mech recorded no dominance contests at all.

He published the correction in 1999 in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology*, under the title "Alpha status, dominance, and division of labor in wolf packs." Trying to understand wild wolf families from captive assemblages, he wrote, was like studying refugee camps to draw conclusions about human family life.

<FunFact>
The book that spread the myth outlasted the paper that corrected it by more than two decades. *The Wolf* stayed in print until 2022, twenty-three years after Mech published his 1999 retraction. In the 448 page textbook he co-edited in 2003 with 23 contributors, the word "alpha" appears in only six places, every one of them explaining why the term is outdated.
</FunFact>

## The Part That Usually Gets Overcorrected

This is where the internet tends to overshoot. "There is no alpha wolf" gets flattened into "wolves have no dominance at all," and that is not what the research found.

Mech's own data show a clear postural order. Every member of the Ellesmere pack, including the breeding female, submitted posturally to the breeding male. Food is where rank does something practical: the parents control who eats and when, which mostly means feeding the youngest first. What does not exist is the constant jockeying, the fight for the top slot, the deposed leader.

Mech also kept a narrow use for the word. In the relatively rare large packs that contain more than one breeding female, usually a mother and her grown daughters, competition between them is real, and terms like dominant and subordinate breeder are useful. He noted that Yellowstone is exactly that case: prey is unusually abundant there, so young wolves stay until two or three years old instead of one or two, and packs grow larger than average.

His objection was never to the syllable. It was to what the word smuggles in alongside it, which he described in 1999 as "a rigid, force-based dominance hierarchy."

## Taking It Back Took Decades

The 1999 paper was not a one-off. In 2000 he followed it with a paper in the *Canadian Field-Naturalist* working out how leadership is actually divided between the parents. He wrote the 2008 magazine piece specifically to explain to a general audience where the alpha idea had come from and why it should be dropped. His website still hosts Schenkel's original 1947 study with a note flagging it as the source of an outmoded idea.

He was realistic about the timescale. New science, he wrote in 2008, generally takes about twenty years to reach general acceptance. It has now been more than twenty-five years since the 1999 paper, and the alpha wolf is doing fine.

## Yellowstone, Where a Second Story Is Being Corrected

Wolves returned to Yellowstone in 1995. The National Park Service counts 41 released into the park between 1995 and 1997, part of 76 total including central Idaho. Elk numbers have fallen since, which the Park Service attributes to higher carnivore densities along with other factors.

Something real happened. The argument is about how big it was.

In early 2025, a team led by William Ripple reported a roughly 1,500% increase in willow crown volume between 2001 and 2020 and called it one of the strongest trophic cascades measured anywhere.

That October, a group led by Daniel MacNulty at Utah State University published a comment in the same journal arguing the result does not hold. Their central objection is that the model used to convert willow height into crown volume had height on both sides of the equation, so a strong relationship was guaranteed by arithmetic rather than by biology. They also found that of the 25 plots in the analysis, only 22 were measured in 2020, and just three of those had a 2001 baseline to compare against. And they noted that elk hunting outside the park, which affects elk numbers inside it, was left out of the causal story.

A 2024 field experiment that fenced plots and manipulated the water table had already reported a weak cascade, with willow growth limited by groundwater availability as well as by continued browsing.

None of this means the wolves did nothing. MacNulty's team describe the effect as modest and patchy, not absent. What it undercuts is the version that escaped into popular culture, in which wolves alone remade the park down to the course of its rivers.

## Why This One Matters

Most animal myths are harmless. This one is not, because it never stayed with wolves. It became the basis of dominance-based dog training, including the alpha roll, where a person pins a dog on its back. Citing Mech's own corrections, the American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior now advises vets not to refer clients to trainers who teach dominance hierarchy theory. Its position statement also points out that in real packs, higher-ranking wolves do not roll others onto their backs. The subordinate offers the posture.

Wolves are not the only animal whose reputation ran well ahead of the evidence. The same gap shows up with [wolverines](/blog/wolverine-facts-the-toughest-animal-pound-for-pound/) and with [sharks](/blog/how-dangerous-are-sharks-really/).

The wolves, for their part, are just raising their kids.
