# Common Western Hognose Snake Health Issues

Western hognoses are hardy snakes, and most of what shows up on this list traces back to substrate, humidity, or feeding habits, all fully within an owner's control.

## Respiratory Infection

One of the most common issues in this species. Watch for open-mouth breathing, wheezing, drooling or visible mucus, and lethargy. Always see a vet, this needs antibiotics and won't resolve on its own.

## Impaction

Hognoses are enthusiastic, somewhat messy eaters, and they're prone to swallowing substrate along with their food. Watch for a failure to pass waste, bloating, and lethargy. Feeding in a separate container away from the [loose, diggable substrate this species needs](/blog/hognose-snake-tank-setup-guide/), using a set of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CBKD64LP?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Lasnten Extra Long Stainless Steel Reptile Feeding Tongs with Rubber Tip">feeding tongs</AffiliateLink> to keep prey scent off your hands in the process, is the single most effective prevention, worth doing as standard practice rather than only after a problem shows up. Mild cases sometimes resolve on their own; anything that doesn't pass within a reasonable window needs a vet, and severe cases can require surgery.

## Obesity and Fatty Liver

A quieter but genuinely serious risk in captivity, where snakes get less activity than they would in the wild and are sometimes fed too much or too often for their enclosure size. This is a real contributor to shortened lifespans in this species. It's manageable at home by matching feeding frequency and portion size to your snake's actual activity level and adult size, rather than defaulting to a schedule that suited it as a fast-growing juvenile.

## Scale Rot (Blister Disease)

Caused by damp, dirty substrate, discolored or blistered belly scales are the sign to watch for. Prevention is straightforward: keep the <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TkxAVF" product="Aspen shavings substrate">substrate dry and clean</AffiliateLink>, which lines up directly with what this species needs anyway. See a vet if it progresses beyond mild discoloration.

## Mites and Internal Parasites

Both are treatable. Periodic fecal checks, especially for a newly acquired snake, catch parasites before they become a bigger issue. Isolating a new arrival for its first weeks keeps mites off the animals already in the room, which is the point of a [reptile quarantine period](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/).

## Regurgitation

A hognose that brings up a meal undigested or half digested is telling you something went wrong in the days around feeding. The usual triggers are handling within 48 hours of a meal, an enclosure running too cool, stress, a meal that was too big, dehydration, a dirty enclosure, intestinal parasites, contaminated prey, or sharing the enclosure with another snake.

What to do next is mostly nothing. Leave the snake completely alone, with no handling and no feeding, and check the temperatures. After two weeks of steady improvement, offer a small meal, a quarter to half the usual size, then rest it again until the next feeding. A snake that looks worse after regurgitating, or that brings up that small meal too, goes to a reptile vet.

## Shedding Problems

Young hognoses shed most often. A shed is coming when the colors fade, the eyes turn cloudy blue, and the snake eats less, hides more and turns more defensive, so leave it alone for that stretch. In a dry setup like this one, a snake that is struggling to shed usually needs more moisture in one spot while the rest of the enclosure stays dry. Mix water into the substrate in the humid hide to help it start or finish. Once the shed is off, check that both eye caps and the tail tip came away with it. A retained eye cap or tail tip that extra moisture does not loosen needs a vet, and the general method for [stuck shed on any reptile](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/) applies here.

## Weigh It Weekly

A hognose that is getting sick often shows it on the scale before it shows it anywhere else. Weigh once a week on a gram scale and keep a simple record of weight, meals, sheds and behavior. A sudden drop is the first thing to show a vet.

<FunFact>
Two of the most common problems on this list, respiratory infection and scale rot, both trace back to the exact same root cause: humidity or dampness running too high. That's really the throughline of hognose care. This species wants dry, not damp, and getting that one variable right prevents most of what shows up on a vet's exam table.
</FunFact>

## The Husbandry Pattern Worth Remembering

Humidity or dampness that runs too high causes both respiratory infection and scale rot, two of the more common problems on this list. Feeding on loose substrate causes impaction, easily avoided by feeding in a separate container. And overfeeding relative to activity level contributes to obesity over time. Get the dry setup and feeding habits right, and this is a genuinely low-maintenance snake to keep healthy.
