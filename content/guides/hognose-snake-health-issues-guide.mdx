# Common Western Hognose Snake Health Issues

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

Western hognoses are hardy snakes, and most of what shows up on this list traces back to substrate, humidity, or feeding habits, all fully within an owner's control.

## Respiratory Infection

One of the most common issues in this species. Watch for open-mouth breathing, wheezing, drooling or visible mucus, and lethargy. This comes from humidity running too high, temperatures too cool, or poor ventilation. This species prefers a dry setup, and dampness is more of a problem here than it is for many other snakes. Always see a vet: this needs antibiotics and won't resolve on its own.

## Impaction

A hognose strikes its food with enthusiasm and some mess, and a mouthful of substrate often goes down with the prey. Watch for a failure to pass waste, bloating, and lethargy. The single most effective prevention is feeding in a separate container away from the [loose, diggable substrate this species needs](/blog/hognose-snake-tank-setup-guide/). A set of <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CBKD64LP?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Lasnten Extra Long Stainless Steel Reptile Feeding Tongs with Rubber Tip">feeding tongs</AffiliateLink> keeps prey scent off your hands in the process. Make it standard practice from the first meal, before any problem shows up.

There is no fixed number of days to wait: how soon a snake passes waste depends on meal size and temperature, so learn your snake's usual gap after a meal. A missed stool past that gap is the early sign, since an impacted snake usually stops passing waste before it stops eating. A missed stool together with bloating, lethargy, refused food or regurgitation needs a reptile vet, not more waiting, and severe cases can require surgery.

## Obesity and Fatty Liver

A quieter but serious risk in captivity, where snakes get less activity than they would in the wild and are sometimes fed too much or too often for how little they move. It contributes to shortened lifespans in this species. It's manageable at home by matching feeding frequency and portion size to your snake's activity level and adult size, and dropping the schedule that suited it as a fast-growing juvenile.

## Scale Rot (Blister Disease)

Damp, dirty substrate causes it; discolored or blistered belly scales are the sign. Prevention is straightforward: keep the <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3TkxAVF" product="Aspen shavings substrate">substrate dry and clean</AffiliateLink>, which lines up directly with what this species needs anyway. See a vet if it progresses beyond mild discoloration.

## Mites and Internal Parasites

Both are treatable. Periodic fecal checks, especially for a newly acquired snake, catch parasites before they become a bigger issue. Isolating a new arrival for 3 to 6 months keeps mites off the animals already in the room, which is the point of a [reptile quarantine period](/blog/reptile-quarantine-guide/).

## Regurgitation

A hognose that brings up a meal undigested or half digested is telling you something went wrong in the days around feeding. The usual triggers are handling within 48 hours of a meal, an enclosure running too cool, stress, a meal that was too big, dehydration, a dirty enclosure, intestinal parasites, contaminated prey, or sharing the enclosure with another snake.

What to do next is mostly nothing. Leave the snake completely alone, with no handling and no feeding, and check the temperatures. After two weeks of steady improvement, offer a small meal, a quarter to half the usual size, then rest it again until the next feeding. A snake that looks worse after regurgitating, or that brings up that small meal too, goes to a reptile vet.

## Shedding Problems

Young hognoses shed most often. A shed is coming when the colors fade, the eyes turn cloudy blue, and the snake eats less, hides more and turns more defensive, so leave it alone for that stretch. In a dry setup like this one, a snake that is struggling to shed usually needs more moisture in one spot while the rest of the enclosure stays dry. Mix water into the substrate in the humid hide to help it start or finish. Once the shed is off, check that both eye caps and the tail tip came away with it. A retained eye cap or tail tip that extra moisture does not loosen needs a vet, and the general method for [stuck shed on any reptile](/blog/reptile-shedding-complete-guide/) applies here.

## Weigh It Weekly

A hognose that is getting sick often shows it on the scale before it shows it anywhere else. Weigh once a week on a gram scale and keep a simple record of weight, meals, sheds and behavior. A sudden drop is the first thing to show a vet.

## Dry Is the Whole Prescription

Dampness starts more hognose health problems than anything else a keeper controls, so the hygrometer is the reading to trust over how the enclosure looks. A dry bed, meals served off the substrate and a weekly weight that holds steady are the routine of a healthy hognose. When one of them slips, fix the enclosure first, and bring in a vet as soon as the snake shows a symptom.
