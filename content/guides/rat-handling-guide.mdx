# How to Handle a Rat (And Why It's the Least Likely Small Pet to Bite)

Rats are, by a wide margin, one of the most hands-on and trainable small pets on this site, and genuine biting is rare enough that most of what gets mistaken for it is something else entirely.

## One of the Most Hands-On Small Pets

This is where rats set themselves apart from almost everything else on this site. RSPCA behavior guidance describes rats as animals that learn quickly, memorize routes and pathways, and build real memories of people and environments, and with regular, gentle handling from a young age they become confident pets that actively seek out interaction rather than merely putting up with it. Using the same reward-based methods used to train dogs, rats can learn to come when called, respond to their own name, and perform simple tricks. That's not just a cute party trick either: rats have been the standard model animal for maze and spatial-learning research in psychology since Willard Small built the first rat maze at Clark University in 1900, and the famous "Kerplunk" experiments of 1907 specifically documented how quickly rats convert a learned route into fast, fluent, near-automatic behavior.

## Why Rats Rarely Bite

One exotic-pet care resource puts it plainly: rats are the least likely pocket pet to bite. When biting does happen, fear is overwhelmingly the most common cause, a startled or cornered rat defending itself, not an aggressive one looking for a fight, and fearful rats generally try to flee a scary situation instead of confronting it. A meaningful share of what new owners read as biting isn't biting at all. Rats groom each other through a fast, nibbling motion called allogrooming, and some rats extend that same gentle mouthing to their owners' fingers, easy to misread as aggression if you don't know what it actually is. Fingers that smell like food can also get investigated with the mouth simply because a rat's eyesight is poor enough that scent tells it more than sight does at close range.

## Getting Handling Right

Give a newly acquired rat a few days to settle into its surroundings before handling begins in earnest, and approach calmly instead of reaching in fast. Pick a rat up using both hands in a scooping motion, one hand under the chest, the other supporting the hindquarters, and hold it close to your body for security, never out at arm's length. **Never pick a rat up by the tail.** A rat's tail skin can shear away from the tissue underneath under pulling or grasping pressure, an injury called degloving or tail slip, and it's specifically caused by exactly this kind of handling. Scoop from underneath instead, and make sure children and unfamiliar guests know the same rule.

<FunFact>
The maze-learning link isn't a loose comparison, it's the origin of an entire field. Willard Small built the first rat maze at Clark University in 1900, under advisor Edmund Sanford, modeled on the hedge maze at England's Hampton Court Palace, and by 1907 the "Kerplunk" experiments run by John B. Watson and Harvey Carr, foundational figures in early behaviorism, were using rats specifically because they learned routes quickly and consistently enough to be scientifically useful. More than a century later, a well-socialized pet rat that responds to its own name is drawing on the exact same trainable intelligence that made the species the default choice for that research in the first place.
</FunFact>

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Reading gentle allogrooming or curious mouthing as aggression
- Grabbing or lifting by the tail
- Handling a newly arrived rat immediately instead of letting it settle in first
- Expecting the same confidence from a rat that's had little to no early socialization

## The Relationship You're Building

Daily handling, playtime, and supervised time outside the cage matter for a rat's wellbeing, not just for the bond with its owner, and a rat that gets that consistently tends to become a genuinely affectionate, easy-to-handle companion.
