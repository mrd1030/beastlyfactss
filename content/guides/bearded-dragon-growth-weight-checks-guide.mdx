# Bearded Dragon Growth, Weight, and At-Home Health Checks

<VetDisclaimer />
<AffiliateDisclosure />

A bearded dragon can't tell you when something's off, but its weight trend, growth rate, and stool can, if you're paying attention. This covers how to sex a dragon, what normal growth looks like month by month, how to read body condition at a glance, and how to turn a weekly weigh-in and a look at the litter tray into a genuinely useful early warning system. For everything else about keeping one, see our full [Bearded Dragon care guide](/guides/bearded-dragon/).

## Telling Male From Female

Sexing is reliable from about 4 to 6 months old. VCA Hospitals notes that sexing dragons under three months is very difficult, so treat any hatchling sexing, including from a pet store, as a guess to be rechecked later.

The most direct method is a flashlight check. In a dim room, gently lift the tail to about 90 degrees and shine a phone flashlight down through the base of the tail from above:

- **Males** show two hemipenal bulges, one on either side of a central dip, just past the vent.
- **Females** show a single central bulge or nothing at all.

Beyond the flashlight check, VCA Hospitals points to secondary traits in adults: males have larger femoral pores along the underside of the thighs, are slightly larger overall, and have bigger heads and darker beard displays than females. None of these are reliable on their own in a young dragon, they firm up with age.

## Growth Reference by Age

Ranges here are deliberately wide, and plenty of healthy dragons fall outside them. What actually matters is the trend: steady gains for a juvenile, a stable weight for an adult. Weigh weekly in grams on a kitchen or postal scale that reads to at least single grams, and log it somewhere you'll check back on.

That adult length range lines up with what VCA Hospitals reports for full-grown healthy dragons, 18 to 22 in (45 to 56 cm) nose to tail tip, which sits comfortably inside the wider range above. Most of the length gain happens in the first year, growth slows sharply once a dragon reaches sexual maturity around 12 to 18 months.

Frame size varies between individual dragons the same way it does between people, so two healthy adults at the same age can land at noticeably different points in that 300 to 500 g range without either one being over- or underweight. A dragon from smaller genetic stock that has always tracked toward the low end of its age bracket isn't a problem to fix, it's just that dragon's normal. That's a big part of why the chart above is framed as a reference range rather than a target, and why body condition, covered further down, matters more day to day than matching an exact number on a table.

<FunFact>
A hatchling bearded dragon can grow from under 10 grams to over 300 grams in its first year and a half, roughly a thirtyfold weight increase. That kind of growth rate is exactly why juveniles need more frequent feeding and calcium than adults, covered in our [feeding guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-feeding-guide/).
</FunFact>

## The Weekly Weigh-In Routine

A single weight reading tells you almost nothing on its own, a bearded dragon's healthy weight varies by frame size, sex, and age. A trend line over weeks is what actually matters, and it's also the single most useful thing you can hand a vet if something does go wrong.

1. Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before the first feeding, so readings aren't skewed by a full stomach.
2. Use a scale that reads in grams, not just ounces. A kitchen or postal scale works fine for a dragon of any size.
3. Log the date and number somewhere permanent rather than trusting memory, a notebook or a simple spreadsheet both work.
4. Watch the trend, not the number. A juvenile should show a generally upward line week to week. An adult's line should stay roughly flat, aside from a temporary dip around a shed or a female's egg-laying cycle.

The Merck Veterinary Manual makes the same point from the clinical side: relating body weight to length and overall conformation is how vets assess reptile body condition, and serial weight measurements over time are what let them evaluate growth, treatment response, and disease progression. The habit you're building at home is the same one a vet relies on during a workup, which is exactly why a weight log is worth bringing to any appointment.

## Body Condition at a Glance

Weight alone doesn't tell the whole story, a light dragon on a small frame can be perfectly healthy while a heavier dragon on the same frame is overweight. Body condition, judged by eye and touch, fills that gap.

The tail base is one of the most reliable places to check, both under- and overweight dragons show it first. VCA Hospitals describes the same landmark from the other direction: in a healthy dragon, the tail's girth near the back legs should look "round, plump, and full" from fat storage there, not sunken and bony.

If your dragon reads underweight, rule out low basking temperatures and internal parasites before assuming it just needs more food, an <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4flGgUg" product="Infrared temp gun">infrared thermometer</AffiliateLink> aimed at the actual basking surface (not the air) is the fastest way to confirm the enclosure itself isn't the problem, see our [tank setup guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-tank-setup-guide/) for target temperatures. If your dragon reads overweight, cut back on fatty feeders like waxworms and superworms, shift the diet further toward greens, and add climbing and free-roam time, our [enrichment guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-enrichment-guide/) covers how to do that safely. Fatty liver disease is the long-term risk of staying overweight, and it's largely preventable with an accurate read on body condition now.

<KeyTakeaway>
When a weight change is a vet visit: a juvenile that stops gaining for 3 to 4 weeks, or an adult that loses about 10 percent of its body weight outside brumation, or any weight loss paired with appetite loss, lethargy, or abnormal stool. Bring your weight log, a trend line tells a vet more than a single reading.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Reading Stool and Urates

Stool is the earliest free health check you get, look at it before you clean it up.

    ["Undigested insect parts", "Basking temperature too low, or prey too large", "Re-measure the basking surface and downsize the insects."],
    ["Black, tarry, or with red streaks", "Possible blood in the digestive tract", "Vet the same day."],
    ["Only urate, no feces, for several days", "Not eating, or brumation", "Check appetite and temperatures."],

NC State's veterinary hospital lists the same red flag directly in its bearded dragon feeding guidance, if your dragon stops defecating or its stool looks abnormal, watery, foul-smelling, or discolored, that's a sign veterinary care is needed. Our [health issues guide](/blog/bearded-dragon-health-issues-guide/) covers impaction and parasites in more detail if you're already seeing a concerning pattern, and VCA Hospitals' overview of bearded dragon diseases notes that parasitic infections in particular can show up first as loose stool before progressing to foul-smelling feces and weight loss, which is exactly why a runny stool that lasts more than a couple of days is worth a fecal test rather than a wait-and-see approach.

## Hydration and Reading Dehydration

Most of a dragon's water comes from food rather than a dish. Many barely touch standing water, some drink from it regularly, so keep a shallow dish available and refresh it daily regardless. Beyond that:

- Mist the salad itself, not the enclosure walls. Water droplets on greens get eaten; a damp enclosure just raises humidity into a range that risks respiratory infection.
- A shallow lukewarm <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FNVWJRQL?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Reptile Tray Bathing Pool (38in, large)">soak</AffiliateLink>, 15 to 20 minutes, once or twice a week, chest-deep at most and never unsupervised. Most dragons drink during it.
- Hydrating foods as extras, not staples: hornworms, silkworms, cucumber or melon pieces, cactus pad.
- A dragon that won't drink on its own can have water dripped onto the snout with a syringe or dropper and licked off. Never squirt water directly into the mouth.

Signs of dehydration include wrinkled or loose skin outside a shed, sunken eyes, thick or stringy saliva, yellow or hard chalky urates, lethargy, and skin that stays tented for a moment when gently pinched on the side. That last sign has real backing in general reptile medicine, the Merck Veterinary Manual specifically lists skin tenting as a visual clue for dehydration and cachexia in reptiles, alongside the note that reptile dehydration usually traces back to prolonged appetite loss or a lack of accessible water rather than the vomiting or diarrhea patterns seen in mammals. Mild dehydration responds well to soaking and hydrating foods; a dragon that looks sunken and lethargic on top of it needs a vet visit for fluids rather than more soaking at home.

## Worked Example: Reading a Real Trend

Say a 4-month-old juvenile weighs 55 g, then 62 g, then 58 g, then 64 g over four consecutive weekly weigh-ins. That's a normal juvenile pattern, gains most weeks with the occasional flat or slightly down week around a shed, still trending upward overall and within the 30 to 90 g range expected at that age. Compare that to a juvenile that weighs 55 g, then 54 g, then 52 g, then 50 g over the same four weeks, a steady decline with no shed or brumation to explain it. That's the "stopped gaining for 3 to 4 weeks" threshold that warrants a vet visit, and it's a pattern you would only catch by weighing weekly and writing the numbers down, not by eyeballing the dragon each day.

For an adult, the read is different because the baseline isn't supposed to move much. A 2-year-old female sitting at 420 g who logs 415 g, 422 g, 410 g, and 418 g across a month is just showing normal week-to-week fluctuation around a stable weight, nothing to act on. If that same dragon instead reads 420 g, 395 g, 370 g, and 350 g over four weeks, that's roughly a 17 percent drop, well past the 10 percent threshold, and worth a vet visit even before any other symptom shows up. The math only works if the earlier weigh-ins happened, which is the argument for starting the habit the week a dragon comes home rather than waiting until something already looks wrong.

## What to Watch

None of these checks require special equipment beyond a kitchen scale and a notebook, but together they catch most serious problems weeks before they'd otherwise become obvious. Weigh weekly, look before you clean up, and treat a changing trend as more meaningful than any single reading.
