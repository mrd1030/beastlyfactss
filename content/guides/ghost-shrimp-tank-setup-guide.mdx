# Ghost Shrimp Tank Setup: Minimal Requirements, But Compatibility Is Where People Get Caught Out

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Ghost shrimp tolerate a genuinely wide range of tank conditions, which is exactly why they end up added to an existing tank as an impulse "cleanup crew" purchase more often than almost any other animal on this site, cheap, undemanding, and sold right at the checkout counter of a fish store. The setup itself really is easy. Where it pays to slow down is compatibility, since the same small size that makes ghost shrimp easy to house also makes them either prey or predator depending entirely on what else is in the tank. For costs, see our [cost guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-cost-guide/), or the full [Ghost shrimp care guide](/guides/ghost-shrimp/) for everything else.

## Tank Size

[The Shrimp Farm](https://www.theshrimpfarm.com/posts/shrimp-caresheet-ghost-shrimp-palaemonetes-sp/) lists 5 gallons as the workable minimum. Bigger is always more forgiving here, but ghost shrimp genuinely don't demand the larger footprint some other tank residents need.

## Temperature

72 to 82°F is the range most care sources converge on. Tolerance stretches noticeably wider than that on both ends, sources commonly cite a survivable range closer to 65 to 85°F, which is a real part of why this species ships and sells so cheaply and in such bulk. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/4eQY1L5" product="Submersible aquarium heater">heater</AffiliateLink> is only strictly necessary if your room regularly runs below that comfortable range; a stable indoor room can often get by without one.

## Water Chemistry

Aim for pH 7 to 8 and general hardness of 3 to 15 dGH, according to [The Shrimp Farm](https://www.theshrimpfarm.com/posts/shrimp-caresheet-ghost-shrimp-palaemonetes-sp/). Wide tolerance doesn't mean water quality stops mattering, ammonia and nitrite both need to read zero before stocking, exactly what a completed nitrogen cycle is protecting against, covered in more depth in our [health issues guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-health-issues-guide/).

<FunFact>
Whether ghost shrimp can actually breed in a plain freshwater tank turns out to be a more precise question than "yes" or "no." A 1990 laboratory study in the [Journal of Crustacean Biology](https://doi.org/10.2307/1548408) tested *Palaemonetes paludosus* across salinities from 0 to 35 parts per thousand, and found egg hatching and successful metamorphosis were reliably limited to a 0 to 5 ppt band, meaning true freshwater is inside the viable window, not excluded from it, while adult survival held up to a 96-hour LC50 between 30 and 35 ppt. The researchers concluded the species "can complete its life cycle in brackish water," which lines up with why home breeding attempts in a plain freshwater tank are hit-or-miss rather than reliable: the larvae can survive there, but the odds genuinely improve with a trace of salinity, and it's also exactly why shipments labeled simply "Palaemonetes spp." sometimes mix in other Palaemonetes species that need brackish conditions outright.
</FunFact>

## Filtration

A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/3T8xyAd" product="Sponge filter">gentle sponge filter</AffiliateLink> is the standard, sensible choice. It keeps water clean without a current strong enough to stress a small shrimp, and critically, it has no exposed intake tube capable of pulling in a shrimp or a freshly molted individual the way a standard hang-on-back filter's intake can.

## Substrate

Sand or fine gravel both work well. [Chewy](https://www.chewy.com/education/fish/general/ghost-shrimp-aquarium-care) makes a specific, genuinely useful recommendation here: dark gravel, especially black, makes a translucent shrimp's markings noticeably easier to actually see against the background. ## Hiding Spots and Planting

This matters more than the easygoing reputation suggests. Dense live plants and a piece of driftwood give a molting shrimp somewhere to disappear during the hours it's soft and defenseless after shedding its shell, real cover against both predatory fish and other ghost shrimp, which will opportunistically target a freshly molted tankmate, a risk covered in full in our [feeding guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-feeding-guide/). Driftwood carries an added benefit here too: the biofilm that grows on its surface over time is exactly the kind of food this species scavenges on its own.

## Compatibility: Prey or Predator Depending on Size

Ghost shrimp sit in an unusual spot on the food chain: small enough to be prey for a lot of fish, large enough as adults to be a real predator toward smaller shrimp and their own molting kind. [FishLab](https://fishlab.com/ghost-shrimp/) is blunt about the first half of that: "many fish would happily munch on legs or antennae, or, if the fish is large enough, even swallow the shrimp whole," and recommends small, unaggressive tankmates plus a heavily planted tank for cover.

[Betta fish](/guides/betta-fish/) are the pairing that comes up constantly, and the risk runs mostly in one direction. [BettaCareFishGuide](https://www.bettacarefishguide.com/can-ghost-shrimp-and-bettas-live-together/) is direct: "ghost shrimp pose no threat to your betta," aside from a small choking risk, while the shrimp themselves can genuinely end up eaten, especially smaller or juvenile individuals. The same source recommends a tank no smaller than 10 gallons for the pairing, plenty of hiding places, and adding the shrimp to the tank before the betta where possible so the fish doesn't imprint on them as a target from day one.

The reverse risk, ghost shrimp preying on smaller tankmates, applies most directly to [cherry shrimp](/guides/cherry-shrimp/) and to their own freshly molted colony members, both covered in detail in our [feeding guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-feeding-guide/). The short version for stocking purposes: think about size in both directions before adding any tankmate, not just whether the pairing has a reputation for being "peaceful."

## Why This Is Often an Impulse Add-On, and What to Check First

Because ghost shrimp are this cheap and this tolerant of imperfect conditions, they're an easy add to a tank that already exists rather than something most people research and plan around first, exactly the [cost guide](/blog/ghost-shrimp-cost-guide/)'s point about the price tag hiding the real story. That's mostly fine, their actual setup needs are genuinely minimal. The one thing worth pausing on before dropping a bag of shrimp into an existing community tank is exactly what this section covers: check what's already swimming in there, and whether the size balance runs safely in both directions.
