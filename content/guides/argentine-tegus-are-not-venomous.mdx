Type "are tegus" into a search bar and the autocomplete fills in with "dangerous," "aggressive," and "poisonous" before anything else. It's the single most common question about this species, by a wide margin, so it's worth answering properly instead of with a one-line reassurance.

## Are Tegus Venomous?

No. No venom gland or venom-delivery apparatus has ever been documented in tegus (family Teiidae), and nothing in the herpetological literature suggests otherwise.

The confusion has a real, if slightly indirect, source. In 2005/2006, researchers led by Bryan Fry found that several lizard groups long assumed to be non-venomous, monitor lizards (Varanidae) among them, actually do produce venom, overturning decades of assumption. A 2009 follow-up from the same group went further and confirmed venom specifically in the Komodo dragon, the largest living monitor. Both findings got wide press coverage, and "some big lizards turned out to be venomous after all" is exactly the kind of headline that outlives its own details in casual conversation. Tegus get lumped in partly because they're often compared to monitors, our [tegu guide covers that comparison directly](/guides/tegu/), being a similarly large, intelligent, dog-like lizard popular with the same keepers. But tegus and monitors aren't closely related, and both venom findings were specific to monitors and a few other lineages. Neither was ever about tegus.

## Are Tegus Aggressive?

This one deserves a real answer instead of a flat no, because there's a genuine behavior underneath the reputation, it's just not the one people assume.

Wild-caught tegus, and tegus that were captive-bred but never handled consistently as juveniles, absolutely can be defensive: tail-whipping, hissing, and biting when they feel cornered or threatened. That's not a myth. What's misleading is treating that as a fixed trait of the species rather than a predictable result of how the individual animal was raised.

<KeyTakeaway>
Well-socialized, captive-bred tegus are so consistently described as calm that "the most dog-like reptile" has become the standard comparison in the hobby, recognizing their names, coming when called, and seeking out physical contact with a familiar keeper. The gap between that and a defensive wild-caught animal isn't a different species, it's the same species raised two different ways.
</KeyTakeaway>

The bite itself is real and worth respecting: tegus have powerful jaws built to crush hard-shelled prey like eggs and snails, and an adult bite hurts. But that's a statement about jaw strength, not about temperament. A defensive bite from a startled or mishandled animal is a husbandry and socialization issue, the same category of risk as any pet capable of a painful bite, not evidence of an unusually dangerous species.

## The Research Behind Why Tegus Seem So Aware

Part of what makes tegus read as more alert and responsive than a typical reptile has a genuine physiological basis. A 2016 study published in *Science Advances* documented facultative endothermy in tegus: during breeding season, their body temperature rises measurably above ambient temperature, something close to warm-blooded regulation in an animal that's supposed to be a strict ectotherm. It's one of the only documented cases of this in a reptile.

That doesn't make a tegu warm-blooded, and it isn't a direct explanation for temperament. But it's a real, peer-reviewed data point behind why this species behaves less like the sluggish, purely instinct-driven reptile stereotype and more like an animal that's actually tracking its environment, which likely feeds into both the "surprisingly smart" reputation and, in an undersocialized individual, a more alert defensive response too.

<FunFact>
Tegus can move surprisingly fast for a lizard their size, and keepers occasionally see one lift its front half entirely off the ground at a full sprint, running a few strides on its hind legs alone. It's the same basic mechanism seen in some smaller lizards built for speed, just unexpected in an animal that can weigh as much as a large housecat.
</FunFact>

## The Danger That Is Real, Just Not to You

If there's an accurate "tegus are dangerous" headline, it's not about keepers, it's about Florida. Argentine tegus have established real, breeding wild populations in several counties there, where they prey on the eggs and nests of native species, including gopher tortoises and American crocodiles. That's a documented ecological problem, not a myth, and it's exactly why the state banned new acquisition outright in 2021. ## The Bottom Line

Not venomous, full stop. Not inherently aggressive either, the "dangerous" reputation tracks back to wild-caught or poorly socialized individuals, not the species as kept and raised properly. The risk from tegus runs the other direction: an established invasive population that's genuinely bad news for Florida's native wildlife, which is the reason the [tegu legal guide](/blog/argentine-tegu-legal-guide/) reads the way it does.
