# How to Handle a Russian Tortoise

Russian tortoises tolerate being picked up, but they are not a species that needs it or seeks it out. Handling is for health checks, soaks, weighing and moving the tortoise, and most of the risk in those moments comes from a fall.

## Picking One Up

Scoop the tortoise from below with a palm under the plastron instead of grabbing the shell from above, then hold the shell with two hands so the body and legs are both supported. Keep the grip firm but gentle, and work low, sitting or kneeling over the floor or a table. A fall can do serious damage to a tortoise and can be fatal. Wash hands before and after, as with any reptile.

## Two Tortoises in One Enclosure

Male Russian tortoises do not tolerate each other. During the mating period males fight viciously, and the loser ends up injured, flipped onto his back, or chronically stressed and prone to disease. A male's courtship is rough on females as well: he circles her and bites at her head and legs, and with no place to escape him a female takes that attention constantly.

Females are usually more tolerant of each other, but not always. Some become bullies, and the subordinate female starts hiding most of the day and can become sick from the stress. That is the sign to separate them. A shared enclosure needs extra floor, at least 10 more square feet for each added tortoise, and enough hides, plants and logs to break every sightline, so either animal can get fully out of the other's view. [Sexing a Russian tortoise](/blog/tortoise-sexing-eggs-and-egg-binding-guide/) comes before any pairing, because a male housed with a female will probably produce eggs.

## Escape-Proofing Checklist

Russian tortoises climb better than their shape suggests and dig persistently, and wild ones dig burrows several meters long. Before trusting an enclosure, check:

- **Walls are solid and smooth.** Wire, mesh or chain link invites climbing, and a tortoise that can see through a gap will fixate on it and hurt itself trying to get out.
- **Nothing makes a ladder.** Hides, rocks, water dishes and heaped substrate stay away from the walls, and corners are checked for a foothold.
- **Outdoor walls go down as well as up.** Sink them at least 12 inches into the ground, in concrete or buried wire mesh, and build them about 2 feet high with a lip of around 4 inches projecting inward along the top.
- **The top is covered outdoors.** Wide mesh over the pen keeps predators out.
- **Tunnels are checked regularly.** Walk the perimeter and look for fresh digging along the base.

Pen construction, shade and predators are laid out in full in the [outdoor reptile housing guide](/blog/outdoor-reptile-housing-guide/), which applies directly to a digger this determined.

## First-Timer Mistakes

- Housing in a glass aquarium instead of an open-topped table. Glass ventilates poorly, and a tortoise that can see through a wall it cannot pass tends to pace along it.
- Over-humidifying the enclosure. This is an arid-adapted species, and excess moisture causes more problems than it solves.
- Housing two males together, or adding a second tortoise to an enclosure without the extra space and cover to keep them apart.

## Size Is the Reason This Works Indoors

A Russian tortoise stays small enough to live indoors for its entire multi-decade life. A sulcata grows into a giant that needs outdoor space and cannot be kept inside long-term, which the [sulcata tortoise handling guide](/blog/sulcata-tortoise-handling-guide/) shows in practice.
