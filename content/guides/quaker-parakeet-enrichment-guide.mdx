# Quaker Parakeet Enrichment: The Only Parrot That Builds a House

<AffiliateDisclosure />

Nearly every enrichment guide on this site works out what an animal does in the wild and tries to allow some version of it. With quakers, the answer is unusually specific. For everything else about them, see our complete [Quaker Parakeet care guide](/guides/quaker-parakeet/).

<KeyTakeaway>
Quakers are the **only parrots that build their own nests** rather than using a tree cavity, and they build large communal stick structures with separate chambers. Captive quakers still weave, given anything to weave with. That is a species-specific enrichment channel no other pet parrot has, and most owners never open it.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the Research Actually Says

Nothing on quaker enrichment specifically. The parrot literature borrows across, covered in full in our [African Grey Parrot enrichment guide](/blog/african-grey-parrot-enrichment-guide/) and our [Cockatoo enrichment guide](/blog/cockatoo-enrichment-guide/).

**Foraging opportunity and increased physical complexity both prevented and reduced psychogenic feather picking** in young Amazon parrots. **Training sessions outperformed medication and feeding enrichment** for plucking in sulphur-crested cockatoos. And wild parrots spend **up to six hours a day** working for food against a captive bird's few minutes.

The nest building is not a research finding, it is natural history, and it is well documented and specific enough to build a section on.

<FunFact>
Feral quaker colonies in the United States and Europe build their communal nests on electricity pylons and transformers, which is the actual reason the species is banned in several states. The enrichment behaviour and the legal restriction are the same behaviour.
</FunFact>

## Weaving Material, Which Is the Species-Specific One

Start here, because it is the thing you can do for a quaker that you cannot do for any other parrot.

Safe untreated twigs, willow strips, palm, seagrass and plain paper strips. Quakers push material into cage bars, weave it through toys, and work at the structure for hours. A <AffiliateLink href="https://amzn.to/45a3irm" product="Rope Perch Toy with Bell (chew and shred)">rope and shred toy</AffiliateLink> gives something to work into, and natural material is what actually gets woven.

Two cautions. Avoid loose stringy fibre, which wraps around toes and necks. And watch for territorial behaviour: nest building sometimes brings cage aggression, particularly in spring, in which case scale the material back rather than cutting it off entirely.

## Foraging, Which Is Still the Bulk of It

The six hour gap applies here as much as anywhere. Nothing served in a plain bowl as the default.

## Company, Because They Are Colonial

Unusually social even by parrot standards, since colonial nesting is rare in the group. They need either a companion bird or a genuinely involved owner.

Interaction has to be daily and real. A quaker in a cage in a busy room is not getting social contact, it is watching some.

## Training and Talking

Quakers are among the better small talkers and take well to training. Short daily positive-reinforcement sessions cover both the mental work and the social attention that the cockatoo study identified as the effective ingredient.

Ten minutes twice a day, ended while the bird is still keen. Our [handling guide](/blog/quaker-parakeet-handling-guide/) covers the approach, including managing the cage territoriality this species is known for.

## Sleep

Ten to twelve hours of dark, quiet sleep. Under-slept parrots get louder, nippier and more prone to plucking, and it is the cheapest problem on this page to fix.

## Priority Order

1. Weaving and nest material, which nothing else replaces for this species
2. All food delivered through foraging
3. A large cage with a clear flight path, and hours outside it
4. Daily social interaction or a companion
5. Short daily training sessions
6. Ten to twelve hours of dark, quiet sleep
7. Novel objects, rotated

## What Not to Do

Do not skip the weaving material, since it is the one thing this species uniquely needs. Do not use loose fibrous string as nesting material. Do not ignore spring cage aggression, and do not respond to it by removing every outlet at once. Do not feed from a plain bowl. Do not let a quaker escape, which is how feral colonies start and why the bans exist. And check your state law before buying, because keeping a quaker is prohibited outright in several of them, with the ownership rules covered in our [cost guide](/blog/quaker-parakeet-cost-guide/).
