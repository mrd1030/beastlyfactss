<LegalDisclaimer />

A coati is a procyonid, like the raccoon and the [kinkajou](/blog/kinkajou-legal-guide/), and most of the country regulates it exactly the way it regulates the kinkajou: by the family name or the order name, or not at all. Where a clause takes "the family Procyonidae" or "the order Carnivora", the coati is caught. Where the clause says raccoon, the coati walks past it. Counted across all 52 jurisdictions, the white-nosed coati is legal with no state permit in 24 states, banned in 13 jurisdictions, a permit animal in 11, restricted in two and undecided in two. The kinkajou's row is 23, 14, 11, 2 and 2. The difference is three cells, and every one of them comes from a list that wrote the word "coatimundi" down.

That word matters because it was written by drafters who were thinking about the coati and not about its cousins. Oklahoma's exemption list names the coatimundi and frees it from a license the kinkajou still needs. Nebraska's import list names the coatimundi and lets it in while the kinkajou stays out. Texas's threatened species list names the white-nosed coati, a native at the far edge of its range, and that single entry turns the most permissive state on the kinkajou's map into a ban for the coati. And in Arizona and New Mexico, the only two states where coatis live wild, being native makes the animal harder to keep, not easier.

## The Three Lists That Say Coatimundi

Oklahoma is the clearest. OAC 800:25-25-3(a) opens "The following wildlife species are exempt from import and export permits, commercial wildlife breeders licenses, noncommercial wildlife breeders licenses and commercial hunting area license requirements", and the list that follows runs from alpacas and bison through sugar gliders, wallabies and kangaroos to:

> (35) Fennec Fox. (36) Coatimundi. (37) Primates.

A coati in Oklahoma needs no import permit and no breeder's license, the same footing as a fennec fox. The kinkajou does not appear anywhere on the list, so it falls to the default and needs both. Two animals sold by the same breeders, and the state's paperwork turns on which one the drafter remembered.

Nebraska wrote the same word into a list that does the opposite job. 166 NAC 8 § 001.08A reads "Importation: the following wildlife may be imported", and it is a closed list: anything not on it comes in only to an accredited or public zoo, a licensed exhibit or a circus under § 001.08A1. Between the chinchilla and the degu sits "Coatimundi, Nasua spp.", which takes the whole genus. Because the coati is not native to Nebraska, it is not a "wild mammal" under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-246 either, so no Captive Wildlife Permit attaches once it is in. The kinkajou, absent from the list, cannot lawfully enter the state at all. In Oklahoma the missing word costs a kinkajou keeper a license; in Nebraska it costs them the animal.

Texas is the third, and it runs the other way. The white-nosed coati is a Texas native, a small population at the northern edge of a range that runs to Colombia, and 31 TAC § 65.175 lists it as state threatened: "White-nosed Coati, Nasua narica, Mammal, T". TPWD says its regulations "prohibit the taking, possession, transportation, or sale of any of the animal species designated by state law as endangered or threatened without the issuance of a permit", and those permits go to science, zoos, education and rehabilitation. The nongame possession caps that clear so many Texas exotics cannot help here, because § 65.325(b)(8) takes threatened species out of that subchapter. So the state with no rule at all for a kinkajou bans the coati by name, and a breeder's animal born in Ohio is in the same position as one trapped in the Texas brush country, because the listing attaches to the species rather than the animal's origin.

<FunFact>
Five more lists name the coati to put it somewhere specific. New Jersey's 7:25-4.3(a)2.iii carries "Coatimundi--Nasua spp." on its permit list, Maine's § 7.18(3)(C) carries "Nasua narica White-nosed Coati" in Category 2, Oregon's 635-056-0060(1)(b)(E) carries "Coatimundis -- Procyonidae -- Nasua All species" on its noncontrolled list, Idaho's IDAPA 02.04.27 § 409 carries "Coatimundi" as a deleterious exotic animal, and Arkansas's Addendum R1.02 carries the white-nosed coati beside the ring-tailed coati and the mountain coatis. Hawaii and New Mexico take the whole genus to prohibit it. The kinkajou is named on fewer lists than this, and the raccoon on far more.

## The Native-Range Problem

Arizona and New Mexico are the only two states where a coati might wander through a backyard on its own, and in both the animal's being native closes doors rather than opening them.

Arizona's R12-4-406(F) restricts "All species of the order Carnivora", so a coati needs a special license whatever its origin, and none of the exemptions is a pet route. On top of that, because the white-nosed coati lives wild in southeastern Arizona, a coati taken from the wild falls under the live-take rules at R12-4-404, R12-4-405 and Commission Order 43, which require a hunting license and treat the animal as wildlife taken, not as a pet acquired. A captive-bred coati from another state and a wild one from the Arizona mountains both end up at the same special license, with the wild one carrying a take rule on top.

New Mexico's 2023 Director's Species Importation List files the genus in Group IV with a note: "Procyonidae | Nasua | Coatimundi | IV | State Protected Species". Group IV importation is prohibited for the general public and allowed only for scientific study, department-approved restoration, zoological display, temporary events, use as a service animal, or by a qualified expert. The rule governs bringing the animal into New Mexico rather than keeping one already there, and the protected species status governs the wild one, so neither direction has a pet in it.

## Where the Family Is the Rule

Everything else on the banned side of the map is the kinkajou's story told again, and the [raccoon's](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/) before that. California's 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(K)(4) reads "Family Procyonidae-All species-(D)", the order heading names coatis, and the department's caging standards list the coatimundi under Procyonidae, so there is no doubt what the family line reaches; none of the four exceptions beneath it is a coati, and no California permit type covers a pet. Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(3)(B)(xxi) makes "a member within the family Procyonidae (including, but not limited to, common raccoon)" a Category Two animal, open under (b)(3) only to veterinarians, parks, zoos, nature centers, museums, exhibitors and laboratories. New York City Health Code § 161.01(b)(5) prohibits "All Procyonidae" and ends its list of examples with "panda and coatimundi".

Georgia's R. 391-4-8-.05(1)(a)3 requires a license for "Order Carnivora (weasels, ferrets, cats, bears, wolves, etc.) - All species", with the European ferret as the one exception, and O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues those licenses only to the animal trade and to public exhibitors. Rhode Island's § 3.7(A) prohibits every carnivore "for which there is no USDA conditionally licensed or unconditionally licensed rabies vaccine" to anyone but researchers, exhibitors and AZA facilities, and there is no licensed rabies vaccine for a coati. Hawaii's § 4-71-6.5 Part A lists "Nasua (all species in genus) coati", the research and exhibition tier.

New Hampshire is a ban by a near miss. Its controlled table names "Coatimundi Nasua nasua", the South American coati, in permittee category 5, the exhibitor category, and does not name *Nasua narica* at all. Fis 804.04(b) says "All species not specifically listed under the categories of non-controlled, prohibited, or controlled shall be designated as prohibited", so the white-nosed coati is prohibited by default, and the listed one is exhibitor-only; a private keeper gets no permit either way. Colorado's page says any species not listed as domestic or unregulated "is regulated wildlife and a CPW license is required" and that "CPW will not issue a license for private possession (eg pet) of regulated wildlife"; its mammal list rules the South American coati regulated by name, and the white-nosed one lands in the same place unlisted. Montana's § 87-5-705(1) bars any exotic wildlife not "allowed by law or commission rule", and no procyonid is on any of its three lists. Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars a pet permit for any game animal, and Alaska counts every mammal as game. The District of Columbia's § 8-1808(j) closed list of seven pet categories has nothing a coati fits.

## The Permit States

Eleven jurisdictions issue a permit, and ten of them issue it to a private keeper, one more than for the kinkajou. The extra one is Maine. 09-137 CMR ch. 7 § 7.18(3)(C) lists "Nasua narica White-nosed Coati" in Category 2, the one restricted tier a private keeper can apply to, while the kinkajou sits in Category 1 with the exhibitors. The Category 2 permit under § 7.07(3) goes to "an individual with demonstrated experience with the care and husbandry of the species", documented as a year of practical husbandry or 100 hours of experience, training or apprenticeship with two professional references, plus a plan for the animal's care if the keeper dies or falls ill. It is the only permit on this page that asks the applicant to prove they can keep the animal.

New Jersey names the genus at 7:25-4.3(a)2.iii, "Coatimundi--Nasua spp.", which makes it a hobby permit animal under 4.6(a)1, subject to the possession criteria at 4.7; nondomestic dogs, bears and cats are kept out of the hobby category, and procyonids are not. Florida reaches a permit by default: the coati is in neither Class I nor Class II under 68A-6.002, which makes it Class III, FWC's own personal pet page lists the coatimundi among common Class III species, and 68A-6.003(8) says "Persons possessing Class III wildlife as personal use wildlife shall obtain a no-cost permit from the Executive Director." Delaware's 903-6.2 requires a $25 Individual permit for a nonnative wild mammal not on the exempt list. Nevada's NRS 503.597 requires the Department's written consent to bring any wildlife into the state, and the coati is on neither Nevada list, so the consent is the permit; the only procyonid Nevada prohibits is the raccoon. Arkansas's Addendum R1.02 carries "White-nosed Coati (Nasua narica)", so one may be kept if it came from a Commission-permitted wildlife breeder or in on a Wildlife Importation Permit, with acquisition records and a veterinary disease certificate. Idaho's IDAPA 02.04.27 § 409 lists "01. Coatimundi" under Procyonidae, and § 100.01 bars possessing a listed animal without a possession permit from the Administrator, with no AZA facility condition for a procyonid. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 13(d) says a permit "may be authorized upon approval for other warm-blooded wildlife", discretionary rather than owed. Virginia's 4VAC15-30-40 table takes "Procyonidae, All Species, Raccoons and Relatives", so a special permit is required to import, possess or sell one.

The coati is on neither Massachusetts's nor Vermont's exemption list. Massachusetts keeps a permit on paper, and MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard.

## The Rest

Utah is restricted rather than banned: Table 3b-1 does not list the coati, and its header says "All species not listed will require an assessment before collection, importation, or possession will be authorized", an application with no published answer. Pennsylvania and North Dakota are the two unclear cells, for the kinkajou's reasons. Pennsylvania's § 137.1(a)(4) reads "In the family Procyonidae. All species of raccoons.", which names the family and then limits itself to raccoons, and whether the catch-all at (a)(11) on wildlife "captive bred in another state or nation" reaches a coati is a question no Pennsylvania text answers. North Dakota's NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(6) sends an animal in none of its three categories to the Board of Animal Health "for determination of importation requirements and licensure requirements prior to importation", so the answer is whatever the board decides.

Oregon is the third state that names the coati to clear it: OAR 635-056-0060(1)(b)(E) lists "Coatimundis -- Procyonidae -- Nasua All species" among noncontrolled species, which need no ODFW permit. The remaining 21 legal states are silence. In each, the dangerous animal list names cats, bears, wolves and primates and no procyonid, the furbearer definitions name the raccoon by common name, and the game chapters reach natives only. New York's § 180.1(b)(5) stops at "Raccoons in the Genus Procyon", and *Nasua* is not *Procyon*. Ohio's fur-bearing definition names the raccoon and no other procyonid. Alabama bars "species of raccoon", which does not reach a coati on its words. South Dakota's possession permit covers five carnivore families and Procyonidae is not among them. Kentucky allows it but needs a transportation permit to bring it in; Wisconsin and Washington need an agriculture import document.

The state's silence is not the city's. Texas, Ohio, Illinois, Michigan, Kentucky, Washington, Maryland, West Virginia, Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi, Louisiana, Oklahoma, Nebraska and Oregon all say in terms that a city or county may prohibit what the state does not, and a coati is the kind of animal municipal exotic lists were written for.

## The Takeaway

The coati's map is the kinkajou's with three cells moved, and the three cells are where someone wrote the animal's name down. Oklahoma and Nebraska named it to clear it; Texas named it to protect it. Where no one named it, the family clause decides, and the family clause reaches the coati in California, Connecticut, Georgia, Rhode Island, Virginia, Arizona and New York City and nowhere else. Ten states issue a keeper's permit, Maine's the only one that asks for experience.
