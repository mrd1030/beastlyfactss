<LegalDisclaimer />

A red fox lives wild in nearly every state, and that fact decides more of its legal map than anything about the animal in a living room. Because it is native, the red fox belongs to the chapters that govern trapping, pelts and fur farming, and those chapters were written with three keepers in mind: the trapper, the fur farmer and the hound-pen operator. The pet owner is not among them. So the question "can I keep a red fox" gets answered, over and over, by a sentence about fur. Pennsylvania lets licensed propagators hold one "specifically for propagation for fur farming purposes" and bars their offspring from the pet trade. Oregon exempts the fox from its canid ban only "when part of a commercial fur farming operation". Montana's rabies law allows one in "a fur-bearing enterprise". Idaho's only exception is "fur farms holding domestic fur-bearing animals". Where the chapter offers a license a keeper can hold, it is nearly always a furbearer license: Texas, Ohio and Illinois call it a propagation or breeder permit and issue it to people who will never breed anything.

Read across all 52 jurisdictions, that gives 28 bans, 18 permits, three states where a red fox is legal on a condition about its pedigree or capture, one that restricts the whole order Carnivora, and two whose text will not decide. The fennec fox, a three-pound Saharan animal with no wild population here, is banned in fewer places and permitted in more, and the reason is nativity: a state that writes "fox" with no species catches both, but a state that writes a native-wildlife rule catches only the red.

## Fur Farms Can Have One. You Cannot.

Pennsylvania wrote the sentence that explains this whole map. 58 Pa. Code § 137.1(a)(2) prohibits, in the family Canidae:

> Species and subspecies of the coyote, the red and gray fox and a full-blooded wolf or crossbreed thereof not licensed by the Department of Agriculture. Lawfully acquired coyotes and red or gray foxes may be imported or possessed, or both, by licensed propagators specifically for propagation for fur farming purposes. Coyotes and red and gray foxes imported under this exception and their progeny may not be sold or transferred for wildlife pet purposes or released into the wild.

That is a ban with a fur-farm door in it, and the door is locked from the pet side: the propagator's kits may not be sold "for wildlife pet purposes". The only other live-fox permit in the chapter, § 137.31(a)(1), lets a trapper hold five wild foxes for urine collection until the end of the season and then requires them to be euthanized. Oregon's prohibited species list reads the same way. OAR 635-056-0050 bars "Wild canids | Canidae | All native species", then adds:

> However, fox (Vulpes vulpes and Urocyon cinereoargenteus) are exempt from this prohibition if when part of a commercial fur farming operation or for wildlife rehabilitation purposes by a licensed wildlife rehabilitator;

A commercial fur farm licensed by the Department of Agriculture, or a rehabilitator. Neither is a pet. Montana's rabies statute, MCA § 50-23-102, bars possessing a "wild animal", defined as "a skunk, fox, raccoon, or bat", unless it "is used in a fur-bearing enterprise", kept in a no-contact zoo exhibit, or held for research, with a grandfather clause that ran out in 1982. Idaho Code § 25-236(1) says:

> No person shall possess, offer for sale, trade, barter, exchange or importation into the state of Idaho any fox, skunk or raccoon, except as provided in subsection (2) or (3) of this section.

Subsections (2) and (3) are fur farms holding domestic fur-bearing animals, and parks, zoos, museums and educational institutions holding an Agriculture permit. New Jersey gets there through its own agency: the red fox is a game animal at N.J.A.C. 7:25-10.6(a)16, which would put it on the Individual Hobby permit, but 10.10 makes every nondomestic dog a potentially dangerous species and 10.11(a)6 says one "may not be kept as a household pet", and the Division confirms it "does not currently issue new permits for the possession of potentially dangerous species within the hobby permit category". What it still issues are fur farming, propagation, zoo, exhibitor and theatrical permits.

Delaware does not bother with the fur farm. Del. Code tit. 7 § 789:

> No live red fox shall be sold, purchased, possessed or exposed for sale in this State, or shipped by freight or express, or otherwise, or taken from any place within this State to any place outside this State.

The exceptions are a government animal exhibition and § 791, which lets someone who keeps five or more foxhounds raise wild whelps under permit, for the chase, and release them by 15 August. The Agriculture department's exotic animal permit, which answers the fennec fox in Delaware, never reaches a native red fox at all.

## Virginia Wrote the Pet-Fox Exception, and Then Closed It

Only one state has looked at the pet red fox as a thing distinct from the wild one, and it did so in order to wind the trade down. Virginia's 4VAC15-20-50(B) is headed "Exception for red foxes and European rabbits", and it allowed "Domesticated red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) having coat colors distinguishable from wild red foxes" that were "possessed in captivity on July 1, 2017" to be kept "until the animal dies", provided the owner declared them to the department in writing by 1 January 2018 with the number, sex, age, coloration and a photograph of each fox. The animal may not be bred or sold without a permit. That is the color-variant fox of the pet trade, the silver and the marble, described in a regulation for the first time, and described only so that existing ones could be registered and no new ones admitted. A fox acquired since is a wild animal, and Va. Code § 29.1-521(A)(10) makes it unlawful "to ... possess ... any wild bird or wild animal ... except as specifically permitted by law", with Chapter 290's permits running to exhibits and scientific collection. The permit table at 4VAC15-30-40, which puts the fennec on a special permit, does not apply, because that table covers nonnative animals and the red fox is not one.

## The Word "Fox", and What It Catches

Several of these statutes say "fox" and stop, and that single word is what joins the red fox's map to the [fennec fox's](/blog/fennec-fox-legal-guide/). Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1) bars a live "fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear" and then, two clauses later, any "member of the dog family other than the domestic dog", so the red fox is caught twice. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 § 6(1) lists "Red fox (Vulpes vulpes)" under "Rabies Vector Species", and defines native wildlife "regardless of ... being captive-bred or taken from the wild". Washington's rabies rule, WAC 246-100-197(5)(a), prohibits all persons from "acquiring, selling, bartering, exchanging, giving, purchasing, distributing, or trapping to retain any bat, skunk, fox, raccoon, or coyote" except a zoo, exhibitor, research facility or rehabilitator. Louisiana's fox rule, LAC 76:V.113, states its purpose as prohibiting "the importation and exportation of any species of foxes", and D.6 says "No person shall transport, possess, purchase or sell any live foxes or coyotes taken outside the state of Louisiana". The licenses it offers are a trapper's license and the nongame quadruped breeder and exhibitor licenses, which the rule defines as licenses to engage in a business, for stocking hound-running preserves. In each of those four states a fennec fox is banned by the same words, and in Idaho, Washington and Louisiana the word does the work alone.

Where a state wrote "Canidae" rather than "fox", the red fox and the fennec usually part company only in New York. ECL § 11-0103(6)(e) and 6 NYCRR § 180.1 put every canid on the wild animal list "with the exception of domesticated dogs and captive bred fennec foxes (vulpes zerda)", and § 11-0512 bars keeping a wild animal as a pet. The same sentence that frees the captive-bred fennec bans every other fox, and the small game definition's own carve-out for "captive bred red fox" takes a farmed fox out of the hunting rules, not out of the pet ban. California restricts "Family Canidae-All species" for welfare and offers no pet permit. Connecticut makes every canid Category One, open to institutions only. Maine lists all canids in Category 1, which is exhibitors, rehabilitators and laboratories. Iowa's dangerous wild animal definition reaches "a member of the family canidae of the order carnivora, including but not limited to wolves, coyotes, and jackals", and that outranks the red fox's own listing as a fur-bearer. Nevada names "All species in the genera Vulpes, Fennecus, Urocyon, Alopex, Lycalopex and Pseudalopex", New Mexico's importation list puts "Fox (any type)" in Group IV, and Hawaii's Part A takes "Vulpes (all species in genus)".

<FunFact>
Indiana still lists the red fox under a binomial the taxonomists retired decades ago. 312 IAC 9-11-7(a)(4) reads "Red fox (Vulpes fulva)", the old name for the North American red fox from the years it was treated as a species of its own rather than a population of Vulpes vulpes. The permit is real whatever the name: apply within five days of possession, pass a cage inspection, show lawful acquisition, file a vet's statement. The fennec, two lines down the taxonomy and on no Indiana list at all, needs nothing.
</FunFact>

## Native, Therefore Wildlife, Therefore No

Eight more jurisdictions ban the red fox without naming a family or a disease, by treating it as the wild animal it is. Georgia's Department of Natural Resources names the red fox on its list of native species that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology", and the licensing rule, which reaches all of Carnivora, issues licenses only to dealers and exhibitors. Rhode Island lists "Red Fox (Vulpes vulpes)" among native mammals whose possession as pets is prohibited, "regardless of where the animal originated". New Hampshire's controlled table carries "Fox, Red" with permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitor category, the fennec's row beside it. Colorado's Parks and Wildlife says "CPW will not issue a license for private possession (eg pet) of regulated wildlife", and the red fox is on none of the lists that would make it anything else. Alaska bars a pet permit for any game animal. Alabama bars "species of fox" from outside the state and treats an Alabama fox as a protected furbearer. Mississippi classes "fox" as a nuisance animal and Rule 7.1(F) allows a live one only "in permitted fox enclosures", the pens for running hounds. The reasoning is the one that bars the [flying squirrel](/blog/flying-squirrel-legal-guide/) in most of the same states: a native-wildlife statute never mentioned on the exotic pet list.

## The Permit States

Eighteen states issue a permit, and fifteen issue it to a private keeper. The first seven do it through a furbearer or game license. Texas's § 71.005(c) says "No person may capture or possess a live fur-bearing animal for any purpose ... unless he has acquired and possesses a fur-bearing animal propagation license", $50.75, and "fox" is a fur-bearing animal under § 71.001(1); the dangerous wild animal list names the coyote and the jackal and no fox. Ohio's R.C. 1531.01(W) names "fox" as a fur-bearing animal and § 1533.71(A)(2) sells a noncommercial propagating license for twenty-five dollars a year, "for the licensee's own use". Illinois names "Vulpes vulpes" among its protected fur-bearing mammals and § 3.25 requires a $25 fur-bearing mammal breeder permit of anyone who holds one, breeding or not; the state's dangerous animal list stops at the wolf and the coyote. Wisconsin's § 169.15(1)(b) says the department "shall issue" a Class B captive wild animal farm license to any applicant who files and pays, and NR 16.15(1) applies it to hobbyists. Tennessee moves native furbearers into Class II, $10 an animal with documentary evidence of the supplier. Indiana's Class II permit is above. West Virginia's § 20-2-51 issues a two-dollar pet permit for a wild animal "acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season".

The next eight attach a sourcing condition. Michigan's Permit to Hold Game in Captivity names "red and gray fox" and covers captive-bred animals only, which is the exact line between the red fox and the fennec there: the native fox needs the permit and the non-native one does not. Florida's answer is a free Class III permit, closed to a wild-caught fox by 68A-6.003(13). Nebraska lists "Red - (Vulpes vulpes)" on the Captive Wildlife Permit list with no commercial-only asterisk, for a captive-bred animal only. Oklahoma's exemption for "Dogs (except coyotes and native foxes)" carves the red fox out by name, so it needs the import permit and the noncommercial breeder's license. Missouri's Wildlife Hobby Permit allows one game mammal bought from a Missouri breeder, and 3 CSR 10-9.223(2) bars importing live foxes, so the animal has to be Missouri bred. South Dakota's possession permit covers all nondomestic Canidae at ten dollars an animal. North Dakota puts "red and gray foxes" in category 2, a license to be in hand before the fox is, with the premises inspected first; category 1, which needs no license, includes "ranch foxes", a term the rule never defines, and a red fox from a fur ranch in a ranch color phase may sit there instead. South Carolina names "red and gray fox" first in its furbearer list and requires a Department permit to bring one in.

Massachusetts, Vermont and North Carolina round out the eighteen with paper permits. MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 limits captivity permits to "bona fide scientific or educational purposes". North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) bars a captivity license for an animal "acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet".

## Three States Where the Pedigree Decides It

Minnesota is the one state that wrote the farmed fox into law as a domestic animal. Minn. Stat. § 17.351 subd. 3 says:

> "Fur-bearing animal" means a fox, mink, fitch, chinchilla, karakul, marten, nutria, or fisher that is the second or later generation raised in captivity.

And § 17.352 adds: "Fur-bearing animals are domestic animals and products of fur-bearing animals are agricultural products." A fox two generations removed from the wild is therefore outside the game laws; a wild-caught or first-generation fox is still small game, a protected wild animal, and needs the § 97A.401 special permit the commissioner may issue for pets. Canids are not regulated animals under § 346.155. Utah reaches a similar place by table: Table 3b-1 lists "Fox, red Vulpes vulpes" in the domestic column with the condition "privately owned, domestically bred and raised", so a bred fox is outside the wildlife rule and a wild one is not. Wyoming's condition is the reverse, and it is about capture rather than breeding. The statute names the red fox a "predatory animal", Chapter 10 § 12 bars importing predatory animals, and § 14(b) says they "may be possessed without a permit if captured in the state". A breeder's fox cannot cross the border; a fox caught in Wyoming needs no paper at all.

Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora, so a special license is needed. Arkansas and Kansas are the two gray cells. Arkansas's hand-capture route for the red fox closed on 1 July 2022, with animals caught before then kept until death, and the captive-born route needs a Commission-permitted breeder or an import permit; Addendum R1.03 refuses those permits only for foxes that originate from or have lived in a list of twenty states and Canada, and R1.02, the permit list, never names the red fox. Kansas bars possessing wildlife unless a regulation permits it, the only regulation that permits a live furbearer lets a Kansas-caught one be held "only through the last day of the season in which taken", and no text says whether the statutory exception for animals "legally taken outside this state" reaches one bred in captivity.

## The Takeaway

In 28 jurisdictions there is no lawful red fox outside a fur farm, a hound pen, a zoo or a laboratory, and in most of them a silver or marble fox from a breeder is in exactly the position of one trapped in the yard. In 15 states a keeper can hold a permit, and in nearly every one of them it is a furbearer license that was written for someone else. Minnesota and Utah are the only states whose text says the farmed fox is a different animal, and Virginia is the only one that ever said so about the pet fox, in a sentence written to close the door. The vaccine problem is the same everywhere: no rabies vaccine is licensed for any fox, so a bite ends with the animal tested, permit or no permit.
