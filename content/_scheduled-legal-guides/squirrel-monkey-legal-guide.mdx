The squirrel monkey is the primate that nobody wrote a law about, and that is the whole of its legal story. It is a two-pound New World monkey of the genus *Saimiri*, kept in the same houses and sold by the same breeders as the [capuchin](/blog/capuchin-legal-guide/), and in every one of the 52 jurisdictions it gets the capuchin's answer. The capuchin earned a few sentences of its own, as the service monkey and the organ-grinder's animal. The squirrel monkey earned four: Ohio's, Arkansas's, Idaho's and New Hampshire's, and only Ohio's changes anything.

Read across all 52 jurisdictions, the squirrel monkey is banned in 28, a permit animal in nine, restricted in one, unclear in one and unregulated at state level in 13. Forty jurisdictions restrict the order Primates by name, and a rule that says "all nonhuman primates" gives a two-pound monkey the same answer it gives a gorilla; five more take the family Cebidae, which the squirrel monkey belongs to on any taxonomy. The [pet monkey legal guide](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) walks those sentences and the CDC import rule underneath them, which has barred every primate from the pet trade at the border since 1975 and means a lawful squirrel monkey is a US-bred one. This page is for the places where the squirrel monkey's own name or family appears, and for the thirteen states where nothing does.

## The Shape of It

Every jurisdiction below either names the squirrel monkey or its family, or draws its line in a way that lands on it. The rest give it the primate answer and are listed further down.

## Ohio Named It and Did Not Say Why

Ohio is the one state where the squirrel monkey's name is in the statute, and the one state where its answer is gray. R.C. 935.01(C)(19) defines a dangerous wild animal to include "Nonhuman primates other than lemurs and the nonhuman primates specified in division (C)(20) of this section", and (C)(20) is a closed list of tamarins, night monkeys, titis, muriquis, Goeldi's monkeys, sakis, uakaris, spider monkeys, woolly monkeys and howlers. The squirrel monkey is not on it, so on the words of (C)(19) it is a dangerous wild animal, and § 935.02 says no person may acquire one on or after the act's effective date or possess one on or after 1 January 2014 without a permit that only 2012 registrants could apply for. But the same bill enacted R.C. 935.041:

> A person that possesses any of the following animals shall register the animal in the same manner as provided in section 935.04 of the Revised Code: (A) Pygmy, white-tufted-ear, silvery, and black-pencilled marmosets; (B) Squirrel monkeys; (C) Brown, white-faced, weeping, and white-fronted capuchins; (D) Lemurs.

A registration section for animals the chapter elsewhere bars outright only makes sense if those animals are not dangerous wild animals. The lemur has no problem, because (C)(19) writes it out in terms. The squirrel monkey does, because (C)(19) does not, and the two sections cannot both be read at full strength. The likelier reading is that § 935.041 works the way it reads for the lemur, as a registration for a small primate Ohio decided not to ban. But a keeper who buys on that reading and meets an inspector who holds the other one has a felony-grade problem, so write to the Department of Agriculture, ask which section governs a newly acquired squirrel monkey, and keep the answer. The [marmoset](/blog/marmoset-legal-guide/) sits in the same gray cell for the same reason.

## Texas Stops at the Baboon

Texas is the question people type most, and its statute answers it by omission. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) is a closed list of twenty clauses, nineteen named mammals and their hybrids, and the primates on it are "(P) a baboon; (Q) a chimpanzee; (R) an orangutan; (S) a gorilla". A squirrel monkey is on none of them and is not a hybrid of anything that is, so § 822.103's certificate of registration and § 822.107's $100,000 of liability insurance never attach. Nothing in the Parks and Wildlife Code reaches a nonnative primate either. What Texas does say, at § 822.116(b), is that a municipality or county may prohibit the animal outright, and Texas cities and counties regulate primates far more often than the state does. The state answer is yes; the ordinance is the one to read.

<FunFact>
Texas registers a baboon and ignores a squirrel monkey. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) lists the baboon at clause (P) alongside the lion and the bear, so a baboon in Texas needs a certificate of registration and $100,000 of liability insurance, while a squirrel monkey, a two-pound animal with the same hands, needs nothing from the state at all. The list was written about cats and bears, with the four largest primates added at the end, and it stops where it stops.
</FunFact>

## The Family Does the Rest

Five jurisdictions ban the squirrel monkey with a sentence about its family. Hawaii's Restricted List Part B names "Cebidae (all species in family)" and then § 4-71-6.5(b)(3) withdraws the private and commercial use that Part B normally allows from every primate, leaving research, government zoos, approved institutions and nonprofit primate sanctuaries. New Jersey's potentially dangerous species table opens with "Cebidae--New World Monkeys", and 4.9(a)3 says a potentially dangerous species "shall not be kept as a pet, for hobby purposes", so the only routes are institutional. New Mexico's Director's Species Importation List files "Cebidae all species" in Group IV, closed to the general public. Montana's ARM 12.6.2215(4)(g) lists the "Cebidae family (new world primates)" among prohibited mammals, and the entry's own gloss reaches a squirrel monkey on any taxonomy. Iowa's § 717F.1(5)(a)(7) names cebidae among its thirteen primate families and bars owning, possessing, breeding or importing anything in them, with the only private route the 2007 registration of animals already held.

New Hampshire wrote the squirrel monkey's name and spelled the genus wrong. Table 800.2 lists "Squirrel Monkey Saimici spp" on its primate row, against permittee category 5, and Fis 804.05(b) says a permit "shall be issued only to a person in the permittee category(ies) ... cited in Table 800.2". Category 5 is exhibitors, so no permit can issue to an individual. California's § 671 restricts "Order Primates-Monkeys, Apes: All species", and the one place it mentions squirrel monkeys is in the caging standards, which let New World monkeys "such as squirrel, titis, and owl" that were held before 3 August 1989 stay in their approved housing. That is a housing allowance for permittees who have had the animal for more than thirty-five years, and the only possession permit, Animal Care, requires lawful possession in the state before January 1992.

## The Permits

Nine jurisdictions issue a permit, and seven of them issue it to a private keeper. Florida's is the one most people will use. The captive wildlife rule writes its primate classes genus by genus, and *Saimiri* is on neither Class I nor Class II, so 68A-6.002(1)(c) catches it with "Class III: All other wildlife not listed herein", and 68A-6.003(8) says a person possessing Class III wildlife for personal use "shall obtain a no-cost permit from the Executive Director". Arkansas names the genus: Addendum R1.02 says permits "for breeding, sale, and importation may be issued for the following species", and the mammal list carries "Squirrel Monkeys (Genus Saimiri)", so one may be kept if it came from a Commission-permitted breeder or dealer or in on an importation permit. Code 09.02(H) then sends the owner to Ark. Code Ann. §§ 20-19-601 to 20-19-610, which bar only apes, baboons and macaques, and § 20-19-605 requires the owner to register the animal with the county sheriff within 30 days, with no outdoor tethering and no public contact under § 20-19-602.

Idaho exempted the squirrel monkey from its zoo-only rule twice. IDAPA 02.04.27.407 says "All non-human primates must be possessed on a facility that is AZA accredited" and then exempts "02. Marmoset (Saimiri spp)" and "04. Squirrel Monkeys (Callithrix, Cebuella, Callibella, and Mico spp)", filing each animal's name against the other's genus. *Saimiri* is the squirrel monkey's genus, so it is exempt under .02 by genus and under .04 by name, and either way it needs a Fish and Game captive possession license, because the conventional pets list has no primate on it. Delaware's regulation 903 writes the Individual Permit for "an exotic ... kept as a pet", per animal for three years, South Dakota's possession permit covers the whole order at ten dollars an animal, and North Dakota makes primates category 3 nontraditional livestock, licensed and inspected before the animal arrives. Missouri is a permit state by registration: RSMo § 578.023 makes it an offense to keep a "nonhuman primate" without registering it with the local law enforcement agency, and because the squirrel monkey is a nonnative mammal and not federally endangered, 3 CSR 10-9.110(2) clears it from any Wildlife Code permit.

Massachusetts and Vermont have the permit on paper only: MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet", and Vermont's Regulation 881 has the Commissioner refuse any captivity permit except for bona fide scientific or educational purposes. Arizona is restricted rather than permitted, with "All nonhuman primates" held only under a special license that has no pet exemption.

## Where the Order Is the Rule

The other 21 jurisdictions that ban a squirrel monkey do it with a sentence that never needed to know the species. Pennsylvania prohibits "The order Primates. All families of nonhuman primates." Wyoming says a permit "shall not be issued" for "All members of the order nonhuman primates". Utah, Colorado, Maine, Rhode Island, Alaska, Nebraska, the District of Columbia and New York City reach the same answer by a closed list, a research-only tier or an import list with no primate on it. Georgia licenses primates only to the wild animal trade and exhibitors, New York bars every nonhuman primate as a wild animal that may not be kept as a pet, Maryland and Connecticut name monkeys outright, and Minnesota makes every primate a regulated animal. Oregon, Washington, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana and West Virginia ban with a grandfather window that closed between 1995 and 2022 and lets no new animal in. The [pet monkey legal guide](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) carries each state's sentence and its closing date.

## The Thirteen With No Rule

Wisconsin, Texas, Michigan, Nevada, Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina, Kansas, South Carolina, Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi and Oklahoma restrict a squirrel monkey nowhere in their state law. Texas is above. Tennessee sends "Primates not otherwise listed" to Class III, which needs no wildlife permit, and Mississippi's inherently dangerous list stops at the apes, macaques, baboons and the gelada. South Carolina's 2018 ban reaches primates only through the great apes. Nevada and Oklahoma name primates to clear them. Virginia, Alabama and Indiana list carnivores, rodents and reptiles and nothing with hands, Kansas lets lawfully obtained exotic wildlife be possessed "without limit in time and number", Wisconsin frees every nonnative wild animal that is not harmful, Michigan's Large Carnivore Act names cats and bears, and North Carolina hands dangerous animals to its counties under § 153A-131.

Thirteen states with no rule is not thirteen states where a squirrel monkey is safe to buy. Primates appear on municipal exotic animal lists more often than almost any other pet, Texas and Nevada both say in terms that a city or county may prohibit what the state does not, and in a state with no primate sentence the city code is the whole answer.

## The Takeaway

The squirrel monkey is banned in 28 jurisdictions and permitted in nine, and every one of those numbers is the capuchin's, because the law reached both animals through the family and the order and never stopped to tell them apart. The four places that wrote the squirrel monkey's name did not change the count. Arkansas and Idaho named it to permit it, New Hampshire named it to send it to exhibitors, and Ohio named it for registration while leaving the definition that bars it standing beside the registration, which is the one cell on this map a keeper cannot read for themselves. Texas left it off a list that stops at the baboon, Florida caught it by not naming it at all, and the federal import rule under all of it decides that a lawful squirrel monkey was born here.
