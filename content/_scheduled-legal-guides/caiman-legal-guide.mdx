A spectacled caiman is the crocodilian a keeper can buy, and it is the one the law has the least to say about. Caimans are South and Central American, so no state has a caiman season, a caiman farm or a caiman chapter, and the American alligator's game-code answer never applies to one. What is left is the dangerous-animal list, where "crocodilian" or "Crocodilia" is usually the first reptile named, and in 25 jurisdictions that word is the caiman's whole answer. Seven states issue a permit, Missouri attaches a registration, Arizona restricts the order, four states cannot decide, and 14 have no rule reaching the species. The alligator has six.

The gap between six and fourteen is made of one word. Where a state wrote "alligator" and stopped, a caiman walks through: Texas's alligator chapter, Alabama's game list and Oklahoma's alligator permit all name the American alligator and nothing else, and all three states leave the caiman unregulated. Where a state wrote the family or the order, the caiman is caught with everything else. Tennessee's statute wrote "crocodiles and alligators" in Class I and "caimans and gavials" as an exception to Class III, and the commission had to pass a rule to say which class the caiman was in. Ohio named caimans and then excluded the dwarf ones. Colorado cleared the subfamily by name and refused the alligator in the same list. This page walks the states where the word matters.

## Where the Rule Says Alligator and Stops

Texas, Alabama and Oklahoma each have an alligator rule written for their own wild population, the game-code answer that decides the [American alligator](/blog/american-alligator-legal-guide/) there, and each wrote it narrowly enough that a caiman is outside it. Texas's alligator subchapter opens by defining its subject: "'Alligator' means a living or dead American alligator (Alligator mississippiensis)". The subchapter bars possessing a live alligator outside farming, dealing, control hunting, research and display, and it reaches nothing else. The dangerous wild animal list at Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4), the one that names lions, tigers and cougars, names no reptile, and the controlled exotic snake permit covers snakes. A spectacled caiman is reached by none of the three, so Texas, which bars a pet alligator outright, does not restrict a caiman at all. Alabama's rule 220-2-.06 designates "Alligator" a game animal, which 220-2-.26(3) then bars keeping alive; a caiman is not venomous, not on the federal injurious list and not native, so none of the restricted entries in 220-2-.26(1) reaches it. Oklahoma's 800:25-7-7(8) makes it "unlawful to possess an American Alligator without appropriate permit from the Department", names only that species, and 800:25-25-3(c) exempts reptiles not indigenous to Oklahoma from its import and breeder rules unless they are federally injurious, which a caiman is not.

Tennessee is the state where the word had to be fixed by rule. The statute's Class I names "Order Crocodylia: Crocodiles and alligators--All species", and its Class III entry for reptiles reads "Nonpoisonous reptiles and amphibians except caimans and gavials", which took the caiman out of the free class without saying where it went. The commission's rule 1660-01-18-.03(3)(a) settles it:

> (3) (a) The following species or groups of wildlife are added to the existing listing designated by legislation as Class III: ... 2. Ostriches, cassowaries, caimans, and gavials.

Class III needs no wildlife permit, so a caiman in Tennessee is in the same class as an ostrich, while an alligator sits in Class I with the crocodiles, open only to zoos, circuses and commercial propagators. The rule's general provision adds that "The possession of state or federally threatened or endangered species is permitted only when the species have been legally obtained in the state or country of origin", which for a federally threatened caiman means keeping the purchase records.

Colorado wrote the subfamily into its clearance. Appendix A of Chapter W-7 lists, in the block of species that are not native only to the tropics:

> 5. Any species from the subfamily Caimaninae (caimans)

No license or documentation is needed, and the 26-degree range test that leaves the green anaconda unresolved does not apply, because the caimans are cleared by name rather than by latitude. The alligator is the opposite case in the same state: CPW's species determination reads "Not an authorized species for private possession. CPW license required", and CPW issues no license for a pet. Ohio runs the other way and names the caiman to bar it, with one carve-out. R.C. 935.01(C)(17) lists "Caimans, excluding dwarf caimans" among dangerous wild animals, and § 935.02(A) provides that "No person shall possess a dangerous wild animal on or after January 1, 2014" unless it was registered in 2012 and permitted. A dwarf caiman of the genus *Paleosuchus* is outside the entry; the spectacled caiman is in it, with no route for a new keeper.

## Where the Family Word Catches It

New Hampshire, Montana and Kentucky never wrote "caiman" and reach it anyway, because each wrote "Alligatoridae". New Hampshire's Fis 804.02(b)(4) frees exotic reptiles from any permit "except: a. Alligator (Alligatoridae, spp); b. Crocodile (Crocodylidae, spp); and c. Venomous reptiles", and Table 800.2 lists the Alligatoridae row for permittee category 5, the exhibitor category, so no permit can issue to an individual. The spectacled caiman is *Caiman crocodilus*, a member of Alligatoridae, so the alligator row takes it in. Montana's ARM 12.6.2215(6)(b) prohibits the "Alligatoridae family", with Crocodylidae separately at (h). Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:082 opens its inherently dangerous list with "(a) Alligators or caimans (Family Alligatoridae)", naming both and the family besides, with the only routes left an AZA-accredited facility and the commissioner's exemption for municipal zoos, colleges and circuses.

Arkansas uses a broader word still. Code 12.01 provides:

> It is unlawful to hunt, buy, sell or possess an American alligator (Alligator mississippiensis) or other crocodilian species, or any part, nest or eggs.

"Other crocodilian species" takes the caiman in the same sentence as the alligator, and the only live-animal exception is a display and education authorization for established facilities such as parks, zoos, aquariums and nature centers. Louisiana's January 2025 rewrite of its reptile rule did the same from the exotic side, putting "All crocodilians" first on its prohibited nonnative list:

> a. The following nonnative reptile and amphibian species are prohibited from importation, possession, sale, attempting to sell, transfer, release, and reproduction in the state due to the potential risk of establishment in the wild and detrimental hazard to native wildlife and public health and safety. ... i. All crocodilians;

Owners had 365 days from the effective date to register the animal and obtain a permit, with no breeding and no further acquisition. In Louisiana the native alligator is governed by the alligator rule and the exotic caiman by this one, and both arrive at a bar for a private keeper, one through farming licenses and the other through a prohibited list.

<FunFact>
Three states on this map have a caiman answer that is the exact opposite of their alligator answer, and in each the reason is the word the drafter chose. Texas bars a live alligator by proclamation and does not restrict a caiman, because its alligator chapter defines "alligator" as the American alligator alone. Alabama bars a live alligator as a game animal and leaves a caiman unregulated, because the game list names one species. Colorado refuses the alligator by name and clears every caiman by subfamily. The crocodilian most likely to bite a keeper is the one in the pond either way, and the law in those three states is sorting by taxonomy rather than by teeth.
</FunFact>

## The Listing That Exists Because of Rarer Crocodilians

The common caiman has its own row in 50 CFR 17.11: "Caiman, common | Caiman crocodilus crocodilus | Wherever found | T(S/A)", listed on 4 May 2000 with the brown caiman beside it and a special rule at 50 CFR 17.42(c). The Apaporis River caiman, a subspecies almost nobody has seen alive in captivity, is listed as endangered. "T(S/A)" means threatened due to similarity of appearance, and the listing exists because a caiman hide and a live caiman are hard to tell from those of protected crocodilians, so the trade in the rarer species could pass as caiman without it. On its own the listing reaches commerce and not a captive animal in a keeper's enclosure, and the [federal laws guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/) sets out why a similarity listing and an injurious listing do such different work.

It reaches a keeper the moment a state rule points at the list, and three do. Wyoming's Chapter 69 § 8 provides:

> An importation/possession permit shall not be issued for importation or possession of the live cold-blooded wildlife in this section, except as otherwise specified. ... (d) Endangered and threatened wildlife listed in 50 CFR 17.11.

The bar takes in threatened as well as endangered, so the free reptile clause in § 10 does not reach a spectacled caiman and no permit may issue. Nebraska's § 37-806(1) makes every federally threatened species threatened in Nebraska, and § 37-477(1) requires a permit to "keep in captivity in this state ... any wildlife determined to be an endangered or threatened species under the Endangered Species Act or section 37-806". The captive wildlife rules name no reptile, so whether and how that permit would issue for a pet is not settled, and the map records Nebraska as unclear. North Carolina is unclear for a different reason: its crocodilian housing statute, § 14-417.2, provides that "All crocodilians, excluding the American alligator, shall be regulated under this Article" and makes it unlawful to possess one "that is not housed in a sturdy and secure enclosure", which alone would make the caiman a conditional animal. But § 113-129(15) counts as wild animals any wild reptiles "which are on the federal list of endangered or threatened species", the caiman is on that list, and a wild animal needs a captivity license that § 113-272.5(a) withholds from anything acquired "merely as a pet". Whether that definition reaches a nonnative species is not said, so the map records the cell as unclear between the two readings. New Mexico's importation list marks the common caiman "Federally Threatened" and files it in Group IV either way.

Wisconsin is the state where the listing does not bite, and the reason is worth knowing because it is the alligator's problem in reverse. Wis. Stat. § 169.01(9) defines an endangered or threatened species as one "indigenous to the United States or Canada" and on the federal list. The alligator is both, so Wisconsin's alligator cell is unclear; the caiman is not indigenous, so whatever its federal row says it is outside the definition, and the exemption for nonnative wild animals that are not harmful applies. No state license, and local ordinances were not checked.

## The Permits That Are Real

Florida's is the plainest. 68A-6.002 names "Alligators, caimans (family Alligatoridae)" at Class II item 33, and only the black caiman, *Melanosuchus niger*, is Class I. A Permit to Possess Class II Wildlife for Personal Use costs $140 a year, requires an applicant of 18 with documented experience with the species, and requires caging that meets 68A-6.010 and 68A-6.011. Unlike the alligator, the caiman has no reptile rule stacked on top, so the Class II permit is the whole Florida answer.

Delaware's is the one written for a pet. The regulation's reptile class expressly includes crocodilians and exempts none, so a caiman is an exotic that needs the Individual Permit: "When an exotic is kept as a pet, the owner or custodian of the exotic must apply to the Department for an Individual Permit on a form supplied by the Department." The permit is issued per animal for three years, after the Department is satisfied the animal will be held in a sturdy, escape-proof primary enclosure inside a secondary enclosure that keeps the public out, and a background check may be run. Caimans are named in the regulation only once, at 7.5.2.10, which bars Sales Permit holders from breeding "All members of the Crocodilian group: Alligators, Caimans, Crocodiles, and Gharials". That is a breeding limit on one permit class, not a bar on keeping one, and Delaware's only outright refusals are the gila monster, the beaded lizard and the komodo dragon. Rhode Island's reptile exemption list in § 3.17 clears turtles, some snakes and many lizards and no crocodilian, so a caiman is an exotic animal needing an exotic animal possession permit, which § 3.9(A) makes available to private persons for animals § 3.7 does not expressly prohibit. Indiana's permit arrives with the animal's growth: 312 IAC 9-11-8(a)(5) lists "Crocodilians that are at least five (5) feet long" as Class III, so a spectacled caiman under five feet needs nothing and a larger one needs a permit obtained before taking possession, with a recapture plan and a cage inspection.

Virginia, Vermont and West Virginia are permits a hobbyist is unlikely to hold. Virginia's exotic table lists "Alligators, caimans" under Alligatoridae and the department issues the special permit "if consistent with the department's fish and wildlife management program", without saying whether a private keeper qualifies. Vermont restricts the caiman by absence from all three of its lists, and Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner permit one outside science or education only on a finding that it does not "conflict with the purpose of this regulation". West Virginia reaches a caiman as wildlife from outside the state, which needs an importation permit the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for. Missouri's § 578.023 requires "any deadly or dangerous reptile over eight feet long" to be registered with county law enforcement, and a spectacled caiman, which rarely reaches that length, may never need the registration; whether a smaller one counts as a dangerous reptile is not settled.

## Where the Order Is the Rule

The rest of the bans are the crocodilian clause, usually the first reptile on the list. California restricts "Order Crocodilia-Crocodiles, Caimans, Alligators and Gavials: All species (D)" with no permit type for a pet. Georgia's inherently dangerous entry is "Order Crocodilia (alligators and caimans, crocodiles, gharials) - All species", licensed only to dealers and exhibitors. Hawaii lists "Caiman crocodilus caiman, spectacled" on Part A, for research and exhibition only. New York City's Health Code bars "all members of the order Crocodylia, including, but not limited to, alligator, caiman and crocodile". Connecticut's Category Two names "alligator, crocodile and caiman" and allows the category only to institutions. Washington's RCW 16.30.010 lists "Order crocodilia, all species, such as crocodiles, alligators, caimans, and gavials", closed since 2007. Maryland's § 10-621 bars a live "caiman, alligator, or crocodile", with the caiman first. Iowa's crocodilian clause names caimans and closed in 2007. Illinois defines crocodilians as "any species of the Order Crocodilia, such as crocodiles, alligators, caimans, and gavials" and issues its special use permit only for educational programs. Utah's R657-53-9(1)(b) classifies "Species of the Order Crocodilia (crocodiles, gharials, caimans, and alligators)" as prohibited. New Mexico files the common caiman in Group IV. Nevada's prohibited list takes "All species in the order Crocodilia". Oregon's exotic animal definition takes "Any member of the order Crocodylia", with a permit window that closed in 2010. The District of Columbia's closed list admits no crocodilian, New Jersey's potentially dangerous table lists "Alligatoridae--Alligators and caiman", Maine's Category 1 takes the order, New York's ECL lists "Crocodylia" among its wild animals, MassWildlife says "You cannot have any crocodilian species in your possession in Massachusetts" and names caimans on their own line, and Arizona restricts the order with no exemption that reads as a pet route.

That leaves fourteen jurisdictions with no rule, nine of them with nothing that reaches a nonvenomous exotic reptile at all: Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, Kansas, South Carolina, Alaska, South Dakota and Mississippi. Minnesota protects turtles, snakes and lizards and not crocodilians. Kansas lets exotic wildlife be possessed "without limit in time and number" if legally obtained where it came from. Alaska's clean list clears "Any nonvenomous reptile" and glosses the class as "crocodile, alligator, snake, turtle, or lizard". South Carolina's alligator regulations reach caimans only to require caiman leather to be labeled as such. Local ordinances were not checked in any of the fourteen, and a crocodilian is on more city codes than almost any animal a keeper might bring home.

Four states cannot decide. North Carolina and Nebraska turn on the federal listing. North Dakota's category 3 reaches "Venomous reptiles and nonvenomous injurious reptiles", the caiman is not federal injurious wildlife, and the rule does not define the word; if it means the federal list the caiman is in no category and needs only an importation permit number and a veterinary certificate, and if it means harmful a crocodilian is the obvious case. Idaho's conventional pets exemption covers "non-venomous or non-dangerous reptiles and amphibians", and non-dangerous is defined nowhere.

## The Takeaway

Read the crocodilian entry first, because in 25 jurisdictions it is the whole answer and the caiman is in it whether the drafter wrote "caiman", "Alligatoridae" or "Crocodilia". Then read the alligator rule, because where a state wrote "alligator" and stopped, the caiman is outside it, and Texas, Alabama and Oklahoma are the three states where a pet alligator is barred and a pet caiman is not. Then read the federal row for what it is: a similarity listing that bars nothing by itself and bars a caiman in Wyoming the moment the state points at it. The real permits are Florida's Class II, Delaware's Individual Permit, Rhode Island's exotic animal permit and Indiana's at five feet. Our [interactive map of caiman laws](/exotic-pet-laws/caiman/) shades every jurisdiction and quotes the sentence each answer comes from, and for this animal the sentence is usually one word long.
