The ring-tailed lemur is a prosimian from the dry south of Madagascar, and it is the only small primate in the pet trade whose whole family is on the federal endangered list. 50 CFR 17.11 lists "Lemurs | Lemuridae" as endangered wherever found, one row for the family, listed in 1970 and widened in 1976, and *Lemur catta* is on CITES Appendix I, which bars commercial international trade in the species. Neither rule says a word about keeping one at home. The [federal laws guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/) explains why CITES is a border rule and not an ownership rule, and the endangered listing is the same: it bars import, export and interstate sale without a permit, and it reaches inside a state only where that state wrote the federal list into its own law.

Three states did, and that is what makes the lemur's map different from the [pet monkey legal guide's](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) group picture. Across all 52 jurisdictions the ring-tailed lemur is banned in 30, a permit animal in seven, legal with registration in one, restricted in one and unregulated at state level in 13. The capuchin is banned in 28. The two extra bans are Missouri and South Carolina, which clear the capuchin and bar the lemur by pointing at 50 CFR 17.11, and New Jersey bars both but reaches the lemur by that route too. Idaho confines the lemur to accredited zoos and lets the capuchin out on a license. Then Ohio and West Virginia run the other way, and treat the lemur as the gentlest primate in the statute.

## The Shape of It

Every jurisdiction below either names the lemur or its family, reaches it through the federal list, or gives it a different answer from the capuchin. The rest give it the primate order's answer and are listed further down.

## Three States Read the Federal List

Missouri is the cleanest case, because one sentence does all the work. 3 CSR 10-9.110(2):

> (2) Except for federally-designated endangered species and species listed in 3 CSR 10-4.117 and 3 CSR 10-9.240, the following may be bought, sold, possessed, transported, and exhibited without permit: ... amphibians, reptiles, and mammals not native to Missouri except those mammals located in a licensed big game hunting preserve;

A capuchin, marmoset or squirrel monkey is a mammal not native to Missouri and is not federally endangered, so it needs no Wildlife Code permit and owes the state only the county registration that RSMo § 578.023 requires of anyone who keeps a "nonhuman primate". A ring-tailed lemur is inside the carve-out, because the family Lemuridae is on the federal list, and that leaves the general prohibition in 3 CSR 10-4.110(1), which bars possessing wildlife "except as specifically permitted by these rules". No permit in the confined wildlife chapter is written for an exotic endangered mammal kept as a pet, and the Class I, II and III breeder permits are for natives, venomous natives and cervids. The lemur is the only one of the four small primates Missouri bars, and it bars it without ever mentioning a primate.

South Carolina does the same by statute. Its 2018 ban reaches primates only through "Family Hominidae ... all great apes", so a capuchin needs nothing from the state, but S.C. Code § 50-15-30(C)(3) separately makes it unlawful to possess "any species or subspecies of wildlife appearing on any of the following lists: ... (3) the United States' List of Endangered Foreign Fish and Wildlife (Part 17 of Title 50, Code of Federal Regulations, Appendix A, 50 C.F.R. Section 17.11), as such list may be modified hereafter". The family Lemuridae appears on that list. § 50-15-40(D) lets the Department permit possession only for scientific, zoological, educational, propagation or other special purposes, so the paper exists for a zoo and not a keeper.

New Jersey's version closes a door that is otherwise half open. A lemur needs a permit in any case, because N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.2(a) bars possessing any exotic mammal without one, and the question for every other exotic is which permit. 7:25-4.10(a) answers it for the lemur:

> Except as specifically hereinafter provided, no permit shall be issued for the possession of any species designated as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior or the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection pursuant to N.J.S.A. 23:2A-4.

The only exception is 4.10(b), a permit issued after a clear showing under 4.14, which requires a federal endangered species permit, a sponsoring organization with a designated professional, and a written proposal for scientific research that needs the species. That closes the individual hobby permit at 4.6(a)1 whatever the potentially dangerous table says, and the table does not say much: its primate rows are the Cebidae, the Old World monkeys and the apes, and a lemur is none of those. The capuchin is barred in New Jersey by the Cebidae row; the lemur is barred by its endangered listing, and the distinction matters because the lemur's bar has a research exception the capuchin's does not.

Wyoming and Nebraska catch the lemur twice. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 11(b)(iv) refuses a permit for "All members of the order nonhuman primates", and § 11(b)(xv) refuses one for "Endangered and threatened wildlife listed in 50 CFR 17.11", so a lemur has no route on either count. Nebraska's importation list is closed and names no primate, which is the capuchin's bar, and §§ 37-477(1) and 37-806(1) put a federally endangered species behind a captive permit as well.

## Where the Lemur Gets the Gentler Answer

Ohio is the one state whose primate chapter is easier to read for a lemur than for a monkey. R.C. 935.01(C)(19) defines a dangerous wild animal to include "Nonhuman primates other than lemurs and the nonhuman primates specified in division (C)(20) of this section", so the possession bar in § 935.02, which closed every dangerous wild animal to new owners on 1 January 2014, never reaches a lemur. What applies instead is the registration section the same bill enacted:

> A person that possesses any of the following animals shall register the animal in the same manner as provided in section 935.04 of the Revised Code: (A) Pygmy, white-tufted-ear, silvery, and black-pencilled marmosets; (B) Squirrel monkeys; (C) Brown, white-faced, weeping, and white-fronted capuchins; (D) Lemurs.

Registration under § 935.04 means a microchip implanted at the time, and a lemur so registered is lawful. The capuchin, marmoset and squirrel monkey on the same list are not written out of (C)(19), which is why their Ohio cells are gray and the lemur's is not. West Virginia reaches the same answer by exclusion: C.S.R. § 74-1-3.1.o puts "Nonhuman primates" on the Dangerous Wild Animal list and then excludes, before anything else, "3.1.o.1. Lemurs". A ring-tailed lemur is therefore not a dangerous wild animal, and because it is not native it is not a "wild animal" under § 20-1-2 either, so no West Virginia rule reaches it at all. The monkeys stay on the list and are barred.

Oklahoma bars the lemur and lets it go in the next subsection. 29 O.S. § 7-502(A)(2) says no person may possess "Any endangered or threatened species or parts thereof at any time", which the family Lemuridae is, and § 7-502(B)(4) exempts "Persons possessing legally obtained wildlife, from a source other than the wild, as pets or for the purpose of training hunting dogs". Since no lemur in the country has come from the wild since the species went on Appendix I, the exemption is the rule, and the exemption list at OAC 800:25-25-3 closes with "(37) Primates" in any case, so no import permit or breeder's license applies either.

<FunFact>
Idaho sends the capuchin to the permit desk and the lemur to the zoo. IDAPA 02.04.27.407 says "All non-human primates must be possessed on a facility that is AZA accredited" and then exempts four entries: the capuchin, the marmoset, the spider monkey and the squirrel monkey, with two of the genera swapped. No lemur is among them. A ring-tailed lemur in Idaho is a deleterious exotic animal that may be held only on an AZA-accredited facility under a possession permit from the Administrator, which is the same answer Idaho gives a tiger.
</FunFact>

## Montana Bars It by Absence

Montana's prohibited mammal list at ARM 12.6.2215(4) names the Cebidae at (g) and the Callitrichidae at (f), which bars the capuchin, squirrel monkey and marmoset by family, and names no lemur family at all. That is not a gap. Montana runs a white list, and MCA § 87-5-705(1) says a person "may not import into the state, possess, or sell any exotic wildlife unless: (a) the importation, possession, or sale of the exotic wildlife is allowed by law or commission rule". The lemur is on none of Montana's three lists, noncontrolled, controlled or prohibited, and no statute allows it, so the absence is the bar. The same mechanism bars the lemur in Hawaii, where Part B of the restricted list names "Lemuridae (all species in family)" and § 4-71-6.5(b)(3) then withdraws private use from every primate, and in New Mexico, whose importation list carries a row reading "Lemuridae all species | Lemur | IV | Federally Endangered", Group IV being closed to the general public.

## The Permits

Seven jurisdictions issue a permit, and six issue it to a private keeper. Florida's is the one most people will use. The captive wildlife rule writes its primate classes genus by genus, and the lemur, unlike the indri, is on neither Class I nor Class II, so 68A-6.002(1)(c) catches it with "Class III: All other wildlife not listed herein", and 68A-6.003(8) says a person possessing Class III wildlife for personal use "shall obtain a no-cost permit from the Executive Director". Arkansas names the species by binomial: Addendum R1.02 says permits "for breeding, sale, and importation may be issued for the following species", and the mammal list carries "Ring-tailed Lemur (Lemur catta)", so a lemur may be kept if it came from a Commission-permitted breeder or dealer, with county sheriff registration within 30 days under Ark. Code Ann. § 20-19-605 and no outdoor tethering or public contact under § 20-19-602. Delaware's definition of primate names lemurs and exempts none, so a lemur needs the Individual Permit that regulation 903 writes for "an exotic ... kept as a pet", per animal for three years. South Dakota's possession permit covers the whole order Primates at ten dollars an animal, and North Dakota makes primates category 3 nontraditional livestock, licensed and inspected before the animal arrives.

Massachusetts has the permit on paper only. MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet", and adds a lemur-shaped reason: 321 CMR 9.01(3) bars exempting any species assessed as endangered on the IUCN Red List, which *Lemur catta* is. Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard. Arizona is restricted rather than permitted: R12-4-406(F)(8) makes "All nonhuman primates" restricted live wildlife, the rule takes ITIS as its taxonomic authority, which files lemurs in Primates, and no exemption is a pet route.

## Where the Order Is the Rule

The other eleven jurisdictions that ban a lemur do it with a sentence that never needed to know the species. California restricts "Order Primates-Monkeys, Apes: All species" and carves out only humans. Pennsylvania prohibits "The order Primates. All families of nonhuman primates." Utah, Colorado, Rhode Island, Alaska and the District of Columbia reach the same answer by a closed list, a prohibited table or a research-only tier. Oregon, Washington, Kentucky and Louisiana ban with a grandfather window that closed between 1995 and 2010 and lets no new animal in. The [pet monkey legal guide](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) carries each state's sentence and its closing date; nothing in any of them turns on the lemur being a lemur.

## The Thirteen With No Rule

Wisconsin, Texas, Michigan, Nevada, Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina, Kansas, West Virginia, Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi and Oklahoma restrict a ring-tailed lemur nowhere in their state law. Texas is the clearest, because its list is closed and short: the primates on Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) are "(P) a baboon; (Q) a chimpanzee; (R) an orangutan; (S) a gorilla", so the certificate of registration and the $100,000 of insurance never attach to a lemur. Tennessee sends "Primates not otherwise listed" to Class III, which needs no wildlife permit, and Mississippi's inherently dangerous list stops at the apes, macaques, baboons and the gelada. Nevada's no-permit list reads "Monkeys and other primates", which reaches a prosimian. Virginia, Alabama and Indiana list carnivores, rodents and reptiles and nothing with hands, Kansas lets lawfully obtained exotic wildlife be possessed "without limit in time and number", and Michigan's Large Carnivore Act names cats and bears. Wisconsin would not have cared about the federal listing even if it had read it: § 169.01(9) confines "endangered or threatened species" to animals indigenous to the United States or Canada, so a Madagascar primate is just another nonnative wild animal that needs no license.

North Carolina is the state the lemur is known for, and its answer is silence. No statewide rule names a primate, which is how Durham came to host the Duke Lemur Center, the largest collection of lemurs anywhere outside Madagascar. § 153A-131 says a county "may by ordinance regulate, restrict, or prohibit the possession or harboring of animals which are dangerous to persons or property", and several have, so the county code decides most cases. That is true of every one of the thirteen: primates appear on municipal exotic animal lists more often than almost any other pet, and Texas and Nevada both say in terms that a city or county may prohibit what the state does not.

## The Takeaway

The ring-tailed lemur is banned in 30 jurisdictions and permitted in seven, and the two bans that set it apart from the capuchin were written by Congress rather than by any state. Missouri, South Carolina and New Jersey adopted the federal endangered list, and the family Lemuridae is on it, so a lemur is barred there by a sentence that never mentions a primate. Idaho reads the lemur out of its exemption and keeps it zoo-only. Then Ohio writes it out of the dangerous wild animal definition and asks for a microchip, West Virginia excludes it first of all, and Oklahoma bars it as endangered and exempts it as a pet in the next subsection. The lemur is the primate to read twice, because the same listing that bars it in Jefferson City does nothing in Columbus.
