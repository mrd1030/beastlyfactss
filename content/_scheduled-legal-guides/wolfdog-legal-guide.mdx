    a: "Yes, at the state level. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) lists the dangerous wild animals and reaches hybrids only of the animals it names, and its canids are the coyote and the jackal. The wolf is not listed, so a wolfdog is outside the statute at any generation, while a coydog is inside it. Texas cities and counties regulate dogs and may restrict wolf hybrids, and several do."

One that reads as a dog needs nothing from FWC; one considered indistinguishable from a wolf is a Class II canid and needs the $140 personal use permit with documented experience and caging."
  - q: "Can I have a wolfdog in California?"

<LegalDisclaimer />

A wolfdog is the one animal on this map that the law cannot agree on what to call. A statute that lists "any wolf" may or may not reach an animal that is one quarter wolf. A statute that lists the domestic dog may or may not include one. Only a handful of states wrote a definition, and the definitions disagree: Michigan and Massachusetts count every generation in, Iowa counts every generation with a dog parent out, California draws the line after F1, North Dakota says "any part wolf", and Florida asks what the animal looks like. Across all 52 jurisdictions that produces 12 bans, 5 permits, 8 conditional cells, 17 states where it is a dog, and 10 where the text will not say. No other animal splits five ways like that.

The [Savannah cat](/blog/savannah-cat-legal-guide/) is the only comparable case, a wild-by-domestic hybrid the law counts by generation. The cat has a registry, and Massachusetts and New Hampshire free a cat whose pedigree shows no wild parentage for three generations; no wolfdog registry exists that any statute recognizes, so the states that wrote a cat exit wrote none for the dog. The other half of the wolfdog's law it shares with the [fennec fox](/blog/fennec-fox-legal-guide/): the family-wide Canidae clauses in Connecticut, New York and Maryland that take every canid but the domestic dog, and the rabies problem underneath, since no rabies vaccine is licensed for either animal.

## Written for the Wolfdog

Two states wrote a statute about this animal and nothing else, and both wrote a ban. Michigan's Wolf-dog Cross Act, MCL 287.1001 to 287.1023, is the model. § 287.1003(1)(a) bars possessing a wolf-dog cross except in compliance with the act, and § 287.1004(1) sets out the only compliance there is:

> A person shall not possess 1 or more wolf-dog crosses unless all of the following apply: (a) The person owns the wolf-dog crosses or has temporarily been given possession of the wolf-dog crosses by the owner. (b) The owner was in possession of those individual wolf-dog crosses on the effective date of this act. (c) The owner applies for a permit for those wolf-dog crosses within 4 months after the effective date of this act, and obtains a permit for those wolf-dog crosses.

The effective date was 29 June 2000, the permit came from local animal control with a veterinarian's certificate of sterilization, and it applied only to those individual animals. The act also bars breeding, transferring or receiving a wolf-dog cross, its definition at § 287.1002(p) reaches a wolf-dog cross bred with a dog, so there is no generation at which the animal stops being one, and an owner who has represented a canid as a wolf-dog cross is presumed to have one. A quarter century on, the permitted animals are gone, and what is left is a ban.

Massachusetts got there six years earlier with a shorter statute. M.G.L. c. 131, § 77A:

> No person shall possess, sell, trade, breed, import, export or release a wild canid hybrid or wild felid hybrid, except as otherwise provided by rules and regulations of the division. Any mammal which is the offspring of the reproduction between any species of wild canid or hybrid wild canid and a domestic dog or hybrid wild canid, or is represented by its owner to be a wolf hybrid, coyote hybrid, coy dog or any other kind of wild canid hybrid ...

The second sentence is the reach. It takes the offspring of a hybrid and a dog, so an F3 is caught, and it takes any animal "represented by its owner to be a wolf hybrid", so an animal that is all dog and was sold as a wolfdog is caught too. The grandfather required a permit by 31 July 1994. The section's one exit for later generations is written for a registered cat, and MassWildlife says plainly that wolf-dog hybrids "are not domestic animals".

## Caught by the Family

Six more jurisdictions ban it inside a canid clause that names hybrids. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1)(vi) bars a live "hybrid of a member of the dog family and a domestic dog", and unlike the cat hybrid clause beside it, which stops at thirty pounds, there is no weight test and no generation line. Connecticut's Category One reaches "any wild animal, gamete or hybrid" of a canid, with a hybrid exception written for exactly one animal, the Bengal cat. New York's ECL § 11-0511 bars possessing "any live wolf, wolfdog, coyote, coydog" without a license that issues only for scientific, educational, exhibition, zoological or propagation purposes. New York City's wild dog clause ends with "any hybrid or cross-breed offspring of a wild dog and domesticated dog", the District admits "Domestic dogs, excluding hybrids with wolves, coyotes, or jackals", and Hawaii names "Canis familiaris crossed with wolf" on its prohibited list.

Georgia's DNR says in terms that "Wolf hybrids are not a legal pet in Georgia". Missouri's Class II list names "wolves or wolf-hybrids", and its approved species list carries no hybrid at all. Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029 carves "a hybrid of a game animal" out of its clean list, and because the wolf is game, subsection (c) bars a pet permit for exactly that hybrid.

Wyoming is the one worth reading twice, because it contains both answers and picks one. Chapter 10 § 2(i) defines domestic animals to include "any hybrid or transgenic product thereof" of a listed domestic animal, and the dog is listed, which on its own would make a wolfdog domestic. But § 11(b) then says a permit "shall not be issued for importation or possession of the following live warm-blooded wildlife", and the list runs "(ix) Gray wolf (Canis Lupis); (x) Red wolf (Canis Rufus); ... (xiii) Wolf hybrid". A specific entry naming the animal governs over a general definition that would sweep it in, so the wolfdog is on Wyoming's no-permit list at any generation.

<FunFact>
Louisiana's rule bans the wolfdog and says the ban is over, in the same section. LAC 76:V.115.C.1 still prints "f. wolf dog hybrid (Canis lupus or Canis rufus x Canis familiarus)" in its list of animals that may not be imported, possessed, bought or sold. Subsection D.1 then reads: "Wolf-Dog Hybrids. The prohibition against wolf-dog hybrids expired January 1, 1997. Persons are cautioned that local ordinances or other state regulations may prohibit possession of these animals. Any animal which appears indistinguishable from a wolf, or is in any way represented to be a wolf shall be considered to be a wolf in the absence of bona fide documentation to the contrary." On the D.1 reading a wolfdog is legal and a wolf-looking one is a wolf unless documented. The map records Louisiana unclear because the list was never cleaned up.
</FunFact>

## The Permits

Five states issue a permit an individual can hold, and each attaches a condition the pure wolf never gets. Maine's § 7.12 says no wolf hybrid may be permitted unless the applicant first presents a licensed veterinarian's proof that the animal "has been permanently identified in accordance with 7 M.R.S. § 3921-A" and "has been spayed or neutered"; Maine permits no pure canid to an individual, so the hybrid is the one canid with a route, and the Title 7 dog license is a second requirement rather than an alternative. Wisconsin's NR 16.11(5) names wolf-dog hybrids "and subsequent generations from such matings" as harmful wild animals needing a captive wild animal farm license, with the keeper's conditions written in: neutered by six months, tattooed or microchipped, penned or leashed, never in a dog park. The department may presume a canid is a wolf-dog from wolf-like looks or the owner's own description, and the owner may rebut that with a DNA test. South Dakota's ARSD 12:68:18:03(1)(b) requires a permit to possess any nondomestic canid "or any of its hybrids", ten dollars an animal.

North Dakota is the only state with a percentage rule, and it sets the percentage at zero. NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(3)(b) puts "Wolves" and their hybrids in category 3, the inherently dangerous tier, which needs a license before acquisition and an approved premises, and NDCC § 36-01-08.2 adds the mark:

> Any person who keeps a mountain lion, wolf, or wolf hybrid in captivity must obtain an identification number from the board. The number must be tattooed in indelible ink inside the ear of the animal

The Century Code's definition is the one that matters: "wolf hybrid" means "any animal that is any part wolf". A sixteenth-wolf animal is a wolf hybrid in North Dakota and wears the tattoo.

Mississippi's permit is the most expensive to hold. Rule 8.3(A)(5)(b)(i) lists "Genus Canis (wolves, jackals, and dingos; all species, including crosses between wolves and domestic animals)" as inherently dangerous, with no generation cut-off. The personal possession permit is $75 an animal, and the conditions are the ones Mississippi writes for a tiger: age 21, two years' experience or a written examination, a recapture plan, $100,000 of liability insurance for each animal, a department microchip and an inspection.

## Counted by Generation, or by Looks

Eight states allow a wolfdog on a condition, and the conditions are where the three ways of defining the animal show most clearly. California counts generations. 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(K)(2)(a) says a *Canis familiaris* by *Canis lupus* cross is "considered F1 generation wolf hybrids and are restricted (W)", then: "No state permit is required to possess the progeny of F1 generation wolf hybrids, but cities and counties may prohibit possession or require a permit." Iowa counts the other way. § 717F.1(5)(b)(1) says a dangerous wild animal does not include "The offspring of a domestic dog and a wolf, or the offspring from each subsequent generation in which at least one parent is a domestic dog", so a wolfdog with a dog parent is out at any generation and one bred from two wolfdogs is not. Delaware's regulation 903 defines a hybrid by its parents, one wild and one domestic, so a first cross needs the $25 Individual permit and an animal whose parent is itself a wolfdog is outside the requirement on the regulation's words.

Florida, North Carolina and Oregon count by looks. FWC says "Wolf-dogs considered to be indistinguishable from a wolf will be regulated as a Class II canid and will require a Class II license for possession", and one that reads as a dog needs nothing. North Carolina's § 113-129(16) extends its wildlife definitions to species "normally wild, or indistinguishable from wild species, which are raised or kept in captivity". Oregon's OAR 635-044-0460(5) puts "the burden of proof of the hybridity of a bobcat-cross or wolf-cross animal" on the owner, and an animal the owner cannot prove is a hybrid is classified as a wolf, which since 2017 no new private keeper may hold. Pennsylvania bars "a full-blooded wolf or crossbreed thereof not licensed by the Department of Agriculture", the agency that runs dog licensing, so a wolfdog carrying a dog license is outside the Game Commission's list. Nevada's NAC 503.140 frees "Wolves that are lawfully acquired and bred in captivity" and defines wolves to include "any canid hybrid resulting from the mating of a wolf and a dog".

## The Word Wolf

Ten states are recorded unclear, six of them for the same reason: the statute says "wolf" and nothing about hybrids, in a scheme that elsewhere shows it knows how to say "hybrid" when it means to. Illinois's 720 ILCS 5/48-10 names "wolf" as a dangerous animal and has no hybrid clause either way, where Ohio and Washington exclude hybrids in terms and Texas includes them. Minnesota lists "wolf" as small game that may not be possessed without a special permit, and its regulated animal statute reaches hybrids of cats, bears and primates and not canids. Kentucky prohibits "Wolf (Canis lupus)" as a native and writes hybrid language twice without naming the wolf in either clause; since many authorities class the domestic dog as *Canis lupus familiaris*, a bare binomial does not settle it. Kansas's K.A.R. 115-20-4 requires a possession permit for "wolf, Canis lupus Say" and writes no canid hybrid rule, so the answer is a personal permit or nothing. Nebraska's § 37-477(2) bans "any wolf" and says nothing about hybrids, while § 37-247 defines wildlife as a nondomesticated species, which a dog cross arguably is not. Tennessee puts wolves in Class I and writes three hybrid rules that do not fit this cross, because a domestic dog is in no class at all.

The other four are definitions that cut both ways. New Jersey's dangerous table reaches "Nondomestic dogs" and stops, so a wolfdog is half of each category the word separates. Rhode Island's dog entry excludes "hybrids with wild canids", which plainly bars the F1, and then defines a hybrid as "the cross breeding of a domestic and wild animal", so the offspring of a wolfdog rather than a wolf is arguably not a hybrid at all. New Mexico's dog row puts "Dog, Common & Hybrids" in Group I "except as restricted in another group", and the gray wolf is Group IV. Louisiana is above.

In every one of the ten, a high-content animal that looks like a wolf is where enforcement would begin, and the practical step is the same: ask the wildlife agency in writing, keep the breeder's pedigree with the animal, and read the county ordinance, which in most of these states is the rule that actually applies to a dog.

## Seventeen States Where It Is a Dog

The legal column is built from two kinds of clause. The first defines the dog to include the hybrid. Virginia's 4VAC15-20-50 lists among domestic animals "Domestic dog (Canis familiaris), including wolf hybrids", Colorado clears "Domestic dog (Canis familiaris) including hybrids with wild canids", and Utah's domestic column carries the dog "including hybrids between wild and domestic species and subspecies" while listing the wolf as prohibited. Vermont wrote the animal into the dog law: 20 V.S.A. chapter 193 is headed "Domestic Pet or Wolf-Hybrid Control", and § 3581(a) requires the owner of a wolf-hybrid to license it with the town clerk each year at the dog fees. Arizona's R12-4-401 says "Offspring from a wildlife species and a domestic animal species are not considered wildlife", the same sentence that settles the Savannah cat.

The second kind writes the hybrid out of the wolf's own entry. Ohio lists "Gray wolves, excluding hybrids" on a list that otherwise includes hybrids by default, Washington's RCW 16.30.010 reads "Family canidae, wolves, excluding wolf-hybrids", and West Virginia's § 74-1-3.1.b reads "Gray wolves, excluding hybrids". Indiana's 312 IAC 9-11-8(a) makes "Wolves that are purebred" Class III and closes with "A hybrid of any of these species with a domestic dog or domestic cat is exempt from permitting under this section". Texas reaches hybrids only of the animals it lists, and its canids are the coyote and the jackal. Arkansas names "Wolf/Dog hybrids" among species exempt from every Commission permit, and New Hampshire's Fis 802.03(f) exempts "a wolf hybrid" with none of the registry condition it writes for cats in the same sentence. Idaho, South Carolina, Montana, Alabama and Oklahoma are silence: no text names a wolf hybrid, and the state's canid entries name coyotes and foxes.

Nearly every one of the seventeen says, in its own words, that the city or county may do what the state does not, and for a wolfdog that is not a formality: local animal control ordinances regulate wolf hybrids far more often than state wildlife codes do.

## The Takeaway

Where the wolfdog is banned, it is because a drafter wrote the hybrid in: Michigan and Massachusetts by name, Maryland, Connecticut, New York and the District by a canid clause that reaches hybrids, Wyoming by a list entry that beats its own definition. Where it is legal, it is because a drafter wrote the hybrid out, into the dog or out of the wolf. Where it is unclear, nobody wrote anything, and the animal is a wolf in Illinois if a prosecutor can persuade a court that it is one. The five permit states and the eight conditional ones are the places that tried to describe the animal, by sterilization, by tattoo, by generation or by looks, and they are the only places on the map where the law and the wolfdog have met.
