<LegalDisclaimer />

An otter in a pet store is an Asian small-clawed otter, *Aonyx cinereus*, the smallest otter species and the only one anyone breeds for the trade. Two facts about it decide the law before any state is consulted. The first is that it is a mustelid, so every statute that takes "the family Mustelidae" or "the order Carnivora" and then frees the ferret by name reaches the otter with the ferret taken out. The second is that since 2019 it has been on CITES Appendix I, which bars commercial import, so every lawful pet otter in the United States was bred here from animals already in the country, and nobody can buy one abroad and bring it home.

Counted across all 52 jurisdictions, the Asian small-clawed otter is legal with no state permit in 15 states, a permit animal in 12, banned in 15 jurisdictions, restricted in two, conditional in one, and undecided in seven. That last number is the highest on this map for any mammal, and it comes from one word. New York, Minnesota, Ohio, Texas and North Carolina each write "otter" into a list of the state's own game or furbearers with no species and no nativity qualifier, and each then bars possessing what the list names. Read as the native river otter, which is what the drafter was thinking of, the Asian animal is untouched. Read literally, it needs a license that was never designed for it.

## The Border Is Closed

The federal rule comes first because it decides where every lawful animal can come from. In 2019 the parties to CITES moved the Asian small-clawed otter from Appendix II to Appendix I, and 50 C.F.R. part 23 implements that in the United States: an Appendix I specimen may not be imported for primarily commercial purposes, and a pet sale is a commercial purpose. CITES is a border rule and not an ownership rule, a distinction the [federal exotic pet laws guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/) walks through for every listed species, so the listing does not make an otter already in Ohio illegal. What it does is cut the supply to domestic captive breeding. Every state cell on this page is written about that animal, a captive-bred Asian otter from a breeder inside the country.

Three states have written the international listing into their own rules. Maine's § 7.06(3)(A)(1)(b) puts any CITES Appendix I species in Category 1, the exhibitor tier, on top of the family entry that already names otters. Massachusetts's § 23 says no animal "listed in any category" of the IUCN Red Data Books may be added to the exemption list, and this otter is IUCN Vulnerable. New Jersey's restricted species notice says the Division classifies an animal as endangered when it appears on "any relevant State or Federal endangered species list, including ... IUCN Red List", and endangered species may not be kept as pets for any reason; whether a Vulnerable rating counts is the question that leaves New Jersey unclear.

## Where the Family Is the Rule

The otter's bans and permits come mostly from one word, and the word is not "otter". It is "Mustelidae". The family clause was written to catch weasels, mink and polecats, and because the ferret is a domesticated polecat, nearly every family clause then writes the [ferret](/blog/ferret-legal-guide/) back out by name. What is left is the otter's rule.

Maine's § 7.18(2)(F) lists "Family Mustelidae All Species in Family Mustelidae (Badgers, Otters, Weasels, and Relatives)" in Category 1, where the permit goes only to exhibitors, rehabilitators and research facilities. New York City Health Code § 161.01(b)(4) prohibits "All fur bearing mammals of the family Mustelidae, including, but not limited to, weasel, marten, mink, badger, ermine, skunk, otter, pole cat, zorille, wolverine, stoat and ferret", one of the few clauses in the country that bans the ferret as well. Georgia's R. 391-4-8-.05(1)(a)3 requires a license for "Order Carnivora (weasels, ferrets, cats, bears, wolves, etc.) - All species, except that European ferret (Mustela putorius furo) may be sold, purchased, exhibited, or held as a pet without a license or permit", and O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues that license only to dealers and exhibitors. Rhode Island's § 3.7(A) prohibits every carnivore "for which there is no USDA conditionally licensed or unconditionally licensed rabies vaccine" to anyone but researchers, exhibitors and AZA facilities, and there is no licensed rabies vaccine for an otter.

California restricts the family and then names this otter in the exception, which is the opposite of a reprieve. 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(K)(5) reads:

> Family Mustelidae-All species (D), except: a. Ambloynx cinerea (Oriental small-clawed otter)-(W)

The genus spelling is decades old, but the animal is this one, and the exception only moves it from the detrimental category to the welfare category. It is still restricted, and the only possession permit California issues, Animal Care, requires lawful possession in the state before January 1992. Oregon simply names it. OAR 635-056-0050(1)(a)(B)(iv) lists "Asian Small-clawed Otter | Mustelidae | Lutrinae Aonyx cinerea (also classified as Aonyx cinereus)" among prohibited species, under both spellings of the binomial so that no one can argue the list means a different animal, and OAR 635-056-0150 lets someone who held one before it was listed keep it for life with no transfer, sale or transport inside Oregon. Hawaii's § 4-71-6.5 Part A carries "Aonyx cinerea otter, Asian small-clawed" on the research and exhibition tier, and Colorado's regulated wildlife list rules on *Aonyx cinereus* by name as an animal it will not license as a pet.

On the permit side the family clause ends in a license. South Dakota's ARSD 12:68:18:03(1) requires a permit for:

> (1) Of the order Carnivora, all nondomestic members of the following families: (a) Felidae; (b) Canidae; (c) Ursidae; (d) Mustelidae; and (e) Hyaenidae;

So 12:68:18:03.03(4)'s possession permit applies, ten dollars an animal to a maximum of one hundred, with a free entry permit for bringing one in. Virginia's 4VAC15-30-40 table reads "Mustelidae, All Species, Weasels, Badgers, Skunks and Otters (except Mustela putorius furo) Ferret", so a nonnative otter needs a special permit to import, possess or sell, and only the ferret walks. North Dakota's NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(2)(b) names "otters" in category 2 of its nontraditional livestock scheme with no species limit, so the license under 48.1-09-01-03 is required before acquiring one. Arizona's R12-4-406(F) restricts "All species of the order Carnivora", which reaches every otter, so a special license is needed there.

Connecticut is the one conditional cell. § 26-55-6(a)(4)(B)(iii) makes "a member within the family Mustelidae (including, but not limited to, short-tailed weasel, long-tailed weasel, mink, fisher, otter and marten)" a Category Three animal, the tier for Connecticut's own natives, drawn by family so that it reaches an Asian otter as well. Subsection (b)(4) bars importing or exporting a Category Three animal except by institutions and authorized persons, and says nothing about possession. An otter already lawfully in Connecticut may be kept; one may not be brought in, which in practice is the whole of the pet trade.

<FunFact>
South Carolina's furbearer definition is the only one on this map that answers the "which otter" question by refusing to close its list. § 50-16-20(A)(1) says "Furbearer includes, but is not limited to, red and gray fox, raccoon, opossum, muskrat, mink, skunk, otter, bobcat, weasel, and beaver". Because the list is open-ended, an Asian otter reads as a furbearer whether or not the drafter meant the river otter, and § 50-16-20(B) lets the Department issue the import permit only if the animal was taken lawfully where it came from and its possession is not expected to harm the state's wildlife. The same word that leaves five states unclear makes South Carolina a permit state.
</FunFact>

## The Word Otter

Five states name "otter" with no species, inside a definition of the state's own game or furbearers, and then attach a possession rule to the definition. They are recorded unclear for one reason, applied the same way to each: the word is bare, the surrounding list is native fauna, and no agency has said which reading it uses. The rule for telling them apart from the fifteen legal states is whether the statute carries an anchor. Kentucky's prohibited natives list names "Otter (Lontra canadensis)" by binomial. Mississippi's Rule 8.4 names the "river otter". Iowa's § 481A.1(36) limits its wild animals to wildlife "found in this state". Illinois protects *Lontra canadensis* by name. West Virginia's wild animals are "all mammals native to the State of West Virginia". Louisiana's R.S. 56:8(151)(a)(iv) names "otter" among nongame quadrupeds, but those are Louisiana's furbearers and no captivity rule applies to nongame quadrupeds generally, so the otter in that statute is the river otter. Each of those has a word that ties "otter" to the native animal. The five below do not.

New York's ECL § 11-0103(2)(c) defines small game to include "raccoon, opossum, or weasel, skunk, bobcat, lynx, muskrat, mink, except mink born in captivity, fisher, otter, beaver, sable and marten", and § 11-0107(2) bars possessing small game "whether taken within the state or coming from without the state". The sentence writes "native" in front of every herp group and leaves it off the mammals, carves out captive-born mink and nothing else, and names the sable, which is not a New York animal at all. Minnesota's § 97A.015 subd. 39 names "otter" in its small game list, and § 97A.501 bars possessing a protected wild animal without a special permit; a wild animal is one "wild by nature", which a captive-bred otter still is. Ohio's R.C. 1531.01(W) says "Fur-bearing animals includes minks, weasels, raccoons, skunks, opossums, muskrats, fox, beavers, badgers, otters, coyotes, and bobcats", and § 1533.71 licenses holding fur-bearing animals in captivity. Texas's § 71.001(1) reads "wild beaver, otter, mink, ring-tailed cat, badger, skunk, raccoon, muskrat, opossum, fox, or nutria", and § 71.005(c) requires a propagation license to "capture or possess a live fur-bearing animal for any purpose". North Carolina's § 113-129(7a) lists "otter" among fur-bearing animals, § 113-129(16) extends the definitions to animals of wild species kept in captivity, and § 113-272.5(a) says the captivity license will not be issued for an animal "acquired merely as a pet". In each, one reading leaves the Asian otter untouched and the other puts it under a license that was never designed for it.

In all five, write to the wildlife agency, name the species and its binomial, ask whether the furbearer or small game definition reaches a captive-bred Asian small-clawed otter, and keep the answer with the animal's paperwork.

## The Closed Lists

Seven jurisdictions ban it without a family clause, by running a list the otter is not on. New Mexico's importation list has a row for the river otter and none for the Asian one, so its default clause designates it Group IV. New Hampshire's controlled table names the river otter alone, and Fis 804.04(b) says an unlisted species "shall be designated as prohibited". Arkansas's Addendum R1.03(B) says "Species not listed in Addenda R1.01, R1.02, or R1.03 are prohibited until evaluated". Montana's § 87-5-705(1) bars any exotic wildlife not "allowed by law or commission rule", and no otter is on any of its three lists. Nebraska's 166 NAC 8 § 001.08A is a closed import list with no otter on it, and the only otter Nebraska names is its native river otter. Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars a pet permit for any game animal. The District of Columbia's § 8-1808(j) names the ferret in its seven permitted categories, and that category does not stretch to other mustelids.

## The Permit States

Twelve jurisdictions issue a permit, and eleven of them issue it to a private keeper. Florida's is the simplest: no otter is named in Class I or Class II under 68A-6.002, the badgers are the only mustelids there, so the Asian small-clawed otter is Class III, and 68A-6.003(8) says "Persons possessing Class III wildlife as personal use wildlife shall obtain a no-cost permit from the Executive Director." Delaware's 903-6.2 requires a $25 Individual permit for a nonnative wild mammal not on the exempt list, and the ferret is the only mustelid exempted. Nevada's NRS 503.597 requires the Department's written consent to bring any wildlife into the state, and the only mustelids on the no-permit list are the ferret and domesticated mink. Idaho's IDAPA 13.01.10.200.01 requires a Fish and Game captive possession license for any live wildlife not exempted, and the conventional pets list has no mustelid but the ferret. Oklahoma's exemption list has no otter, so the import permit and the noncommercial wildlife breeder's license apply. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 13(d) permit "may be authorized upon approval for other warm-blooded wildlife", discretionary rather than owed.

Massachusetts keeps a permit on paper: MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard. Utah is restricted rather than banned: Table 3b-1 has no otter, and its header says "All species not listed will require an assessment before collection, importation, or possession will be authorized". Pennsylvania is the seventh unclear cell for a different reason: its only mustelid line at § 137.1(a)(6) reads "In the family Mustelidae. All species of the skunks", and whether the catch-all at (a)(11) on wildlife "captive bred in another state or nation" reaches an otter is the fennec fox's unanswered question.

## The Takeaway

A pet otter is lawful somewhere in the country, but the route to one is narrower than the fifteen green states suggest. The border has been closed since 2019, so the animal has to be domestically bred and the breeder's records are the proof. The family name Mustelidae decides most of the rest, and after the ferret is written out of it the otter is what the clause means. In fifteen states nothing reaches it because the state's own word "otter" is tied to the river otter by a binomial or a nativity clause, and in five states that tie is missing and the answer is a letter to the agency.
