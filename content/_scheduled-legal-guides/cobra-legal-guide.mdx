A cobra is the simplest venomous snake on this map, and it is simple because of where it is not from. No cobra lives wild in any state, so there is no hunting season to carve one out, no sport-take limit to let one in, and no nongame list to put it on. The state's venomous-reptile clause is the whole answer, and the [venomous snake laws by state](/blog/venomous-snake-laws-by-state/) page walks that clause for every jurisdiction with the cobra as its example. Under state law the cobra is banned in 26 jurisdictions, a permit animal in 13, conditional in three, restricted in Arizona, and unregulated in nine states.

The federal layer is thinner than most keepers assume. No venomous snake is on the injurious wildlife list under the Lacey Act, so nothing federal bars bringing a cobra across a state line. The one federal rule that touches these animals is CITES, which lists the king cobra and the Asian *Naja* species on Appendix II, and the regulations at 50 CFR 23.5 define what that reaches: "International trade means the import, introduction from the sea, export, or re-export across jurisdictional or international boundaries for any purpose whether commercial or noncommercial." An Appendix II listing means export permits at the border, not a rule about the cage, and the [federal laws guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/) sets out why a CITES listing and an injurious listing are different things. Everything that follows is state law.

## The Class Rule Decides Thirty-Nine Cells

Twenty-six bans and thirteen permits, and nearly every one of them is a sentence about a family. Georgia puts "Family Elapidae - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, licensed only to dealers and exhibitors. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621 bars a live "poisonous snake in the family groups of Hydrophidae, Elapidae, Viperidae, or Crotolidae". New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(5) reaches "All reptiles that are venomous by nature" and the department's regulation lists all Elapidae. New Mexico files "Elapidae, all venomous species" in Group IV, closed to the public. Tennessee's Class I is "Order Serpentes: Snakes--All venomous species", open only to zoos, circuses and commercial propagators since 1991. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "All venomous reptiles" for the exhibitor category only. Alaska's clean list clears "Any nonvenomous reptile" and nothing else, and the department may not permit game as a pet. Massachusetts has a permit on paper for any unlisted snake and says it is not for a pet, and Vermont issues its permit for one only at the Commissioner's discretion.

The variations are in how the family is reached, not whether. Kansas's K.S.A. 32-1301(d)(3) makes "all non-native, venomous snakes" dangerous regulated animals, closed to new keepers since 1 July 2006, and a cobra is never native to Kansas. Alabama's 220-2-.26(1) bars "Any species of venomous reptile which has never naturally existed in the wild in Alabama including but not limited to venomous snakes of the families Viperidae, Atractaspididae, Elapidae, Hydrophiidae and Colubridae", with exemptions only for accredited educational, research and rehabilitation facilities on the Commissioner's written permission. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:082 uses nearly the same list of families for its "Venomous exotic snakes". Utah's R657-53-9 writes a three-part test, venomous, not native, and dangerous to bite, and a cobra passes all three into the prohibited class with a possession limit of zero. Delaware's statute bars any venomous snake "not native to or generally found in Delaware" and adds that "no permit for the same shall be issued by the Department of Agriculture". Hawaii prohibits every snake, the District of Columbia's closed list admits "non-venomous snakes" only, and Colorado reaches the cobra by absence: Appendix A of Chapter W-7 clears file snakes, pipe snakes, boas, colubrids, pythons, shield-tails and sunbeam snakes, no elapid among them, so a cobra is regulated wildlife, and Colorado Parks and Wildlife "will not issue a license for private possession (eg pet) of regulated wildlife". The licenses Chapter W-11 does issue are commercial wildlife park licenses, which is why Colorado reads as a ban rather than a permit state.

The permits are the same family clause with a form attached. Florida's is the most demanding: 68A-6.017 requires an applicant of 18 who can "demonstrate no less than one (1) year of substantial practical experience (to consist of no less than 1,000 hours) in the care, feeding, handling and husbandry of the species or other species within the same biological family", two references, and a facility "inspected and approved by Commission personnel prior to the issuance of the permit". Ohio's restricted snake possession permit is the one dangerous-animal permit in that state still open to new keepers: every elapid is a restricted snake under § 935.01(L)(2)(b), and a person who acquires one after 1 January 2014 "shall obtain a restricted snake possession permit under this section not later than one hundred twenty days after acquiring the snake". Texas's § 43.851 has the commission establish permits for "a live nonindigenous snake, including a hybrid of any kind, that is: (1) venomous", recreational and commercial separately. Idaho's captive possession license comes with locked, double-walled enclosures and safety glass. Indiana's Class III permit must be in hand before possession and needs a recapture or destruction plan. North Dakota's category 3 license and Wyoming's Chapter 69 permit both want a veterinary certificate before import. Rhode Island's exotic animal possession permit is open to private persons because § 3.7 does not name venomous snakes. Missouri asks only that a "deadly, dangerous, or poisonous reptile" be registered with county law enforcement. West Virginia reaches a cobra as wildlife from outside the state, which needs an importation permit the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for.

## Where the King Cobra Is Named

*Ophiophagus hannah* is the cobra most people picture and the one almost no statute mentions, and the silence does not matter: it is an elapid, and "Elapidae" or "all venomous species" takes it in. Three rules name it, and they point in three directions.

Oregon is the one state that answers cobras one species at a time. OAR 635-056-0060(4)(c) lists among the noncontrolled species:

> (G) Egyptian cobra -- Elapidae -- Naja haje; (H) Black & white cobra -- Elapidae -- Naja melanoleuca; (I) Indian cobra -- Elapidae -- Naja naja; (J) Red spitting cobra -- Elapidae -- Naja pallida; (K) King cobra -- Elapidae -- Ophiophagus hannah;

A noncontrolled species needs no ODFW permit at all. The black-necked spitting cobra and the Cape cobra are on the prohibited list at 0050, and any cobra on neither list is unlisted, which 0130(1) treats as barred. So under Oregon law a king cobra is unregulated and a Cape cobra is prohibited, and the answer for a monocled cobra, which is on neither list, is no. Oregon cities and counties may prohibit exotic animals by ordinance on top of that.

Arkansas names both genera and sends them to the same permit. Addendum R1.02 lists "Cobras (Genus Naja)" and "King Cobras (Genus Ophiophagus)" as separate entries, and Code 09.17 provides:

> It is unlawful to possess any medically significant venomous reptile after July 1, 2021, whether native or non-native to Arkansas, without obtaining a valid Venomous Reptile Permit issued by the Commission and complying with all permit requirements in Addendum F1.08.

Addendum F1.08 issues the permit to an applicant at least 18 with no captive wildlife or cruelty conviction in five years who shows written proof that local ordinances allow the animal, and sets locked enclosures, a locked escape-proof room and warning signage. The animal must also come from a permitted breeder or dealer, or in on an importation permit under Code 09.02.

Louisiana names the genus to bar it. Its January 2025 rewrite of LAC 76:XV.101 put on the prohibited nonnative list:

> xi. All species in the Genera Naja, Ophiophagus, Pseudohaje, Aspidelaps, and Walterinnesia (cobras); Dendroaspis (mambas), Oxyuranus (taipans), and Bungarus (kraits) of the Family Elapidae;

Possessing, importing, selling, transferring or breeding one is barred. Owners had 365 days from the effective date to register the animal and obtain a permit, with no further acquisition. The same rewrite left rattlesnakes on the Restricted Snake Permit, so Louisiana is a state where the [rattlesnake](/blog/rattlesnake-legal-guide/), the cobra's twin on this map, has a permit route and the cobra does not. The rattlesnake's row is the mirror image of this one almost everywhere: fourteen states carve their own species out of the viper ban, and no state has a cobra to carve out.

<FunFact>
Nebraska is the only state on the map where a cobra needs nothing and a native rattlesnake has a bag limit. The state has no venomous rule, no constrictor rule and no size rule. Its reptile chapter, 166 NAC 10, applies "to all species and subspecies of reptiles and amphibians native to Nebraska", and its captive wildlife chapter covers wild birds and mammals, so a king cobra is outside both. The prairie rattlesnake, as a native, may be taken ten at a time, and the timber rattlesnake may not be possessed at all. A keeper in Omaha is regulated by the snake that lives in the ditch and not by the one that could kill them in the living room, and that is the shape of a state code written about wildlife rather than about danger.
</FunFact>

## The Three Conditions

North Carolina regulates venomous reptiles by housing rather than by species, and does it for a cobra and a copperhead alike. G.S. § 14-417(a) makes it unlawful to "own, possess, use, transport, or traffic in any venomous reptile that is not housed in a sturdy and secure enclosure", designed to be "escape-proof, bite-proof, and have an operable lock". Each enclosure must be labeled "Venomous Reptile Inside" with the scientific and common names, the appropriate antivenin and the owner's identifying information, a written bite protocol and escape recovery plan must be posted within sight of the housing, and an escape must be reported to local law enforcement at once. Counties may go further under § 153A-131.

Minnesota makes every snake a protected wild animal, whatever its origin, and § 97A.401 subd. 8 is the allowance: "A snake, lizard, or salamander that is obtained from a permitted breeder or that was possessed before August 1, 2021, may be possessed as a pet." No Minnesota statute treats a venomous snake differently from a corn snake, so a cobra from a permitted breeder is a pet on the same terms. The breeder permit the condition depends on is not on the DNR's permits page and no rule implements it, so the condition is real on paper and its machinery is not visibly in place. Oregon's species list is the third condition, and it is the only one that turns on which cobra it is.

## The Nine With No Cobra Rule

Nine states have no rule reaching a cobra, and local ordinances were not checked in any of them. Pennsylvania's Fish and Boat Commission reaches a nonnative snake only to say that importers "shall institute appropriate safeguards to prevent their introduction into the natural environment", and the Game Commission's prohibited list covers birds and mammals. Wisconsin frees every nonnative wild animal that is not harmful or endangered, and a cobra is neither. Michigan's Fisheries Order 224 governs "any state reptile or amphibian from the wild", and the Animal Industry Act asks only for a certificate of veterinary inspection at import. Virginia's exotic permit table has one snake on it, the brown tree snake. South Dakota's bar is written for nondomestic mammals and the state has no reptile rule. South Carolina's § 50-16-60 lets reptiles be imported for the pet trade without a permit, and nothing in Title 50 or the native reptile regulations restricts a venomous exotic. Mississippi's nongame rule reaches natives only and its inherently dangerous list names no reptile. Oklahoma's OAC 800:25-25-3(c) exempts "monotypic species and subspecies of reptiles and amphibians not indigenous to Oklahoma" from its import and breeder rules. Nebraska regulates reptiles only where they are its own.

Arkansas's permit application asks for written proof that the local ordinance allows the animal, and that is the right instinct in all nine: a cobra is on more city codes than almost any other animal a keeper might bring home, and a state with no rule is not a city with no rule.

## The Takeaway

Read the class rule, because for a cobra it is the whole state answer. If the statute says Elapidae, venomous species, or non-native venomous snakes, the cobra is closed, and no venomoid surgery changes that. If the state issues a permit, it is a real one in Florida, Ohio, Texas, Arkansas, Idaho, Indiana, North Dakota, Wyoming, Rhode Island, Missouri and West Virginia, a discretionary one in Vermont, and an institutional one in Massachusetts and Arizona. Oregon decides by species, North Carolina by the cage, Minnesota by the breeder, and nine states say nothing. Then read CITES for what it is: a border rule for the king cobra and the Asian *Naja* species, with nothing to say about the terrarium. Our [interactive map of cobra laws](/exotic-pet-laws/cobra/) shades every jurisdiction and quotes the sentence each answer comes from, and for this animal the sentence is almost always the family name.
