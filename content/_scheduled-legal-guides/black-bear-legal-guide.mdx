A black bear is the most restricted animal on this map. Thirty-five of the 52 jurisdictions ban it outright, fourteen issue a permit, one restricts it, one allows it on a condition, one cannot say, and none allows it without paperwork. The serval, which usually holds that title, gets twenty bans. The reason is that the black bear is caught from two directions at once. It is a bear, so every dangerous-animal list in the country names it, usually in the same clause as the lion and the tiger. And it is a native game animal across most of its range, so every state wildlife code that bars possessing game or wildlife reaches it as well, by a mechanism the [raccoon](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/) is caught by too.

The second mechanism is also where the permits come from. A state that regulates its own bears as game has game-breeder licenses, menagerie permits and captive-wildlife licenses on the books, written for people who propagate deer or run a roadside zoo, and in fourteen states none of those permits says the word "pet" to exclude one. West Virginia's costs two dollars. Montana's is written for a place where bears are kept "for use other than public exhibition", which is a private home by another name. None of them was written with a pet bear in mind, and all of them reach one.

## Named With the Big Cats

The clearest bans are the ones that put the bear in the same sentence as the tiger. Ohio's R.C. 935.01(C)(8) lists every bear among dangerous wild animals, and § 935.02(A) says "No person shall possess a dangerous wild animal on or after January 1, 2014". The black bear is also an Ohio game quadruped, but the Division of Wildlife's § 1533.71 captivity license does not lift the bar, because § 935.03(A)(3) exempts only possession under a Chapter 935 permit. Michigan's Large Carnivore Act names "a bear of a species that is native or nonnative to Michigan, whether wild or captive bred" alongside seven big cats, and allows possession only of animals held on 7 July 2000. Iowa's § 717F.1(5)(a)(4) takes "the family ursidae of the order carnivora, including bears and pandas", and the only route was a 2007 registration. Kansas's K.S.A. 32-1302(c) bars taking possession of a dangerous regulated animal after 1 July 2006, and K.A.R. 115-20-4's black bear possession permit is a wildlife rule that cannot override the statute. Minnesota closed possession of bears on 1 January 2005. Nebraska's § 37-477(2) reads "no person shall keep in captivity in this state any wolf, any skunk, or any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae", with pre-1986 animals grandfathered. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1)(i) bars a live "fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear" in four words. The [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) walks each of those statutes for the cats they name, and in every one the bear sits a line above or below.

Illinois, Connecticut, New York and New York City have no grandfather. Illinois's 720 ILCS 5/48-10 names "bear" as a dangerous animal and allows one only at a zoo, licensed exhibit, circus, university, laboratory, veterinary hospital or refuge. Connecticut's Category One clause names the black bear as its first Ursidae example, New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e) lists "Bears in the Family Ursidae" among dangerous animals "including those which are captive bred", and New York City's § 161.01(b)(3) prohibits "All bears, including polar, grizzly, brown and black bear".

Texas bans it by a different list. The black bear is state threatened under 31 TAC § 65.175, and TPWD says its regulations "prohibit the taking, possession, transportation, or sale of any of the animal species designated by state law as endangered or threatened without the issuance of a permit". The dangerous wild animal statute names "a bear" too, but a county certificate does not authorize possessing a threatened animal, so the listing comes first.

## The Native Game Bar

The second set of bans never mentions danger. It treats the bear as what it is across most of its range, a game animal, and applies the rule that nobody keeps game alive without the agency's say. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 § 6(1) says a person shall not import, transport into Kentucky or possess "(c) Black bear (Ursus americanus)", and § 1(3) defines native wildlife "regardless of ... being captive-bred or taken from the wild", so a bear from a breeder in Oklahoma is in exactly the position of one from the Kentucky woods. Virginia's § 29.1-521(A)(10) bars possessing any wild animal "except as specifically permitted by law", and the only permits are for exhibits and scientific collection. Wyoming's statute makes the black bear a "trophy game animal", and Chapter 10 § 11(a) says "There shall be no private ownership, private importation or private possession of live big or trophy game animals". Tennessee's § 70-4-403(4)(A) names "Black bear (Ursus americanus)" in Class IV, "those native species that may be possessed only by zoos and temporary exhibitors", and bears of every species are also in Class I, closed to personal possession since 1991. Rhode Island lists "Black Bear (Ursus americana)" on its native mammal list, prohibited as a pet "regardless of where the animal originated". New Hampshire gives it permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitor category. Alabama names it among animals barred from outside the state and treats an Alabama bear as a protected wild animal. Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars a permit for any game animal "for use as a pet". Arkansas defines a large carnivore to include "all species of bears" and refuses breeding, sale and importation permits for them. Colorado will not license a pet regulated mammal, Maine never categorized the bear and so cannot permit it, Georgia's DNR names it among natives that may not be held as a pet regardless of origin, and New Jersey lists it as a game animal and then says the Division "does not currently issue new permits for the possession of potentially dangerous species within the hobby permit category".

<FunFact>
Mississippi wrote a bear permit and then wrote over it. Rule 8.3 lists every bear as inherently dangerous and offers a personal possession permit at $200. Rule 8.4(A) then defines game and furbearing animals as "white-tailed deer, black bear, gray squirrel, fox squirrel, cottontail rabbit, swamp rabbit, raccoon, opossum, bobcat, river otter, muskrat, mink, and weasel", and 8.4(B) says "Possession of live game and furbearing animals is prohibited with the following exceptions", which are public zoos, veterinarians treating an animal, someone carrying a sick animal to a vet, and permitted deer and fox pens. A Rule 8.3 permit is not on the list. So a Mississippian can permit a grizzly and not the bear in the back yard.
</FunFact>

## Four Doors That Closed

Four states allowed a black bear within living memory and record the date they stopped. Missouri's approved species list confines Class II bears to "Bear, Black (& hybrids) legally obtained under a valid Class II Wildlife Breeder Permit prior to August 30, 2021", and the Wildlife Hobby Permit excludes Class II, so no new bear may be acquired. Oregon's ORS 609.305 reaches "Any bear, except the black bear", which hands the black bear to the wildlife agency rather than freeing it, and OAR 635-044-0460(3) confines any black bear acquired after 20 January 2017 to AZA, exhibitor and sanctuary facilities, with pets held on that date grandfathered for life and no breeding.

Louisiana's is the oldest. LAC 76:V.115.C.1 makes it unlawful "to import into, possess, purchase or sell within the state of Louisiana ... any of the following species or its subspecies of live wild quadrupeds, big exotic cats, or non-human primates, domesticated or otherwise", and the first animal on the list is "a. black bear (Ursus americanus)". C.2 then grandfathers the holders of a potentially dangerous wild quadruped permit "valid on the effective date of this regulation", first published in the Louisiana Register in December 1995: the permit "will be renewed annually until existing permitted captive animals expire", and "No additional listed animals may be acquired by any means whatsoever, including breeding." Any bear held under that grandfather today was alive in 1995, so the clause is close to spent.

## The Permits, Read for What They Are

Fourteen states issue a permit, and the permits sort into three kinds. The first kind an individual can hold, and seven states have one.

West Virginia's is the strangest on the map. The Dangerous Wild Animal Board's list at § 74-1-3.1.h reads:

> 3.1.h. Bears; (excluding black bears native to this state);

The carve-out hands the native bear back to Chapter 20, where § 20-1-2 names the black bear as a game animal and § 20-2-4(a) bars holding wildlife in captivity except as the chapter provides. What the chapter provides is § 20-2-51: "The director may issue a permit to a person to keep and maintain in captivity as a pet, a wild animal or wild bird that has been acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season. The fee therefor shall be $2." On the words, a black bear bought from a dealer is in the same position as a raccoon, and the permit is discretionary, "may issue". That is surprising enough that the DNR's practice deserves a letter before anyone relies on it, and the map records the words.

Montana writes the permit for private keeping in so many words. MCA § 87-4-801(3) defines a "wild animal menagerie" as "any place where one or more bears or large cats, including cougars, lions, tigers, jaguars, leopards, pumas, cheetahs, ocelots, and hybrids of those large cats are kept in captivity for use other than public exhibition". A roadside menagerie is the exhibition kind; a wild animal menagerie is the other kind, which is a private keeper. § 87-4-803(2)(b) asks the applicant for the site and their title to the land, "a list of species and the number of animals to be held", the cage specifications, and "information demonstrating that the applicant is responsible", and the department inspects before issuing. § 87-4-804 sets the supply: a menagerie may not obtain a healthy wild animal "by capture from the wild or by purchase", but "may obtain captive-bred wild animals from a licensed zoo, menagerie, alternative livestock ranch, fur farm, game bird farm, wildlife sanctuary, or animal rehabilitation center", and "The number of wild animals in a wild animal menagerie may not exceed 10".

Oklahoma's permit is called a commercial wildlife breeder's license and is nothing of the kind for a bear. 29 O.S. § 7-502(A)(3) says no person may possess "Any native bear or native cat that will grow to reach the weight of fifty (50) pounds or more" except as otherwise provided by law, and § 4-107(E) is the provision: anyone who keeps such a bear "shall be licensed under this section with a commercial wildlife breeder's license", shall keep it "confined to the premises described in the commercial wildlife breeder application", and shall control it "so that there is no direct contact between the public and the wildlife". § 4-107(C) issues the license to "any person whom the Director believes to be acting in good faith", § 4-107(D) prices it at $48 a year, and a licensee may sell a native bear only to another license holder.

South Dakota's ARSD 12:68:18:03(1)(c) puts "Ursidae" on the possession permit list and nowhere on the prohibited list, so a black bear takes the same ten-dollar permit as a serval, once the board finds the facilities adequate. North Dakota's NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(3)(b)(3) puts "Bears" in category 3, the inherently dangerous tier, licensed on the same footing as category 2: a license before acquisition and an approved, inspected premises. Indiana's 312 IAC 9-11-8(a)(2) makes "Bears (all species)" Class III, with a permit before possession, a recapture or destruction plan and a cage inspection. Wisconsin's § 169.11(1)(b) bars possessing a harmful wild animal "unless specifically authorized to do so by the department", the authorization is a captive wild animal farm license naming the species, and NR 16.15(2)(f) approves no first application without written assurance that it complies with local ordinances and zoning, which is where most applications would stop.

The second kind is a permit the agency can refuse. Pennsylvania prohibits "All species of the bears" and leaves only the exotic wildlife possession permit under Chapter 147, a separate permit per animal with two years of documented hands-on experience at a recognized facility. Idaho's IDAPA 13.01.10.200 requires a captive possession license for any live wildlife and lets the Department refuse on public safety grounds, which for a bear is the likely answer. Nevada needs the Department's written consent under NRS 503.597, and whether it would consent to a private keeper holding a game mammal is not something the rule says. South Carolina's § 50-16-20(A)(1) names "bear" among the animals needing an import permit, its 2018 ban reaches non-native bears only, and § 50-16-20(B) lets the Department issue the permit only if possession is not expected to harm the state's wildlife. Vermont's Regulation 881 issues one outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", which includes human safety, and a bear is restricted there by absence from all three lists.

The third kind is a permit in name only. MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet", and North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) tells the Executive Director not to issue a captivity license unless the animal "was not acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet", with a $2,000 minimum fine for unlawfully possessing a bear.

## The Three That Do Not Fit

Arizona restricts rather than bans: R12-4-406(F) restricts "All species of the order Carnivora", so a special license is needed. Washington is conditional, and thinly so. RCW 16.30 lists "Family ursidae, all bears" as potentially dangerous, but § 16.30.020(1)(k) exempts "native wildlife subject to Title 77 RCW", and the black bear is a Washington animal, so the dangerous animal ban does not reach it. Title 77 then asks only for lawful acquisition under WAC 220-450-030: "It is unlawful to possess or hold in captivity live wild animals, wild birds, or game fish unless lawfully acquired", with a health certificate to import, documented proof of source, and no sale of the animal or its progeny. That is a thin answer for a bear, and it turns on the native-wildlife exemption doing what it says. Delaware is the one unclear cell. The black bear is historically native there and has no resident population now. Treated as native, DNREC says it may not be owned; treated as not "generally found in Delaware", it is an Agriculture exotic needing the Individual permit under regulation 903, which refuses no bear by name. Neither agency has said which.

## The Takeaway

A pet black bear is banned in 35 jurisdictions and permitted in 14, and the fourteen are not a list of lenient states. They are the states whose game-breeder, menagerie and captive-wildlife rules were written without a pet exclusion, so that a license meant for a propagator or a roadside zoo reaches a private keeper on its words. Nowhere is it legal without one, and in Missouri, Louisiana, Oregon and Mississippi the door that was once open has a date on it.
