A Savannah cat is a serval crossed with a domestic cat, and the law in most states was written about one half of that sentence or the other. Where a statute reaches the serval, the question is whether its hybrid comes along. Where a statute reaches hybrids by generation, the question is how many generations the cat is from the serval. Only a handful of states have looked at the animal itself, and they give the sharpest answers on the map: Ohio frees the Savannah by name in the same line that restricts the serval, Georgia's wildlife agency names it as the hybrid it will not allow, and Texas treats it as the serval itself, with a certificate of registration and $100,000 of liability insurance.

The generation numbers do the rest of the work. A serval bred to a domestic cat gives F1 kittens; an F1 bred to a domestic cat gives F2, then F3, then F4. Each step is the point where some state's law lets go of the animal. The breed is recognized by The International Cat Association, and that registration is itself a legal condition in New York, Massachusetts, New Hampshire and Utah. Early generations are the large, serval-shaped cats these laws were written about, and a male F1 is the one hybrid cat on this map that can grow toward Maryland's thirty-pound line.

## Three States Looked at the Cat Itself

Ohio Rev. Code § 935.01(C)(7) brings "hybrids with domestic cats unless otherwise specified" into the dangerous wild animal definition, then lists cheetahs, lynxes, cougars and caracals before arriving at:

> (e) Servals, excluding hybrids with domestic cats commonly known as savannah cats.

That is the only sentence on the map that names this breed in order to free it. The serval has been barred under § 935.02 since 2014, for anyone who did not register one in 2012; the Savannah, at any filial generation, needs nothing from the state.

Georgia names it in order to bar it. The wild animal rule lists "Family Felidae (cats) - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, and the Department of Natural Resources states the hybrid position in its own guide to legal pets:

> Hybrids or crosses between any combination of domestic animals, wildlife, or regulated wild animals and all subsequent generations are regulated in Georgia and may not be held without a license. ... Most exotic cat hybrids, such as a savannah cat, are not a legal pet in Georgia.

The license is what turns that into a ban: O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues wild animal licenses "only to persons engaged in the wholesale or retail wild animal business or persons exhibiting wild animals to the public." A private keeper has nothing to apply for.

Texas never names the Savannah and does not need to. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) lists nineteen animals, the serval at clause (J), and closes with "(T) any hybrid of an animal listed in this subdivision." The same sentence that frees the Bengal, whose Asian leopard cat parent is not on the list, catches the Savannah, whose serval parent is. § 822.103(a) bars keeping a dangerous wild animal without a certificate of registration from the local animal control office, § 822.107 requires liability insurance of at least $100,000 per occurrence, and § 822.116(b) lets any city or county prohibit the animal outright. No generation line is drawn.

Florida's agency also answered by name, in the other direction. The serval is a Class II animal and 68A-6.002(1)(d) regulates a hybrid at its wild parent's class when it is "indistinguishable from the wild animal," which on its words could reach an early-generation cat. FWC closes the question in its captive wildlife FAQ: "Savannah cats, Bengal cats, and Chausie cats are considered domesticated species and are not regulated by the Captive Wildlife Office."

## Counting Generations, and One State That Weighs the Cat

Six jurisdictions answer by counting, and they do not count the same way. Iowa is the one that wrote the breed into the statute. Iowa Code § 717F.1(5)(b)(2)(b) carves out:

> The offspring of a domestic cat and another member of the family felidae classified as a savannah with an ancestor classified as a serval which is a member of the species leptailurus serval. The savannah must be the fourth or later filial generation of offspring with the first filial generation being the offspring of a domestic cat and a serval, and each subsequent generation being the offspring of a domestic cat.

Meet that and the cat is outside the definition of a dangerous wild animal. Fall short and it is in the same class as the serval, which § 717F.3 bars anyone from owning. Note the last clause: every generation after the first must be the offspring of a domestic cat, so a kitten bred from two Savannahs does not meet those words on their face.

New York's Environmental Conservation Law § 11-0103(6)(e) treats the family and its hybrids as wild animals, then removes "hybrids of Felis catus that are registered by the American Cat Fanciers Association or the International Cat Association provided that such cats be without any wild felid parentage for a minimum of five generations." Five generations clear is an F6; an F5 is a wild animal, and § 11-0512 bars keeping one as a pet. Massachusetts and New Hampshire use three generations and attach the registry condition more tightly: M.G.L. c. 131, § 77A exempts only "a domesticated show or pet cat registered with a nationally or internationally recognized breeding association or registry which certifies the pedigree and registration of such cat to be without any wild felid parentage for a minimum of three generations," which MassWildlife reads as F4 and says for the Savannah by name, and New Hampshire's Fis 802.03(f) lifts the same sentence into an exemption from its whole possession chapter. In both, an unregistered cat fails at any generation and there is no private permit behind the rule. Vermont writes the number straight into its Unrestricted Wild Animal List, "All wild-domestic hybrid cats of F4 generation or greater," and an earlier generation needs a restricted animal permit that Regulation 881 leaves to the Commissioner's discretion.

Delaware reaches a generation line without meaning to. Del. Code tit. 3 § 7201 requires a permit before anyone possesses a "hybrid of a wild mammal," and regulation 903 defines that as a mammal with "one parent being a wild mammal not native to or generally found in Delaware and the other parent being a domestic mammal." An F1 is exactly that animal and needs the $25 Individual permit. An F2 has a Savannah parent rather than a serval, so on the regulation's own words it is not a hybrid of a wild mammal at all.

Maryland ignores ancestry. Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1)(iv) prohibits a

> hybrid of a member of the cat family and a domestic cat if the hybrid weighs over 30 pounds

The Savannah is the cat this clause was written for. A Bengal never gets near thirty pounds; a large male F1 Savannah can, and a cat that is lawful at twelve months may not be at three years. There is no generation test and no registry requirement. Subsection (e) preserves stricter county and municipal rules.

## Where No Generation Is Far Enough

Four places reach the hybrid and never let go of it. New York City Health Code § 161.01(b)(2) prohibits "All cats other than domesticated cats (Felis catus), including, but not limited to, ... serval, caracal, jaguarundi, margay and any hybrid or cross-breed offspring of a wild cat and domesticated or other cat." The state's five-generation carve-out works in Buffalo and does nothing in Brooklyn. Alaska clears *Felis catus* on its clean list at 5 AAC 92.029(b), then opens that list by excluding "a hybrid of a game animal and a species listed in this subsection," and subsection (c) bars any permit for such a hybrid "for use as a pet." Hawaii puts "cat, hybrid" on its prohibited list and defines the class as "all hybrid-crosses where one or both parents are prohibited or restricted," with the serval on the restricted list.

Connecticut is where the Savannah and the Bengal come apart most sharply. Regs. Conn. State Agencies § 26-55-6(a)(2) makes any wild animal, gamete "or hybrid" of a member of Felidae a Category One Wild Animal, "except Bengal cat pursuant to section 26-40a." The Bengal is carved out by name and the Savannah is not, so only zoos, nature centers, exhibitors and research facilities may hold one. The [serval legal guide](/blog/serval-legal-guide/) walks through how a family-wide clause like Connecticut's catches a thirty-pound cat with the same words it uses for a lion.

## The Permit States

Three states will issue a private keeper a permit, and the three permits have little in common. South Dakota's ARSD 12:68:18:03 requires a possession permit for "any nondomestic mammal, or any of its hybrids" in the listed families, Felidae first, so the Savannah needs the same permit as the serval: ten dollars an animal from the Animal Industry Board, renewed each year. North Dakota's NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(2)(b) makes category 2 the listed species "and their hybrids," including "nondomestic cats not listed in category 3," and the license must be in hand, with the premises approved, before the animal is acquired; the counter-reading is that NDCC 36-01-00.1 lists "cat" as a domestic animal. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 2(z) folds "hybrids between wildlife and domestic or domesticated animals" into warm-blooded wildlife, which needs a permit before import or possession, and Section 13(d) says only that one "may be authorized upon approval."

## Why the Middle of the Map Is Legal

In more than half the country no state rule reaches the Savannah, for one of three reasons. The commonest is a cat list drawn at the big cats with a hybrid clause tied to that list: Louisiana's six exotic cats and their hybrids, Kansas's "lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, cheetahs and mountain lions, or any hybrid thereof," South Carolina's genus *Panthera*, Washington's eight named species, Michigan's seven. Illinois and Missouri name eleven wild cats apiece with no hybrid clause at all. The serval is on none of those lists, so its hybrid is outside every one of them.

The second is a domestic cat definition that includes the hybrid. Virginia's 4VAC15-20-50 defines the domestic animal as "Domestic cat (Felis catus), including hybrids with wild felines," and Colorado's domestic animal list says the same words. California's § 671 restricts the whole family and then writes under it that "domestic cats and hybrids of domestic cats are not restricted," with no generation condition. New Mexico's importation list puts "Common house cat & hybrids" in Group I, and the District of Columbia excludes only "hybrids with ocelots or margays" from its domestic cat category.

The third is a definition of wildlife the Savannah falls out of. Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora, but R12-4-401 says "Offspring from a wildlife species and a domestic animal species are not considered wildlife." Utah's table takes in *Felis catus* "including breeds that are recognized by The International Cat Association," so the test is recognition rather than generation. Idaho lists the serval as a deleterious exotic animal and writes "tiger-hybrids" two entries above it; the serval entry carries no hybrid words.

<FunFact>
Indiana answers the Savannah through its parent, in one sentence. 312 IAC 9-11-7 lists the serval as a Class II wild animal needing a permit, then closes the list with: "A hybrid of any of these species with a domestic dog or domestic cat is exempt from permitting under this section." The serval needs a permit applied for within five days of possession, and its kitten with a house cat needs nothing at all.
</FunFact>

Nebraska is the one state in this group with a condition attached. Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-477(2) bans keeping "any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae" and excepts "the species Felis domesticus." Whether a Savannah is that species is the whole question: an F1 is a serval hybrid, a registered F5 sold as a domestic cat has the better claim, and nothing in the statute says where the line falls.

## Where the Law Will Not Say

Six states reach the serval and leave the hybrid genuinely open, so they are gray on the map rather than guessed at. Minnesota's § 346.155 excludes "cats recognized as a domestic breed, registered as a domestic breed, and shown as a domestic breed by a national or international multibreed cat registry association," which the Savannah is, then includes "any hybrid or cross between an animal listed in clause (1), (2), or (3) and a domestic animal and offspring from all subsequent generations." Both sentences are in force and the statute never says which governs. Rhode Island defines the domestic cat as "Felis catus excluding hybrids with wild felines," which plainly catches an F1, but defines a hybrid as "the cross breeding of a domestic and wild animal," which arguably misses an F2.

Maine lists all Felidae as Category 1, a tier open only to exhibitors and research, and applies its chapter to wildlife "that has been hybridized," yet a domestic cat is not wildlife there and the chapter writes a permit scheme for wolf hybrids and nothing for cat hybrids. Arkansas turns on whether a Savannah is "living in a state of nature and not domesticated," and no felid hybrid appears on any of its lists. Pennsylvania's § 137.1 reaches "a crossbreed or hybrid of the wild animals ... which are similar in appearance" and then excepts "species which are commonly called house cats." New Jersey bars keeping a nondomestic cat as a pet and excludes companion animals "as defined by the Fish and Game Council," a term the Council has not defined. In each of these, ask the agency in writing before buying, and keep the answer.

## The Takeaway

In most of the country the practical answer is yes: buy from a TICA-registered breeder who can document the generation, and keep the paper. That one document is what New York, Iowa, Vermont, Massachusetts and New Hampshire will ask for, and it is the document a casual seller will not have. In New York City, Alaska, Hawaii, Connecticut and Georgia there is no generation of this cat you can lawfully keep. In Texas, budget for the certificate and the insurance before the kitten. In South Dakota, North Dakota and Wyoming, get the permit, and in North Dakota get it first. In Maryland, know what the cat will weigh.

The [Bengal cat legal guide](/blog/bengal-cat-legal-guide/) covers the hybrid most of these laws were drafted around, and the two cats part company exactly where a state's rule reaches the serval and not the Asian leopard cat: Texas, Connecticut, Idaho and Hawaii. Everywhere a state wrote a generation line, the two cats get the same answer. Local ordinances are the realistic risk for the rest: California's own rule says cities and counties may prohibit species that need no state permit, and hybrid cats appear on municipal exotic animal lists far more often than on state ones.
