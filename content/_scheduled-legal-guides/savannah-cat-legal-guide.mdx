A Savannah cat is a serval crossed with a domestic cat, and the law in most states was written about one half of that sentence or the other. Where a statute reaches the serval, the question is whether its hybrid comes along. Where a statute reaches hybrids by generation, the question is how many generations the cat is from the serval. Only a handful of states have looked at the animal itself.

Those few give the sharpest answers on the map. Ohio frees the Savannah by name in the same line that restricts the serval. Georgia's wildlife agency names it as the hybrid it will not allow. Texas treats it as the serval itself, with a certificate of registration and $100,000 of liability insurance. The rest of the country answers by counting generations, by weighing the cat, or by a definition of "domestic cat" that was drafted with the Bengal in mind and happens to catch the Savannah too.

## What the Filial Numbers Mean

The serval, *Leptailurus serval*, is a long-legged African cat several times the size of a house cat. Cross one with a domestic cat and the kittens are F1. Breed an F1 to a domestic cat and you get F2, then F3, then F4. Each step halves the serval share on paper, and each step is the point where some state's law lets go of the animal.

The breed is recognized by The International Cat Association, and that recognition matters legally in Utah, where it is the whole test, and in New York, Massachusetts and New Hampshire, where registration is one of two conditions. Early generations are the large, serval-shaped cats that the laws below were written about, and a male F1 is the one hybrid cat on this map that can grow toward Maryland's thirty-pound line.

## Three States Looked at the Cat Itself

Ohio Rev. Code § 935.01(C)(7) brings "hybrids with domestic cats unless otherwise specified" into the dangerous wild animal definition, then lists cheetahs, lynxes, cougars and caracals before arriving at:

> (e) Servals, excluding hybrids with domestic cats commonly known as savannah cats.

That is the only sentence on the map that names this breed in order to free it. The serval needs a dangerous wild animal permit; the Savannah, at any filial generation, needs nothing from the state. Ohio's hybrid clause works the other way for every other cat under it, so a caracal cross is a dangerous wild animal and a serval cross is a house cat, one line apart. West Virginia's board copied the same structure into its own list and simply left the serval off, which reaches the same result by omission.

Georgia names the Savannah in order to bar it. The wild animal rule lists "Family Felidae (cats) - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, and the Department of Natural Resources states the hybrid position in its own guide to legal pets:

> Hybrids or crosses between any combination of domestic animals, wildlife, or regulated wild animals and all subsequent generations are regulated in Georgia and may not be held without a license. ... Most exotic cat hybrids, such as a savannah cat, are not a legal pet in Georgia.

The license is what turns that into a ban. O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues wild animal licenses "only to persons engaged in the wholesale or retail wild animal business or persons exhibiting wild animals to the public." A private keeper has nothing to apply for, at any generation.

Texas never names the Savannah and does not need to. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) lists nineteen animals, the serval at clause (J), and closes with "(T) any hybrid of an animal listed in this subdivision." So the same sentence that frees the Bengal, whose Asian leopard cat parent is not on the list, catches the Savannah, whose serval parent is. § 822.103(a) bars keeping a dangerous wild animal "for any purpose" without a certificate of registration, issued by the local animal control office or the county sheriff where there is none; § 822.107 requires liability insurance of at least $100,000 per occurrence; and § 822.116(b) lets any municipality or county prohibit the animal outright. No generation line is drawn, so on the statute's words an F5 is as much a hybrid of a serval as an F1.

Florida sits beside these three because its agency also answered by name, in the other direction. The serval is a Class II animal and 68A-6.002(1)(d) regulates a hybrid at its wild parent's class when it is "substantially similar in size, characteristics and behavior so as to be indistinguishable from the wild animal," which on its words could reach an early-generation cat. FWC closes the question in its captive wildlife FAQ: "Savannah cats, Bengal cats, and Chausie cats are considered domesticated species and are not regulated by the Captive Wildlife Office."

## The Generation States

Six jurisdictions answer by counting, and they do not count the same way.

Iowa is the one that wrote the breed into the statute. Iowa Code § 717F.1(5)(b)(2)(b) carves out:

> The offspring of a domestic cat and another member of the family felidae classified as a savannah with an ancestor classified as a serval which is a member of the species leptailurus serval. The savannah must be the fourth or later filial generation of offspring with the first filial generation being the offspring of a domestic cat and a serval, and each subsequent generation being the offspring of a domestic cat.

Meet that and the cat is outside the definition of a dangerous wild animal. Fall short and it is in the same class as the serval itself, which § 717F.3 bars anyone from owning, possessing, breeding or importing. Note the last clause: every generation after the first must be the offspring of a domestic cat. A kitten bred from two Savannahs does not meet those words on their face, and the statute raises that question without answering it.

New York's Environmental Conservation Law § 11-0103(6)(e) uses the family and its hybrids as the starting point, then removes "hybrids of Felis catus that are registered by the American Cat Fanciers Association or the International Cat Association provided that such cats be without any wild felid parentage for a minimum of five generations." Five generations without a serval among the parents, grandparents and the three generations above them is an F6. An F5 is a wild animal, and § 11-0512 bars keeping a wild animal as a pet with no personal permit behind it.

Massachusetts and New Hampshire use three generations instead of five and attach the registry condition more tightly. M.G.L. c. 131, § 77A prohibits possessing a wild felid hybrid outright and exempts only "a domesticated show or pet cat registered with a nationally or internationally recognized breeding association or registry which certifies the pedigree and registration of such cat to be without any wild felid parentage for a minimum of three generations." MassWildlife reads that as F4 and says it for the Savannah by name. New Hampshire's Fis 802.03(f) lifts the same sentence into an exemption from its whole importation and possession chapter. In both states an unregistered cat fails the test at any generation, and a cat inside the chapter is a serval hybrid with no private permit: New Hampshire's controlled table gives the serval permittee category 5, the exhibitor category, and nothing else.

Vermont writes the number straight into its Unrestricted Wild Animal List: "All wild-domestic hybrid cats of F4 generation or greater." An F1 to F3 is on no list, which the list's closing line makes restricted, and Vermont issues restricted animal permits only for bona fide scientific or educational purposes.

Delaware reaches a generation line without meaning to. Del. Code tit. 3 § 7201 requires a permit before anyone possesses a "hybrid of a wild mammal," and regulation 903 defines that term as a mammal with "one parent being a wild mammal not native to or generally found in Delaware and the other parent being a domestic mammal." An F1 Savannah is exactly that animal and needs the $25 Individual permit, since no cat is on the exempt list. An F2 has a Savannah parent rather than a serval, so on the regulation's own words it is not a hybrid of a wild mammal. That is the regulation's reading rather than a filial rule anyone wrote down, and the statute itself leaves the term undefined.

## Maryland Weighs It

Maryland ignores ancestry. Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1)(iv) prohibits a

> hybrid of a member of the cat family and a domestic cat if the hybrid weighs over 30 pounds

The Savannah is the cat this clause was written for. A Bengal never gets near thirty pounds; a large male F1 Savannah can, and a cat that is lawful at twelve months may not be at three years. There is no generation test and no registry requirement, so an F1 under the line is lawful and an F5 over it is not. Subsection (e) preserves stricter county and municipal rules, and the serval itself is caught by the clause above with no permit route.

## Where No Generation Is Far Enough

Four places reach the hybrid and never let go of it.

New York City Health Code § 161.01(b)(2) prohibits "All cats other than domesticated cats (Felis catus), including, but not limited to, ... serval, caracal, jaguarundi, margay and any hybrid or cross-breed offspring of a wild cat and domesticated or other cat." No filial number, no weight test, no registry exit. The state's five-generation carve-out works in Buffalo and does nothing in Brooklyn.

Alaska clears *Felis catus* on its clean list at 5 AAC 92.029(b), then opens that list by excluding "a hybrid of a game animal and a species listed in this subsection," and subsection (c) bars the department from issuing a permit for such a hybrid "for use as a pet." A serval is game under the state's definition, so a Savannah is the hybrid both subsections describe.

Hawaii puts "cat, hybrid" on its prohibited list and defines the class by the parents: "all hybrid-crosses where one or both parents are prohibited or restricted." The serval is on the restricted list, Part A, under its older name *Felis serval*. The named crosses on the prohibited list run through the lynx, ocelot, margay, puma and leopard cat and end in "etc.", and the parenthetical is the rule.

Connecticut is where the Savannah and the Bengal come apart most sharply. Regs. Conn. State Agencies § 26-55-6(a)(2) defines a Category One Wild Animal as any wild animal, gamete "or hybrid" of a member of Felidae, "except Bengal cat pursuant to section 26-40a." The Bengal is carved out by name and the Savannah is not, so it is a Category One animal, and subsection (b)(1) lets only municipal parks, zoos, nature centers, museums, exhibitors, laboratories and research facilities hold one. The [serval legal guide](/blog/serval-legal-guide/) walks through how a family-wide clause like Connecticut's catches a thirty-pound cat with the same words it uses for a lion.

## The Permit States

Three states will issue a private keeper a permit, and the three permits have little in common.

South Dakota's ARSD 12:68:18:03 requires a possession permit for "any nondomestic mammal, or any of its hybrids," in the listed families, and Felidae is first on the list. The hybrid words sit in the operative sentence, so the Savannah needs the same permit as the serval: ten dollars an animal from the Animal Industry Board, renewed by 1 January each year.

North Dakota writes hybrids into the category itself. NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(2)(b) makes category 2 the listed species "and their hybrids," and the list includes "nondomestic cats not listed in category 3." Category 3 stops at the big cats, so the serval is a category 2 cat and a Savannah is a category 2 hybrid. 48.1-09-01-03(1) requires the license in hand, with the premises approved, before the animal is acquired. The counter-reading is that NDCC 36-01-00.1 lists "cat" as a domestic animal, and a late-generation Savannah sold as one has the better claim to it.

Wyoming defines warm-blooded wildlife, in Chapter 10 § 2(z), to include "hybrids between wildlife and domestic or domesticated animals," and Section 3 requires a permit before importing or possessing any live warm-blooded wildlife. The serval is on neither the prohibited list nor the domestic list, which leaves Section 13(d): a permit "may be authorized upon approval," and may be authorized is not shall be issued.

## Why the Middle of the Map Is Legal

In more than half the country no state rule reaches the Savannah, and the reasons sort into three kinds.

The commonest is a cat list drawn at the big cats, with a hybrid clause tied to that list. Louisiana's six exotic cats and "hybrids resulting from cross breeding of the above listed exotic cats." Kansas's "lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, cheetahs and mountain lions, or any hybrid thereof." South Carolina's genus *Panthera*. Washington's Felidae entry limited by the word "only" to eight species. Michigan's seven named cats "including a hybrid cross with such a cat." Illinois and Missouri name eleven wild cats apiece with no hybrid clause at all. The serval is on none of those lists, so its hybrid is outside every one of them, and the reason the serval itself is unregulated in those states is the reason the Savannah is.

The second is a domestic cat definition that includes the hybrid. Virginia's 4VAC15-20-50 defines the domestic animal as "Domestic cat (Felis catus), including hybrids with wild felines," and the permit table that catches the serval governs nonnative animals only, which that section defines as excluding domestic species. Colorado's domestic animal list says the same words. California's § 671 restricts the whole family and then writes under it that "domestic cats and hybrids of domestic cats are not restricted," with no generation condition, while drawing an F1 line for wolf hybrids in the very next entry. New Mexico's importation list puts "Common house cat & hybrids" in Group I. The District of Columbia excludes only "hybrids with ocelots or margays" from its domestic cat category, which assumes every other hybrid is in it.

The third is a definition of wildlife the Savannah falls out of. Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora and makes hybrid wildlife restricted wherever one parent is, but R12-4-401 defines hybrid wildlife as "an offspring from two different wildlife species or genera" and adds: "Offspring from a wildlife species and a domestic animal species are not considered wildlife." A Savannah is the offspring of a serval and a domestic cat, so Arizona's live wildlife rules do not reach it. Utah's classification table takes in "Cat - Felis catus - including breeds that are recognized by The International Cat Association as Preliminary New, Advanced New, Non-championship, and Championship Breeds," and the Savannah is a TICA breed, so the test is recognition rather than generation. Idaho lists the serval as a deleterious exotic animal and writes "All tigers and tiger-hybrids" two entries above it; the serval entry carries no hybrid words, and cats are conventional pets under the state's animal care rules.

<FunFact>
Indiana answers the Savannah through its parent, in one sentence. 312 IAC 9-11-7 lists the serval as a Class II wild animal needing a permit, then closes the list with: "A hybrid of any of these species with a domestic dog or domestic cat is exempt from permitting under this section." No generation, no weight, no registry. The serval needs a permit applied for within five days of possession, and its kitten with a house cat needs nothing at all.
</FunFact>

Nebraska is the one state in this group with a condition attached. Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-477(2) bans keeping "any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae" with no permit behind it, and excepts "the species Felis domesticus." Whether a Savannah is that species is the whole question. An F1 is a serval hybrid; a registered F5 sold as a domestic cat has the better claim; and nothing in the statute or in the regulations says where the line falls.

## Where the Law Will Not Say

Six states reach the serval and leave the hybrid genuinely open. They are rendered gray on the map rather than guessed at.

Minnesota's § 346.155 defines a regulated animal as all members of Felidae, naming servals, "but not including domestic cats or cats recognized as a domestic breed, registered as a domestic breed, and shown as a domestic breed by a national or international multibreed cat registry association." The Savannah is all three. The same definition then closes by including "any hybrid or cross between an animal listed in clause (1), (2), or (3) and a domestic animal and offspring from all subsequent generations." Both sentences are in force, the statute never says which governs, and enforcement sits with local animal control. If the hybrid sentence wins, possession has been closed to new animals since 1 January 2005.

Rhode Island defines the domestic cat as "Felis catus excluding hybrids with wild felines" and bars any permit to a private person for a Carnivora hybrid. An F1 is plainly caught. But the regulation defines a hybrid as the result of "the cross breeding of a domestic and wild animal," and an F2 is the offspring of a Savannah and a domestic cat, which on that definition is arguably no hybrid at all.

Maine lists "All Species in Family Felidae (Cats)" as Category 1 restricted, a tier open only to exhibitors, rehabilitators and research facilities, and its chapter applies to wildlife "that has been hybridized." Against that, a domestic cat is not wildlife in Maine, and the chapter writes a whole permit scheme for wolf hybrids and nothing for cat hybrids. Arkansas turns on whether a Savannah is wildlife at all, since "wild" means "living in a state of nature and not domesticated," and no felid hybrid appears on any of its three addenda, whose closing clause makes an unlisted species prohibited until evaluated. Pennsylvania's § 137.1 reaches "a crossbreed or hybrid of the wild animals ... which are similar in appearance" and lists every felid species, then excepts "species which are commonly called house cats." A Savannah is bred as a house cat, and an F1 is also the cat most likely to look like a serval. New Jersey lists "Felidae--Nondomestic cats" as potentially dangerous and bars keeping one as a pet, and its statute excludes "domesticated companion animals ... as defined by the Fish and Game Council," a term the Council has not defined. In each of these, ask the agency in writing before buying, and keep the answer.

## The Takeaway

If you want a Savannah, the practical answer in most of the country is yes, buy from a TICA-registered breeder who can document the generation, and keep the paper. That one document is what New York, Iowa, Vermont, Massachusetts and New Hampshire will ask for, and it is the document a casual seller will not have.

If you live in New York City, Alaska, Hawaii, Connecticut or Georgia, there is no generation of this cat you can lawfully keep. In Texas, budget for the certificate and the insurance before the kitten. In South Dakota, North Dakota and Wyoming, get the permit, and in North Dakota get it first. In Maryland, know what the cat will weigh. In Minnesota, Rhode Island, Maine, Arkansas, Pennsylvania and New Jersey, get an answer in writing before you buy.

The [Bengal cat legal guide](/blog/bengal-cat-legal-guide/) covers the hybrid most of these laws were drafted around, and the two cats part company exactly where a state's rule reaches the serval and not the Asian leopard cat: Texas, Connecticut, Idaho and Hawaii. Everywhere a state wrote a generation line, the two cats get the same answer. Local ordinances are the realistic risk for the rest: California's own rule says cities and counties may prohibit species that need no state permit, and hybrid cats appear on municipal exotic animal lists far more often than on state ones.
