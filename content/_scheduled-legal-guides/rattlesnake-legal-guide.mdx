A rattlesnake is answered twice by most state codes, and the two answers rarely agree. The first is the venomous-reptile clause, which names the family Viperidae or "all venomous species" and treats a timber rattlesnake exactly like a cobra. The second is the native wildlife rule, which exists because rattlesnakes live wild in nearly every state, and it reaches the same snake as game, as nongame, as a protected animal or as a nuisance, with its own seasons and bag limits. The [venomous snake laws by state](/blog/venomous-snake-laws-by-state/) page carries the class rule for every jurisdiction, with the cobra as its example. This page is about the second answer, which is where the rattlesnake's row stops looking like the cobra's.

The result is the most varied row on the map: 17 jurisdictions ban every rattlesnake, 14 issue a permit, 14 allow one on a condition, Arizona restricts the family, Montana's rule will not decide its own species, and five states have no venomous snake rule at all. The fourteen conditional cells are the native-take rules at work. Texas lets a keeper hold twenty-five western diamondbacks with no permit and sends a rattlesnake from elsewhere to a permit. California allows two of a native species under its fishing regulations and bars every nonnative pit viper. Kentucky allows five, Kansas two, Nebraska ten of its prairie rattlesnake and none of its timber rattlesnake. The same genus of snake can be a hunting-license animal, a sport-take animal, a nongame species and a flat prohibition inside one state's code, depending only on which side of the border it hatched.

## Where the Native Rule Is the Whole Answer

California and Texas are the two states where a keeper's answer depends entirely on the snake's origin, and the line is drawn in the fishing and nongame regulations rather than in any exotic animal law.

California's restricted species rule, 14 CCR § 671(c)(7)(E), restricts "Family Crotalidae-Pitvipers: All nonnative species (D)", so a rattlesnake from Arizona or Mexico is barred with no pet route. The seven species native to California are sport-take animals under § 5.60, and the rule is plain about what that means:

> No sportfishing license is required for the sport take of any rattlesnake, but bag and possession limits do apply.

The western diamondback, Mojave, western, speckled and Panamint rattlesnakes and the sidewinder carry the default limit of two, a daily bag and possession limit, and the red diamond rattlesnake's limit is zero. A western diamondback inside that limit is lawful to hold. A western diamondback bought from a breeder across the state line is not, because its nonnative origin puts it back under § 671.

Texas draws the same line with bigger numbers and no license at all. Five native rattlesnakes sit on the nongame list at 31 TAC 65.331(d):

> Western diamondback rattlesnake (Crotalus atrox) Rock rattlesnake (Crotalus lepidus) Blacktail rattlesnake (Crotalus molossus) Mojave rattlesnake (Crotalus scutulatus) Prairie rattlesnake (Crotalus viridis)

A person may hold up to 25 of each without a permit, captive-bred animals included. A rattlesnake that is not native to Texas is a nonindigenous venomous snake, and Parks and Wildlife Code § 43.851 sends it to the controlled exotic snake permit, which is the cobra's route and is available to a private keeper for recreational purposes. Texas is the one state where the same genus is free in one species and permitted in the next, with the line drawn by the state border rather than by the venom.

Kentucky, Kansas and Nebraska write the same split as a possession limit. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 allows "Five (5) or fewer individuals of each species of native reptile or amphibian" for personal use without a permit, which covers the timber rattlesnake, while 2:082 bars every exotic viper as inherently dangerous. Kansas's K.A.R. 115-20-2 allows two of a native species, five reptiles in all, and excludes the timber rattlesnake by listing it as a species in need of conservation; a nonnative rattlesnake is a dangerous regulated animal under K.S.A. 32-1301(d)(3), closed to new keepers since 2006. Nebraska's reptile chapter reaches only its own species, allows ten prairie rattlesnakes, bars the timber rattlesnake outright, and says nothing about a rattlesnake from anywhere else. Oklahoma, Mississippi and South Carolina are the same shape with a hunting license or a no-sale rule in place of a number: Oklahoma opens its three rattlesnakes from 1 March to 30 June, Mississippi caps a keeper at four of a species with a "DANGER" label on the cage, and South Carolina bars buying, selling or re-homing a native rattlesnake so that catching one yourself is the only native route.

## Where the Native Is Carved Out and Nothing Replaces It

Nevada, Washington, Alabama and Montana all carve their own rattlesnakes out of the viper ban, and what is left behind ranges from a clear answer to no answer at all.

Nevada's prohibited list reads "All species in the family Viperidae, except species indigenous to this State", and under NAC 503.080(3) any reptile not classified as protected, sensitive, threatened or endangered is unprotected, so a native Nevada rattlesnake needs no license to keep. Washington's RCW 16.30.010 names "rattlesnakes" in its viper entry, then 16.30.020(1)(k) exempts native wildlife subject to Title 77, which asks only that a captive animal be lawfully acquired, with a department permit needed to take one from the wild. That is a thin reading for a venomous snake, but it is the one the statute writes. Alabama's 220-2-.26(1) bars "Any species of venomous reptile which has never naturally existed in the wild in Alabama", so a native rattlesnake is outside the sentence and outside the restricted rules, with one exception: 220-2-.92(3) bars possessing a live eastern diamondback without the Commissioner's permit.

Montana carves out and stops. ARM 12.6.2215(6) classifies as prohibited:

> (m) Pit vipers and true vipers (all species in family Viperidae except species indigenous to Montana);

A prairie rattlesnake is indigenous to Montana, so it is outside the exotic wildlife scheme altogether, and no Montana rule read for the map says whether or how a native rattlesnake may be kept. The map records that cell as unclear rather than as legal, because a carve-out from a ban is not a permission.

Georgia is the opposite problem: a clear ban with a hint beside it. The wild animal rule puts "Family Viperidae (vipers and pit vipers) - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, which R. 391-4-8-.04 licenses only to the wild animal trade and exhibitors, so a rattlesnake from outside the state has no pet route. The DNR's own guide to legal pets then says that Georgia law "allows the taking of certain native species" including venomous snakes "because of their status as a nuisance", and the rule incorporates a statutory definition of wild animal that was not read for the map. Whether a timber, eastern diamondback or pygmy rattlesnake caught in Georgia may be held under that sentence is a question for the Special Permit Unit. The map records Georgia as banned, which is what the rule says, and leaves the hint where it is.

<FunFact>
Arizona has more rattlesnake species than any other state and a rule that names them only to restrict them. R12-4-406 lists "The family Viperidae. Common names include: pit and true vipers, including rattlesnakes" as restricted live wildlife, held only under a special license, with no exemption that reads as a pet route. The hunting regulations are a separate world: live take of native reptiles under a hunting license runs through R12-4-404, R12-4-405 and Commission Order 43, none of which was read for the map. So the state with the most rattlesnakes is recorded as restricted rather than conditional, because the exotic rule was read and the take rule was not, and a keeper in Tucson should read the second before trusting the first.

## The Permits That Reach a Native

Florida's venomous reptile permit is the most demanding on the map and it reaches every rattlesnake, because the no-permit list at 68A-6.003(14)(a) clears only nonvenomous reptiles. 68A-6.017 requires that applicants "shall be at least 18 years of age", that they "demonstrate no less than one (1) year of substantial practical experience (to consist of no less than 1,000 hours) in the care, feeding, handling and husbandry of the species or other species within the same biological family" backed by two references, or pass the Commission's written examination, and that the facility be "inspected and approved by Commission personnel prior to the issuance of the permit". An eastern diamondback caught in the Panhandle and a Mojave rattlesnake bought from Arizona take the same permit.

Utah's certificate of registration runs the other way: it exists only for the native. R657-53-12 requires one before "collecting, possessing, importing, or propagating any reptile, whether wild caught or captive-bred", that is native to Utah, venomous and medically significant, with the applicant 18, experienced, backed by local government verification that the activity fits its ordinances, and near medical treatment. A rattlesnake not native to Utah is a prohibited species under R657-53-9 with a possession limit of zero. Arkansas requires its Venomous Reptile Permit "whether native or non-native to Arkansas", lists "Rattlesnakes (Genus Crotalus)" on the permit list, and then frees a native rattlesnake caught by hand as one of six native pets under Code 09.14, still caged to the permit's rules. Idaho's § 36-201 lists "Rattlesnake" as predatory wildlife, so one taken under a hunting or trapping license needs nothing further, while a rattlesnake from elsewhere is venomous, outside the conventional pets definition, and needs a captive possession license with locked, double-walled enclosures and safety glass.

Missouri, Rhode Island and West Virginia issue a permit for the exotic and close the door on the native. Missouri's § 578.023 makes it an offense to keep "any deadly, dangerous, or poisonous reptile" without registering it with county law enforcement, which is the whole state rule for a western diamondback; the timber rattlesnake is Class II wildlife under 3 CSR 10-9.240, held only by a Class II breeder. Rhode Island's exotic animal possession permit is open to private persons for "All venomous snakes", and § 3.7(C) restricts the native timber rattlesnake to the Department, researchers and zoos. West Virginia reaches a rattlesnake from outside the state only as wildlife brought in without an importation permit, and § 58-73-1.1 bars taking or possessing any native reptile the rule does not list, which is every snake. Indiana's Class III permit is a pet permit for a venomous reptile, and the timber rattlesnake sits on the state endangered list on top of it.

## The Class Rule With No Native Wrinkle

Fourteen jurisdictions answer the rattlesnake exactly as they answer the cobra, either because they wrote no native rule or because the one they wrote changes nothing. Hawaii prohibits the whole snake suborder. The District of Columbia's closed list admits non-venomous snakes only. New York City prohibits "all front-fanged venomous snakes, even if devenomized", naming all Viperidae. Maine's Category 1 takes the whole viper family and issues permits only to exhibitors, rehabilitators, laboratories and research facilities. Tennessee's Class I covers "Snakes--All venomous species", which reaches its native rattlesnakes as well as any from elsewhere. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "All venomous reptiles" for the exhibitor category only, with the state's own rattlesnake not among the indigenous reptiles listed for individuals. Alaska's clean list clears only "Any nonvenomous reptile", and the department may not permit game as a pet.

Among the permit states, Ohio's restricted snake possession permit is open to a new keeper for any viper, Louisiana's Restricted Snake Permit covers "All Venomous Snakes" with 500 hours of experience and a locked room, North Dakota's category 3 license and Wyoming's Chapter 69 permit both want a veterinary certificate before import, Massachusetts has a permit on paper that the agency says is not for a pet, and Vermont leaves its permit to the Commissioner's discretion. North Carolina's § 14-417 asks the same locked, labeled enclosure of a timber rattlesnake as of a cobra, and Minnesota's breeder-sourced pet allowance treats every snake alike. Illinois is the one ban state that mentions rattlesnakes to exclude them further: its educational special use permit allows only US-native venomous species and then names "the eastern and western diamondbacks, the Mojave, the southern Pacific" and the canebrake as species even a permittee may not hold.

The five states with no venomous snake rule regulate only their own. Pennsylvania's timber rattlesnake needs a Venomous Snake Permit, carries a season and an annual limit of one snake at least 42 inches long, and may not be held two at a time, while nothing in the Commonwealth reaches a rattlesnake from outside it. Virginia's timber rattlesnake is limited to one per physical address and its coastal plain canebrake population may not be held in any number. Wisconsin's timber rattlesnake and Michigan's eastern massasauga are protected animals that may not be taken or possessed except as authorized. South Dakota has no reptile rule at all. The pattern is the opposite of the exotic-animal pattern on most of the map: in these five the native is the only snake the law has an opinion about.

## The Lookalike Question

Every venomous-snake statute on this map is written around venom, and the one snake that confuses keepers is the one that mimics it. The hognose spreads a hood, hisses and strikes with its mouth shut, and a seller will sometimes call it a "puff adder". Under the law it is a rear-fanged colubrid with a weak venom, and the states that considered the question said so: Alabama's venomous ban excepts "hognose snakes (genus Heterodon)" by name, and Kentucky's does the same. Everywhere else the rule names Elapidae and Viperidae, or front-fanged snakes, and the hognose is in neither family. The [hognose snake legal guide](/blog/hognose-snake-legal-guide/) walks a row that looks nothing like this one: twenty-seven jurisdictions with no rule, six bans that are mostly native-take rules, and a question that is never the venom.

## The Takeaway

Read the venomous clause first, because it decides every rattlesnake that was not born in the state. Then read the native rule, because that is where the answer changes. A western diamondback is a two-animal sport-take species in California, a twenty-five-animal nongame species in Texas, a hunting-license animal in Oklahoma and Mississippi, a ten-animal take species in Nebraska, a Class II breeder-only animal in Missouri, a Venomous Reptile Permit animal in Arkansas, and a flat prohibition in Illinois, which names it. Montana will not say what happens to its own prairie rattlesnake, Georgia's DNR hints at a native exception its rule does not write, and Arizona's take rules were never read. Our [interactive map of rattlesnake laws](/exotic-pet-laws/rattlesnake/) shades every jurisdiction and quotes the sentence each answer comes from, and for this animal the sentence is as likely to be in the hunting regulations as in the exotic animal law.
