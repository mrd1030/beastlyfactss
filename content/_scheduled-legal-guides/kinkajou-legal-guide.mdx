A kinkajou is a rainforest procyonid with a prehensile tail, a tongue built for nectar, and almost no statute of its own. Of the 52 jurisdictions, only a handful name *Potos flavus*: Hawaii, Maine, New Hampshire, New Mexico, New Jersey, Oregon, Idaho, Arkansas and New York City. Everywhere else the law reaches the kinkajou, or fails to, through a word written about a different animal. Where a clause takes "the family Procyonidae" or "the order Carnivora", the kinkajou is caught with the raccoon. Where the clause says "raccoon", or "Genus Procyon", or "species of raccoon", the kinkajou walks past it, because it is a procyonid and not a raccoon.

That is why its map looks nothing like the raccoon's. The raccoon is a native rabies reservoir and a furbearer, and 26 jurisdictions ban it on those grounds. The kinkajou is native to nowhere north of Mexico, so no rabies-vector statute names it and no furbearer chapter licenses it. Counted on the text, a pet kinkajou is legal with no state permit in 23 states, banned in 14 jurisdictions, a permit animal in 11, restricted in two, and undecided in two. It is the most permissive row among the carnivores on this map, and the reason is an accident of drafting: the lists were written about raccoons.

## Where the Family Is the Rule

The bans that reach the kinkajou cleanly are the ones that never bothered to name a species. California's 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(K)(4) reads "Family Procyonidae-All species-(D)", the D marking the family as detrimental, and the order heading above it lists kinkajous by name among the carnivores the section is about. The four exceptions written under the family line are the two pandas, the ringtail and the cacomistle, and even those are only moved to the welfare category. § 671 reaches any live animal of a listed family whatever its origin, and no California permit type covers a pet. Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(3)(B)(xxi) makes "a member within the family Procyonidae (including, but not limited to, common raccoon)" a Category Two animal, and subsection (b)(3) allows a Category Two animal only to a treating veterinarian, a municipal park, a zoo, a nature center, a museum, an exhibitor or a research facility. New York City Health Code § 161.01(b)(5) prohibits "All Procyonidae: All raccoon (eastern, desert, ring-tailed cat), kinkajou, cacomistle, cat-bear, panda and coatimundi", which is the one list in the country that names every procyonid a keeper might ask about.

Two states go a taxonomic level higher and take the order. Georgia's R. 391-4-8-.05(1)(a)3 requires a license for "Order Carnivora (weasels, ferrets, cats, bears, wolves, etc.) - All species", with a single exception written for the European ferret, and O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues those licenses only to the wholesale and retail wild animal trade and to public exhibitors. Rhode Island's § 3.7(A) prohibits every carnivore "for which there is no USDA conditionally licensed or unconditionally licensed rabies vaccine" to anyone but bona fide researchers, licensed exhibitors and AZA facilities, and § 3.9(B) adds that no permit shall be granted to a private person for such an animal. There is no licensed rabies vaccine for a kinkajou, so the door is as closed as it is for the fennec fox and the serval.

Four more name the animal to put it on the wrong list. Hawaii's § 4-71-6.5 Part A carries "Potos flavus kinkajou" under Procyonidae, the research and exhibition tier. Maine's § 7.18(2)(F) puts it in Category 1, where the permit goes to exhibitors, rehabilitators and laboratories, while the coati sits in Category 2, the tier a keeper can apply to. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 gives "Kinkajou Potos flavus" permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitor category, with no category 1 for an individual. New Mexico's 2023 Director's Species Importation List files "Procyonidae | Potos | Kinkajou" in Group IV, whose importation is prohibited for the general public.

## Where the Word Raccoon Stops

The other half of the map is the [raccoon's](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/) clause failing to reach its cousin, and it fails in four different ways.

The first is a genus line. New York's 6 NYCRR § 180.1(b)(5) lists "Raccoons in the Genus Procyon" among dangerous animals, and *Potos* is a different genus. Nothing else on the six-clause list names a procyonid, the kinkajou is not native so it is not small game, and the state has no exotic pet permit to fall back on, so the animal is simply unregulated in New York State while New York City bans it by name. The second is a furbearer definition. Texas's Parks and Wildlife Code § 71.001(1) defines a fur-bearing animal as "wild beaver, otter, mink, ring-tailed cat, badger, skunk, raccoon, muskrat, opossum, fox, or nutria", and the kinkajou is neither of the two procyonids on it, so the $50.75 propagation license that catches a pet raccoon never applies. Ohio's R.C. 1531.01(W) does the same work with "minks, weasels, raccoons, skunks, opossums, muskrats, fox, beavers, badgers, otters, coyotes, and bobcats", and because the kinkajou is not on the § 935.01 dangerous wild animal list either, Ohio has no rule for it at all. The third is a prohibited list drawn by common name. Alabama's r. 220-2-.26 bars bringing in "species of raccoon", and the kinkajou is not one. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1)(i) bars "fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear", four words, and the native wildlife permit in Natural Resources § 10-902 attaches only to animals native to Maryland.

The fourth is the most interesting, because the drafter started to take the family and then changed their mind. Pennsylvania's 58 Pa. Code § 137.1(a)(4) reads:

> (4) In the family Procyonidae. All species of raccoons.

The line names the family and then limits itself to raccoons, so by its words it does not reach the kinkajou. What arguably does is § 137.1(a)(11), which bars possessing "Game or wildlife held in captivity or captive bred in another state or nation", read with the Game Code's definition of a wild animal as every mammal but livestock. That is the agency reading the fennec fox cell turns on, and nothing in Pennsylvania names the kinkajou, so the map records it unclear rather than banned. A keeper there should ask the Game Commission in writing about the exotic wildlife possession permit under Chapter 147 before buying, and keep the answer.

<FunFact>
Nebraska and Oklahoma both draw the line between the kinkajou and the coati, and they draw it in opposite directions from the same fact. Both states' lists name the coatimundi and not the kinkajou. In Oklahoma, the list is an exemption list, so the coati needs no license and the kinkajou does. In Nebraska, the list is a closed import list, so the coati may be brought in and the kinkajou may not. One missing word, a permit in one state and a ban in the other.

## The Coati Problem

That pair deserves its own paragraph, because the [coati](/blog/coati-legal-guide/) is the kinkajou's closest legal relative and the two are routinely sold by the same breeders. Oklahoma's OAC 800:25-25-3(a) lists the wildlife species that "are exempt from import and export permits, commercial wildlife breeders licenses, noncommercial wildlife breeders licenses and commercial hunting area license requirements", and item (36) is "Coatimundi". The kinkajou is not on the list, so the default applies: an import permit to bring it in and a noncommercial wildlife breeder's license to keep it, the capybara's position. It is paperwork, not a ban. Nebraska's 166 NAC 8 § 001.08A opens "Importation: the following wildlife may be imported", and the list that follows names "Coatimundi, Nasua spp." between the chinchilla and the degu, with no kinkajou anywhere on it. Because the kinkajou is not native, no Nebraska source can supply one, and § 001.08A1 lets an unlisted animal in only to an accredited or public zoo, a licensed exhibit or a circus. So the same omission that costs an Oklahoma keeper a license costs a Nebraska keeper the animal.

## The Permit States

Eleven jurisdictions issue a permit, and nine of them issue it to a private keeper. New Jersey is the one that names the animal. N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.3(a)2 lists the exotic mammals requiring a permit for possession, and the second entry reads:

> ii. Kinkajou--Potos flavus;

That puts it in the hobby permit category under 4.6(a)1, subject to the general possession criteria at 4.7. New Jersey treats nondomestic dogs, bears and cats as potentially dangerous and keeps them out of the hobby category; procyonids are not on that list, so the kinkajou gets the permit a fox cannot. Florida reaches the same answer by default. The kinkajou is in neither Class I nor Class II under 68A-6.002, which makes it Class III, "all other wildlife not listed herein", and 68A-6.003(8) says "Persons possessing Class III wildlife as personal use wildlife shall obtain a no-cost permit from the Executive Director." Delaware's regulation 903-6.2 requires a $25 Individual permit for any nonnative wild mammal not on the exempt list. Nevada's NRS 503.597 requires the Department's written consent to bring any wildlife into the state, and the kinkajou is on neither Nevada list, so the consent is the permit. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 13(d) says a permit "may be authorized upon approval for other warm-blooded wildlife", which is discretionary rather than owed.

Arkansas and Idaho name it. Arkansas's Code Addendum R1.02 is the permit list, and it carries "Kinkajou (Potos flavus)" between the impala and the lesser spot-nosed guenon: permits "for breeding, sale, and importation may be issued for the following species", so a kinkajou may be kept if it came from a Commission-permitted wildlife breeder or in on a Wildlife Importation Permit, with acquisition records, a veterinary disease certificate and the sexes housed apart unless neutered. Idaho's IDAPA 02.04.27 files it at § 409:

> 409. DELETERIOUS EXOTIC ANIMALS - MAMMALS: PROCYONIDAE. 01. Coatimundi. 02. Kinkajou.

Section 100.01 bars possessing a listed animal without a possession permit from the Administrator, and only the large felids and most primates carry the extra requirement of an AZA-accredited facility, so a route exists. Virginia takes the family rather than the species: 4VAC15-30-40's table reads "Procyonidae, All Species, Raccoons and Relatives", so a special permit from the department is required to import, possess or sell one, the same clause that catches the fennec fox.

Massachusetts and Vermont round out the eleven, and the kinkajou is on neither state's exemption list. Massachusetts's permit exists on paper only, since MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard.

## The Closed Lists

Five jurisdictions ban it without a family clause, by running a list the kinkajou is not on. Colorado's regulated wildlife page says any species not listed as domestic or unregulated in Chapter W-11, or in Appendix A or B of Chapter W-07, "is regulated wildlife and a CPW license is required", and "CPW will not issue a license for private possession (eg pet) of regulated wildlife". *Potos flavus* is on none of Colorado's lists, and W-11 says noncommercial pet possession of regulated mammals has been prohibited since 1983. Montana's § 87-5-705(1) says a person may not import, possess or sell any exotic wildlife unless "allowed by law or commission rule", and the kinkajou appears on none of the noncontrolled, controlled or prohibited lists, which on a white list is a no. Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars the department from issuing a permit for any game animal "for use as a pet", no procyonid is on the clean list at (b), and the only route is a petition to the Board of Game. The District of Columbia's § 8-1808(j) bars every "living member of the animal kingdom, including those born or raised in captivity, except the following", and none of its seven categories fits a kinkajou. Nebraska, covered above, bans by omission from an import list.

Arizona and Utah are restricted rather than banned. Arizona's R12-4-406(F) restricts "All species of the order Carnivora", so a special license is needed and none of the exemptions is a pet route. Utah's Table 3b-1 does not list the kinkajou, and its header says "all species not listed will require an assessment before collection, importation, or possession will be authorized", which is an application with no published answer. North Dakota is the second unclear cell: the kinkajou is in none of the three nontraditional livestock categories, and NDAC 48.1-09-01-02(6) sends an animal in no category to the Board of Animal Health "for determination of importation requirements and licensure requirements prior to importation", so the answer is whatever the board decides.

## The Twenty-Three

Oregon is the one state that names the kinkajou to clear it. OAR 635-056-0060(1)(b)(F) lists "Kinkajou -- Procyonidae -- Potos flavus" among noncontrolled species, which the Department says may be imported, possessed, sold and transported without a permit, and ORS 609.305, the exotic animal statute, does not reach procyonids. Oregon cities and counties may still prohibit it by ordinance.

The other 22 are silence. In each of them the raccoon's clause stops at the raccoon, the dangerous animal list names cats, bears, wolves and primates and no procyonid, and the game and furbearer definitions reach natives only. South Dakota's possession permit list covers five carnivore families, Felidae, Canidae, Ursidae, Mustelidae and Hyaenidae, and Procyonidae is not among them. Tennessee's Class III "requires no permits except those required by the department of agriculture, and includes all species not listed in other classes". Missouri's 3 CSR 10-9.110(2) clears mammals not native to Missouri to be "bought, sold, possessed, transported, and exhibited without permit". Kentucky allows it but requires a transportation permit to bring it across the line; Wisconsin and Washington require an agriculture import document for an animal coming from out of state.

Silence at the state level is not silence everywhere. Texas, Ohio, Illinois, Michigan, Kentucky, Washington, Maryland, West Virginia, Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi and Louisiana all say in terms that a city or county may prohibit what the state does not, and Illinois is a home rule state where the municipal ordinance usually decides.

## The Takeaway

The kinkajou is legal in more states than any other carnivore on this map, and the reason is not that legislatures weighed it and approved. It is that the lists were written about the raccoon, and most of them say so. Where a clause takes the family or the order, in California, Connecticut, Georgia, Rhode Island, Virginia, Arizona and New York City, the kinkajou is caught. Where it says raccoon, in New York, Texas, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Alabama and Maryland, the kinkajou is not. Nine states issue a keeper's permit, Virginia a tenth by family, one keeps a permit on paper that no pet will get, and two more cannot decide.
