Ask where a pet skunk is legal and the same seventeen states come back from every site that sells one, and the list has been copied so many times that nobody checks it. The statutes do not agree with it. Alabama appears on most versions of it and bars "species of skunk" in regulation. Several states on it issue a permit that exists for exhibitors and never for a pet. And every version carries the same unstated assumption, that a descented skunk from a breeder is a different animal from the one under the porch. No statute in the country says so. Read across all 52 jurisdictions, the striped skunk gets 29 bans, 14 permits, two states that allow it on a condition, two that restrict it, four whose text points both ways, and one clean yes.

The skunk's map looks like the raccoon's for the same reason: it is a rabies reservoir and a furbearer, so the rules that catch it were written for the wild animal. Kentucky files it under "Rabies Vector Species", Maryland bans it in a four-word list, Montana's prohibition names it first. What the skunk has that the raccoon does not is a taxonomy problem. Skunks were classed in the weasel family, Mustelidae, when most of these rules were written, and were moved into their own family, Mephitidae, afterward. Pennsylvania, Hawaii, New York City and Maine still file the skunk under the mustelids, and in three of those the rule names the skunk by common name so the family does not matter. In South Dakota the family is the whole question.

## Three States That Wrote the Skunk's Name Into a Ban

Nebraska did it in the broadest sentence on the map. Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-477(2):

> Except as provided in subsection (3) of this section, no person shall keep in captivity in this state any wolf, any skunk, or any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae.

The skunk is the one small animal in a sentence otherwise about wolves, cats and bears, and the exceptions in subsection (3) are public and accredited zoos and animals held on 1 March 1986. There is no permit behind it. North Dakota Century Code § 36-01-08.4 reads "A person may not keep a skunk or raccoon in captivity", with a USDA-licensed zoo as the only exception, and the Board of Animal Health's rule adds that skunks may not be imported into the state for any purpose. Missouri closes every door in turn. 3 CSR 10-9.223(3):

> Live skunks may not be imported, bought, sold, transported, given away, or otherwise disposed of in this state.

The Wildlife Hobby Permit, which lets a Missourian hold one game mammal bought from a Missouri breeder, excludes skunks by name, and as a furbearer the skunk is outside the take-five rule for natives. A skunk cannot be brought in, bought, sold or given away in Missouri, which leaves nothing. Tennessee reads the same way, and it is easy to miss, because the five-class exotic animal scheme would file a native furbearer in Class II on a $10 permit. Tenn. Code § 70-4-208(a), a one-section statute in the hunting and trapping part, says it is unlawful "to import, possess, or cause to be imported into this state any type of live skunk, or to sell, barter, exchange or otherwise transfer any live skunk", with bona fide zoological parks and research institutions the only exemption and a rehabilitation permit holder allowed to take skunks from the wild for release only. A specific ban on the animal controls over the general class, and "any type" reaches the descented one.

## The Rabies List

Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 § 6(1) says a person shall not import, transport into Kentucky, or possess "(j) Rabies Vector Species", a list that runs through the coyote, the gray fox, the raccoon and the red fox to "5. Spotted skunk (Spilogale putorius); 6. Striped skunk (Mephitis mephitis); or 7. Any hybrid of rabies vector species." The same regulation defines native wildlife "regardless of ... being captive-bred or taken from the wild", so a descented skunk from a breeder in Indiana stands where a wild one does. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621(b)(1) bars a live "fox, skunk, raccoon, or bear", with exemptions for zoos, sanctuaries, research and exhibitors and none for a keeper. Washington's rabies rule, WAC 246-100-197(5)(a), prohibits all persons from "acquiring, selling, bartering, exchanging, giving, purchasing, distributing, or trapping to retain any bat, skunk, fox, raccoon, or coyote" except a zoo, exhibitor, research facility or rehabilitator. Montana's MCA § 50-23-101(4) defines a wild animal for its rabies part as "a skunk, fox, raccoon, or bat", the skunk first, and § 50-23-102 bars possessing one outside a fur-bearing enterprise, a no-contact zoo exhibit or research. Idaho Code § 25-236 bars possessing "any fox, skunk or raccoon" outside fur farms and permitted parks, zoos, museums and educational institutions.

The reason these rules are absolute is the same one that decides the [raccoon](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/): there is no USDA-licensed rabies vaccine for the skunk, so no health department can treat one as vaccinated, and a bite ends with the animal euthanized and tested. New Jersey, which does issue a pet permit, builds its import rule on exactly that. The Division's policy on captive rabies vector species bars importing a skunk from any state "reported as having a variant of rabies other than the raccoon variant", and gives its own example: "the skunk variant is found in Texas. Therefore, no one can import a RVS from Texas, even if the RVS originates from a captive breeder."

## Descenting Changes Nothing

Every one of these rules is written about a species, and descenting does not change the species. No state statute or regulation read for this page distinguishes a descented skunk from any other. The one that comes closest says the opposite of what the trade assumes. Wisconsin Statutes § 169.04(2)(e):

> No person may operate on a live skunk to remove its scent glands unless the person holds a Class A or Class B captive wild animal farm license or unless the person is a veterinarian and the person bringing the skunk to the veterinarian holds such a license. ... If the person does not hold such a license, the veterinarian shall notify that person that possession of a live skunk is illegal and shall notify the department.

In Wisconsin the surgery is the thing that exposes an unlicensed owner. The license comes first, then the vet. Elsewhere the point goes unsaid because it does not need saying: Colorado's prohibited list names "Striped skunks" by common name, Nevada's names "All species in the genera Spilogale, Mephitis and Conepatus", and neither asks whether the animal can spray.

## Where the Family Is the Rule, and the Family Has Moved

Pennsylvania's Game Commission list at 58 Pa. Code § 137.1(a)(6) reads "In the family Mustelidae. All species of the skunks." Hawaii's Part A takes "Mephitis (all species in genus) skunk", filed under the mustelids. New York City's Health Code § 161.01(b)(4) prohibits "All fur bearing mammals of the family Mustelidae, including, but not limited to, weasel, marten, mink, badger, ermine, skunk, otter, pole cat, zorille, wolverine, stoat and ferret." All three follow the taxonomy of their day, and all three name the skunk, so the family is a label rather than a test. New York's 6 NYCRR § 180.1 lists "Skunks in the Family Mephitidae", the current name, and Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(3)(B)(xx) makes "a member within the family Mephitidae (including, but not limited to, striped skunk)" a Category Two animal, with the clause applying "notwithstanding changes in taxonomic nomenclature". New Mexico's importation list puts "Mephitidae all species | Skunk (any type)" in Group IV. California's § 671 names skunks in its order heading and files them with the mustelids, and reaches the new family through "All other Families" in any case. Maine lists all of Mustelidae in Category 1, and if the skunk is instead an uncategorized Mephitidae species, § 7.06(4) makes it ineligible for any permit; the answer is no on either reading.

South Dakota is the one place the move matters. ARSD 12:68:18:03 requires a possession permit for:

> (1) Of the order Carnivora, all nondomestic members of the following families: (a) Felidae; (b) Canidae; (c) Ursidae; (d) Mustelidae; and (e) Hyaenidae;

The rule took effect on 31 December 1993, when the skunk was a mustelid. On the taxonomy of its day the striped skunk needs the ten-dollar possession permit; on today's it is a member of Mephitidae, a family the list does not name, and needs nothing beyond the free entry permit. Either way there is no ban, and South Dakota is gray on the map rather than green because the text itself cannot say which permit applies.

<FunFact>
Delaware has a skunk permit in its statute and will not issue it to a keeper. Del. Code tit. 7 § 795 says "No live skunks or raccoons shall be sold or possessed in this State or transported into this State for any purpose without a permit from the Division." The Division is DNREC, which issues that permit to rehabilitators and, when people ask about buying a skunk from an out-of-state breeder, tells them "this is not allowed". It is a ban with a permit sentence in it.

## Native Wildlife, No Pet Route

Twelve jurisdictions ban the skunk without naming a disease or a family, by treating it as native wildlife. Georgia's Department of Natural Resources puts "skunk" on its list of natives that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology", and its carnivore licenses go only to dealers and exhibitors. Rhode Island names "Striped Skunk (Mephitis mephitis)" among native mammals whose possession as pets is prohibited, "regardless of where the animal originated". Virginia's § 29.1-521(A)(10) bars possessing any wild animal except as specifically permitted, and the permits are for exhibits and scientific collection. New Hampshire's controlled table carries "Skunk, Striped" with permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitor category. Colorado names striped skunks on its prohibited list, with a grandfather clause that closed in September 1990. Alabama's rule bars "species of skunk" from "any area outside the state of Alabama", an Alabama-caught striped skunk is a protected wild animal, and the spotted skunk is on the protected nongame list as well; it is the state most often placed in the legal column, and it is not there. Mississippi classes skunks as nuisance animals and Rule 7.1(F)'s exceptions cover coyotes, foxes and hogs, not skunks, so no live skunk may be possessed at all. Arkansas's Addendum R1.03 says the Commission "will not issue permits for breeding, sale, or importation" of "Skunks (Genera: Mephitis, Spilogale)", and the hand-capture allowance in Code 09.14 covers nongame wildlife, which a furbearer is not. Alaska bars a pet permit for any game animal. The District of Columbia's closed list of seven pet categories admits the ferret and no other carnivore but the dog and cat.

## The Permit States, Sorted

Fourteen states issue a permit, and twelve issue it to a private keeper. Texas's § 71.005(c) requires a fur-bearing animal propagation license to "possess a live fur-bearing animal for any purpose", $50.75, and the skunk is a fur-bearing animal under § 71.001(1). Ohio names skunks as fur-bearing animals in R.C. 1531.01(W) and sells a $25 noncommercial propagating license under § 1533.71(A)(2). Illinois protects "Mephitis mephitis" by name and § 3.25 requires a $25 fur-bearing mammal breeder permit of anyone who holds one, breeding or not. Wisconsin's § 169.15(1)(b) says the department "shall issue" a Class B captive wild animal farm license to any applicant, and NR 16.15(1) applies it to hobbyists. Indiana lists "Striped skunk (Mephitis mephitis)" at 312 IAC 9-11-7(a)(9) as a Class II animal: apply within five days, ten dollars, a cage inspection and a vet's statement. West Virginia's § 20-2-51 issues a two-dollar pet permit for a wild animal "acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season".

New Jersey's Individual Hobby permit "may be issued to persons holding game animals for hobby purposes or as pets", the animal must not be of wild origin, and the Division's synopsis uses a pet skunk as its worked example, reminding owners to notify it within 48 hours if someone else keeps the skunk while they are away. Michigan's Permit to Hold Game in Captivity names the striped skunk and covers bred animals only. Florida's Class III permit is free and 68A-6.003(13) closes it to a skunk taken from the wild. Oklahoma requires the import permit and the noncommercial wildlife breeder's license for a native animal not on its exemption list, with a captive-bred pet outside the statutory possession bar. South Carolina names the skunk as a furbearer and requires a Department permit to bring one in. Massachusetts and North Carolina have the permit on paper only: MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet", and North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) refuses a captivity license for an animal "acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard.

## Conditions, Restrictions, and the Gray Cells

Iowa makes possession lawful for a fur-bearer "obtained from a licensed game breeder", and the striped skunk is one of Iowa's named fur-bearing animals, so the condition is an Iowa-licensed breeder. Wyoming's condition runs the other way: the statute names the skunk a predatory animal, Chapter 10 § 14(b) allows one "possessed without a permit if captured in the state", and § 12 bars importing one, which closes the route for every descented pet-trade skunk, since those are always imports. Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora, and Utah's Table 3b-1 has no skunk on any of its five sections, so the animal is presumed prohibited until the Division assesses it.

Minnesota is the one clean yes. Minn. Stat. § 97A.015 subd. 53 defines unprotected wild animals as "wild animals that are not protected wild animals including coyote, plains pocket gopher, porcupine, striped skunk, and unprotected birds", and the possession bar in § 97A.501 reaches protected wild animals only. It is not a regulated animal under § 346.155 either. Local ordinances can still restrict it.

Three states besides South Dakota are gray. Oregon's OAR 635-044-0460 covers "Skunk (Mephitis spp.)" and reads two ways, as it does for the raccoon: 0460(1) lists a Wildlife Holding Permit among the permits that cover a skunk and 0460(6)(b) writes sterilization conditions for held skunks, while 0450(3) says an exhibitor or sanctuary permit is required for any 0460 species, 0470 frames pets as grandfathered from 20 January 2017, and 0460(4)(d) bars offering a skunk for sale as a household pet in Oregon. Kansas bars possessing wildlife unless a regulation permits it, the only regulation that permits a live furbearer lets a Kansas-caught one be held "only through the last day of the season in which taken", and no text says whether the exception for animals "legally taken outside this state" reaches one bred in captivity. Louisiana's only text on keeping a skunk is the wildlife rescuer rule, under which a sick, injured or orphaned skunk may be held for ninety days on notice and kept for life only on a special purpose permit after a vet finds it non-releasable; nothing read decides a captive-bred, descented skunk bought as a pet. In each, ask the agency in writing and keep the answer.

## The Takeaway

The seventeen-state list fails on its own terms: it counts Alabama, which bars the skunk in regulation, and it counts permit states where the permit goes to exhibitors. Counted on the statutes, a pet skunk has a route in Minnesota without a permit, in twelve states with one, in Iowa from a licensed breeder, and in Wyoming only if it was caught there, which no descented skunk ever was. In 29 jurisdictions there is no lawful pet skunk at all, and in most of them the breeder's animal is in exactly the position of the one under the porch. The vaccine problem underneath is the same one that decides the [fennec fox](/blog/fennec-fox-legal-guide/) and every other carnivore without a licensed rabies vaccine: a bite ends with the animal tested, permit or no permit.
