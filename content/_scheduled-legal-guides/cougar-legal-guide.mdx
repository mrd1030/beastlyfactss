The cougar is the eighth and last species on the Big Cat Public Safety Act's list, and the only one of the eight that lives wild in the United States. That makes its legal question different from the tiger's in a way the Act does not touch. Since 20 December 2022, 16 U.S.C. 3372(e) has made it unlawful for a private person to "breed or possess" a cougar unless the animal was born before that date and registered with the Fish and Wildlife Service by 18 June 2023, and the [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) carries the Act's text, the Service's guidance on what registered owners may do, and the 52-jurisdiction table for the tiger, lion and cougar together. Under state law alone the cougar is banned in 36 jurisdictions, a permit animal in 14, restricted in one and unregulated in one. No other animal on the map is restricted in as many places.

The reason is the second answer. A tiger is an exotic everywhere, so a state reaches it through an exotic animal rule or not at all, and five states never wrote one. A cougar is wildlife in every western state, a historical native from Georgia to Maine, and a game or trophy animal wherever it is hunted, so the state game code reaches it whether or not the exotic rule does. The [lion](/blog/lion-legal-guide/), the cougar's twin under the Act, has five states with no rule; the cougar has one, and that one hands the question to its counties.

## Four States With No Tiger Rule and a Cougar Rule

Wisconsin, Nevada, Alabama and Oklahoma are the states where the cougar's answer differs most from the tiger's, and in each the difference is one word: native.

Wisconsin frees every nonnative wild animal that is not harmful or endangered, which is why a tiger needs nothing from the state. Then NR 16.11(2) designates exactly one cat harmful:

> Felidae. The species felis concolor commonly known as cougars.

Wis. Stat. § 169.11(1)(b) bars possessing a harmful wild animal "unless specifically authorized to do so by the department", and the authorization is a captive wild animal farm license naming the species. NR 16.15(2)(f) requires written assurance that local zoning allows the animal before a first license for a harmful species is issued, and NR 10.02(1) separately lists the cougar among Wisconsin's protected wild animals. The rule's own note acknowledges that possession "is heavily restricted federally" since the Act. Under state law the license is real and a hobbyist can hold it; under federal law no new cougar can be acquired to put on it.

Oklahoma's statute is written around weight. 29 O.S. § 7-502(A) provides:

> Except as otherwise provided by law, no person may possess: ... 3. Any native bear or native cat that will grow to reach the weight of fifty (50) pounds or more.

A cougar is a native cat and an adult runs well past fifty pounds, so the bar applies, and § 4-107(E) is the "otherwise provided": a commercial wildlife breeder's license, the cat confined to the licensed premises, no public contact. The license goes to anyone the Director believes is acting in good faith, at $48 a year, and § 4-107(B) lets a breeder sell a native cat only to another license holder. A tiger is outside the clause because it is not native, and Oklahoma's exemption list clears "Cats (except native cats and bears)", which is the same line drawn from the other side.

Nevada and Alabama reach the same place with less text. Nevada's no-permit list at NAC 503.140 clears "All felines, except mountain lions and bobcats", so the mountain lion is written out of the exemption and, as a Nevada game mammal, needs the Department's written consent under NRS 503.597 to be brought into the state. Nothing in the rule says the Department would consent for a private keeper, and NAC 503.140(4) preserves county and city ordinances on top. Alabama names "mountain lion (Felis concolor)" among the animals that may not be possessed or imported "from any area outside the state of Alabama", bars transporting one within the state except by a licensed game breeder, designates the mountain lion a game animal in rule 220-2-.06, and bars possessing a live protected wild animal in 220-2-.26(3). A tiger is on none of those lists.

## Where the Act and the Native Clause Answer Differently

Inside the ban states the same split runs through the statutes, and it decides which sentence a cougar is caught by.

Wyoming is the clearest case. Its Chapter 10 would treat a tiger like any other exotic cat, under a discretionary permit, except that § 11(b)(xv) refuses a permit for wildlife on the federal endangered list. The cougar never gets that far, because Wyo. Stat. § 23-1-101 makes the mountain lion a trophy game animal and Chapter 10 § 11(a) opens with the rule for those:

> There shall be no private ownership, private importation or private possession of live big or trophy game animals, except as otherwise specified in this regulation.

Oregon's exotic animal statute, ORS 609.305, reaches only felids "not indigenous to Oregon", so the cougar falls to the Department of Fish and Wildlife's native holding rule instead. OAR 635-044-0460 names it, bars taking one from the wild, and provides that cougars "acquired by transfer or new acquisition as part of an existing permit or new holding application after January 20, 2017, shall only be held at AZA accredited facilities or as approved by the Department in Wildlife Exhibitor/Animal Entertainment or Wildlife Sanctuary facilities." A pet lawfully held on that date could be kept for its life under 0470 if its owner applied by 21 January 2018, with no breeding and no transfer except to one of those facilities. The tiger's Oregon ban is the exotic clause, with a window that closed in 2010; the cougar's is this one, which closed seven years later.

Kentucky bars the cougar on its native wildlife list at 301 KAR 2:081, "Cougar or mountain lion (Felis concolor)", while the tiger sits on the exotic list at 2:082, and the practical difference is that the exotic rule's captive wildlife permits do not reach a native at all. Arkansas's Code 09.02(D) says "It is unlawful for any person to possess mountain lions (Puma concolor) except in compliance with Codes 09.07", and Addendum R1.03 lists both "Large carnivores" and "Mountain lion (Puma concolor)" among the species the Commission will not permit under that code. Missouri makes the mountain lion Class II wildlife, held only under a Class II wildlife breeder permit, and its approved species list now stops at animals "legally obtained under a valid Class II Wildlife Breeder Permit prior to August 30, 2021"; the tiger's Missouri answer runs through the federal endangered list instead. Washington's statute names "captive-bred cougars" in its felid entry because a wild cougar is native wildlife under a different title, and either way there is no pet route.

Georgia, Rhode Island, Illinois, Minnesota and South Carolina each name the cougar twice, once as a dangerous or regulated animal and once as a native: Georgia's DNR lists it among natives that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology", Rhode Island's native mammal list counts species native "either currently or historically", Illinois lists the cougar among its protected mammals, Minnesota makes it small game, and South Carolina's Regulation 123-150 lists the eastern cougar as state endangered.

Idaho is the one state where the native clause is the lighter one. The tiger and lion are deleterious exotic large felids confined to AZA-accredited facilities, while the mountain lion is Idaho big game under IDAPA 13.01.06.100.08, outside the Agriculture list entirely, and held under a Fish and Game captive possession license with every captive big game animal marked and a felid enclosure fenced with eight feet of chain link and a top. Under state law a cougar is more keepable in Idaho than a tiger; under the Act, neither may be newly acquired.

## Texas Still Has the Form

Texas is the question people type most, and its statute reads as it did before the Act. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) defines a dangerous wild animal as "(A) a lion; (B) a tiger; (C) an ocelot; (D) a cougar" and sixteen more clauses, ending with "(T) any hybrid of an animal listed in this subdivision". Section 822.103 bars keeping one without a certificate of registration from the local animal control office, or the county sheriff where there is none; § 822.107 requires liability insurance of at least $100,000 per occurrence; and § 822.116(b) lets any municipality or county prohibit the animal outright instead. The cougar is also a Texas native, and 31 TAC 65.325(b)(2) leaves it out of the nongame rule by name, so the dangerous wild animal chapter is its only state rule. A Texan who registered a cougar with the Fish and Wildlife Service by 18 June 2023 lives under all of that and under the Act's own conditions as well: no breeding, no new animal, no public contact. A Texan who did not register is in violation of federal law whatever the county certificate says.

Montana's wild animal menagerie permit is the other detailed one. MCA § 87-4-801(3) defines a menagerie as a place where "one or more bears or large cats, including cougars, lions, tigers, jaguars, leopards, pumas, cheetahs, ocelots, and hybrids of those large cats are kept in captivity for use other than public exhibition", § 87-4-406(1) sends the mountain lion to the same part, and the permit is inspected, capped at ten animals and closed to anything captured from the wild. North Dakota, Indiana, South Dakota and Delaware reach the cougar the way they reach a serval, and the [bobcat legal guide](/blog/bobcat-legal-guide/) walks the same permits for the small native cat that takes them in twenty states rather than fourteen.

<FunFact>
The Florida panther has been on the federal endangered list since 11 March 1967, six years before the Endangered Species Act existed, under the list's predecessor statute. Its row in 50 CFR 17.11 reads "Puma (=Felis) concolor coryi", endangered wherever found. That is a different listing from the Big Cat Public Safety Act, with different machinery: the Act bars possession of any cougar, and the Endangered Species Act bars taking, possessing or selling a Florida panther in particular. New Mexico's importation list marks the whole species "Federally Threatened", which is a reference to the Florida similarity-of-appearance rule rather than to the panther itself, and files Puma concolor in Group IV either way. A cougar of unknown origin in a Florida enclosure is answered three times: by Class I, by the Act, and by a 1967 listing that nobody has ever been able to argue a captive animal out of.
</FunFact>

## Named in a Ban With No Window Left

Ten states name the cougar in a dangerous animal ban whose grandfather window is closed, and most of them name it more than once. Ohio's R.C. 935.01(C)(7)(c) reads "Cougars, also known as pumas or mountain lions", hybrids with domestic cats included, and § 935.02(A) says "No person shall possess a dangerous wild animal on or after January 1, 2014". West Virginia's list uses the same three names and closed in 2022, Michigan's Large Carnivore Act names "a cougar, a panther" and closed in 2000, Tennessee's Class I closed personal permits in 1991, Kansas closed in 2006 with a $250,000 bond for anyone who stayed, Iowa in 2007, Louisiana in 1995. Mississippi names Felis concolor in its inherently dangerous felid entry and, since 20 December 2022, issues no new permit for any felid at all. Utah's prohibited mammal table names "cougar (= puma)" on the big cat row that cites the federal Act in terms, and New Mexico files "Puma concolor (all subsp)" in Group IV, closed to the public.

Five more name it without a window ever having existed. Hawaii's Part A lists "Profelis concolor (=Felis concolor) puma, (cougar), (mountain lion)" for research and exhibition only. New York City's § 161.01(b)(2) names "puma, panther, mountain lion" and "cougar" in one clause, four names for one cat. Connecticut's Category One felid entry names the puma and allows the category only to institutions. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Cougar Felis concolor" for the exhibitor category alone. Alaska treats every mammal as game and 5 AAC 92.029(c) says the department "may not issue a permit for the capture, possession, import, or export of any game animal ... for use as a pet".

## Where the Family Is the Rule

The remaining nine jurisdictions ban the cougar with a sentence about cats in general, the serval's sentence, and the cougar's nativity changes nothing. California restricts "Family Felidae-All species (W)" at 14 CCR § 671 and marks the family (W) for welfare whatever the animal's origin, so the native mountain lion is covered the same way as an exotic. New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(2) reaches "Felidae and all hybrids thereof" but *Felis catus*. New Jersey puts "Felidae--Nondomestic cats" on its potentially dangerous table and rules out keeping one as a pet; the cougar has no hunting season there, so the exotic rule rather than the captive game rule applies. Maine's Category 1 takes the whole family and issues permits only to exhibitors, rehabilitators, laboratories and research facilities. Maryland bars any "member of the cat family other than the domestic cat", Nebraska's § 37-477(2) bans keeping "any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae", Colorado will not license a pet regulated mammal, the District of Columbia's closed list admits domestic cats only, and Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora.

Pennsylvania, Virginia, Massachusetts and Vermont sit between the bans and the permits. Each has a permit on paper that a cougar could in theory take, and none of the four says it would issue one to a keeper. Pennsylvania names cougars in its statutory definition of exotic wildlife, so the exotic wildlife possession permit, one per animal after two years of documented work at a recognized facility, is the route. Virginia restricts "Felidae All Species" and issues the special permit "if consistent with the department's fish and wildlife management program", without saying whether a private keeper qualifies. Massachusetts has no felid on its exemption list and MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 has the Commissioner refuse any captivity permit except for science or education.

## The Takeaway

Read the Act first, because it answers the acquisition question for the whole country: no private person may obtain a cougar, and the only private cougars left are the ones registered by 18 June 2023. Then read the state row, because the cougar's row is not the tiger's. A tiger in Wisconsin, Nevada, Alabama or Oklahoma was free under state law until December 2022; a cougar in those states never was. Wyoming bars it as trophy game, Oregon closed its native holding rule in 2017, Kentucky and Arkansas bar it on native lists the tiger is not on, and Idaho makes the native cat the easier one to license. Our [interactive map of cougar laws](/exotic-pet-laws/cougar/) shades every jurisdiction under its own law and quotes the sentence each answer comes from, and for the cougar that sentence is as likely to be in the hunting regulations as in the exotic animal law.
