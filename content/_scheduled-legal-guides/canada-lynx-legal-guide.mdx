<LegalDisclaimer />

The Canada lynx is the only cat on this map with a federal status that matters to a pet keeper. The population in the contiguous United States is listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act, and that listing does two separate things. In a handful of states it decides the cell outright, because the state law adopts the federal list by reference: Wisconsin hands any federally listed species to its endangered species permits, Wyoming says no permit shall be issued for anything in 50 CFR 17.11, Oregon makes it unlawful to hold a federally listed animal without authorization, and Pennsylvania's prohibited list reaches any animal the Interior Department lists as threatened. Everywhere else it sits underneath the state answer: wherever a lynx is lawful, moving one across a state line in commerce still needs a federal permit, and no state rule can lift that.

Read across all 52 jurisdictions, the Canada lynx gets 27 bans, 13 permits, nine states with no state rule, two with a restricted-list status, and one that cannot be read either way. The bobcat, its closest relative, gets 20 permits from the same statutes, because the bobcat is a native furbearer and the lynx is a wild cat that most states have never had.

## Where the Federal List Is the State Rule

Wyoming is the plainest. Chapter 10 § 11(b) of the Game and Fish Commission's regulation says a permit "shall not be issued for importation or possession" of a closed list of warm-blooded wildlife, and the list ends:

> (xv) Endangered and threatened wildlife listed in 50 CFR 17.11; and (xvi) Protected animals

The Canada lynx is on 50 CFR 17.11 as threatened in the contiguous states, and it is a "protected animal" by statute as well: Wyo. Stat. § 23-1-101(a)(ix) defines that term as "black-footed ferret, fisher, lynx, pika or wolverine". Barred twice, by federal reference and by state name, and the second would hold if the first were ever delisted. Wisconsin's chapter 169 defines an "endangered or threatened species" as one "indigenous to the United States or Canada" that is "identified on the federal list of endangered and threatened species or on the Wisconsin list", which hands the lynx to § 169.30 and § 29.604 rather than to the captive wild animal farm license a bobcat gets; endangered and threatened species permits go to education, research and conservation. Oregon's OAR 635-044-0430(1)(a) makes it unlawful to hold "federally listed threatened and endangered listed species" without specific authorization, and if the lynx is instead treated as non-indigenous it is an exotic animal under ORS 609.305 with the Agriculture permit window closed since 2010; banned on either reading. Pennsylvania prohibits every felid but the house cat and then separately, at § 137.1(a)(8), reaches any animal the Department of the Interior lists as threatened; the exotic wildlife possession permit that answers the serval there is written around facilities, with two years of documented hands-on work with the species before a first application. Massachusetts's 321 CMR 9.01(3) bars exempting any species listed under the federal Endangered Species Act, so the lynx could not be added to the exemption list even if the Division wanted it there, and MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet".

Two states list the lynx themselves. Washington's WAC 220-610-010 classifies the North American lynx, *Lynx canadensis*, as endangered, and RCW 77.15.120 makes possessing endangered wildlife a crime unless authorized by commission rule, a department permit or a federal permit, all of which serve research and recovery. Michigan's R 299.1027 puts *Lynx canadensis* on the state endangered list, and Part 365 bars possessing a listed species without a DNR permit issued for scientific, zoological and conservation purposes. Michigan's Large Carnivore Act never mentions the lynx; the endangered species rule does the work. The [federal exotic pet law guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/) covers the Endangered Species Act's own prohibitions, which is where the lynx's interstate rule comes from.

## Named on a Dangerous Animal List

Ohio names it to close it. Ohio Rev. Code § 935.01(C)(7)(b) lists "Lynxes, including Canadian lynxes, Eurasian lynxes, and Iberian lynxes" among dangerous wild animals, and § 935.02:

> (A) No person shall possess a dangerous wild animal on or after January 1, 2014. (B)(1) Except as provided in divisions (G)(1) and (2) of section 935.06 of the Revised Code, no person shall acquire, buy, sell, trade, or transfer possession or ownership of a dangerous wild animal on or after the effective date of this section.

Only a person who registered the animal with the Department of Agriculture in 2012 and still held it on 1 October 2013 could apply for a wildlife shelter or propagation permit, and the exemptions in § 935.03 are accredited zoos, research facilities, circuses, sanctuaries, rehabilitators and vets. The [bobcat](/blog/bobcat-legal-guide/), *Lynx rufus*, is not among the three lynxes named and is listed separately as a fur-bearer, which is why Ohio permits the bobcat and bans its cousin.

West Virginia wrote the same three names into a newer list. The board's comprehensive list of dangerous wild animals, W. Va. C.S.R. § 74-1-3.1, took effect on 5 April 2022, and 3.1.g.2 reads:

> Lynxes, including Canadian lynxes, Eurasian lynxes, and Iberian lynxes;

W. Va. Code § 19-34-4(a) then says "a person may not possess a dangerous wild animal", and subsection (b) offers the only permit, for an applicant who "was in legal possession of the animal prior to the effective date of the rules". A lynx held before April 2022 can be permitted annually; § 19-34-6(b)(1) bars breeding it, acquiring another or replacing it. The bobcat is excluded from that Act as "wildlife" and goes to Chapter 20's two-dollar pet permit instead. Illinois's 720 ILCS 5/48-10 names "lynx" among dangerous animals allowed only at zoos, exhibits, universities, laboratories, veterinary hospitals and refuges. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:082 § 4(2)(o) lists "Lynx (Genus Lynx)" as inherently dangerous exotic wildlife, which takes the whole genus and so the bobcat with it.

Texas names it and still lets a keeper have one. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) reads "(H) a bobcat; (I) a lynx; (J) a serval", so the lynx needs the certificate of registration under § 822.103, the $100,000 of liability insurance under § 822.107, and can be banned by any city or county under § 822.116(b); the state threatened list does not reach it because it is not a Texas animal. Missouri's RSMo § 578.023 names the "Canada lynx" on its dangerous wild animal list, which does not ban it but makes it an offense to keep one without registering it with the local law enforcement agency in the county where it is kept; as a non-native mammal it needs no Wildlife Code permit. The [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) walks the Texas and Missouri statutes for the cats they were written about, and the lynx rides on the same lists two entries down.

<FunFact>
Nebraska bans every cat in one sentence and then writes the lynx an exception no pet owner can use. Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-477(2) says no person shall keep in captivity "any wolf, any skunk, or any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae", and excepts "any person who holds a captive wildlife permit issued pursuant to section 37-479 and who raises Canada Lynx (Lynx canadensis) or bobcats (Lynx rufus) solely for the purpose of producing furs". The Captive Wildlife Permit list marks the lynx "For commercial purposes only", and § 37-477(1) separately puts a federally threatened species under special permit. A lynx fur farm is lawful in Nebraska. A lynx on the sofa is not.
</FunFact>

## The Family Clauses

Twelve jurisdictions ban the lynx the way they ban the serval, with a sentence that takes the family. California's § 671 restricts "Family Felidae-All species (W)", and the department's caging standards name the Canada lynx in Group III beside the bobcat and the serval. Georgia's inherently dangerous tier is "Family Felidae (cats) - All species", with licenses for the wild animal trade and public exhibitors only. New York's 6 NYCRR § 180.1 reaches "Cats in the Family Felidae with the exception of the species Felis catus", and "lynx" is named in the small game definition too. Connecticut names "lynx" among its Category One Felidae examples. Maine puts all Felidae in Category 1, and the Canada lynx meets the Category 1 criteria twice, as a Maine native and as a federally threatened species. Maryland bars any "member of the cat family other than the domestic cat". Iowa's § 717F.1(5)(a)(3) reaches "a member of the family felidae of the order carnivora", with the only route the 2007 registration. Minnesota's § 346.155 makes every felid but domestic cats a regulated animal closed to new possession since 2005. New Jersey's N.J.A.C. 7:25-4.9(a)3 says a potentially dangerous species "shall not be kept as a pet, for hobby purposes", and the Division treats any federally listed animal as endangered. New York City names "lynx" in its wild cat clause, and the District of Columbia's closed list admits domestic cats only.

Six more get there without a family word. Hawaii has *Lynx canadensis* on no list and bars an unlisted animal by default. New Mexico's importation list carries the row "Lynx canadensis | Lynx, Canada | IV | Federally Threatened". Colorado's Parks and Wildlife will not license private possession of regulated wildlife, and the lynx is a reintroduced Colorado native as well, which adds the native-wildlife bar. Rhode Island restricts every carnivore "for which there is no USDA conditionally licensed or unconditionally licensed rabies vaccine" to researchers, exhibitors and AZA facilities. New Hampshire's controlled table carries "Lynx, Canadian, Siberian" with permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitor category. Alaska's lynx is a native furbearer and game, the only cat on the clean list is the house cat, and 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars a pet permit; Alaska's population is not federally listed, which changes nothing there.

## The Permit States

Eleven states issue a permit a keeper can use. Florida's Class II item 16 is "European and Canadian lynx (Lynx lynx)", a binomial from the years the Canada lynx was treated as a subspecies of the Eurasian, and the class list covers "taxonomic successors and subspecies", so the $140 Class II personal use permit applies with documented experience and caging standards. Virginia's permit table restricts "Felidae | All Species", and because the Canada lynx is not a Virginia animal that nonnative table is its rule: a special permit to import, possess or sell. Delaware's regulation 903-6.2 requires an Individual permit, $25, for any exotic animal not on the exempt list, issued where possession will be in the public interest. Arkansas names "Canadian Lynx (Lynx canadensis)" on Addendum R1.02, the permit list, beside the Eurasian lynx and the serval, so one from a Commission-permitted breeder or imported on a permit may be kept with acquisition records and a veterinary certificate. Idaho treats the lynx as Idaho wildlife needing a Fish and Game captive possession license, and § 200.03 lets the Department refuse any license that would threaten native wildlife, which for a native, federally threatened cat makes this a permit on paper more than a likely one. South Dakota's possession permit covers all nondomestic Felidae at ten dollars an animal. Indiana puts a wild cat not on its Class II list into Class III: permit first, recapture plan, cage inspection, proof of lawful acquisition. North Dakota's category 2 takes a "nondomestic cat not listed in category 3", with the license and an approved premises in hand before the animal is. Texas and Missouri are above. In every one of these, the federal permit for interstate movement in commerce sits on top of the state one.

Vermont's is the eleventh, discretionary rather than closed like Pennsylvania's and Massachusetts's: Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue one outside science and education on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", with no published standard.

## Nine States With No State Rule

The lynx is on no list at all in nine states, and the reason in every one is where the state drew its cat line. Nevada's NAC 503.140(1)(q) clears:

> (q) All felines, except mountain lions and bobcats;

The Canada lynx is neither, so Nevada requires no permit, and the same sentence that clears it carves out the bobcat. NAC 503.140(4) says the clearance does not authorize anything that violates federal law, and preserves county and city ordinances; Clark County, Las Vegas and Henderson are all stricter than the state. South Carolina's large cat ban is drawn at genus *Panthera*, and the lynx is not among its named furbearers. Mississippi's Rule 8.3 names the lion, tiger, jaguar, leopard, clouded leopard, cheetah and cougar, and the lynx is not on the Rule 8.4 game list. Louisiana's § 115 cat list is six named big cats and their hybrids, and the lynx is not a Louisiana wild quadruped. Alabama's rule names only its own mountain lion and bobcat among cats. Kansas treats a non-Kansas animal as exotic wildlife that "may be possessed without limit in time and number", and its dangerous regulated animal list names six big cats. Tennessee's Class I cats are the lion, tiger, leopard, jaguar, cheetah and cougar, so the lynx falls into the Class III catch-all that requires no state permit, and rule .01(2) requires a threatened species to have been legally obtained where it came from. North Carolina has no statewide rule reaching a nonnative wild cat, though § 153A-131 lets counties regulate dangerous animals and several have. Oklahoma's exemption list clears "Cats (except native cats and bears)", and the Canada lynx is not native to Oklahoma; § 7-502(A)(2) then bars possessing a threatened species "except as otherwise provided by law", which a reader should weigh before relying on the exemption.

In all nine, the federal layer is the same: the Canada lynx is threatened in the lower 48, which does not bar keeping one but requires a federal permit to move one across state lines in commerce. A breeder in one of these states selling into another is the transaction the Endangered Species Act was written to reach, and the state's silence does not answer it.

Montana is the one gray cell. The lynx is native, so the exotic wildlife white list does not reach it, and the only permit that might is the wild animal menagerie permit for "bears or large cats, including cougars, lions, tigers, jaguars, leopards, pumas, cheetahs, ocelots", a list that reaches the smaller ocelot and never names the lynx. Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora, and Utah's Table 3b-1 names the bobcat and the Eurasian lynx as controlled, captive bred, and not the Canada lynx, which is presumed prohibited until the Division assesses it.

## The Takeaway

A Canada lynx is banned in 27 jurisdictions, and in three of them the ban is the federal threatened listing read into state law, with two more resting on a state endangered listing. Eleven states will permit a keeper, Texas with a certificate and insurance, Florida with its Class II permit, Vermont at the Commissioner's discretion, the rest with a wild-cat or exotic animal permit. Nine states have no rule, and none of those nine can lift the federal permit a lynx needs to cross a state line in commerce. The bobcat, in the same genus, gets a permit in 20 states because it is a furbearer; the lynx gets a ban in Ohio and West Virginia because three lynxes are named and it is one of them.
