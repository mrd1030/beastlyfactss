<LegalDisclaimer />

The tiger is the animal the Big Cat Public Safety Act was written about, and since 20 December 2022 its legal question has had one federal answer. The Act amended the Lacey Act so that 16 U.S.C. 3372(e) makes it unlawful for any person to "breed or possess" a prohibited wildlife species, and the Fish and Wildlife Service lists the tiger second on that list, after the lion. The exceptions are licensed exhibitors, sanctuaries, state institutions, veterinarians, and one private one: a person holding an animal "born before December 20, 2022" who registered it with the Service within 180 days and who does not breed, acquire or sell a big cat or allow the public to touch one. Registration closed on 18 June 2023.

So why read the state rules at all? Because the Act does not preempt them, and the Service says so: registration "does not constitute authorization to engage in any activity prohibited by" state or local law. The states set the terms a registered tiger lives under, Texas's certificate and insurance, Kansas's bond, Ohio's sterilization rule, Michigan's local permit, and they are the rules a sanctuary or exhibitor still has to satisfy. Under state law alone the tiger is banned in 36 jurisdictions, a permit animal in ten, restricted in one and unregulated in five. This page is for the places where a statute names the tiger, writes a permit a tiger could take, or catches it twice.

## The Shape of It

Every jurisdiction below names the tiger or its hybrids, writes a permit it could take, or reaches it through the federal list. The eleven jurisdictions that ban it with a family-wide cat clause are listed further down.

## Texas Still Has the Form

Texas is the question people type most, and its statute reads exactly as it did before the Act. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) defines a dangerous wild animal as "(A) a lion; (B) a tiger; (C) an ocelot; (D) a cougar" and sixteen more clauses, ending with "(T) any hybrid of an animal listed in this subdivision". Section 822.103 bars keeping one without a certificate of registration from the local animal control office, or the county sheriff where there is none; § 822.107 requires liability insurance of at least $100,000 per occurrence; and § 822.116(b) lets any municipality or county prohibit the animal outright instead. That is a personal permit, and before December 2022 it made a private tiger lawful on paper in most of Texas, which is why Texas is the state the tiger question is asked about.

What the Act changed is who can use the form. A Texan who registered a tiger with the Fish and Wildlife Service by 18 June 2023 lives under all of the Texas rules and under the Act's own conditions as well: no breeding, no new animal, no public contact. A Texan who did not register is in violation of federal law whatever the county certificate says, and no new certificate can make a new tiger lawful. The same is true of the other nine permit states. Montana's wild animal menagerie permit is the most detailed of them, with an inspection, a cap of ten animals and a rule that they come captive bred from a licensed zoo, menagerie or fur farm, and MCA § 87-4-801(3) names the tiger in the definition. North Dakota names the tiger among its category 3 big cats, Indiana's Class III catches it as a wild cat not on the small cat list, South Dakota's ten-dollar permit covers every nondomestic felid, and Delaware's Individual Permit refuses no big cat by name. None of those permits can be used for a new tiger, because the Act removed the supply. What they still do is set the terms for an animal that was already there, and for the serval, which the Act never reached. The [serval legal guide](/blog/serval-legal-guide/) walks the same ten permits for a cat that can still take them.

## Ligers and Tigons

A tiger hybrid gets the tiger's answer almost everywhere, because the drafters saw it coming. The federal Act reaches any hybrid of its eight species. Texas closes its list with clause (T). Idaho's IDAPA 02.04.27.403 lists "06. Tiger (Panthera tigris). All tigers and tiger-hybrids" among the large felids confined to AZA-accredited facilities. Michigan's definition of a large carnivore covers each named cat "whether wild or captive bred, including a hybrid cross with such a cat". Kansas's K.S.A. 32-1301(d) reaches "Lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, cheetahs and mountain lions, or any hybrid thereof". Utah's big cat row bars the seven named species "or any hybrid of these species". North Dakota's category 3 covers the big cats "and their hybrids", South Dakota's permit list covers each felid "or any of its hybrids", and New York City's wild cat clause ends on "any hybrid or cross-breed offspring of a wild cat and domesticated or other cat". A liger is a lion-tiger cross and a tigon is the reverse, and both are a hybrid of a listed animal on every one of those lists. The states that take the whole family Felidae do not need a hybrid clause, since a liger is a felid on any reading.

<FunFact>
Michigan dated its tiger window to the day, twenty-two years before Congress did. The Large Carnivore Act, which took effect in 2000, lets a person possess a tiger only if they owned that individual animal on the effective date and applied for a local permit within 90 days, and MCL 287.1104(1) says the permit "is not transferable to another person except through testate or intestate succession". That is the shape the federal Act copied in 2022: a registry of named animals, closed on a date, with nothing added after it. Michigan's registry has been closing by attrition for a quarter century.
</FunFact>

## Named, With a Window That Shut

Eight states name the tiger in a ban that once let existing owners stay, and every window is closed. Florida's is the oldest: 68A-6.002(1)(a) names "Tigers (Panthera tigris)" at Class I item 11, and 68A-6.003(6) provides:

> Class I wildlife shall not be possessed for personal use, except as follows: (a) Any Class I wildlife possessed for personal use on August 1, 1980, shall be eligible for a permit in accordance with the provisions for Class II wildlife.

Tennessee's Class I names "Lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, cheetahs, cougars--All species", Class I "may only be possessed by zoos, circuses and commercial propagators", and § 70-4-404(c)(1) says that after 25 June 1991 "no new animals shall be brought into possession under authority of a personal possession permit". Kansas's § 32-1301 names the tiger as a dangerous regulated animal, closed possession on 1 July 2006, and lets an animal held that day stay only if registered, caged to § 32-1306, not bred and covered by insurance or a bond "of not less than $250,000 for each occurrence". Arkansas's § 20-19-502 lets a person possess a large carnivore only if they held it "on or before August 12, 2005" and got a sheriff's permit within 180 days. Washington's RCW 16.30.010 names "lions, tigers, captive-bred cougars, jaguars, cheetahs, leopards, snow leopards, and clouded leopards" and closed on 22 July 2007. Ohio's R.C. 935.01(C)(4) names tigers, and § 935.02(A) says "No person shall possess a dangerous wild animal on or after January 1, 2014" unless registered in 2012 and permitted, with sterilized males, insurance and an acre of ground. South Carolina's 2018 Act names tigers in its genus *Panthera* definition and closed on 1 January 2018 with a $500 site fee and $100 a year per animal for registrants. Mississippi's Rule 8.3 is the newest, and it points at the federal date: "Effective December 20, 2022, with the enacting of the Big Cat Public Safety Act ... no new permits for the transfer or possession of members of the order Carnivora, family Felidae, or 'big cats,' will be issued."

Twelve more jurisdictions name the tiger with no window at all, or with one that closed long ago. Hawaii's Part A lists "Panthera tigris tiger" for universities, government agencies and government zoos. New York City's § 161.01(b)(2) names it second in a clause that runs from the lion to the margay. Illinois names it among its dangerous animals and allows one only at a zoo, licensed exhibit, circus, university, laboratory, veterinary hospital or refuge. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Tiger; Golden Tabby, Siberian, White Siberian, Bengal" for permittee category 5, the exhibitor category. New Mexico files "Panthera tigris" in Group IV, closed to the public. Utah's prohibited mammal table names it on a row that cites the federal Act in terms. Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Minnesota and West Virginia name it in bans whose grandfather windows closed in 2007, 2005, 1995, 2005 and 2022.

## The Federal Endangered List Catches It Twice

Four states bar the tiger a second time, by pointing at 50 CFR 17.11, where the tiger has been listed endangered wherever found since 1970. Wyoming's Chapter 10 would otherwise treat a tiger like a serval, under the discretionary permit in § 13(d), but § 11(b)(xv) says a permit "shall not be issued" for "Endangered and threatened wildlife listed in 50 CFR 17.11". Missouri's 3 CSR 10-9.110(2) lets nonnative mammals be possessed without a permit "Except for federally-designated endangered species", which takes the tiger out of the free pass and leaves it under the general prohibition on possessing wildlife except as the rules permit; the lion, listed in two subspecies, gets a different reading there, and the [lion legal guide](/blog/lion-legal-guide/) explains why. New Jersey's 7:25-4.10 issues no permit for a species the Interior Department lists as endangered, outside a research project, on top of the Felidae row on its potentially dangerous table. Maine's § 7.18(2)(C) reaches federally endangered species in the same category as its Felidae clause.

## Where the Family Is the Rule

The other eleven jurisdictions ban the tiger with a sentence about cats in general, the serval's sentence. California restricts "Family Felidae-All species (W)" and carves out only domestic cats and their hybrids. Georgia puts "Family Felidae (cats) - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, licensed only to the wild animal trade and exhibitors. New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(2) reaches "Felidae and all hybrids thereof" but *Felis catus*, Connecticut's Category One names "lion, leopard, cheetah, jaguar, ocelot, jaguarundi cat, puma, lynx and bobcat" as its examples and stops short of the tiger, Maryland bars any "member of the cat family other than the domestic cat", and Nebraska's § 37-477(2) bans keeping "any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae". Oregon's exotic animal definition takes every felid not indigenous to Oregon, with a window that closed in 2010. Colorado will not license a pet regulated mammal, Rhode Island refuses a private permit for any carnivore without a licensed rabies vaccine, Alaska cannot permit a game animal as a pet, and the District of Columbia's closed list admits domestic cats only. Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora. In every one of them the tiger's answer is the serval's answer, and the [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) carries each clause in full.

## Five States With No Tiger Rule

Wisconsin, Nevada, Alabama, Oklahoma and North Carolina have no state rule reaching a tiger, and before December 2022 that was a real answer. Wisconsin frees every nonnative wild animal that is not harmful or an endangered species, § 169.01(9) confines "endangered or threatened species" to animals indigenous to the United States or Canada, and the only cat Wisconsin designates harmful is the cougar. Nevada's NAC 503.140(1)(q) clears "All felines, except mountain lions and bobcats". Alabama's restricted list names "black bear (Ursus americanus), mountain lion (Felis concolor), bobcat (Felis rufus)" and no other cat. Oklahoma's exemption list clears "Cats (except native cats and bears)", and its statute's big cat clause reaches only a native cat over fifty pounds. North Carolina hands dangerous animals to its counties under § 153A-131. In those five the federal Act is the only law on the subject, and it says no.

## The Takeaway

Read the Act first, because it answers the acquisition question for the whole country: no private person may obtain a tiger, and the only private tigers left are the ones registered by 18 June 2023. Then read the state row, because it answers everything else. Texas never repealed its certificate and insurance, Kansas never repealed its bond, Michigan's window has been closed since 2000 and Florida's since 1980, and Mississippi stopped issuing permits on the federal date. The hybrid clauses mean a liger gets the same answer, and the federal endangered list means Wyoming and Missouri would bar a tiger even if the Act were repealed tomorrow.
