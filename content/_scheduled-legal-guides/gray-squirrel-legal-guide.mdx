An eastern gray squirrel is a game animal, and that single fact decides more of this map than anything written about pets. Across most of its native range the squirrel has a hunting season, and the chapter of the code that sets the season is also the chapter that says what a private person may do with a live one. New York defines "small game" to begin with it. Texas puts it beside the white-tailed deer and the pronghorn. Alabama, Iowa, Illinois, Ohio, Mississippi, West Virginia and North Carolina all name it in their game lists. So "can I keep a pet squirrel" lands in a game code far more often than in an exotic pet statute, and the answer that comes back is usually a season and a license rather than a pet permit.

Read across all 52 jurisdictions, that produces 19 bans, 20 permits, six states where it is legal with no permit, three where it is legal on a condition, two where the whole squirrel family is on a restricted list, and two, Montana and Kansas, whose text will not decide. The [flying squirrel](/blog/flying-squirrel-legal-guide/) row already read every squirrel clause in the country, and the gray squirrel lands differently in a dozen states for one reason: the flying squirrel is nongame nearly everywhere, the gray squirrel is game nearly everywhere, and a game animal gets a game animal's rules.

## Small Game by Name

The clearest statement of why a squirrel is not a hamster is New York's. Environmental Conservation Law § 11-0103(2)(c) reads:

> "Small game" means black, gray and fox squirrels, European hares, varying hares, cottontail rabbits, native frogs, native salamanders, native turtles, native lizards, native snakes, coyotes, red fox (Vulpes vulpes) and gray fox (Urocyon cinereoargenteus) except captive bred red fox or gray fox, raccoon, opossum, or weasel,

Small game is game, game is protected wildlife under § 11-0103(6)(c), and § 11-0107(2) bars possessing protected wildlife "whether taken within the state or coming from without the state". A squirrel bred in Ohio and driven across the state line is in exactly the position of one lifted out of a Brooklyn park. The only possession license, § 11-0515, is for propagation, banding, scientific or exhibition purposes. New York City adds a second bar in its Health Code, "All squirrels (Sciuridae)", and imports state-protected wildlife into the city besides.

Four more states ban it the same way, by designating it game and then barring live possession. Alabama's rule 220-2-.06 lists the animals "hereby designated as game animals", with "Squirrel" between the raccoon and the nutria, and 220-2-.26(3) makes it unlawful to possess any live protected wild animal except under a permit issued before that amendment. A squirrel from outside Alabama is caught separately as a "wild rodent" that 220-2-.26(1) bars bringing in. Mississippi's Rule 8.4 names the gray squirrel on its closed list of game and furbearing animals and says "Possession of live game and furbearing animals is prohibited", with exceptions for public zoos, treating vets and permitted enclosures. Pennsylvania reaches the same place through 58 Pa. Code § 137.31(a), "It is unlawful for a person to possess live wildlife taken from a wild state within this Commonwealth", and § 137.1(a)(11), which bars one held captive or captive bred in another state. Both doors are shut, as they are for the [raccoon](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/), the other native that every one of these game codes names.

## The Native-Wildlife Bars

Eight jurisdictions never needed the word "game", because a native-wildlife rule reaches the squirrel first. Georgia's Department of Natural Resources lists the gray squirrel among species native to Georgia that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology", and its wild animal licenses go only to the animal trade and public exhibitors. Rhode Island names "Eastern Gray Squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis)" on its list of native mammals "which possession as pets is prohibited", restricted to the institutions in § 3.7(C), and the native definition applies regardless of where the animal originated. Virginia carries the gray squirrel on its native fauna list, which puts it under § 29.1-521(A)(10), the bar on possessing any wild animal "except as specifically permitted by law". New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Squirrel, Gray Sciurus carolinensis" for permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitors. Delaware's DNREC says state regulations "explicitly forbid Delawareans from owning" native wildlife outside rehabilitation permits. Maine never categorized the gray squirrel, and an uncategorized species "will not be eligible for a permit under this chapter". Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars the department from issuing a permit for any game animal "for use as a pet", and the District of Columbia's closed list admits domesticated rodents only, which a squirrel is not.

Four jurisdictions take the family or the order wholesale. California's 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(J) heads its rodent entry "Hamsters, Field Mice, Voles, Muskrats, Gerbils, Squirrels, Chipmunks, Woodchucks, and Prairie Dogs 1. All species (D)", and the exceptions beneath it are muskrats and five domesticated cage rodents. Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(3)(B)(xxv) makes "A member within the order Rodentia" a Category Two animal, with nine named exceptions and no squirrel among them. Hawaii prohibits "Sciuridae (all in family" with two tree squirrels excepted for government zoos. Oregon is the odd one, because the gray squirrel is not an Oregon native: OAR 635-056-0050(1)(a)(J)(xxi) names "Eastern gray squirrel ... Sciurus carolinensis" on the prohibited species list, so the nonnative wildlife rules answer it. Colorado and New Mexico close the group by default: no squirrel is on Colorado's unregulated list, so "CPW will not issue a license for private possession (eg pet)", and New Mexico puts "Sciurus spp" in Group IV as a state protected species.

<FunFact>
Texas is the state most often listed as squirrel-friendly, and the squirrel it is friendly to is the flying squirrel, which the nongame rule lets a keeper hold 25 of with no permit. The gray squirrel is in a different chapter: § 63.001 makes "gray or cat squirrels" game animals, § 63.002 says "No person may possess a live game animal in this state for any purpose not authorized by this code", and the only authorization is the game breeder's license in Chapter 44. Two squirrels in the same tree, in different codes.
</FunFact>

## The Permit States, Sorted by What the Permit Is For

Twenty states issue a permit, and sixteen of them issue it to a keeper.

The first group has a game or captive wildlife license written broadly enough for a pet. Ohio names gray squirrels among its game quadrupeds in R.C. 1531.01(V), and § 1533.71(A)(2) describes the license:

> "Noncommercial propagating license" permitting the licensee to propagate game birds, game quadrupeds except captive white-tailed deer, reptiles, amphibians, or fur-bearing animals and to hold the animals in captivity. ... The fee for such a license is twenty-five dollars per annum.

The animals held under it "are for the licensee's own use and shall not be sold". Illinois names the gray squirrel among its game mammals in 520 ILCS 5/2.2 and offers the Class A Noncommercial game breeders permit in § 3.23 to "Any person desiring to possess, propagate, hold in captivity but not offer for sale any species protected by this Act", ten dollars a year. Indiana lists "Squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis, Sciurus niger, and Glaucomys volans)" as Class I wild animals at 312 IAC 9-11-6(a)(2): apply within five days of possession, ten dollars, a cage inspection and a vet check within 45 days. West Virginia's § 20-2-51 is the cheapest, a $2 permit "to keep and maintain in captivity as a pet, a wild animal or wild bird that has been acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season". Wisconsin's § 169.15(1)(b) says the department "shall issue" a Class B captive wild animal farm license to any applicant who files properly and pays; the gray squirrel needs it because the native animals § 169.04(4)(a) frees from licensing include ground and red squirrels and not gray ones. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 requires a noncommercial captive wildlife permit "prior to acquiring wildlife" and sets the squirrel's enclosure size by name. Nebraska names "Gray (Sciurus carolinensis)" on its Captive Wildlife Permit list, at a fee of not more than $30, and bars a permit for one taken from the wild. Louisiana's rule says "A game breeder's license must be issued before any game quadrupeds (deer, rabbits, squirrels, etc.) are obtained" and writes pen specifications for "Squirrels (gray, fox, red, flying and others endemic to North America)". Oklahoma leaves the squirrel off its exempt list, so the import permit and the noncommercial wildlife breeder's license both apply, and only an animal legally obtained from a source other than the wild has the pet exemption in 29 O.S. § 7-502(B)(4).

The second group issues a pet permit that is discretionary or closed to a wild-origin animal. New Jersey's captive game rule names Sciurus carolinensis among the game animals needing a possession permit, and 10.7(a)1 offers the permit written for a keeper:

> "Individual Hobby" may be issued to persons holding game animals for hobby purposes or as pets;

Under 10.8(a)1 the animal must not come from the wild stock of any state, so a squirrel caught in the yard does not qualify. Michigan's Permit to Hold Game in Captivity puts "squirrels" on its closed list and covers captive-bred game only. Minnesota makes the gray squirrel small game, and § 97A.401 subd. 3 lets the commissioner issue special permits "without a fee to take, possess, and transport wild animals as pets" on conditions the commissioner sets, a route that exists and is discretionary. Missouri's Wildlife Hobby Permit covers "not more than one (1) game mammal purchased from a Missouri wildlife breeder permit holder", never bred or sold. Nevada lists no eastern gray squirrel, only its own western gray squirrel as a protected mammal, so bringing one in needs the Department's written consent under NRS 503.597, and county and city ordinances are preserved. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 13(d) says a permit "may be authorized upon approval for other warm-blooded wildlife", and may is not shall. Vermont's Regulation 881 is the same shape: outside science and education, no permit issues unless the Commissioner determines the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation".

The third group is a permit in name only. Texas is the one that fools the most readers. Chapter 44 defines a game animal for its purposes as "a pronghorn antelope, a collared peccary or javelina, or a red or gray squirrel", § 44.002 says:

> No person may sell, place in captivity, or engage in the business of propagating any game animal of this state unless the person has obtained a license issued under this chapter from the department.

and § 44.006 lets the licensee "sell or hold in captivity for the purpose of propagation or sale a game animal". The license starts at $10 and a keeper could hold it, but only on those terms, since no section of the code authorizes holding a squirrel purely as a pet. Massachusetts lists no gray squirrel on its exemption list, so a § 23 license is needed, and MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) bars a captivity license unless the animal "was not acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet". Maryland's § 10-902(a) attaches a permit to possessing "any wildlife native to Maryland", and no pet permit class for a native mammal was located in the regulations.

## Six Clean States and Three Conditions

Florida is the clearest yes. 68A-6.003(14) opens "No permit shall be required to possess the following wildlife for personal use, unless possession of a species is otherwise regulated by other rules of the Commission", and (g) is "Squirrels; chipmunks". The proviso keeps the hunting rules in play, so a squirrel taken from the Florida wild is a hunting question; the wild-taken bar at 68A-6.003(13) names foxes, skunks, bats, raccoons and white-tailed deer and leaves squirrels out. Tennessee's Class III "requires no permits except those required by the department of agriculture" and names "squirrels and chipmunks" among its rodents, which overrides the Class II default for natives. Idaho's conventional pets include "rodents" that are "not native wildlife captured from the wild in Idaho". South Carolina sets a season and a bag limit of ten a day and nowhere bars keeping a live one; the squirrel is not a furbearer, so the § 50-16-20 import permit does not reach it. North Dakota's category 2 squirrel entry reads "prairie dogs, and other ground squirrels", and South Dakota's possession permit list has no rodent on it; an import into either still needs a health certificate or a free entry permit.

Three states allow it on a condition. Iowa names the gray squirrel as its first example of game, "The Sciuridae: such as gray squirrels and fox squirrels", and § 481A.61(1) provides that "Possession and use of the game birds, game animals, or fur-bearing animals obtained from a licensed game breeder are lawful". The buyer needs no license; the breeder does. Arkansas runs the condition the other way. Code 09.14(A)(1)(i) says "No more than 6 per household of any combination of the following animals may be possessed as pets: Hand-captured opossum, rabbit, raccoon and squirrel", with no sale and no removal from the state, so the animal from the woods is the lawful one. Washington treats the introduced gray squirrel as a wild animal because the definition turns on whether a species lives wild in Washington, and then asks for lawful acquisition: "It is unlawful to possess or hold in captivity live wild animals, wild birds, or game fish unless lawfully acquired", meaning no taking from the wild, a health certificate to import and documented proof of source.

Arizona and Utah are restricted rather than banned. Arizona's R12-4-406(F)(9)(g) restricts "The family Sciuridae", whose "Common names include: chipmunks, marmots, prairie dogs, squirrels, and woodchucks", and none of the exemptions is a pet route, so a special license is the only door. Utah's Table 3b-1 lists no gray squirrel, and its header says "Except as otherwise listed, all species are presumed prohibited. All species not listed will require an assessment before collection, importation, or possession will be authorized."

## Where the Text Will Not Say

Montana and Kansas are gray on the map, for different reasons. Montana runs a white list for exotic wildlife, and the eastern gray squirrel is on none of its three lists, which would bar it, except that MCA § 87-5-702(2) defines a domestic animal as one "bred to a degree that has resulted in genetic changes affecting color, temperament, conformation, or other attributes of the species" and then adds:

> The term includes livestock, as defined in 81-2-702, dogs, cats, rodents, Eurasian ferrets, and poultry.

Domestic animals are not wildlife under § 87-5-702(12)(b), so if "rodents" means every rodent, a gray squirrel is outside the exotic wildlife scheme entirely. If it means rodents bred to the definition's standard, a wild-type tree squirrel is exotic wildlife with no listing and therefore barred. No Montana text says which, and the southern flying squirrel, named on the prohibited list by binomial, shows the state knows how to name a squirrel when it means to. It is the same open question Montana leaves on the prairie dog and the capybara.

Kansas makes a squirrel small game in K.S.A. 32-701(r), "'Small game' means any game bird, hare, rabbit or squirrel", and 32-1002(a)(2) bars possessing any wildlife "unless and except as permitted by law or rules and regulations adopted by the secretary". The personal-use regulation reaches ground squirrels and not tree squirrels, and the exotic regulation covers only species not native to Kansas, so no regulation read permits keeping a gray squirrel. What keeps the cell open is 32-1002(b), which lifts the possession bar for "animals legally taken outside this state". Whether a squirrel bred in captivity in Missouri was "legally taken outside this state" is a question no Kansas text answers. In both states, ask the agency in writing before buying and keep the reply.

## The Takeaway

In 19 jurisdictions there is no lawful pet gray squirrel, and in most of them the reason is that the animal is game or native wildlife, not anything an exotic pet list says. In 16 states a keeper can hold a permit, usually a game or captive wildlife license costing between two and thirty dollars, and nearly all of them require a bred animal. Six states need no permit, Iowa allows a bred one from a licensed game breeder, Arkansas allows six caught by hand, and Washington allows one lawfully acquired. Montana and Kansas will not say. Wherever the answer is yes, the next thing to read is the hunting regulation, because a squirrel in the yard is small game nearly everywhere and keeping it is taking it.
