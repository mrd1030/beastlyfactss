The capuchin is the monkey that worked for a living. It sat on the organ-grinder's shoulder in nineteenth-century cities, and from the late 1970s it was the monkey a few nonprofits trained to open drawers, turn pages and fetch for people who could not move their arms. That working history is the reason it is the one small primate whose name turns up in statutes. Georgia, New York and Maryland each wrote a capuchin exception into a primate ban. Ohio wrote a capuchin registration. Nobody wrote one for a marmoset or a squirrel monkey, because nobody ever asked a marmoset to fetch anything.

Read across all 52 jurisdictions, the capuchin is banned in 28, a permit animal in nine, restricted in one, unclear in one and unregulated at state level in 13. Almost none of that comes from a sentence about capuchins. Forty jurisdictions restrict the order Primates by name, and a rule that says "all nonhuman primates" gives a seven-pound capuchin the same answer it gives a gorilla. The [pet monkey legal guide](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) walks those forty sentences and the two federal rules underneath them; this page is for the handful of places where a statute looked at the capuchin itself.

## The Shape of It

Every jurisdiction below either names the capuchin, its genus or its family, or draws its line in a way that lands on it. The rest give it the primate answer and are listed further down.

## Every Lawful Capuchin Was Born Here

Before any state rule applies, a federal one has already decided where a capuchin can come from. Since 1975 the Centers for Disease Control has admitted live nonhuman primates only through registered importers and only for scientific, educational or exhibition use, and 42 CFR 71.53(d)(3) bars anyone from keeping, selling or distributing an imported primate or its offspring "for use as pets, as a hobby, or as an avocation with occasional display to the general public". The [pet monkey legal guide](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) quotes the rule in full. Its effect on the capuchin trade is simple: no breeder can bring stock in from Central or South America, so every capuchin sold lawfully in the United States descends from animals that were already in the country fifty years ago. A seller who says otherwise is describing a federal offense.

The age of the stock also explains why the statutes name the genus *Cebus* and nothing else. When Georgia, Idaho and New Hampshire wrote "Cebus", every capuchin was *Cebus*. In 2012 the tufted capuchins, the brown and black-capped animals that make up most of the pet trade, were moved to a new genus, *Sapajus*, and none of the statutes was rewritten. A tufted capuchin in those states is covered on the older usage, which is almost certainly what the drafter meant, but it is an argument a reader should know exists before relying on it.

## The Service Monkey Exceptions

Three states banned every nonhuman primate and then wrote a sentence for the capuchin. Georgia's is the most specific, at O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(2):

> The department shall issue a wild animal permit only for an animal in the genus Cebus (capuchin monkeys) to any person who establishes to the satisfaction of the department that: (A) Such person has a permanent disability or disease which interferes with the person's ability to perform one or more routine daily living activities;

The permit is free, the animal must come from a nonprofit with a ten-year record of placing assistance animals, it must be kept only in the permittee's residence, and it must be the only wild animal that person holds. That is the whole of Georgia's private primate route. The rule at R. 391-4-8-.05 puts "Order Primates (monkeys, apes, etc) - All species" in the license tier, § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues licenses only to persons "engaged in the wholesale or retail wild animal business or persons exhibiting wild animals to the public", and the DNR's guide to legal pets lists all primates among exotics that may not be held. A capuchin bought for company has no Georgia paper to stand on.

New York's exception is at ECL § 11-0512(2)(j), and it covers a person "paralyzed from the neck down" who has a New World monkey trained to assist them by a 501(c) organization dedicated to people with that disability. Maryland's is at Criminal Law § 10-621(a)(3), for an animal trained by a 501(c) organization to perform tasks for a person whose disability severely limits mobility. Both are carve-outs from a ban that names monkeys outright: New York's definition of wild animal opens with "Nonhuman primates and prosimians", and Maryland's clause reads "nonhuman primate, including a lemur, monkey, chimpanzee, gorilla, orangutan, marmoset, loris, or tamarin". The exception travels with the trained animal and the named person, and it is not something a keeper can apply for. Federal disability law does not recognize a capuchin at all: the ADA regulations define a service animal as a dog, so a "service monkey" is a creature of these three state statutes and nothing wider.

## Ohio Named Four Capuchins and Did Not Say Why

Ohio is the one state where the capuchin's own name makes the law harder to read rather than easier. R.C. 935.01(C)(19) defines a dangerous wild animal to include "Nonhuman primates other than lemurs and the nonhuman primates specified in division (C)(20) of this section", and (C)(20) is a list of tamarins, night monkeys, titis, sakis, uakaris, spider monkeys, howlers and a few others. No capuchin is on it. Read alone, that makes every capuchin a dangerous wild animal, and § 935.02 says no person may acquire one on or after the act's effective date or possess one on or after 1 January 2014 without a permit that only 2012 registrants could apply for. But the same bill enacted R.C. 935.041:

> A person that possesses any of the following animals shall register the animal in the same manner as provided in section 935.04 of the Revised Code: (A) Pygmy, white-tufted-ear, silvery, and black-pencilled marmosets; (B) Squirrel monkeys; (C) Brown, white-faced, weeping, and white-fronted capuchins; (D) Lemurs.

A registration section for animals the chapter elsewhere bars outright only makes sense if those animals are not dangerous wild animals. The lemur has no problem, because (C)(19) writes it out in terms. The four named capuchins do, because (C)(19) does not, and the two sections cannot both be read at full strength. The map records Ohio as unclear rather than guess. The likelier reading is that § 935.041 works the way it reads for the lemur, as a registration for a small primate Ohio decided not to ban, and the four named capuchins are exactly the species the trade sells. But a keeper who buys on that reading and meets an inspector who holds the other one has a felony-grade problem, so write to the Department of Agriculture, ask which section governs a newly acquired capuchin, and keep the answer.

<FunFact>
Texas registers a baboon and ignores a capuchin. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) lists "(P) a baboon; (Q) a chimpanzee; (R) an orangutan; (S) a gorilla" and no other primate, so a baboon in Texas needs a certificate of registration and $100,000 of liability insurance while a capuchin, a monkey with the same hands and the same teeth at a fifth of the weight, needs nothing from the state at all. The list was written about cats and bears, with the four largest primates added at the end, and it stops where it stops.

## The Permits

Nine jurisdictions issue a permit, and seven of them issue it to a private keeper. Florida's is the one most people will use. The captive wildlife rule writes its primate classes genus by genus, Class I for the great apes, gibbons, baboons and drills, Class II for howlers, uakaris, mangabeys, guenons, patas and vervet monkeys, sakis, colobus, indris, macaques and langurs, and 68A-6.002(1)(c) catches everything else:

> Class III: All other wildlife not listed herein, except those for which a permit is not required pursuant to Rule 68A-6.003, F.A.C.

The capuchin is Class III, so 68A-6.003(8) applies: a person possessing Class III wildlife as personal use wildlife "shall obtain a no-cost permit from the Executive Director". Primates have their own caging standard at 68A-6.0121.

Arkansas names the capuchin on the list of animals a permitted breeder or importer may supply. Addendum R1.02 says permits "for breeding, sale, and importation may be issued for the following species", and the mammal list carries "Capuchin Monkeys (Subfamily Cebinae)", a subfamily entry that reaches both *Cebus* and *Sapajus* without the genus problem. Code 09.02(H) then sends the owner to Ark. Code Ann. §§ 20-19-601 to 20-19-610, which bar only apes, baboons and macaques, and § 20-19-605 requires the owner to register the animal with the county sheriff within 30 days, with no outdoor tethering and no public contact under § 20-19-602. Idaho's IDAPA 02.04.27.407 says "All non-human primates must be possessed on a facility that is AZA accredited" and then exempts four entries, the first of them "Capuchin (Cebus spp)"; the exempt capuchin still needs a Fish and Game captive possession license, because the conventional pets list has no primate on it. Delaware's regulation 903 writes the Individual Permit for "an exotic ... kept as a pet", per animal for three years. South Dakota puts the whole order Primates on its possession permit list at ten dollars an animal, and North Dakota makes primates category 3 nontraditional livestock, licensed and inspected before the animal arrives.

Missouri is a permit state by registration rather than license. RSMo § 578.023 makes it an offense to keep a "nonhuman primate" without registering it with the local law enforcement agency in the county where it is kept, and because the capuchin is a nonnative mammal and not federally endangered, 3 CSR 10-9.110(2) clears it from any Wildlife Code permit. The [marmoset](/blog/marmoset-legal-guide/) and squirrel monkey get the same Missouri answer; the ring-tailed lemur does not, because its family is on the federal endangered list and Missouri's free pass excludes it.

Massachusetts and Vermont round out the nine. Each is a closed-list state where an unlisted mammal needs a license. Massachusetts has its permit on paper only: MassWildlife issues permits for non-exempt wildlife "only for certain scientific, educational, commercial, or other specific reasons" and adds "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard. Arizona is restricted rather than permitted: R12-4-406(F)(8) makes "All nonhuman primates" restricted live wildlife, held only under a special license, with no exemption written for a pet.

## Where the Order Is the Rule

The other 18 jurisdictions that restrict a capuchin do it with a sentence that never needed to know the species. California restricts "Order Primates-Monkeys, Apes: All species" and carves out only humans. Pennsylvania prohibits "The order Primates. All families of nonhuman primates." Wyoming says a permit "shall not be issued" for "All members of the order nonhuman primates". Utah, Colorado, Maine, Rhode Island, Alaska, Nebraska, the District of Columbia and New York City reach the same answer by a closed list, a research-only tier or an import list with no primate on it. Oregon, Washington, Illinois, Minnesota, Kentucky, Louisiana and West Virginia ban with a grandfather window that closed between 1995 and 2022 and lets no new animal in. In every one of them the capuchin's answer is the primate order's answer, and the [pet monkey legal guide](/blog/pet-monkey-legal-guide/) carries each state's sentence and its closing date. Nothing in any of those rules turns on the capuchin being a capuchin.

## The Thirteen With No Rule

Wisconsin, Texas, Michigan, Nevada, Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina, Kansas, South Carolina, Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi and Oklahoma restrict a capuchin nowhere in their state law. Texas is the clearest, because its list is closed and short: the primates on § 822.101(4) are the four above, so the certificate of registration and the insurance never attach to a capuchin, and nothing in the Parks and Wildlife Code reaches a nonnative primate. Tennessee sends "Primates not otherwise listed" to Class III, which needs no wildlife permit. Mississippi's inherently dangerous list stops at the apes, macaques, baboons and the gelada. South Carolina's 2018 ban reaches primates only through the great apes. Nevada and Oklahoma name primates to clear them. Virginia's exotic permit table, Alabama's restricted list and Indiana's three class lists name carnivores, rodents and reptiles and nothing with hands. Kansas lets lawfully obtained exotic wildlife be possessed "without limit in time and number". Wisconsin frees every nonnative wild animal that is not harmful or an endangered species, and a capuchin is neither. Michigan's Large Carnivore Act names cats and bears. North Carolina hands dangerous animals to its counties under § 153A-131.

Thirteen states with no rule is not thirteen states where a capuchin is safe to buy. Primates appear on municipal exotic animal lists more often than almost any other pet, Texas and Nevada both say in terms that a city or county may prohibit what the state does not, and in a state with no primate sentence the city code is the whole answer.

## The Takeaway

The capuchin is banned in 28 jurisdictions and permitted in nine, and the reason it has a page of its own is the handful of places where the law knew its name. Georgia, New York and Maryland wrote an exception for the trained assistance capuchin and nothing for the pet. Ohio named four capuchins for registration and left the definition that bars them standing beside it. Arkansas and Idaho named it to permit it, Florida caught it by not naming it at all, and Texas left it off a list that stops at the baboon. Underneath every one of those, the federal import rule decides that a lawful capuchin is a US-bred capuchin.
