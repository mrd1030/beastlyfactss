The reticulated python is the longest snake in the world, and its legal question is the Burmese python's question three years later. On 10 March 2015 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service published a final rule adding it to the injurious wildlife list under the Lacey Act, with three anacondas, effective 9 April 2015, and the listing did what the 2012 Burmese listing did: it barred importing the species into the United States and shipping it to Hawaii, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico or a territory, and it never touched ownership. In 2017 the D.C. Circuit held in *United States Association of Reptile Keepers v. Zinke* that the shipment clause does not reach transport between the continental states either, and the [Burmese python legal guide](/blog/burmese-python-legal-guide/) carries that story, the Service's implementation guidance, and what the listing still does. For this animal the short version is that a reticulated python can cross from Texas to Oklahoma, cannot cross from Jakarta to Los Angeles, and is otherwise a state question.

The state answer is a wider spread than most keepers expect. Twenty-five jurisdictions have no rule reaching the species. Eleven ban it, ten issue a permit, four attach a condition, Arkansas allows one only from a breeder permitted before 21 May 2020, and Idaho's rule turns on a word it does not define. Two states answer with a tape measure: Ohio at twelve feet and Missouri at eight. Two more bar it without naming it, by pointing at the federal list. The [green anaconda](/blog/green-anaconda-legal-guide/), listed in the same 2015 rule, has a row that matches this one in 50 of 52 cells, and the two differences are both about Colorado's definition of the tropics.

## Ohio Measures the Animal, and Measures It Early

Ohio is the only state that answers by length rather than by species, and for a reticulated python the length arrives sooner than for anything else on the list. R.C. 935.01(L) provides:

> "Restricted snake" means any of the following: (1) All of the following constricting snakes that are twelve feet or longer: ... (c) Reticulated pythons;

A hatchling is unregulated. At twelve feet the same snake is a restricted snake, and the keeper must hold a restricted snake possession permit under § 935.08, obtained within 120 days of acquiring the snake if it was already that long, with proof of financial responsibility, two years' experience or a passed examination, and caging that meets the rules. A healthy reticulated python passes twelve feet in its first few years and a large female can double it, so Ohio's rule is a permit requirement with a delayed start date, and the sensible reading for anyone in Ohio is to apply before the snake makes the question urgent.

Missouri's line is lower and its consequence lighter. RSMo § 578.023 makes it an offense to keep "any deadly or dangerous reptile over eight feet long" outside a zoo, circus, laboratory or similar institution "unless he or she has registered such animals with the local law enforcement agency in the county in which the animal is kept". No state permit, no fee, and as a nonnative reptile the snake needs no Wildlife Code permit either. Failure to register is the offense, not possession. Delaware draws a five-foot line for a different purpose: 903-7.5.2.10 bars Sales Permit holders from breeding "All giant snakes when average adult body length of the species is larger than 5 feet: Anacondas, Pythons and Boas", a breeding limit on one permit class rather than a bar on keeping one, and the keeper's own Individual Permit is unaffected by the measurement.

## Florida's Conditional-Species History

Florida is where the reticulated python's reputation was made, and the state has changed its mind about the animal once. Before the 2021 rewrite of Chapter 68-5, the reticulated python was a conditional species, one of the reptiles of concern held under permit for commercial and exhibition purposes after the 2010 law closed new personal possession, with the Burmese python beside it. The rewrite moved both to the prohibited list. 68-5.006(4) now provides that live specimens of the listed species, "including their taxonomic successors, subspecies, or hybrids or eggs thereof may be possessed only pursuant to permit issued by the Executive Director except as provided in Rule 68-5.007", and item (f) is "Reticulated python (Python reticulatus)". The taxonomic-successors clause matters here because the species was moved to the genus *Malayopython* in 2014, and a seller's paperwork may carry either name; Florida reaches both.

68-5.007 is the grandfather, and it is written to close. Only a person in lawful possession before the species was listed may keep it, for that animal's life, PIT-tagged, with no further specimens acquired. The permit does not transfer and does not renew into a new animal. Florida's venomous reptile permit, the demanding one with 1,000 hours of experience behind it, is a different permit for a different list and does not reach a constrictor.

<FunFact>
Two states have the species on their books under a name science has retired, and two more under a spelling that was never right. Connecticut's Category Two lists "reticulated python (Python reticulates)", with an "e" where an "u" belongs, and opens its definition "notwithstanding changes in taxonomic nomenclature", which saves the typo and the genus change at once. New York names "Reticulated Python (Python reticulatus)" in the statute itself, Texas names "Python reticulatus" at § 43.851, and Florida's rule reaches "taxonomic successors" so that *Malayopython reticulatus* is caught by a row that spells it the old way. Arkansas is the only state on the map that wrote the new genus into its list.

## Iowa Names Three Snakes and This Is the First

Iowa's dangerous wild animal chapter defines its constrictors by naming them, and it names very few. § 717F.1(5)(a)(9)(e) reaches:

> A member of the superfamily henophidia, which are limited to reticulated pythons, anacondas, and African rock pythons.

That is why Iowa is a ban for this animal and a free state for the Burmese python, which is on neither list. § 717F.3 bars owning, possessing, breeding or bringing a dangerous wild animal into the state, and the only private route was the § 717F.4 registration of animals already held on 1 July 2007, with an electronic identification device implanted within sixty days and registration with the department by the end of that year. No new route exists.

Five more states name the species outright. New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(5) lists "Reticulated Python (Python reticulatus)" in the statute's own list of wild animals that may not be kept as pets, and the department's regulation names it again. Maine's Category 1 lists "Python reticulatus Reticulated Python" beside the Indian and the two African rock pythons, and § 7.18(2)(A) catches it a second time as federally injurious; Category 1 permits go only to exhibitors, rehabilitators, laboratories and research facilities. Connecticut's Category Two names it and allows the category only to institutions. New Mexico files "Python reticulatus" in Group IV, closed to the public, while the ball python sits in Group I. Montana's ARM 12.6.2215(6)(o) lists "Reticulated python - Python reticulatus" on the prohibited reptile list, which overrides the statute's general clearance of nonvenomous pythons. New York City bars "any member of the family Pythonidae" with no size line, and Hawaii bars every snake.

Alabama and Wyoming bar it without naming it. Alabama's 220-2-.26(1) reaches "Any species of bird, mammal, fish, crustacea, mollusk, reptile, or amphibian listed as injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act (18 U.S.C. 42) from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service", and Wyoming's Chapter 69 § 8(a) says an importation or possession permit "shall not be issued" for "Injurious wildlife listed in 50 CFR 16". Neither state ever debated the reticulated python. The 2015 listing banned it in both on the day it took effect, and the same two cells turned for the green anaconda on the same day.

## Arkansas Froze Its Permits in 2020

Arkansas is the one state that reads as "restricted" rather than banned or permitted, and the reason is a date. Addendum R1.03 opens:

> The Commission will not issue permits for breeding, sale, or importation of the following species other than in accordance with the exceptions listed in Codes 09.07, 09.10, 09.11, and Addenda F1.03, F1.04 (possession of some species may be allowed in accordance with Codes 09.02, 09.05, 09.08, and 09.14):

Item 4 lists "Large Asian and African Pythons: Reticulated, Burmese, and Rock Pythons (Malayopython reticulatus, Python bivittatus, P. molurus, P. natalensis, P. sebae)" with a double-asterisk note, and the note keeps breeding, sale and importation permits for permittees who held them before 21 May 2020. Possession under Code 09.02 stays open for a non-native animal acquired from a permitted breeder or dealer, so a reticulated python can still be lawfully kept in Arkansas, but it must come from one of the holders the 2020 date left standing, and that pool only shrinks. The green anaconda is on the same list without the asterisk, which is why its Arkansas cell is harder to read.

## North Dakota's New Category

North Dakota rewrote its nontraditional livestock categories, and category 3 now reads:

> b. Category 3 species of nontraditional livestock includes the following species and their hybrids: ... (2) Big cats, including mountain lion, jaguar, leopard, lion, tiger, and cheetah. (3) Bears. (4) Wolves. (5) Venomous reptiles and nonvenomous injurious reptiles. (6) Primates.

"Nonvenomous injurious reptiles" is new, and the rule does not define injurious. The reticulated python is federal injurious wildlife and a giant constrictor on any plain reading of the word, so the map reads it as a category 3 animal needing a license: the owner must hold a nontraditional livestock license from the Board of Animal Health before acquiring one, the board must inspect and approve the premises first under 48.1-09-01-03, and the annual fee is fifteen dollars. The word is the open question. If "injurious" means the federal list, the category reaches exactly the four 2012 snakes and the four 2015 snakes; if it means something broader, the Board decides. Oklahoma's rule keys to the federal list in terms, exempting nonnative reptiles "except those which are biologically capable of establishing self-sustaining populations in the wild of Oklahoma and which may be potentially injurious or detrimental to Oklahoma's wildlife, agriculture or public safety in accordance with existing USDI or APHIS regulations", and losing the exemption means import permits and a breeder's license apply rather than a ban.

The rest of the permit column is familiar from the Burmese guide. Texas names "reticulated python, Python reticulatus" at § 43.851(a)(2)(E) as one of six constrictors needing a controlled exotic snake permit, recreational or commercial. Louisiana's January 2025 rewrite put the species on the restricted nonnative list rather than the prohibited one, so a private keeper may hold one under a Restricted Snake Permit, required "regardless of size in length", with 500 hours of documented experience, locked enclosures in a locked room and an inspection before issue. New Jersey's 4.3 list takes the whole family Pythonidae on the individual hobby permit. Rhode Island's boa and python exemption names "Reticulated Python (Python reticulatus)" as one of the species that need an exotic animal possession permit, open to private persons. Delaware exempts no snake, so an Individual Permit, issued per animal for three years after the Department is satisfied with the enclosure. West Virginia reaches it as wildlife from outside the state, which needs an importation permit the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for. Massachusetts and Vermont both name the species on their lists and both say the permit is not for a pet.

## The Twenty-Five With No Rule

Twenty-five jurisdictions have no state rule reaching the reticulated python, and the reasons are the Burmese python's reasons. California restricts reptiles by family and Pythonidae is not on the list. Georgia names two pythons in its license tier, the Indian and the Burmese, and not this one. Colorado's Appendix A clears pythons "native only to the tropical and subtropical region", which Chapter W-7 fixes at 26 degrees north to 26 degrees south, and CPW has ruled the reticulated python inside it. Illinois's Herptiles-Herps Act says "nothing shall prohibit lawfully acquired possession of any of the Boidae family, such as boas, pythons, and anacondas", and § 15-10 then requires a nonnative boa or python of any length to be kept in a strong, impact-resistant, escape-proof enclosure. Nevada clears every "Nonvenomous, nonindigenous reptile" with no size line, Oregon's noncontrolled list clears all nonnative boas and pythons, New Hampshire clears exotic reptiles except alligators, crocodiles and venomous species, Alaska's clean list clears "Any nonvenomous reptile", and Tennessee's Class III takes nonpoisonous reptiles "except caimans and gavials". Utah, Kansas, Kentucky, South Carolina, Indiana, Mississippi and Nebraska have venomous or crocodilian tiers and no constrictor tier. Pennsylvania, the District of Columbia, Wisconsin, Arizona, Washington, Michigan, Virginia, Maryland and South Dakota have no text that reaches a nonvenomous exotic snake at all. Local ordinances were not checked in any of them, and a twenty-foot snake is on more city codes than most.

Idaho is the one state that cannot be filed. Its conventional pets exemption covers "non-venomous or non-dangerous reptiles and amphibians", and a reticulated python is non-venomous, which on the literal wording makes it a conventional pet exempt from any permit. "Non-dangerous" is defined nowhere, and a giant constrictor is the animal the word appears aimed at. The map records Idaho as unclear, the same reading the Burmese python gets, and a keeper there should ask Fish and Game in writing before relying on either half of the sentence.

## What This Adds Up To

The federal listing sounds like a ban and is not one. It stops a reticulated python at the border and at the water's edge of Hawaii, and since 2017 it does nothing to a snake moving between Texas and Louisiana. The states that bar this animal mostly did it themselves, by name, in Florida, New York, Connecticut, Maine, New Mexico, Montana and Iowa, and two more did it by pointing at the list. Ohio and Missouri decided that the snake's length was the question, Arkansas decided that the date of the breeder's permit was, and North Dakota wrote a word it has not yet defined. None of which is a recommendation. A reticulated python is the longest snake in the world, with a husbandry and safety profile to match, and legality is the easiest question it raises.
