An American alligator is two animals to the law. In the ten states where it lives wild it is a game animal, with a hunting season, a tag system, a farming industry and a chapter of its own in the code, and that chapter decides what a private person may do with a live one. Everywhere else it is a crocodilian, which is the word the dangerous-animal lists use, and that word is the first entry on most of them. The two kinds of rule reach the same result from opposite directions: thirty jurisdictions ban a pet alligator, nine issue a permit, and most of those permits are written for a farmer, an exhibitor or a researcher. The [caiman](/blog/caiman-legal-guide/), the alligator's twin on this map, is an exotic everywhere and gets the dangerous-animal answer alone, which is why it is legal in fourteen jurisdictions where the alligator is legal in six.

There is a federal layer, and it is an odd one. The American alligator was listed as endangered in 1967, recovered, and was moved in 1987 to threatened "due to similarity of appearance", a status that exists because a live alligator or its hide is hard to tell from a protected crocodile or caiman. The listing in 50 CFR 17.11 does not reach a captive animal on its own, and the [federal laws guide](/blog/federal-exotic-pet-laws-guide/) sets out why a similarity listing is a trade rule rather than a possession rule. What it does is turn every state rule that points at the federal list into an alligator rule, and three states on the map are decided that way.

## Where the Alligator Is a Game Animal

Florida, Louisiana, Texas, Alabama, South Carolina, Arkansas, Mississippi and Oklahoma all have alligators in the wild, and each answers a live one through the game code rather than, or as well as, through a dangerous-animal list. The results run from a personal permit to a flat bar.

Florida stacks two rules. The reptile rule, 68A-25.002(1), provides:

> No person shall buy, sell, take, possess, transport, release, or import any American alligator, or any part thereof, or the nests or eggs of any American alligator except under permit from the executive director, as otherwise provided by this Title, or as follows:

The exceptions that follow are for exhibitors and licensed sellers of alligator products. The captive wildlife rule then puts "Alligators, caimans (family Alligatoridae)" at Class II item 33, and 68A-6.003(7) makes a Permit to Possess Class II Wildlife for Personal Use the route: $140 a year, an applicant of 18 with documented experience with the species, and caging that meets 68A-6.004. On the text that personal permit is the only keeper's route, and the map records Florida as a permit state. Whether the Commission issues it for a native alligator rather than steering applicants to exhibitor licensing is worth confirming with the agency before buying a hatchling, because the alligator rule's own exceptions never mention a pet.

Louisiana's answer is the farming industry's answer. LAC 76:V.701 provides:

> No person other than a licensed alligator farmer or licensed nongame quadruped exhibitor shall possess live alligators at any time other than by a permit issued by the department upon request for use in displays and educational purposes, and by holders of valid department issued permits for scientific purposes.

An alligator farmer's license and a nongame quadruped exhibitor's license are business licenses with facility requirements, and the display, educational and scientific permits are issued on request for those purposes. Bringing a live alligator into the state needs written permission besides. Texas is the same shape with a shorter sentence: the alligator has its own subchapter of the wildlife rules, which governs its possession to the exclusion of other licensing laws, and 31 TAC § 65.353(b) provides that "Except as provided in this subchapter or Parks and Wildlife Code, Chapter 43, Subchapter C, no person may possess a live alligator". The subchapter's permits are for alligator farmers, dealers and nuisance control hunters, and Chapter 43, Subchapter C covers scientific research, zoological collection, rehabilitation and educational display. Texas's dangerous wild animal list, the one that names tigers and cougars, names no reptile at all, so a caiman in Texas is unregulated while a native alligator is barred.

Alabama bars it by designation. Rule 220-2-.06 provides:

> The following animals are hereby designated as game animals: Bear, Beaver, Coyote, Deer, Opossum, Eastern Cottontail and Swamp Rabbit, Raccoon, Squirrel, Nutria, Fox, Mountain Lion (Cougar), Groundhog, Bobcat, Red Wolf, Feral Swine (Wild Hog or Wild Pig), and Alligator.

Rule 220-2-.26(3) then makes it unlawful "to have in possession any live, protected wild bird or wild animal" except under a permit issued before that amendment, which is the reading that bars a live black bear and a live raccoon in Alabama as well. South Carolina lists the alligator first among its nongame species in need of management in Regulation 123-150.2, and § 50-15-20(C) makes possessing one unlawful except as the regulations provide; the permits under § 50-15-40(D) are scientific, zoological, educational and propagation, plus an alligator propagator permit for a licensed facility with setbacks and a five-foot chain-link fence, and none is a pet permit. Arkansas's Code 12.01 makes it unlawful to "hunt, buy, sell or possess an American alligator (Alligator mississippiensis) or other crocodilian species", with one live-animal exception for display and education at established facilities such as parks, zoos, aquariums and nature centers.

Mississippi and Oklahoma are the two native states whose rules stop short. Mississippi's nongame rule carves out "American Alligator" as a species regulated elsewhere, Rule 8.4's closed list of game and furbearing animals in captivity does not include it, and the only other alligator rules on the department's record are the hunting season rules, which govern harvest under an alligator possession permit and not the keeping of a live animal. Oklahoma's 800:25-7-7(8) says "It is unlawful to possess an American Alligator without appropriate permit from the Department" and keeps a statewide closed season, so one may not be taken from the wild, and the rule does not say which permit or who may hold it. The map records Mississippi as unclear and Oklahoma as a permit state on the strength of that sentence.

## The Listing That Exists Because of Other Crocodilians

The American alligator's row in 50 CFR 17.11 reads "Alligator, American | Alligator mississippiensis | Wherever found | T(S/A)", and the parenthetical is the whole story. The species was listed as endangered in 1967, recovered across its range, and was reclassified in 1987 as threatened due to similarity of appearance, a status the Endangered Species Act allows for an animal that is not itself at risk but is hard to tell from one that is. A live alligator looks like a live crocodile or caiman, and an alligator hide looks like theirs, so the listing keeps the trade in protected crocodilians from passing as alligator. It does not, on its own, reach a captive alligator in a keeper's pond.

It reaches one the moment a state rule points at the list. Wyoming's Chapter 69 § 8 provides:

> An importation/possession permit shall not be issued for importation or possession of the live cold-blooded wildlife in this section, except as otherwise specified. ... (d) Endangered and threatened wildlife listed in 50 CFR 17.11.

The bar takes in threatened as well as endangered, and the alligator is listed threatened, so the free reptile clause in § 10 does not reach it and no permit may issue. Nebraska's statute does the same with one more step. Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-477(1) provides:

> No person shall keep in captivity in this state any wild birds, any wild mammals, any nongame wildlife in need of conservation as determined by the commission under section 37-805, or any wildlife determined to be an endangered or threatened species under the Endangered Species Act or section 37-806 without first having obtained a permit to do so as provided by section 37-478 or 37-479.

Section 37-806(1) makes every federally threatened species threatened in Nebraska, so the alligator needs a captive permit there, and the captive wildlife rules name no reptile, so whether and how that permit would issue for a pet is not settled. North Carolina's § 113-129(15) counts as wild animals any wild reptiles "on the federal list of endangered or threatened species", which makes a live alligator a captivity-license question, and § 113-272.5(a) tells the Executive Director not to issue that license for an animal acquired "merely as a pet". Wisconsin's § 169.01(9) defines an endangered or threatened species as one "indigenous to the United States or Canada" and "identified on the federal list of endangered and threatened species", which the alligator is on both counts, and the statute does not say whether a similarity-of-appearance listing counts; the map records Wisconsin as unclear. New Mexico's importation list marks the alligator "Federally Threatened" and files it in Group IV either way.

<FunFact>
The alligator is the one animal on this map that is banned in Texas and unregulated in Alaska. Texas's alligator proclamation bars possessing a live one outside farming, dealing, control hunting, research and display, because the state has a wild population to manage and a farming industry to license. Alaska's clean list at 5 AAC 92.029(b) clears "Any nonvenomous reptile" and glosses the class as "crocodile, alligator, snake, turtle, or lizard", so an American alligator may be possessed, imported and sold there without a permit, with the only caveat that an endangered species still needs a federal permit, and the alligator is not one. The state with the most alligators and the state with the fewest answer the question in opposite directions, and both are reading their own wildlife code correctly.
</FunFact>

## Permit on Paper, Never Issued to a Hobbyist

Nine jurisdictions read as permit states, and a keeper should sort them before counting on one. Rhode Island and Delaware are real: Rhode Island's reptile exemption list has no crocodilian, so an alligator is an exotic animal needing an exotic animal possession permit, which § 3.9(A) makes available to private persons for animals § 3.7 does not expressly prohibit; Delaware's reptile class expressly includes crocodilians and exempts none, so an Individual Permit, issued per animal for three years after the Department is satisfied the animal will be held in a sturdy, escape-proof primary enclosure inside a secondary one that keeps the public out. Indiana's Class III permit is real and arrives at five feet: 312 IAC 9-11-8(a)(5) lists "Crocodilians that are at least five (5) feet long", so a hatchling needs nothing and an animal at five feet needs a permit obtained before taking possession, with a recapture plan and a cage inspection. Florida's Class II permit is real on the text, with the caveat above.

The rest are permits a hobbyist is unlikely to hold. North Carolina's captivity license is withheld from anything acquired "merely as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 has the Commissioner refuse any captivity permit except for science or education, and the alligator is restricted by absence from all three of Vermont's lists. Virginia's 4VAC15-30-40 lists "Alligators, caimans" in its exotic table and issues the special permit "if consistent with the department's fish and wildlife management program", without saying whether a private keeper qualifies. Oklahoma's permit is named and not described. West Virginia reaches an alligator as wildlife from outside the state, which needs an importation permit the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for. Missouri sits beside them as the one conditional cell: § 578.023 requires "any deadly or dangerous reptile over eight feet long" to be registered with county law enforcement, the statute does not define a dangerous reptile, and whether a four-foot alligator must be registered is not settled, though one over eight feet plainly must be.

## Where the Word Is Crocodilian

Outside the native range the alligator is caught by the crocodilian entry, and that entry is usually the first reptile on the list. California restricts "Order Crocodilia-Crocodiles, Caimans, Alligators and Gavials: All species (D)" with no permit type for a pet. Georgia's DNR names the alligator first on its native reptile list of animals that may not be a pet "regardless of its origin or morphology", and its wild animal rule puts every crocodilian in the inherently dangerous tier. Hawaii lists "Alligator mississippiensis alligator, American" on Part A, for research and exhibition only. New York City's Health Code bars "all members of the order Crocodylia, including, but not limited to, alligator, caiman and crocodile". Connecticut's Category Two names "alligator, crocodile and caiman" and allows the category only to institutions. Washington's RCW 16.30.010 lists "Order crocodilia, all species, such as crocodiles, alligators, caimans, and gavials", closed since 2007. Nevada's prohibited list opens its reptile section with "Alligators, crocodiles and gharials" and the whole order. Maryland's Criminal Law § 10-621 bars a live "caiman, alligator, or crocodile". Iowa's crocodilian clause names alligators and closed in 2007. Kentucky's inherently dangerous list opens with "Alligators or caimans (Family Alligatoridae)", and since the alligator has never lived wild in Kentucky it is exotic wildlife there. Illinois defines crocodilians as "any species of the Order Crocodilia, such as crocodiles, alligators, caimans, and gavials", special use herptiles allowed only for educational programs, and may not be bred or sold. Utah's R657-53-9(1)(b) classifies "Species of the Order Crocodilia (crocodiles, gharials, caimans, and alligators)" as prohibited. Montana prohibits the "Alligatoridae family". Ohio names alligators as dangerous wild animals, closed to new keepers since 1 January 2014. Tennessee's Class I is "Order Crocodylia: Crocodiles and alligators--All species", open only to zoos, circuses and commercial propagators. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Alligator Alligatoridae, spp" for the exhibitor category alone. Oregon's exotic animal definition takes "Any member of the order Crocodylia" with a permit window that closed in 2010.

The closed-list jurisdictions do not need the word. The District of Columbia admits seven categories of pet and no crocodilian fits one. New Jersey's potentially dangerous table lists "Alligatoridae--Alligators and caiman" and 7:25-4.9(a)3 rules out keeping one as a pet. Maine's Category 1 takes "All Species in Order Crocodilia" and issues Category 1 permits only to exhibitors, rehabilitators, laboratories and research facilities. New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(6) lists "Crocodylia" among the orders that are wild animals, and § 11-0512 bars keeping a wild animal as a pet. MassWildlife says "You cannot have any crocodilian species in your possession in Massachusetts. This includes Alligators Crocodiles Caimans Gavials". Colorado's CPW rules on the species by name: "Not an authorized species for private possession. CPW license required", and CPW issues no license for a pet. Arizona restricts the whole order Crocodilia and none of its exemptions is a pet route.

## The Six With No Rule, and the Five That Cannot Decide

Six jurisdictions have no state rule reaching an American alligator. Alaska's clean list clears "Any nonvenomous reptile" and glosses it as "crocodile, alligator, snake, turtle, or lizard". Kansas's K.A.R. 115-20-3 lets exotic wildlife be possessed "without limit in time and number" if legally obtained where it came from, and the state's dangerous regulated animal statute reaches only big cats, bears and non-native venomous snakes. Minnesota's possession bar reaches protected wild animals, and § 97A.015 subd. 39 protects turtles, snakes and lizards, not crocodilians, so an alligator is an unprotected wild animal outside the bar. Pennsylvania has no text naming a crocodilian, Michigan's Fisheries Order governs natives taken from the wild, and South Dakota's bar is written for mammals. Local ordinances were not checked in any of them, and an alligator is on more city codes than almost any animal a keeper might bring home.

Five more have text that will not decide. Wisconsin and Nebraska turn on whether a similarity-of-appearance listing counts as the federal list, and the statutes do not say. North Dakota's category 3 reaches "Venomous reptiles and nonvenomous injurious reptiles", the alligator is not federal injurious wildlife, and the rule does not define the word; if it means the federal list the alligator is in no category and needs only an importation permit number and a veterinary certificate, and if it means harmful a crocodilian is the obvious case. Mississippi's rules reach the alligator only to hunt it. Idaho's conventional pets exemption covers "non-venomous or non-dangerous reptiles and amphibians", and non-dangerous is defined nowhere. The [snapping turtle](/blog/snapping-turtle-legal-guide/) is the other native reptile on this map answered mostly by fishing rules, and its row shows what the alligator's would look like without a farming industry: a bag limit in most states and a dangerous-animal entry in almost none.

## The Takeaway

Read the game code first in the states where alligators live, because it is the whole answer there: a farmer's license in Louisiana, a proclamation in Texas, a game designation in Alabama, a nongame listing in South Carolina, a Class II permit in Florida that the agency may or may not issue to a keeper. Read the crocodilian entry everywhere else, and expect it to be the first reptile on the list. Then read the federal row for what it is: a similarity listing that bars nothing by itself and bars an alligator in Wyoming, Nebraska and North Carolina the moment the state points at it. Our [interactive map of alligator laws](/exotic-pet-laws/american-alligator/) shades every jurisdiction and quotes the sentence each answer comes from, and for this animal the sentence is as likely to be in the alligator chapter as in the exotic animal law.
