The Big Cat Public Safety Act amended the Lacey Act so that 16 U.S.C. 3372(e) makes it unlawful to breed or possess a lion outside licensed exhibitors, sanctuaries, state institutions and veterinarians, and the lion is the first species the Fish and Wildlife Service lists. A private owner could keep a lion born before 20 December 2022 by registering it with the Service by 18 June 2023, and that window is closed. The lion is the first animal on the Big Cat Public Safety Act's list, and since 20 December 2022 its legal question has had the same federal answer as the tiger's: no private person may breed or possess one, the only exceptions are licensed exhibitors, sanctuaries, state institutions and veterinarians, and the only private lions left are the ones born before that date and registered with the Fish and Wildlife Service by 18 June 2023. Under state law alone the lion is banned in 35 jurisdictions, a permit animal in ten, restricted in one, unclear in one and unregulated in five.

Those are the tiger's numbers with one cell moved, and the cell moved because of a federal listing that cuts the species in half. The tiger is listed as endangered "wherever found" in one row of 50 CFR 17.11. The lion is listed in two rows: *Panthera leo leo*, the lions of India and of West and Central Africa, as endangered, and *Panthera leo melanochaita*, the lions of eastern and southern Africa, as threatened, both by a rule published on 23 December 2015. Every state that bars the tiger by pointing at the federal endangered list has to decide what to do with an animal that is on the list twice under different headings, and one of them has not.

## The Shape of It

Every jurisdiction below names the lion, writes a permit it could take, reaches it through the federal list, or answers it differently from the tiger. The ten jurisdictions that ban it with a family-wide cat clause are listed further down.

## Two Rows on the Federal List

The lion's one difference from the [tiger](/blog/tiger-legal-guide/) comes from Washington rather than from any state capital. The Fish and Wildlife Service's 2015 rule listed the lion in two parts, and 50 CFR 17.11 now carries "Lion | Panthera leo leo | Wherever found | E" and, on the next line, "Lion | Panthera leo melanochaita | Wherever found | T". The first subspecies is the Asiatic lion and the lions of West and Central Africa; the second is the lions of eastern and southern Africa. The tiger sits one row above them, "Panthera tigris | Wherever found | E", listed since 1970. A state that bars endangered species by reference catches every tiger and, on its face, only some lions, and a state that bars endangered and threatened species catches both.

Wyoming is the state that bars both, because it wrote the wider sentence. Chapter 10 § 11(b)(xv):

> (b) In addition, a permit shall not be issued for importation or possession of the following live warm-blooded wildlife in Wyoming, except as otherwise specified in this regulation. ... (xv) Endangered and threatened wildlife listed in 50 CFR 17.11;

Wyoming would otherwise treat a lion as it treats a serval, under the discretionary permit in § 13(d), but the bar takes in threatened as well as endangered, so a lion of either subspecies is caught and the cell is a clean no.

Missouri wrote the narrower sentence, and the lion falls through it. 3 CSR 10-9.110(2):

> (2) Except for federally-designated endangered species and species listed in 3 CSR 10-4.117 and 3 CSR 10-9.240, the following may be bought, sold, possessed, transported, and exhibited without permit: ... amphibians, reptiles, and mammals not native to Missouri except those mammals located in a licensed big game hunting preserve;

A tiger is a federally designated endangered species wherever found, so it is inside the carve-out and left under the general prohibition in 3 CSR 10-4.110(1) on possessing wildlife "except as specifically permitted by these rules". A lion is endangered if it is *Panthera leo leo* and threatened if it is *Panthera leo melanochaita*, and nothing in the rule says how a lion of unrecorded subspecies is treated, or gives a way to assign a captive animal to either row. If it is inside the carve-out it is barred like the tiger; if it is outside, it needs no Wildlife Code permit and only the county registration that RSMo § 578.023 requires of anyone keeping a "lion, tiger, leopard, ocelot, jaguar, cheetah, margay, mountain lion". The map records Missouri as unclear rather than guess, and under the federal Act it makes no practical difference, since no new lion may be privately possessed either way. New Jersey's 7:25-4.10 and Maine's § 7.18(2)(C) both reach "endangered" species by reference as well, and both already bar every felid by family, so the subspecies question never has to be answered there.

## Named First

Where a state wrote a list of big cats, the lion is usually the first word on it, and that is the only way most of these cells differ from the tiger's. Texas's § 822.101(4) opens "(A) a lion; (B) a tiger", so § 822.103's certificate of registration, § 822.107's $100,000 of liability insurance and § 822.116(b)'s local veto apply to a lion exactly as they apply to a tiger, and the [serval legal guide](/blog/serval-legal-guide/) shows the same form in use for a cat the Act never reached. Michigan's Large Carnivore Act defines the term as "a lion, a leopard (including a snow leopard or clouded leopard), a jaguar, a tiger, a cougar, a panther, or a cheetah", and MCL 287.1104(1) lets a person possess one only if they owned that individual animal on the act's effective date in 2000 and applied for a local permit within 90 days. Washington's felid entry reads "only lions, tigers, captive-bred cougars, jaguars, cheetahs, leopards, snow leopards, and clouded leopards". Kansas's definition of a dangerous regulated animal opens "Lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, cheetahs and mountain lions, or any hybrid thereof", with the $250,000 insurance or bond and the 2006 window that go with it. Tennessee's Class I clause reads "Lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, cheetahs, cougars--All species". South Carolina's definition of a large wild cat opens with "all lions". Minnesota and Illinois name lions first, New York City's wild cat clause does too, Utah's prohibited row leads with *Panthera leo*, and North Dakota's category 3 puts "lion" between "leopard" and "tiger". Florida is the one list where the tiger comes first: Class I item 11 is the tiger and item 12 is "Lions (Panthera leo)", and both are closed to personal use since 1 August 1980.

The eight states with a closed window closed it on the same day for both cats. Florida's shut on 1 August 1980, Tennessee's on 25 June 1991, Arkansas's on 12 August 2005, Kansas's on 1 July 2006, Washington's on 22 July 2007, Ohio's on 1 January 2014, South Carolina's on 1 January 2018, and Mississippi's on 20 December 2022, when Rule 8.3 began: "Effective December 20, 2022, with the enacting of the Big Cat Public Safety Act ... no new permits for the transfer or possession of members of the order Carnivora, family Felidae, or 'big cats,' will be issued." Ohio's permit for a 2012 registrant requires sterilizing males, insurance, caging standards and at least an acre, and Kansas's requires the bond.

<FunFact>
New Mexico's importation list names the Asiatic lion and forgets the African one. The Director's Species Importation List carries a row reading "Panthera leo persica | Lion, Asiatic | IV | Federally Endangered" and no row for *Panthera leo* as a species, while the tiger gets "Panthera tigris | Tiger | IV" outright. It makes no difference, because 19.35.7 NMAC says "Any species of live non-domesticated animal not currently on the species importation list will be designated group IV until such time as another determination is made by the director", and Group IV is closed to the general public. A lion from a Texas breeder is barred at the New Mexico line by the default clause rather than by name.

## Where the Lion and the Tiger Are Named Apart

Two states wrote the lion into a sentence that does not mention the tiger, and neither changes the answer. Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(2)(A) makes Category One "A member within the family Felidae (including, but not limited to, lion, leopard, cheetah, jaguar, ocelot, jaguarundi cat, puma, lynx and bobcat) except Bengal cat". The lion is the first example and the tiger is not an example at all, but the words "including, but not limited to" carry it, and subsection (b)(1) allows a Category One animal only to municipal parks, zoos, aquaria, nature centers, museums and USDA-registered exhibitors, laboratories and research facilities, so both cats are banned. New Mexico is above: the tiger is named and the lion is caught by default. Idaho runs the two cats in adjacent entries, "05. Lion (Panthera leo)" and "06. Tiger (Panthera tigris). All tigers and tiger-hybrids", and confines both to AZA-accredited facilities; the lion's entry carries no hybrid clause, so a liger in Idaho is caught by the tiger's line rather than the lion's. Hawaii's Part A lists "Panthera leo lion" beside "Panthera tigris tiger" for universities, government agencies and government zoos, and New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Lion Panthera leo" for permittee category 5, the exhibitor category.

## Where the Family Is the Rule

The other ten jurisdictions ban the lion with a sentence about cats in general. California restricts "Family Felidae-All species (W)" and carves out only domestic cats and their hybrids. Georgia puts "Family Felidae (cats) - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, licensed only to the wild animal trade and exhibitors. New York's ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(2) reaches "Felidae and all hybrids thereof" but *Felis catus*, Maryland bars any "member of the cat family other than the domestic cat", and Nebraska's § 37-477(2) bans keeping "any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae". Oregon's exotic animal definition takes every felid not indigenous to Oregon, with a window that closed in 2010. Colorado will not license a pet regulated mammal, Rhode Island refuses a private permit for any carnivore without a licensed rabies vaccine, Alaska cannot permit a game animal as a pet, the District of Columbia's closed list admits domestic cats only, and Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora. The [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) carries each clause in full, and the [cougar legal guide](/blog/cougar-legal-guide/) shows what happens to the same clauses when the cat is native: the lion and the tiger are exotics everywhere, so the family-wide rule is the whole of their state answer, while a cougar in Wisconsin, Nevada, Alabama or Oklahoma is reached by the game code the lion never meets.

## Five States With No Lion Rule

Wisconsin, Nevada, Alabama, Oklahoma and North Carolina have no state rule reaching a lion, and they are the tiger's five. Wisconsin frees every nonnative wild animal that is not harmful or an endangered species, confines the last term to animals indigenous to the United States or Canada, and designates only the cougar harmful among cats. Nevada's NAC 503.140(1)(q) clears "All felines, except mountain lions and bobcats". Alabama's restricted list names "black bear (Ursus americanus), mountain lion (Felis concolor), bobcat (Felis rufus)" and no other cat. Oklahoma's exemption list clears "Cats (except native cats and bears)", and 29 O.S. § 7-502(A)(3) reaches only a native cat over fifty pounds. North Carolina hands dangerous animals to its counties under § 153A-131. In those five the federal Act is the only law on the subject, and it says no; the [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) carries its text, the Service's guidance, and the full 52-jurisdiction table for the lion, tiger and cougar together.

## The Takeaway

The lion is the tiger with one cell moved. Thirty-five bans, ten permits, one restriction and five silences are the tiger's, drawn by the same sentences, and the lion is usually the first word in them. The moved cell is Missouri, and it moved because the federal government listed the lion twice, as endangered in one subspecies and threatened in the other, while Missouri's rule asks only whether an animal is endangered. Wyoming asked a wider question and got a clean answer. Under the Act none of it decides whether a lion can be acquired, because none can; it decides what a registered lion in Texas, Kansas or Ohio still owes the state.
