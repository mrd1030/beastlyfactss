The green anaconda is the heaviest snake in the world and the second anaconda the federal government listed. The yellow anaconda went onto the injurious wildlife list on 23 January 2012, in the same rule as the Burmese python, because it was the anaconda already turning up in south Florida. The green anaconda followed on 9 April 2015, in the rule that also listed the reticulated python and two anacondas almost nobody has ever kept, DeSchauensee's and the Beni. The listing does what every injurious listing does: it bars importing the species into the United States and shipping it to Hawaii, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico or a territory, and it has never prohibited ownership. In 2017 the D.C. Circuit held in *United States Association of Reptile Keepers v. Zinke* that the shipment clause does not reach transport between the continental states.

What is left is a state question, and the state answer is nearly the reticulated python's. Twenty-four jurisdictions have no rule reaching a green anaconda. Eleven ban it, ten issue a permit, four attach a condition, Arkansas will issue no new breeder or import permit for one, and two states cannot decide. The two rows differ in exactly two cells. Colorado has ruled the reticulated python inside its tropical clearance and has not ruled on the anaconda. Arkansas froze its python permits at a 2020 date and gave the anaconda no date at all. Everywhere else the two snakes get the same sentence, usually because the sentence names a family, a genus, or a length.

## Two Listings, Four Anacondas, and the Two Nobody Sells

The genus *Eunectes* has four species and the federal list carries all of them, in two rules written three years apart. The 2012 rule listed the yellow anaconda, *Eunectes notaeus*, with the Burmese, Northern African and Southern African pythons, because it was already loose in the Everglades watershed. The Service deferred the other five constrictors it had proposed, and the 2015 rule, "Injurious Wildlife Species; Listing Three Anaconda Species and One Python Species as Injurious Reptiles", picked up four of them: the reticulated python, the green anaconda, DeSchauensee's anaconda, *Eunectes deschauenseei*, and the Beni or Bolivian anaconda, *Eunectes beniensis*. The [reticulated python legal guide](/blog/reticulated-python-legal-guide/) walks the same 2015 rule for the snake listed beside the green anaconda, and the [Burmese python guide](/blog/burmese-python-legal-guide/) carries the Service's implementation guidance in full. The boa constrictor was proposed in the same petition and never listed, which is why it sits on a different map entirely.

DeSchauensee's and the Beni anaconda are listed for completeness rather than for the trade. Neither is bred commercially in the United States, neither appears on any state list by name except Connecticut's, which writes "Bolivian anaconda (Eunectus beniensis)" into its Category Two, and a keeper who thinks they have one almost certainly has a yellow or a green. The listing matters because it closes the import door for the whole genus, so that no anaconda of any species can be lawfully brought into the country without a permit, and because several states copy the federal list by reference. Alabama's 220-2-.26(1) bars any reptile "listed as injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act (18 U.S.C. 42) from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service", and Wyoming's Chapter 69 § 8(a) says a permit "shall not be issued" for "Injurious wildlife listed in 50 CFR 16". In both, the yellow anaconda was banned in January 2012 and the green in April 2015, with no state rulemaking either time.

The states that wrote their own rules mostly wrote the genus. Maine's Category 1 lists "Eunectes spp. All Species in Genus Eunectes", and § 7.18(2)(A) catches the green a second time as federally injurious; Category 1 permits go only to exhibitors, rehabilitators, laboratories and research facilities. New Mexico's importation list files "Eunectes ssp" in Group IV, closed to the public. Vermont's Restricted list names "Eunectes, all species, Anacondas". Massachusetts's boa and python clearance at 321 CMR 9.01(10)(b) reads:

> All venomous snakes require a permit. The following snakes may be kept without a permit: 1. All species of boas and pythons (family Boidae), except those species categorically non-exempt pursuant to 321 CMR 9.01(3), and except the African Rock Python (Python sebae), Reticulated Python (Python reticulatus) and all species of Anaconda (Eunectes spp.), which require a permit.

The permit under M.G.L. c. 131, § 23 exists on paper, and MassWildlife says it will not be issued for keeping a wild animal as a pet. Rhode Island's rule copies the Massachusetts sentence nearly word for word and reaches the opposite result, because § 3.9(A) makes the exotic animal possession permit available to private persons:

> (A) All species of boas and pythons (family Boidae), may be kept without an exotic animal possession permit except those species that are endangered, and except the Emerald Tree Boa (Corallus caninus), Green Tree Python (Chondropython spp.), African Rock Python (Python sebae), Reticulated Python (Python reticulatus) and all species of Anaconda (Eunectes spp.).

Louisiana's January 2025 rewrite put "All species in the Genus Eunectes (Anacondas)" on the restricted nonnative list rather than the prohibited one, so a private keeper may hold one under a Restricted Snake Permit, which LAC 76:XV.101.K.1 requires for the listed large constrictors "regardless of size in length", with 500 hours of documented experience, locked enclosures in a locked room, an inspection before issue and annual renewal.

## Where the Green Is Named and the Yellow Is Not

Montana is the one state that names the green anaconda and stops. ARM 12.6.2215(6)(i) lists "Green Anaconda - Eunectes marinus" on the prohibited reptile list, misspelling the species, and the entry overrides the statute's general clearance of nonvenomous boids. The yellow anaconda appears nowhere in the Montana rule, so under state law it falls back into the cleared class while the green does not. New York and Connecticut name both. ECL § 11-0103(6)(e)(5) lists "Green Anaconda (Eunectes maurinus), Yellow Anaconda (Eunectes notaeus)" in the statute's own list of wild animals, with the department's regulation spelling the green correctly at 180.1(b)(15)(ii). Connecticut's Category Two reads:

> (xxxii) Green or common anaconda (Eunectus murinus), yellow anaconda (Eunectus notaeus) and Bolivian anaconda (Eunectus beniensis),

The genus is misspelled on all three, and the definition applies "notwithstanding changes in taxonomic nomenclature", so the typo does not help. Subsection (b)(3) allows a Category Two animal only to a treating vet, municipal parks, zoos, aquaria, nature centers, museums, exhibitors, laboratories and research facilities. Florida names all four anacondas on its prohibited nonnative list, the green at 68-5.006(4)(k), and 68-5.007 lets only a person in lawful possession before the listing keep that animal for its life, PIT-tagged, with no further specimens. Iowa's constrictor clause, "reticulated pythons, anacondas, and African rock pythons", reaches the genus by its common name, with the only private route the registration of animals held on 1 July 2007. New York City's Health Code bars "any member, or hybrid offspring of the family Boidae, including, but not limited to, the common or green anaconda and yellow anaconda", and Hawaii bars every snake.

<FunFact>
Three statutes name the green anaconda and three spell it wrong, each a different way. New York's statute has "Eunectes maurinus", Montana's rule has "Eunectes marinus", and Connecticut's regulation has the genus as "Eunectus" across three species. The correct name is *Eunectes murinus*, which Texas, North Carolina and Florida all manage. None of the typos opens a door: New York's regulation corrects the statute, Connecticut's definition reaches "changes in taxonomic nomenclature", and Montana's list is read by the Fish, Wildlife and Parks officer, not by a copy editor.
</FunFact>

## Ohio's Line Comes Early for a Female

Ohio answers by length, and the green anaconda heads its list. R.C. 935.01(L) provides:

> "Restricted snake" means any of the following: (1) All of the following constricting snakes that are twelve feet or longer: (a) Green anacondas;

Under twelve feet nothing is required. At twelve feet the keeper needs a restricted snake possession permit under § 935.08, with proof of financial responsibility, two years' experience or a passed examination, and caging to the rules, obtained within 120 days of acquisition if the snake was already that long. Green anacondas are strongly sexually dimorphic, and an adult female routinely passes twelve feet where a male may never reach it, so Ohio's rule divides the species by sex as much as by age. A keeper buying a female should treat the permit as a requirement with a start date they can roughly predict.

Missouri's line is eight feet and its consequence is registration. RSMo § 578.023 makes it an offense to keep "any deadly or dangerous reptile over eight feet long" outside an institution "unless he or she has registered such animals with the local law enforcement agency in the county in which the animal is kept". No state permit, no fee, and no Wildlife Code permit for a nonnative reptile. Delaware's five-foot line is a breeding limit on one permit class: 903-7.5.2.10 bars Sales Permit holders from breeding "All giant snakes when average adult body length of the species is larger than 5 feet: Anacondas, Pythons and Boas". A keeper's own Individual Permit, issued per animal for three years after the Department is satisfied with the primary and secondary enclosures, is unaffected by the measurement, and no snake is exempt from it in Delaware.

## Colorado and Arkansas Are the Two Cells That Differ

The reticulated python and the green anaconda share a federal listing date, a Texas permit clause, an Ohio length, an Iowa sentence and a North Carolina statute, and their rows part in two places.

Colorado's Chapter W-7 clears Boidae on Appendix A, but only "Any species of snake native only to the tropical and subtropical region", which Chapter W-11 defines as "the region between 26.0 degrees north latitude and 26.0 degrees south latitude". Colorado Parks and Wildlife decides that species by species, and its published determinations put the reticulated python inside the band and say nothing about the green anaconda. The species' range is centered on the Amazon and Orinoco basins, and whether any of it falls south of 26 degrees is the question the rule turns on. The map records Colorado as unclear until CPW rules, and CPW takes questions on unlisted species by email. A keeper who gets a written answer has a better record than the map does.

Arkansas is the other. Addendum R1.03 lists "Anacondas (Genus Eunectes)" among the species the Commission will not issue breeding, sale or importation permits for, and unlike the entry for the large Asian and African pythons it carries no asterisk keeping those permits for holders who had them before 21 May 2020. The list's own opening leaves possession open under Code 09.02, which requires a non-native animal to come from a permitted breeder or dealer or in on an importation permit, and for an anaconda neither can now be had. An animal already lawfully held is the case the wording leaves open, which is why the map reads Arkansas as restricted rather than banned. For the reticulated python the pre-2020 holders are still a lawful source; for the anaconda there is no such pool.

Idaho cannot decide either snake. Its conventional pets exemption covers "non-venomous or non-dangerous reptiles and amphibians", a green anaconda is non-venomous, and "non-dangerous" is defined nowhere. The map records it as unclear, and a keeper there should ask Fish and Game in writing.

## The Permits and the Twenty-Four

The rest of the permit column is the giant constrictor column everywhere on this map. Texas's § 43.851(a)(2)(D) names "green anaconda, Eunectes murinus" as one of six constrictors needing a controlled exotic snake permit, recreational or commercial. New Jersey's 4.3 list takes "Boas--Family Boidae (other than Boa Constrictors)" on the individual hobby permit, which the Division's printed list renders as "Bosas". North Dakota's category 3 now reaches "Venomous reptiles and nonvenomous injurious reptiles", a license from the Board of Animal Health before acquisition with an inspection first; the rule does not define injurious, and the green anaconda is federal injurious wildlife on any reading. Oklahoma's reptile exemption drops a species that is "potentially injurious or detrimental to Oklahoma's wildlife, agriculture or public safety in accordance with existing USDI or APHIS regulations", so import permits and a breeder's license apply. West Virginia reaches it as wildlife from outside the state, needing an importation permit the Division of Natural Resources publishes no application for. North Carolina's § 14-417.1 names "Green Anaconda, Eunectes murinus" among its large constricting snakes and requires a sturdy, locked, escape-proof enclosure labeled with the species, number and owner, with a posted safety protocol and escape plan. Minnesota allows a snake "obtained from a permitted breeder" as a pet and has no rule implementing the breeder permit.

Twenty-four jurisdictions have no state rule reaching a green anaconda. California restricts reptiles by family and Boidae is not on the list. Georgia's license tier names two pythons and no boa. Illinois's Herptiles-Herps Act says "nothing shall prohibit lawfully acquired possession of any of the Boidae family, such as boas, pythons, and anacondas", and § 15-10 requires an escape-proof enclosure for one of any length. Nevada clears every "Nonvenomous, nonindigenous reptile", Oregon's noncontrolled list clears all nonnative boas and pythons, New Hampshire clears exotic reptiles except alligators, crocodiles and venomous species, Alaska's clean list clears "Any nonvenomous reptile", and Tennessee's Class III takes nonpoisonous reptiles "except caimans and gavials". Utah, Kansas, Kentucky, South Carolina, Indiana, Mississippi and Nebraska have venomous or crocodilian tiers and no constrictor tier. Pennsylvania, the District of Columbia, Wisconsin, Arizona, Washington, Michigan, Virginia, Maryland and South Dakota have no text that reaches a nonvenomous exotic snake at all. Local ordinances were not checked in any of them.

## What This Adds Up To

A green anaconda crosses the same state lines a reticulated python does and stops at the same border. The federal listing closed the import door for the whole genus in two steps, and since 2017 it does nothing to a snake moving between Texas and Louisiana. The states that bar this animal mostly wrote the genus, so the yellow anaconda is caught wherever the green is, with Montana the one exception. Ohio asks how long the snake is, Missouri asks whether it has been registered, Delaware asks whether a dealer is breeding it, Colorado has not yet asked whether the Orinoco is in the tropics, and Arkansas closed its list without leaving the python's back door. None of which is a recommendation. A green anaconda is a two-hundred-pound aquatic constrictor, and legality is the easiest of the questions it raises.
