<LegalDisclaimer />

A white-tailed deer is the one animal on this map with no clean legal state. Everywhere it lives wild it is big game, usually the first animal the game code names, and the chapter that sets the season is the chapter that says what a private person may do with a live one. Where a state does allow a captive deer, it does not allow it as a pet. It allows it as livestock, behind an eight-foot fence the state has inspected, inside a chronic wasting disease program that tests the animal when it dies, under a license written for a herd. Read across all 52 jurisdictions, that produces 28 bans, 20 permits that are nearly all deer-farm licenses, one condition in Connecticut, two restricted lists in Arizona and South Carolina, and one state, Indiana, whose text will not decide.

The [raccoon](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/) is the animal to read beside this one, because the mechanism is the same and the result is one notch harsher. A raccoon is a furbearer, and a furbearer license costs between two and fifty dollars and covers one animal in a cage. A deer is big game, and the only captive route most states ever built for big game is a farm. The [black bear](/blog/black-bear-legal-guide/) is the other native game animal with no clean legal state, and the deer joins it for a reason no dangerous-animal list would give: it is a disease vector with a hunting season.

## Big Game by Name

The rule that decides most of this map is the game definition, and it reaches a deer bred in a pen as surely as one in the woods. New York's ECL § 11-0103(2)(b) reads:

> "Big game" means deer, bear, moose, elk, except captive bred and raised North American elk (Cervus elaphus), caribou and antelope.

The one captive-bred carve-out in that sentence is for elk, not deer. Game is protected wildlife under § 11-0103(6)(c), § 11-0107(2) bars possessing protected wildlife "whether taken within the state or coming from without the state", and the only license, § 11-0515, is for propagation, banding, scientific or exhibition purposes. Wyoming is blunter. Chapter 10 § 11(a) says "There shall be no private ownership, private importation or private possession of live big or trophy game animals", and deer are big game by statute. Minnesota makes deer big game and then closes the one door the raccoon walks through: § 97A.401 subd. 3(b) says "A special permit may not be issued to take or possess wild or native deer for exhibition, propagation, or as pets". Tennessee's § 70-4-403(4)(B) puts the white-tailed deer in Class IV, "those native species that may be possessed only by zoos and temporary exhibitors", and its Class III takes other cervids "except white-tailed deer and wild elk", which shuts the farm route that farmed elk have. Utah's Table 3b-1 lists "White-tailed deer Odocoileus virginianus" among its prohibited mammals, the tier where "Personal possession will not be authorized". Washington's WAC 220-450-030(2) makes it unlawful to "import into the state, hold, possess, propagate, offer for sale, sell, transfer, or release live specimens" of a list of native cervids that names the white-tailed deer, with written department approval for research or accredited display as the only route. Nevada's NAC 503.110 prohibits "Deer, elk, wapiti, moose and caribou. All species in the family Cervidae", with permits going only to zoos, universities and research. Alaska's clean list has no whitetail, and the department may not permit a game animal "for use as a pet".

Three states had a route and closed it. Montana's MCA § 87-4-407(1) says a person may not operate an alternative livestock ranch "without having first obtained an alternative livestock ranch license from the department prior to November 7, 2000", the date Initiative 143 set, and a privately owned white-tailed deer is alternative livestock that may be kept nowhere else. Louisiana's game breeder rule for deer says "Except as specified herein, licenses will not be issued" beyond applications received by 4:30 p.m. on 4 October 2002, bars any license in metropolitan or urban areas, and bars importing a live white-tailed deer. Arkansas closed both pet routes: "Deer that were captured by hand prior to July 1, 2012, may be retained until their death", a captive-born whitetail counts only if acquired before 30 June 2005 and registered by 30 June 2008, and Addendum R1.03 lists cervids among the species the Commission will not permit for breeding, sale or importation. A grandfathered Arkansas deer lives behind an eight-foot fence and is tested for chronic wasting disease when it dies.

New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Mississippi and Nebraska round out the game-animal bans. New Jersey's Division puts Cervidae on its potentially dangerous table and says possession "will be allowed only within the scientific holding, propagation and sales, zoological, animal exhibitor, animal theatrical and fur farming categories", lets hobbyists who already hold one keep it with no new acquisitions, and has banned importing any member of the deer family because of chronic wasting disease until further notice. Pennsylvania's § 137.31(a) bars possessing wildlife taken from the wild here and § 137.1(a)(11) bars wildlife captive bred in another state; its deer farms run under a separate agricultural scheme, and the Commission issues no new cervid permit inside a disease management area. Mississippi's Rule 8.2 says "No person may possess a live white-tailed deer in Mississippi unless that person possesses a valid permit", and the permit is for a registered high-fenced enclosure of at least 300 acres, with no sale, no import and no deer moved from the wild into it. Nebraska's 166 NAC 8 § 001.07A2 lists "White-tailed Deer (Odocoileus virginianus) constitutes both economic and ecologic threat" among the species that may not be imported, possessed or retained in captivity.

The native-wildlife states need no game definition at all. Georgia's DNR names the white-tailed deer among natives that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology", and the only captive route is a deer-farming license. Rhode Island lists "White-tailed Deer (Odocoileus virginianus)" among native mammals "which possession as pets is prohibited". Virginia carries it on the native fauna list, so § 29.1-521(A)(10) bars possession "except as specifically permitted by law". New Hampshire's Table 800.2 marks "Deer, White-tailed" for permittee category 5, the exhibitors, while opening red deer, sika and elk to propagators in category 2. Maine's Category 1 holds "All Species in Family Cervidae (Deer)", for exhibitors, rehabilitators and laboratories. Delaware's DNREC says state regulations "explicitly forbid Delawareans from owning" native wildlife. Alabama bars "any member of the family Cervidae" from outside the state and keeping a designated game animal alive, with licensed game breeders the one exception to the transport bar. California restricts "Order Artiodactyla-Swine, Peccaries, Camels, Deer, Elk, Moose, Antelopes, Cattle, Goats, Sheep, etc. All species (D)" with no route for a whitetail; Hawaii lists deer one species at a time and never the whitetail, so its default bar applies; New York City bars "All even-toed ungulates (Artiodactyla) including, but not limited to, deer"; the District of Columbia lets hoofed animals in only as goats and sheep brought in temporarily for grazing.

Kansas is the one state whose statute says the word. K.S.A. 47-2101 defines a domesticated deer as any legally obtained cervid "being sold or raised in a confined area for" breeding stock, parts, exhibition "or companionship", and subsection (a) says "It shall be unlawful for any person to possess domesticated deer unless such person has obtained from the animal health commissioner a domesticated deer permit." The permit is annual, under the Department of Agriculture's livestock law rather than the wildlife code, at a fee of up to $400. A wild Kansas deer is still big game under K.S.A. 32-701(a) and cannot be taken for one.

## The Permits Are Farm Licenses

Twenty states issue a permit, and the word hides what the permit is. Texas is the model. The white-tailed deer is a game animal under § 63.001, § 63.002 bars possessing a live one "for any purpose not authorized by this code", and the authorization is Chapter 43's deer breeder's permit:

> The department shall issue a permit to a qualified person to possess live breeder deer in captivity.

The holder may keep breeder deer "for the purpose of propagation or sale", in an enclosure capable of keeping other deer out, with records, tagging, genetic and disease testing rules on top. Ohio carves captive white-tailed deer out of the $25 noncommercial license that covers the raccoon and the squirrel and gives them their own:

> "Captive white-tailed deer propagation license" permitting the licensee to propagate captive white-tailed deer, hold the animals in captivity, and sell the animals and carcasses.

That license needs an enclosure surrounded by an eight-foot fence built before applying and a Department of Agriculture license under R.C. 943.03 or 943.031, the chronic wasting disease side of the scheme, before any deer is placed; R.C. 1531.01(GGG) defines a captive white-tailed deer as one held at a facility licensed under both. Wisconsin takes the deer out of its captive wildlife chapter altogether: § 169.01(7) makes a farm-raised deer a domestic animal, and § 95.55(1)(a) says "no person may keep farm-raised deer unless the person is registered with the department under this section", the department being Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection, with fencing and CWD testing rules. Iowa's § 170.1A says "A landowner shall not keep whitetail unless the whitetail are kept as farm deer under this chapter or kept as preserve whitetail on a hunting preserve", and § 170.4 requires a certified fence of at least eight feet. Michigan's § 287.955(1) says "A person shall not possess cervidae or engage in a cervidae livestock operation unless he or she obtains from the department a cervidae livestock facility registration", and the deer is not on the Permit to Hold Game in Captivity list. West Virginia's Captive Cervid Farming Act regulates a licensed captive cervid as livestock, with license classes for breeding and selling or for breeding, harvest, hunting and venison; whether the $2 pet permit in § 20-2-51 could reach a deer bought from a farm is not settled, because the farm deer is no longer a "wild animal". Oregon's OAR 635-049-0015(1)(a) requires a Type 1 cervid license to hold a white-tailed deer, 0025(1) bars importing any live cervid, a deer born in the wild may not be held, and every captive cervid that dies at six months or older is tested; the chapter does not say whether new Type 1 licenses issue to private keepers. Oklahoma's 2 O.S. § 6-504(A) says "No person shall breed, possess, or raise cervidae for commercial or noncommercial purposes without first obtaining a farmed cervidae facility license", which may be issued to anyone who obtains the animal lawfully and acts in good faith, and 29 O.S. § 7-502(B)(5) takes licensed farmed cervidae out of the wildlife possession bar. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:083 requires a captive cervid permit for an inspected facility behind an eight-foot fence, and § 8(5) says "A new noncommercial captive cervid permit shall not be issued to a facility after February 28, 2016", so only the commercial class remains for a new keeper. Missouri makes every cervid Class III wildlife, held only under a Class III Wildlife Breeder Permit that requires a written examination, excludes hoofed mammals from the hobby permit, and bars importing a live white-tailed deer. Illinois's § 3.23 requires a game breeder permit to hold a game mammal, $10 noncommercial.

Four states have a permit a private person can hold on something like pet terms. Florida's 68A-6.003(13) bars the wild animal and leaves the bred one to Class III:

> Fox, skunks, bats, raccoons, or whitetail deer taken from the wild shall not be possessed as personal use wildlife and shall be possessed only in accordance with permits issued under Rules 68A-9.002, 68A-9.006, F.A.C., or Section 379.3761, F.S.

A captive-bred whitetail is on neither Class I nor Class II, so it takes the free personal use permit, with cervid caging standards at 68A-6.01210. Kansas has the domesticated deer permit in the box. North Dakota makes "all deer (cervidae)" category 2 nontraditional livestock, so a license from the Board of Animal Health and a premises inspection come before the animal, at $15 a year. South Dakota puts "Of the order Artiodactyla, all nondomestic members" on its possession permit list, ten dollars an animal, and writes no pet exclusion.

Five more have a permit, four of them on paper only. Idaho makes the whitetail big game, limits domestic cervidae to fallow deer, elk and reindeer, requires a captive possession license for the rest, and then says the Department "will not permit the import of any live cervid" that is not a domestic cervid and may refuse any license. Massachusetts and North Carolina issue their licenses for scientific, educational or exhibition purposes, and North Carolina's farmed cervid definition leaves the white-tailed deer out by name, so a captive one is a wild animal that § 113-272.5(a) bars licensing when "acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet". Maryland's native wildlife permit has a cervid part in its regulations that governs captive deer as a farm and disease scheme, and no pet class. Vermont's is the fifth and the one a keeper can apply for: Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue it outside science and education on a finding that the deer "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation".

## The Fawn Question

The question that brings most people to this page is a fawn in the grass, and every state read answers it the same way. Florida's rule says a whitetail "taken from the wild shall not be possessed as personal use wildlife", and Minnesota's special permit "may not be issued to take or possess wild or native deer". Oregon's wild-born deer is a wild cervid that may not be held. Louisiana's game quadrupeds may not be taken from the wild, Mississippi bars moving a deer from the wild into an enclosure, Wisconsin's deer from the wild is not legally obtained, Kansas's wild deer is big game that cannot be taken for a pet, and Arkansas closed hand capture for deer in 2012. Alabama's rule adds that possession "does not include" wild deer behind natural or man-made barriers that are not managed like domesticated animals, which is a fence line, not a pet. Even the farm licenses require a captive-born animal: Kentucky's captive cervid is one "privately owned, born, and raised in captivity". The only lawful place for a found fawn is a licensed rehabilitator.

## Connecticut, the Carolinas and Indiana

Connecticut is the one state where a white-tailed deer is not banned and not a permit animal. Its § 26-55-6(a)(4)(B)(i) names "the family Cervidae (including, but not limited to the, white-tailed deer, elk, red deer and sika)" in Category Three, the native tier, and (b)(4) bars importing or exporting a Category Three animal except by zoos and authorized persons. It does not bar possession. So a deer may be held only if it never crossed the state line, and taking one from the Connecticut wild is a question for the hunting statutes, which could not be read, as its chronic wasting disease rules on captive cervids could not. South Carolina's § 50-11-1910(A) makes it unlawful "to buy or sell, offer for sale, barter, or have in possession for sale" any live deer, and § 50-16-20(A)(1) requires a permit to bring one in; no rule read speaks to keeping a native deer that was neither bought nor imported, so the cell is restricted rather than banned, with no ordinary route. Arizona restricts "The family Cervidae" as restricted live wildlife, and none of its exemptions is a pet route.

Indiana is the gray cell. No deer is on the game breeder species list at 312 IAC 9-10-4(b), which runs from the ring-necked pheasant to the bobcat, and none is on any of the three wild animal possession permit classes. The deer rules in 312 IAC 9-3 cover hunting, processing and disposal and say nothing about keeping a live deer, and no Indiana statute on privately owned cervids was read. Whether and how a white-tailed deer may be kept there is not settled by the texts read, and the Department of Natural Resources is the place to ask in writing.

## The Takeaway

There is no state where a person can buy a white-tailed deer and keep it in the yard the way they might keep a raccoon in Ohio or a squirrel in Florida. Twenty-eight jurisdictions ban it, eleven of the twenty permit states issue a farm license that assumes a herd, a fence and a disease program, five have a license they will not issue for a pet, and only Florida, Kansas and the Dakotas have a permit a single keeper might hold. Connecticut allows one that never crossed its border, South Carolina and Arizona restrict it, and Indiana will not say. The reason runs through every cell: a deer is big game, and a captive deer is a chronic wasting disease question before it is anything else, which is why Montana, Louisiana, Kentucky and Arkansas each closed a route that once existed and why New Jersey has shut its border to the whole family.
