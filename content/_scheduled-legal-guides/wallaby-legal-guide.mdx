<LegalDisclaimer />

A Bennett's wallaby needs no state permit in 31 jurisdictions, and the reason is an absence rather than a decision. The dangerous-animal lists that decide most exotic mammals were written around cats, bears, canids, primates and crocodilians, and almost none of them mention a marsupial. The native-wildlife rules that catch the raccoon and the opossum turn on an animal living wild in the state, and a wallaby lives wild in none of them. So in state after state the search comes back empty, and empty means legal. Read across all 52 jurisdictions that produces 31 where no state permit is needed, 10 that issue a permit, nine bans, and two states, Pennsylvania and Connecticut, whose text will not decide. The [capybara](/blog/capybara-legal-guide/) lands the same way for the same reason, and the two animals are the clearest cases on the map of an exotic that is legal because nobody wrote it down.

Where a state does write it down, it writes a marsupial clause, and the [sugar glider](/blog/sugar-glider-legal-guide/) is the animal those clauses were built around. Six states name a wallaby or a kangaroo in a list of their own: Colorado, Connecticut, Florida, Nebraska, Oregon and Utah. Three of those clear it, two regulate it, and one cannot decide whether it meant to.

## Named and Cleared

Three states did the work of naming the animal and then let it through. Colorado's Chapter W-11, § 1103(B), lists the wildlife that may be "imported, sold, bartered, traded, transferred, possessed, propagated and transported in Colorado" with no license, and its mammal entry reads:

> Mammals: African pygmy hedgehog (Atelerix albiventris, Erinaceus albiventris) Sugar Gliders (Petaurus breviceps) Short-tailed Possum (Monodelphis domestica) Dama (Tammar) Wallaby (Macropus eugenii) Swamp Wallaby (Wallabia bicolor) Bennet (Red-necked) Wallaby (Macropus rufogriseus)

That is three wallabies cleared by binomial, the Bennett's under the older genus Macropus, and CPW's own species list gives the same answer under the current name. Montana's ARM 12.6.2205(1)(g) puts "Wallaby (Bennets) - Macropus rufogriseus" on its noncontrolled list, which matters in a white-list state where anything unlisted is barred; the wallaroo, by contrast, is controlled. Oregon's OAR 635-056-0060(1)(h)(B) clears "Kangaroos and wallabies -- Macropodidae -- All species", so every macropod may be imported, possessed, sold and transported without an ODFW permit, with the rule's own note that some species need a Department of Agriculture permit and Oregon cities and counties free to prohibit exotic animals by ordinance.

Oklahoma names it in an exemption rather than an allow list. OAC 800:25-25-3(a) opens:

> (a) The following wildlife species are exempt from import and export permits, commercial wildlife breeders licenses, noncommercial wildlife breeders licenses and commercial hunting area license requirements.

and entry (33) is "Wallaby", beside the kangaroo, so no import permit or breeder's license applies. Nebraska's 166 NAC 8 § 001.08A lists "Wallabies Macropus spp. & Petrogale spp." among the wildlife that may be imported, and because a non-native mammal is not a wild mammal under Nebraska's definition, the Captive Wildlife Permit scheme never reaches it. Nevada's NAC 503.140(1)(o) clears "Marsupials" from any Department permit, and Tennessee's § 70-4-403(3)(J) names "Marsupials" in Class III, the class that "requires no permits except those required by the department of agriculture".

## Legal by Silence

The other 24 clean states never mention the animal. New York is the one that surprises people, because the state's reputation is for strictness and its wild animal list is closed. ECL § 11-0103(6)(e) says a wild animal "includes, and is limited to" six groups: primates, Felidae, Canidae, Ursidae, a named set of large constrictors and monitors plus all venomous reptiles, and Crocodylia. No marsupial, and none among the dangerous animals in 6 NYCRR 180.1 either, so the pet bar in § 11-0512 never attaches. The native-wildlife mechanism that catches the opossum does not reach an Australian animal. New York City's Health Code is the layer that bites: § 161.01(b)(17) bars "All marsupials, including, but not limited to, Tasmanian devil, dasyure, bandicoot, kangaroo, wallaby, opossum", so the state's silence stops at the city line.

Texas, Ohio, Illinois, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Louisiana and Mississippi work the same way, with a closed dangerous-animal list that names no marsupial. Texas's § 822.101(4) runs from "a lion" to "a gorilla" and stops; Illinois's § 48-10 names fifteen mammals; Louisiana's LAC 76:V.115 is bears, wolves, primates and big cats; Mississippi's Rule 8.3 is apes, wild dogs, bears, hyenas, big cats and the largest herbivores; Ohio's § 935.01 names no marsupial and the Division of Wildlife's captivity license reaches only game, herps and fur-bearers; Maryland's § 10-621 is eight clauses and its native-wildlife permit attaches only to animals native to Maryland; Michigan's Large Carnivore Act names cats and bears, though its Part 413 prohibited species list could not be read and is the open check there; Minnesota's § 346.155 regulates cats, bears and primates, and a wallaby is a wild animal but not a protected one.

A second group clears it by nativity. Washington's RCW 77.08.010(72) defines wild animals as "those species of the class Mammalia whose members exist in Washington in a wild state", which a wallaby does not. West Virginia's § 20-1-2 defines wild animals as "all mammals native to the State of West Virginia", so neither the wildlife chapter nor the importation permit reaches it. Iowa's chapter 481A reaches only wildlife "found in this state" and its dangerous wild animal families include no marsupial. Missouri's 3 CSR 10-9.110(2) says "mammals not native to Missouri" may be "bought, sold, possessed, transported, and exhibited without permit", and the brushtail possum is the only marsupial on the prohibited list. Wisconsin's § 169.04(4)(b) exempts "live nonnative wild animals that are not endangered or threatened species" from licensing, with harmful animals excepted, and a wallaby is not one. Kansas's K.A.R. 115-20-3 lets exotic wildlife be "possessed without limit in time and number" if legally obtained where it came from. Virginia's exotic permit table at 4VAC15-30-40 lists no marsupial. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:082 has no marsupial on either prohibited list, so possession is lawful, a transportation permit is needed to bring one in, and a city or county may prohibit it under KRS 65.877. South Carolina, Alabama, Indiana, Arizona, North Dakota and South Dakota reach the same place from lists that never mention a macropod: Arizona's only restricted marsupial is the genus Didelphis, Indiana's permit reaches only animals its rule references, and the Dakotas' lists stop at carnivores, ungulates and primates, with North Dakota asking its board to review an unlisted species before importation, which is a review rather than a bar.

North Carolina is legal at the state house and decided at the county seat. No statewide rule names a marsupial other than the opossum, and G.S. § 153A-131 says "A county may by ordinance regulate, restrict, or prohibit the possession or harboring of animals which are dangerous to persons or property." Several have, so the county code is the operative law.

<FunFact>
Taxonomy moved under the wallaby while the statutes stood still. Bennett's wallaby was long placed in Macropus, as Macropus rufogriseus, and is now placed in Notamacropus. Colorado, Montana, Arkansas and Nebraska all list it under Macropus, which is harmless because they name the species or the whole genus. Connecticut is the state where the move matters, because it lists macropods by subgenus and the subgenus it names is the one the animal just left.
</FunFact>

## Where "Kangaroo" Reaches a Wallaby

A statute that says "kangaroo" and means the family catches the wallaby too, and four of the nine bans and permits work that way. Georgia's rule 391-4-8-.04(1)(a)3 lists in its inherently dangerous tier:

> Order Diprotodontia: Family Macropodidae (Kangaroos, wallabies, wallaroos, etc.) - All species;

That tier needs a wild animal license and liability insurance, and O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues the license only to dealers and exhibitors, so a Georgia keeper has no route; the DNR pet guide lists marsupials "(wallabies, kangaroos, sugar gliders, etc.)" among exotics that may not be held as pets, with the sugar glider the one exception. New Mexico's importation list reads "Kangaroo | Macropodidae all species | Kangaroos, wallabies | IV", and Group IV importation is prohibited for the general public. Utah's Table 3b-1 lists "Kangaroos and wallabies family Macropodidae" as controlled mammals, the bobcat's column, so a certificate of registration is required before importation or possession and a route exists. Maine's § 7.18(2)(F) makes "All Species in Family Macropodidae (Kangaroos, Wallabies, and Allies)" Category 1, and a Category 1 applicant "must be an exhibitor, wildlife rehabilitator; laboratory registered with the United States Department of Agriculture, or accredited research facility".

Connecticut is where the word does not reach. Its § 26-55-6(a)(3)(B) lists macropods one genus or subgenus at a time, from tree kangaroos through hare-wallabies, nail-tailed and rock wallabies, the quokka, pademelons and the swamp wallaby, and the two big-kangaroo entries read:

> (x) A member within the subgenus Osphranter (including, but not limited to, red kangaroo), (xi) A member within the subgenus Macropus (including, but not limited to, gray kangaroo);

Bennett's wallaby belongs to neither subgenus: it was Macropus (Notamacropus) rufogriseus and is now Notamacropus rufogriseus. Read as written, a rule that names subgenera with this much care and leaves Notamacropus out does not restrict the animal. Read as the drafters likely meant it, "subgenus Macropus" could stand for the old genus, and the definition applies "notwithstanding changes in taxonomic nomenclature". The cell is unclear rather than legal because the text arguably reaches the animal without naming it, and the Connecticut keeper should ask DEEP in writing before buying.

Two more bans and one permit name the animal itself. Hawaii's Part B list reads "Macropus rufogriseus wallaby, Bennett's", which sounds like a permit until § 4-71-2 is read beside it: private use "does not include individual possession of an animal as a pet", so a business, zoo or research body may hold one and a keeper may not. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Wallaby, Dama, Tammar, Bennett" for permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitors. Arkansas's Addendum R1.02 names "Red-necked or Bennet's Wallaby (Macropus rufogriseus)" among species for which breeding, sale and importation permits may be issued, so one may be kept if it came from a Commission-permitted breeder or dealer or in on an importation permit, with acquisition records, a veterinary certificate and the sexes housed apart unless neutered.

## The Permit States

Florida's answer is the one most keepers will meet. No macropod is on Class I or Class II, and the no-permit list's only marsupials are honey possums and sugar gliders, so a wallaby falls to Class III, "All other wildlife not listed herein", and the free Permit to Possess Class III Wildlife for Personal Use, with a marsupial caging standard of its own at 68A-6.0127. New Jersey's subchapter 4 sweeps any exotic not on its exempt or permit lists into the general permit requirement, and the one that fits is the "Individual hobby" permit, "issued to persons holding exotic mammals, birds, reptiles or amphibians or nongame species for hobby purposes or as pets", on criteria of diet, escape-proof caging, lawful acquisition and veterinary care. Delaware's regulation 903 says "When an exotic is kept as a pet, the owner or custodian of the exotic must apply to the Department for an Individual Permit", issued per animal for three years once the Department is satisfied with the double enclosure. Rhode Island's § 3.17 exemption clears only the sugar glider among marsupials, so the wallaby is an exotic animal needing a possession permit from the Division of Agriculture, which § 3.9(A) makes available to private persons for anything § 3.7 does not expressly prohibit. Idaho defines wildlife as animal life "native or exotic, generally living in a state of nature", lists no marsupial but the sugar glider as a conventional pet, and so requires a captive possession license from Fish and Game. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 13(d) says a permit "may be authorized upon approval for other warm-blooded wildlife", and may is not shall.

Massachusetts and Vermont both run closed lists whose only marsupials are gliders. Massachusetts has the permit on paper only, since MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard.

## Pennsylvania and the Catch-All

Pennsylvania has no text that names a wallaby, a kangaroo or a macropod, and it is still not a clean yes. The Game Commission's list at 58 Pa. Code § 137.1(a) ends with clause (11), "Game or wildlife held in captivity or captive bred in another state or nation", and 34 Pa.C.S. § 102 defines wild animal broadly enough that every mammal but livestock is one. The Commission reads that open-ended definition as barring the sugar glider, another marsupial no Pennsylvania text names, which is the reason a wallaby cannot be called legal here. It is recorded unclear rather than banned because no text names the animal, the same answer the capybara and the kinkajou get, and a Pennsylvania keeper should put the question to the Game Commission in writing before buying.

## The Takeaway

In 31 jurisdictions a Bennett's wallaby needs no state permit, and in most of them the reason is that no list mentions it. In nine a keeper can hold a permit, from Florida's free one to the discretionary ones in Wyoming and Vermont. Nine ban it, five of them by naming the family or the animal and four by a marsupial clause or a closed list. Pennsylvania and Connecticut will not say. The number to hold onto is 31, and the caution that goes with it is that every one of those states left the question to cities and counties, which Oregon, Nevada, Kentucky, Illinois and Maryland all say in terms.
