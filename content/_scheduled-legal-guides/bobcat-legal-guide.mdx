A bobcat is a wild cat and a furbearer at the same time, and the law reaches it from both directions. Every family-wide felid clause written to stop someone keeping a serval or a cougar catches the bobcat too, because it is a member of Felidae and most of those clauses stop at the family name. Every furbearer chapter written about trapping seasons and pelt tags catches it as well, because the bobcat has a trapping season across much of the country. The [serval legal guide](/blog/serval-legal-guide/) walks the first set of rules for a cat the trapping chapters never mention. This page walks both, for the one small cat that appears in each.

The result is not what the family clauses alone would predict. Across all 52 jurisdictions the bobcat gets 26 bans and 20 permits, more permits than any other wild cat on the map, with one state that allows it on a sourcing condition, one that restricts the whole order Carnivora, and four whose text will not decide. The reason the permit column is so long is the furbearer chapters. A serval in Ohio is a dangerous wild animal closed to new owners since 2014; a bobcat in Ohio is a fur-bearing animal held on a $25 propagating license, because Ohio's dangerous wild animal list names the lynxes and the bobcat, *Lynx rufus*, is filed under fur instead. Wisconsin, Tennessee, Michigan, South Carolina and West Virginia all reach the same answer by the same route.

## The Serval's Clauses Reach It

Eleven jurisdictions ban the bobcat with a sentence that never mentions it, because the sentence takes the family. California's § 671 restricts "Family Felidae-All species (W)", and the order heading names bobcats among the examples. Georgia's wild animal rule lists "Family Felidae (cats) - All species" in the inherently dangerous tier, and O.C.G.A. § 27-5-4(b)(1) issues the license only to the animal trade and public exhibitors; DNR's list of natives that may not be held as a pet names the bobcat a second time. New York's 6 NYCRR § 180.1 reaches "Cats in the Family Felidae with the exception of the species Felis catus", and ECL § 11-0512 bars keeping a wild animal as a pet. Connecticut's § 26-55-6(a)(2)(A) names the bobcat among its Category One examples, "lion, leopard, cheetah, jaguar, ocelot, jaguarundi cat, puma, lynx and bobcat". Maine lists all Felidae in Category 1, Maryland's § 10-621 bars any "member of the cat family other than the domestic cat", and Iowa's § 717F.1(5)(a)(3) reaches "a member of the family felidae of the order carnivora". Minnesota's § 346.155 makes every felid but domestic cats a regulated animal that no one may take possession of after 1 January 2005. New York City's Health Code names "bobcat" in its wild cat clause, and the District's closed list admits domestic cats only.

Nebraska's statute is the one with a bobcat-shaped hole in it, and the hole is for fur. Neb. Rev. Stat. § 37-477(2):

> Except as provided in subsection (3) of this section, no person shall keep in captivity in this state any wolf, any skunk, or any member of the families Felidae and Ursidae. This subsection shall not apply to ... (c) any person who holds a captive wildlife permit issued pursuant to section 37-479 and who raises Canada Lynx (Lynx canadensis) or bobcats (Lynx rufus) solely for the purpose of producing furs

The bobcat appears on Nebraska's Captive Wildlife Permit list, asterisked "For commercial purposes only", because that fur-producer sentence is the only exception the statute allows. A pet bobcat is a felid kept in captivity, and the ban stands.

## Named to Be Barred

Four states wrote the bobcat's name into a prohibition. Illinois's 720 ILCS 5/48-10(a) defines a dangerous animal as "a lion, tiger, leopard, ocelot, jaguar, cheetah, margay, mountain lion, lynx, bobcat, jaguarundi, bear, hyena, wolf or coyote", and subsection (b) allows one only at a zoo, federally licensed exhibit, circus, university, research laboratory, veterinary hospital, hound running area or animal refuge. The Wildlife Code also lists the bobcat as a fur-bearing mammal, but the fur-bearer breeder permit cannot authorize what the Criminal Code bars. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 § 6(1) lists "Bobcat (Lynx rufus)" among prohibited natives, with native wildlife defined "regardless of ... being captive-bred or taken from the wild". Oregon's holding rule, OAR 635-044-0460(3), says black bears, cougars, bobcats and wolves acquired after 20 January 2017 "shall only be held at AZA accredited facilities" or approved exhibitor and sanctuary facilities; pets held on that date were grandfathered on application, and 0460(5) makes an owner prove a claimed bobcat hybrid is one or it is regulated as a bobcat. Pennsylvania's 58 Pa. Code § 137.1(a)(1) bans every felid but the house cat and then provides:

> Lawfully acquired bobcats ... may be imported or possessed, or both, by licensed propagators specifically for propagation for fur farming purposes. ... Bobcats imported under this exception and their progeny may not be sold or transferred for wildlife pet purposes or released into the wild.

The same sentence Pennsylvania wrote for the red fox, with the same lock on the pet side. New Jersey adds a reason no other state has: the bobcat is on New Jersey's own endangered species list, and the Division says endangered species "may not be kept as pets or for hobby purposes for any reason". Alabama bars the bobcat by its retired binomial, "bobcat (Felis rufus)", among animals that may not come from outside the state, and treats an Alabama bobcat as a protected furbearer. Mississippi names it in Rule 8.4's closed list of game and furbearing animals whose live possession "is prohibited".

The native-wildlife bars do the rest. Virginia's § 29.1-521(A)(10) bars possessing any wild animal except as specifically permitted. Rhode Island lists "Bobcat (Lynx rufus)" among native mammals whose possession as pets is prohibited, "regardless of where the animal originated". New Hampshire's controlled table carries the bobcat with permittee category 5, the exhibitor category, alone. Colorado's Parks and Wildlife will not license private possession of regulated wildlife. Arkansas closed its hand-capture route for the bobcat on 1 July 2022 and lists it on none of its three addenda, which makes it "prohibited until evaluated". Alaska bars a pet permit for any game animal, and the bobcat does not range there, which does not matter under a definition that reaches any mammal. Hawaii has *Lynx rufus* on no list and bars an unlisted animal by default, and New Mexico's importation list names only the Mexican subspecies and sends the species to Group IV by its default clause.

## Where the Fur Chapter Opens a Door the Cat Chapter Shut

This is the bobcat's own section, and Ohio is the clearest case of it. Ohio Rev. Code § 1531.01(W):

> "Fur-bearing animals" includes minks, weasels, raccoons, skunks, opossums, muskrats, fox, beavers, badgers, otters, coyotes, and bobcats.

Section 1533.71(A)(2) then licenses anyone who wants to hold fur-bearing animals in captivity, and the "noncommercial propagating license" covers animals held "for the licensee's own use", twenty-five dollars a year, not for sale. Set against that, § 935.01(C)(7)(b) lists among dangerous wild animals "Lynxes, including Canadian lynxes, Eurasian lynxes, and Iberian lynxes". The bobcat is *Lynx rufus*, a member of the genus, and it is not among the three examples, while the same definitions section names bobcats separately as fur-bearing animals. That separate naming is the better reading, and it matters more than most taxonomy questions do: if the bobcat were a lynx under Chapter 935, § 935.02 would have closed it to new owners on 1 January 2014, as it closed the serval, and there would be no permit at all. The [big cat legal guide](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) walks Chapter 935 for the cats it does name, and the line between them and the bobcat is the word "lynxes".

Wisconsin's § 169.01(14) names the bobcat as a fur-bearing wild animal, the seventeen natives exempted from licensing stop at weasels and opossums, and § 169.15(1)(b) says the department "shall issue" a Class B captive wild animal farm license to any applicant who files properly and pays; NR 16.15(1) applies it to hobbyists, and the cougar is the only cat on Wisconsin's harmful list. Tennessee moves native furbearers into Class II, $10 an animal with documentary evidence of the supplier; the statute puts bobcat hybrids with domestic cats in Class III by name and leaves the bobcat itself in Class II. Michigan's Permit to Hold Game in Captivity names the bobcat on its closed list, for captive-bred animals only, and the Large Carnivore Act's cat clause names seven big cats and stops. South Carolina names the bobcat as a furbearer, so bringing one in needs a Department permit, and its large cat ban stops at genus *Panthera*. West Virginia's board list names "Lynxes, including Canadian lynxes, Eurasian lynxes, and Iberian lynxes", as Ohio's does, and § 19-34-2(2) excludes "wildlife" from the Dangerous Wild Animals Act altogether; the bobcat is a native fur-bearer, so it falls to Chapter 20, where § 20-2-51 issues a two-dollar pet permit for an animal "acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season". The lynx line could be read to reach *Lynx rufus* as a member of the genus, and that is worth a letter to the DNR before relying on it.

<FunFact>
Texas treats a bobcat exactly as it treats a tiger, and the chapter says so by numbering. Health and Safety Code § 822.101(4) lists "(H) a bobcat; (I) a lynx; (J) a serval" in the middle of a list that opens with the lion and the tiger. Every animal on it needs the certificate of registration under § 822.103, the $100,000 of liability insurance under § 822.107, and the same caging, and every one can be banned by a city or county under § 822.116(b). A thirty-pound cat that lives wild across Texas, registered on the same form as a four-hundred-pound one.

## The Wild-Cat Permits

Nine more states issue a permit written for a wild cat rather than a furbearer. Florida names "Bobcats (Lynx rufus)" at Class II item 17, between the lynx and the caracal, so the Permit to Possess Class II Wildlife for Personal Use applies: $140 a year, age 18, documented experience, and the caging standards of 68A-6.004. Indiana's Class II list of fifteen small cats does not include the bobcat, so it falls into Class III as a wild cat "except ... cats listed in section 7", and a Class III permit must be in hand before possession, with a recapture or destruction plan and the equipment to carry it out, a cage inspection and proof of lawful acquisition. Utah's Table 3b-1 lists "Bobcat Lynx rufus captive-bred" as a controlled mammal, so a certificate of registration is needed and a route exists, which is more than Utah gives the serval. Idaho requires a Fish and Game captive possession license, offered as an individual animal license at a private facility, which § 200.03 lets the Department refuse on public safety grounds. South Dakota's possession permit covers all nondomestic Felidae at ten dollars an animal. North Dakota puts a "nondomestic cat not listed in category 3" in category 2, a license to be in hand before the animal is. Wyoming's statute makes the bobcat a furbearing animal, and Chapter 10 § 13(a) requires a permit before importing or possessing one. Oklahoma's exemption for "Cats (except native cats and bears)" carves the bobcat out, so it needs the import permit and the noncommercial breeder's license. Nevada's no-permit list clears "All felines, except mountain lions and bobcats", which puts the bobcat under the Department's written consent to bring it in; it is on neither Nevada list, and the serval is cleared by the same words that catch the bobcat.

Texas and Missouri add a registration on top. Texas is above. Missouri's Wildlife Hobby Permit allows one game mammal "purchased from a Missouri wildlife breeder permit holder", held for personal use and never propagated or sold, and because the bobcat is also on the dangerous wild animal list in RSMo § 578.023, the keeper must register it with the local law enforcement agency. Massachusetts and North Carolina have the permit on paper only: MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet", and North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) refuses a captivity license for an animal "acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet". Vermont's Regulation 881 lets the Commissioner issue a permit outside science and education only on a finding that the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", a discretionary call with no published standard.

## Montana's Large Cat, and Three Other Gray Cells

Montana's exotic wildlife white list does not reach the bobcat, because the bobcat is native, and its rabies statute names the skunk, fox, raccoon and bat and no cat. What arguably reaches it is the wild animal menagerie permit. MCA § 87-4-801(3):

> "Wild animal menagerie" means any place where one or more bears or large cats, including cougars, lions, tigers, jaguars, leopards, pumas, cheetahs, ocelots, and hybrids of those large cats are kept in captivity for use other than public exhibition.

The list of examples runs down to the ocelot, a cat no bigger than a bobcat, and does not name the bobcat. Whether a bobcat is a "large cat" that needs the menagerie permit, which allows up to ten animals bought captive bred from a licensed source, or falls outside Part 8 altogether, is not something the text settles. Delaware's question is nativity: tit. 3 § 7201 requires an Agriculture permit for a wild mammal "not native to or generally found in Delaware", and the bobcat is historically native and long gone, with occasional records now. If it counts as native it belongs to DNREC, which forbids owning native wildlife; if not, it needs the $25 Individual permit the serval needs, and neither agency has said which. Kansas bars possessing wildlife unless a regulation permits it, the only regulation that permits a live furbearer lets a Kansas-caught one be held "only through the last day of the season in which taken", and no text says whether the exception for animals "legally taken outside this state" reaches one bred in captivity. Louisiana's statute lists the bobcat as a "protected quadruped" beside the fox, but the fox rule is written for foxes and coyotes and the big cat rule is six named cats, so no Louisiana text sets a captivity rule for a bobcat in either direction.

Washington is the one conditional cell. The bobcat is Washington wildlife, so the big-cat statute, which exempts native wildlife, does not reach it, and Title 77 does not ban keeping one. WAC 220-450-030 makes it "unlawful to possess or hold in captivity live wild animals ... unless lawfully acquired", requires proof of lawful acquisition naming species, origin and source, needs a permit to take one from the wild and a health certificate to import one, and bars selling a captive bobcat or its progeny except as department rule allows. A captive-bred, documented bobcat is the condition. Arizona restricts the whole order Carnivora, so a special license is needed and none of the exemptions is a pet route.

## The Takeaway

A bobcat is barred in 26 jurisdictions and permitted in 20, and the two lists are built from different statutes. Where a state wrote a family-wide cat clause, the bobcat gets the serval's answer, and that answer is nearly always no. Where a state regulates its own furbearers, the bobcat gets the raccoon's answer, and that answer is nearly always a license between two and fifty dollars. Texas is the one state that treats it as a big cat and still lets a keeper have it, with a certificate and insurance. Ohio and West Virginia both decided, by naming three lynxes and not the fourth, that a bobcat is a furbearer rather than a lynx, and the [Canada lynx](/blog/canada-lynx-legal-guide/), its closest relative, is banned in both for exactly that reason.
