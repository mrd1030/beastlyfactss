A Virginia opossum is caught by two kinds of law at once. It is a marsupial, so every clause written to keep sugar gliders, wallabies and kangaroos out of a state reaches it by order or by name: New York City's Health Code lists "All marsupials" and names the opossum in the examples, and California restricts "Order Marsupialia" wholesale. It is also the one marsupial native to the United States, with a trapping season across most of its range, so the furbearer and game chapters written for the raccoon reach it too: Texas, Ohio, Illinois, Iowa, Alabama, Mississippi, South Carolina and West Virginia all name the opossum in the same sentence as the raccoon. Read across all 52 jurisdictions, that produces 20 bans, 20 permits, four states where it needs no permit, four where it is legal on a condition, one restricted list, and three states, Tennessee, Kansas and Louisiana, whose text will not decide.

The name confuses people, and it is the species' name rather than a hint about the law. Didelphis virginiana is the Virginia opossum wherever it lives, and Virginia itself bans it. The animal's reputation for shrugging off rabies gets repeated in nearly every forum thread about keeping one, and no statute on this map cares: the rules that reach an opossum are the rules for a wild native furbearer, and they would reach it whatever its disease profile.

## The Marsupial Clauses

The sugar glider is the marsupial most state lists were written around, and the [sugar glider](/blog/sugar-glider-legal-guide/) map shows how few states bother: six jurisdictions ban it and the rest mostly leave it alone. The opossum is caught by the same clauses and then by a second set the glider never meets. New York City is the cleanest example. Health Code § 161.01(b)(17) prohibits:

> All marsupials, including, but not limited to, Tasmanian devil, dasyure, bandicoot, kangaroo, wallaby, opossum, wombat, koala bear, cuscus, numbat and pigmy, sugar and greater glider.

The opossum sits in the middle of that list, and the Code's only exceptions are the Parks Department and Wildlife Conservation Society zoos, laboratories, veterinary facilities and permitted temporary exhibits. California's 14 CCR § 671(c)(2)(C) reads "Order Marsupialia-Marsupials or Pouched Animals: All species (W)", and the welfare marking changes nothing for a keeper, because the only permits go to research, exhibition and animals held before 1992; the opossum has lived wild in California for a century and the rule reaches a listed order whatever the animal's origin. Hawaii's lists carry a single opossum, Didelphis marsupialis, the common opossum of Central and South America, on the research-and-exhibition tier, so the Virginia opossum falls to the default bar on any animal not on a list. The District of Columbia's seven permitted pet categories run from domestic dogs to racing pigeons, and no marsupial fits one.

Three western states name the species on a prohibited list. Utah's Table 3b-1 puts "Virginia opossum Didelphis virginiana" under prohibited mammals, the tier with "known detrimental impacts to native wildlife populations, human health and safety, or agriculture", and the header says "Personal possession will not be authorized". Oregon's OAR 635-056-0050(1)(a)(F) lists "Order Didelphimorphia: Virginia opossum ... Didelphis virginiana", because the animal is introduced in Oregon rather than native, and Oregon cities and counties may add their own ordinances. Montana's ARM 12.6.2215(4)(s) lists "Virginia opossum - Didelphis virginiana" among prohibited mammals. Arizona stops short of a ban: R12-4-406(F)(5) restricts "All species of the genus Didelphis. Common name: American opossums", which puts it under a special license with no pet exemption.

<FunFact>
The gray short-tailed opossum, Monodelphis domestica, is the opossum that turns up on pet lists, and the states treat it as a different animal from the one in the yard. Maine puts it in Category 1, Colorado lists it as unregulated, New Mexico puts it in Group II as captive bred, Utah lists it as noncontrolled and Oregon as noncontrolled, while each of those states bars or defaults the Virginia opossum. A list that says "opossum" without a binomial is usually talking about the small one.
</FunFact>

## Small Game, Furbearer, Protected Wild Animal

The second set of rules is the one the [raccoon](/blog/raccoon-legal-guide/) meets, and the opossum is named in nearly every one of them. New York's ECL § 11-0103(2)(c) defines small game as a list that runs from "black, gray and fox squirrels" through "raccoon, opossum, or weasel", small game is protected wildlife, and § 11-0107(2) bars possessing protected wildlife "whether taken within the state or coming from without the state". The only license, § 11-0515, is for propagation, banding, scientific or exhibition purposes. Alabama's rule 220-2-.06 designates "Opossum" a game animal, between the deer and the cottontail, and 220-2-.26(3) makes it unlawful to possess any live protected wild animal except under a permit issued before that amendment. Mississippi's Rule 8.4 names the opossum on its closed list of game and furbearing animals and says "Possession of live game and furbearing animals is prohibited", with exceptions for public zoos, treating veterinarians and permitted enclosures. Pennsylvania's § 137.31(a) says "It is unlawful for a person to possess live wildlife taken from a wild state within this Commonwealth", and § 137.1(a)(11) bars one held captive or captive bred in another state, so both doors are shut; licensed rehabilitators hold opossums under their own permits, which is not a pet route.

Nine more jurisdictions ban it as native wildlife without needing the word game. Georgia's Department of Natural Resources names the opossum among species native to Georgia that "may not be held as a pet regardless of its origin or morphology"; the rule's marsupial entry covers two Australian orders and never reaches the opossum's, so the native scheme is what bars it. Rhode Island lists "Virginia Opossum (Didelphis virginiana)" among native mammals "which possession as pets is prohibited", open only to the institutions in § 3.7(C), regardless of where the animal originated. Virginia carries it on the native fauna list, which puts it under § 29.1-521(A)(10) rather than the exotic permit table. New Hampshire's Table 800.2 lists "Opossum, Virginia Didelphis virginiana" for permittee category 5 alone, the exhibitors. Delaware's DNREC says state regulations "explicitly forbid Delawareans from owning" native wildlife outside rehabilitation permits, and the Agriculture rule that exempts "Possums" governs exotics, not a native. Maine names only the short-tailed opossum, so the Virginia opossum, which now ranges into Maine, is uncategorized and "will not be eligible for a permit under this chapter". Colorado's unregulated list carries the short-tailed possum and the Virginia opossum is regulated wildlife for which "CPW will not issue a license for private possession (eg pet)". New Mexico's importation list has one opossum, the short-tailed, so the Virginia opossum is Group IV by default, prohibited to the general public. Alaska's 5 AAC 92.029(c) bars a permit for any game animal "for use as a pet".

## The Permit States, Sorted by What the Permit Is For

Twenty states issue a permit, and seventeen of them issue it to a keeper.

The furbearer licenses come first. Texas is the one most lists get wrong, because the opossum is not on the dangerous wild animal list and looks free. Parks and Wildlife Code § 71.001(1) reads:

> "Fur-bearing animal" means wild beaver, otter, mink, ring-tailed cat, badger, skunk, raccoon, muskrat, opossum, fox, or nutria.

and § 71.005(c) requires a fur-bearing animal propagation license to possess a live one "for any purpose", $50.75 under § 71.010. "For any purpose" makes it a keeper's license as well as a breeder's, and fur-bearers are excluded from the nongame caps. Ohio names opossums among its fur-bearing animals in R.C. 1531.01(W), and § 1533.71(A)(2) offers a "Noncommercial propagating license" to hold fur-bearing animals in captivity "for the licensee's own use", twenty-five dollars a year, no sales. Illinois's § 3.25 says "Before any individual shall hold, possess or engage in the breeding or raising of live fur-bearing mammals, he shall first procure a fur-bearing mammal breeder permit", $25 a year, breeding or not, and a home rule city can be stricter. Indiana's 312 IAC 9-11-7(a)(7) lists "Virginia opossum" as a Class II wild animal, under the binomial of the common opossum but by this animal's common name: apply within five days of possession, ten dollars, a cage inspection. West Virginia's § 20-2-51 is a $2 permit "to keep and maintain in captivity as a pet, a wild animal or wild bird that has been acquired from a commercial dealer or during the legal open season", and the opossum is a West Virginia fur-bearing animal by name. Kentucky's 301 KAR 2:081 requires a noncommercial captive wildlife permit "prior to acquiring wildlife" and sets the opossum's enclosure by name. Nebraska names "Opossum - (Didelphis virginiana)" on its Captive Wildlife Permit list, fee not more than $30, and bars a permit for one taken from the wild. North Dakota makes opossums category 2 nontraditional livestock by name, which needs a $15 license from the Board of Animal Health and a premises inspection before the animal is acquired. Oklahoma leaves the opossum off its exemption list, so the import permit and noncommercial wildlife breeder's license apply, with the pet exemption in 29 O.S. § 7-502(B)(4) reaching only an animal from a source other than the wild. South Carolina names the opossum as a furbearer, so bringing one in needs a Department permit under § 50-16-20, issued only if the animal was taken lawfully where it came from.

The second group issues a pet permit of a narrower kind. Florida is the surprise, because the opossum is nowhere on Florida's lists at all: not Class I, not Class II, and not on the no-permit list, which names honey possums and sugar gliders. So it falls to Class III, "All other wildlife not listed herein, except those for which a permit is not required pursuant to Rule 68A-6.003", and the free Permit to Possess Class III Wildlife for Personal Use. New Jersey treats the opossum as a game animal because it has a trapping season, names Didelphis virginiana at 10.6(a)13, and 10.7(a)1 offers the "Individual Hobby" permit "to persons holding game animals for hobby purposes or as pets", on condition under 10.8(a)1 that the animal did not come from wild stock. Michigan's Permit to Hold Game in Captivity names "opossum" on its closed list and covers captive-bred animals only. Minnesota makes the opossum small game and lets the commissioner issue a special permit "without a fee to take, possess, and transport wild animals as pets", on conditions the commissioner sets. Missouri's Wildlife Hobby Permit allows "not more than one (1) game mammal purchased from a Missouri wildlife breeder permit holder", and the opossum counts as a game mammal because furbearers do. Wyoming's Chapter 10 § 13(d) says a permit "may be authorized upon approval for other warm-blooded wildlife", and may is not shall. Vermont's Regulation 881 is the same shape: outside science and education, no permit issues unless the Commissioner determines the animal "does not conflict with the purpose of this regulation", and Vermont's unrestricted list carries the sugar glider and the feathertail glider and no opossum.

The third group is a permit in name only. Massachusetts lists no opossum on its exemption list, its only marsupial being the sugar glider, and MassWildlife says "You will not be issued a permit for keeping a wild animal as a pet". North Carolina's § 113-272.5(a) bars a captivity license unless the animal "was not acquired unlawfully or merely as a pet". Maryland's § 10-902(a) attaches a permit to possessing "any wildlife native to Maryland", and no pet permit class for a native mammal was located in the regulations.

## Four Clean States and Four Conditions

Wisconsin is the clearest yes, and it says so by name. Wis. Stat. § 169.04(4)(a) reads:

> A person is exempt from holding a license or other approval as required under this chapter to possess live native wild animals if the wild animals are not endangered or threatened species and are any of the following: ... 7. Opossums.

NR 10.04(1) makes the opossum an unprotected wild animal with no season or possession limit, the animal must still be legally obtained under § 169.04(1)(a), and taking one from the wild needs a hunting or trapping license. Nevada's NAC 503.140(1) lists the animals that "may be possessed, transported, imported and exported without a permit or license issued by the Department", and (o) is "Marsupials", which takes in an opossum that is not a Nevada native; subsection 4 preserves county and city ordinances. Connecticut's § 26-55-6 sorts wild animals into four categories, no opossum is in any of them, and the Category Two marsupials are all macropods, so the regulation does not restrict keeping one; the statutes governing the taking of wildlife could not be read. South Dakota's possession permit list runs to carnivore families, hoofed mammals and primates with no marsupial, and the free entry permit covers bringing one in.

Four states allow it on a condition. Iowa names the opossum a fur-bearing animal, bars possessing one except as the chapter provides, and provides in § 481A.61(1) that "Possession and use of the game birds, game animals, or fur-bearing animals obtained from a licensed game breeder are lawful", with § 481A.61(4) barring the breeder from stocking with animals taken from the Iowa wild. Arkansas runs the condition the other way: Code 09.14(A)(1)(i) allows "No more than 6 per household of any combination of the following animals may be possessed as pets: Hand-captured opossum, rabbit, raccoon and squirrel", no sale and no removal from the state. Idaho classifies no opossum, so it is unprotected wildlife, and one "lawfully taken by a person licensed or authorized to hunt or trap" may be "exported, transported, possessed, or sold without additional permit from the Department"; one acquired any other way needs a captive possession license, since a marsupial is not a conventional pet there. Washington treats the introduced opossum as a wild animal because it lives wild in the state, and asks for lawful acquisition: no taking from the wild, a health certificate to import and documented proof of source.

## Where the Text Will Not Say

Tennessee is the odd one, because both of its texts allow the animal and they disagree about the permit. The statute's Class III, which "requires no permits except those required by the department of agriculture", names "Marsupials" at § 70-4-403(3)(J). The commission's rule 1660-01-18-.03(3)(b) then says:

> (b) 1. The following species or groups of wildlife are deleted from the existing listing designated by legislation as Class III: (i) All native, nonpoisonous reptiles and amphibians; and (ii) All native, furbearing mammals. 2. All species or groups of wildlife deleted from Class III in part 1. are considered to be Class II wildlife.

Class II needs a TWRA permit, $10 an animal, with supplier documentation. Whether the opossum is a "native, furbearing mammal" for that rule is not defined in anything read, so the animal can be kept and the permit question is open. Kansas makes the opossum a furbearer under K.S.A. 32-701(e), and K.A.R. 115-5-2(b) says "Live furbearers legally taken during a furbearer season may be possessed only through the last day of the season in which taken", which rules out a wild-caught pet; whether the exception in 32-1002(b) for animals "legally taken outside this state" reaches a captive-bred opossum from another state is the question Kansas has never closed. Louisiana classes the opossum as a nongame quadruped, and the only text that speaks to keeping one is the wildlife rescuer rule at LAC 76:V.131.D.3: a sick, injured or orphaned "squirrel, opossum, rabbit, or chipmunk" may be held for up to 90 days, "one animal or litter of animals per individual", and kept for life only on a special purpose permit after a Louisiana veterinarian finds it non-releasable. Nothing read bars or allows a captive-bred opossum bought as a pet. In all three, ask the agency in writing before buying and keep the reply.

## The Takeaway

In 20 jurisdictions there is no lawful pet opossum, and in most of them the reason is that the animal is a native furbearer rather than that it is a marsupial. In 17 states a keeper can hold a permit, usually a furbearer or captive wildlife license costing between two and fifty dollars, and nearly all of them require a bred animal. Wisconsin and Nevada need nothing, Connecticut and South Dakota have no rule that reaches it, Iowa allows one from a licensed game breeder, Arkansas allows six caught by hand, and Idaho allows one lawfully trapped. Tennessee, Kansas and Louisiana will not say. Wherever the answer is yes, the trapping regulation is the next thing to read, because an opossum under the porch is a furbearer in season and a protected animal out of it.
