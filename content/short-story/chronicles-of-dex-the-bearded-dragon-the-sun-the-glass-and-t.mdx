# Chronicles of Dex the Bearded Dragon: The Sun, the Glass, and the Cricket That Got Away

> Dex had a plan for the cricket, a perfect place to wait, and all afternoon to do it. What he did not have was any idea that someone on the other side of the glass was watching him do it.

The cricket was right there, three inches from his nose, twitching its ridiculous antennae like it had no idea who it was dealing with. Dex began the strike the way a bearded dragon of his standing begins anything, which is to say slowly. He lowered his head. He fixed one amber eye on the target. He shifted his weight onto his front legs with the gravity of a king rising from a throne, and somewhere in the middle of all this ceremony the cricket strolled behind the water dish and disappeared into the back corner of the tank.

He did not chase it. A creature of his standing did not *chase*. He turned his head instead, slow and deliberate, to make clear to anyone watching that he had *chosen* to let it go.

Nobody was watching. That was the trouble with a kingdom of 120 gallons. Every inch of it was his, catalogued and ranked, and none of it ever applauded. The basking rock was first, obviously, warm right through and set directly beneath the lamp, which hummed above him with a steady devotion he had come to expect. The driftwood branch was second, for the days he wanted a higher view. The water dish was functional and not worth discussing. The back corner, with its permanent shadow and its baffling appeal to crickets, was territory he acknowledged but did not respect.

And beyond all of it was the glass, and beyond the glass was the room, and beyond the room was a wide window full of light and shapes he had never been able to sort into categories. Dex did not think about the far side of the glass. He had decided long ago that it was not worth thinking about, and he had stuck to that decision with tremendous discipline, most days.

The lamp was climbing toward its afternoon heat, and Dex, warming, made a new decision. He would catch that cricket. Not because he was hungry, though he was, a little. He would catch it for the record. A cricket that escaped a king was a cricket that told other crickets, and he would not have it.

He also knew something about crickets that crickets did not know about him. Sooner or later, every cricket in the back corner got thirsty, and every thirsty cricket crossed the open sand to the water dish. Dex did not need to chase anything. He needed only to wait on the rock, perfectly still, and let thirst do the walking. Waiting, after all, was the thing he was best at. He had been doing it for many warm cycles and had never once been beaten.

So he waited. The heat soaked into his belly and spread through his legs until his muscles felt like sunlight had gotten inside them. He spread his toes wide across the stone. He did not blink, or not often. The light through the window crept across the sill and down the wall in a long gold band, and Dex watched the back corner and the strip of sand in front of the water dish, and the afternoon went still around him.

The cricket came out right on schedule. It picked its way across the sand in short, cautious hops, antennae working, and Dex felt his whole body tighten toward it like a drawn bow. Four inches. Three. He shifted his weight, ready.

That was when something moved beyond the glass.

It was large, and it was orange, and it had arrived on the windowsill without making a sound, the way weather arrives. It sat down on the far side of that window and did not move again, and Dex could not tell how long it had been there, which was somehow the worst part. It was looking straight into his tank. It was looking straight at him.

The cricket could wait. This could not. Dex rose to his full height on the rock, lifted his chin, and opened his mouth a precise and calculated amount. Then he let his beard go dark, darker, darkest, flared wide and black in the oldest signal his kind had ever invented: *I am larger than I appear, and you would do well to remember it.* He bobbed his head once, hard, for emphasis.

![image](/assets/images/dex/dex-chron1.1.jpg)

The orange shape did not retreat. It lowered itself instead, flattening along the sill behind the glass, its tail twitching once at the tip. Dex knew that posture. He had been holding it all afternoon. The shape was not challenging him. It was *waiting*, the same patient, whole-body waiting he had been so proud of an hour before, and its eyes had shifted, just slightly, from him to the sand below him.

The cricket, which had no idea it was the most important animal in the room, walked straight past Dex's rock while he stood there with his beard out. It drank from the water dish, taking its time about it, and then wandered back into the shadows of the back corner without once looking up.

Dex did not move. Neither did the shape. They stayed like that, two hunters with a pane of glass between them and nothing left to hunt, until the gold band of light slid off the wall. Then the orange shape stood up, stretched one long leg behind it, and dropped out of sight below the window as if the whole thing had been a show it had already seen. Dex could not decide whether it had been an ally, a threat, or something he simply had no category for yet.

Mike came in at dusk with the evening crickets and crouched by the tank the way he always did. "Missed one, huh, buddy?" he said, nodding toward the back corner, in a tone Dex filed under *fond and entirely too amused*. Dex did not dignify it with a response. He had not missed. He had been busy.

![image](/assets/images/dex/dex-chron1.2.jpg)

Later, with the lamp off and the room going blue, Dex lay flat on the last warmth of his rock and turned the afternoon over the way he turned over anything that unsettled him. He had spent many warm cycles believing the best waiter in the world lived in this tank. It turned out there was another one, a much larger one, and it lived on the far side of the glass. The cricket that got away could stay in its corner. It had been a cricket, and there would be others. The orange shape was a question, and he had never had one of those before.

He did not know what lay beyond the glass. But he was beginning, in that slow and serious way of his, to want to.
