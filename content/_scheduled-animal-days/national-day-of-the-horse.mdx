A horse can sleep on its feet, and does for much of its rest, because a set of tendons and ligaments in its legs holds the joints in place while the muscles go slack. It cannot dream that way. REM sleep switches off muscle tone, and a horse with no muscle tone on four legs falls down, so the deepest stage of its sleep only happens when it lies on the ground.

That is the part of the horse most people get half right. The day named for it has a history people rarely look at, and it starts with a date that was written to happen once.

## One Resolution, One Date

National Day of the Horse comes from S.Res.452, submitted in the Senate on 7 October 2004 by Senator Ben Nighthorse Campbell of Colorado and three cosponsors. The Judiciary Committee was discharged and the Senate agreed to it on 18 November 2004.

The resolution designates "December 13, 2004" as National Day of the Horse. That year's 13 December, and no other. It asks the President to issue a proclamation and asks Americans to be mindful of what horses contribute to the economy, history and character of the country. A House version introduced the same autumn went less far: it only expressed the sense of Congress that such a day should be established.

The annual version is the American Horse Council's doing. The industry body promotes 13 December every year, with a theme, a fundraising push for its foundation and a hashtag. A single-year designation becoming a fixed annual day by repetition is how a lot of these dates settle. [World Animal Day](/blog/world-animal-day/) took four years of moving around before it landed on the date its founder wanted.

Two clauses in the resolution are worth reading closely. One cites the Congressional Horse Caucus estimate that the horse industry contributes more than $100 billion a year to the US economy. The other says wild and domestic horses rely on humans for adequate food, water and shelter. Some of that reliance is already written into state law: New York and California ban docking a horse's tail, one of [the conformation laws that treat horses and dogs by different rules](/blog/conformation-breeding-laws-guide/).

## Asleep on Four Legs

The mechanism behind standing sleep is the stay apparatus, a system of tendons and ligaments running through the fore and hind legs. When the horse relaxes its leg muscles, those tissues act as tension bands and hold the shoulder, knee and ankle joints locked. The horse stays upright without spending much muscular effort on it.

An adult horse can get by on as little as five hours of sleep a day, most of it standing. Resting in all its forms, including standing inactive and dozing, takes five to seven hours of a horse's day, and most of the sleep falls after midnight.

Horses kept in groups also rest in sync, and when the herd lies down, at least one horse stays standing or awake. In wild herds that standing horse is watching for predators, which matters for an animal whose first defense is running and whose slowest moment is getting up off the ground.

## The Stage That Needs the Ground

REM sleep needs the horse lying on its chest or flat on its side, because the loss of muscle tone that comes with REM would otherwise drop it. The minimum is roughly 30 minutes of lying down in 24 hours to get a minimal amount of REM. Measured nightly REM in small studies has run from about 22 to 37 minutes in one group of seven horses and about 42 to 53 minutes in four ponies.

REM is also very old. [Bearded dragons cycle through it too](/blog/10-surprising-bearded-dragon-facts/), on a loop of about 80 seconds, which pushes the origin of the stage back to the common ancestor of reptiles, birds and mammals.

## When a Horse Will Not Lie Down

A horse that does not lie down still builds up the need for REM. Eventually it slips into REM on its feet, partially collapses, and wakes suddenly as it buckles. The episodes are commonly misdiagnosed as narcolepsy, a rare neurological disorder. The giveaway is often physical: skin lesions on the front of the knees and the front fetlocks from repeated near falls.

A 2021 study from the University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna fitted motion sensors to 83 horses at an animal sanctuary. Daily lying time ranged from zero to 319 minutes, with a mean of about 67. Eight horses showed signs of REM deficit, collapse or the telltale lesions, and those eight lay down for an average of under eight minutes a day against nearly 74 for the rest.

The reasons a horse stays up are well mapped. Chronic pain and the mechanical difficulty of getting back up are one group. Social insecurity and fear are another, since lying down is the most exposed position a horse can take. Environment is the third. In a study of group-housed horses, they strongly preferred a littered area over rubber mats or bare ground for lying, and when the bedded space was tight, low-ranking horses had their lying bouts cut short more often by higher-ranking ones.

So the horse that dozes through the afternoon on three legs is fine. The one that never lies down needs looking at: a bed deep enough to trust, room to get up again, and herd mates it feels safe enough with to let its guard drop for the half hour of dreaming it cannot do standing.
