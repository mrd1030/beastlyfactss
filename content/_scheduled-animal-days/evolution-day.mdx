In January 1977, the island of Daphne Major in the Galápagos held about 1,200 medium ground finches. Twelve months later it held 180. The rains had failed, the small soft seeds the birds prefer ran out first, and the finches that lived through the year were mostly the large ones with the deepest beaks, the birds able to crack the hard seeds that were left.

That was natural selection, measured in a living population, about 118 years after Charles Darwin put the idea in print. The anniversary of the printing is Evolution Day, and the most repeated story about the book's first day is slightly off.

## A Date With No Founder

Evolution Day is November 24th, the day in 1859 that John Murray published *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection* in London. That date is fixed by the book. No organization claims to have created the observance, and no founder is on record. It is kept as a day for explaining the idea the book introduced: that populations change over generations because some individuals survive to breed and others do not.

## Sold Out, in a Manner of Speaking

The usual account says the first edition sold out on the day it was published. Darwin wrote as much in his own journal, and in a letter to Thomas Huxley dated November 24th he reported hearing from Murray that the whole edition had sold on the first day.

The numbers behind that are smaller and more specific. Murray printed 1,250 copies. Of those, 1,192 were available for sale: twelve went to the author, forty-one to reviewers and five to Stationers' Hall for copyright. Darwin took at least twenty more for presentation, which left about 1,170 for the trade.

Those copies were sold on November 22nd, two days before publication, at Murray's autumn trade sale, where booksellers placed their orders. Booksellers ordered around 1,500, more books than there were. Every copy was spoken for by publication day, 500 of them by Mudie's Circulating Library alone.

So the sellout was a wholesale event. The edition was gone before the public could buy a copy, and the Darwin bibliographer R. B. Freeman pointed out the obvious problem with the retail version: once copies had gone out to shops across the country, nobody could know whether they had sold. Demand outran the printing. A second edition of 3,000 copies came out on January 7, 1860.

## Watching It Happen

When Peter and Rosemary Grant began work on Daphne Major, most people would have been skeptical that a wild population could change measurably in a single generation. The Grants spent several months a year on the island from 1973 to 2012. It covers under 125 acres, small enough that they could net its finches, measure each beak with calipers and put colored bands on each bird's leg, and over the decades they tagged roughly 20,000 birds.

The 1977 drought was the first clear result. In a normal year the wet season brings about five inches of rain, and in 1977 it brought less than an inch. Survivors were larger and bigger-beaked than the birds that died, and because beak size is strongly heritable, their offspring were large too. The next generation carried a measurably bigger beak than the generation before the drought, which is evolution in the strict sense: a change in a population across generations, caused by which individuals survived to breed.

Then the direction reversed. In 1982 five large ground finches, a bigger-beaked species from another island, stayed on Daphne and bred. When drought came again from 2003 to 2005, the two species competed for a dwindling supply of large, hard seeds, and this time the medium ground finches with smaller beaks did better. Average beak size in the medium ground finch fell, and the gap between the two species widened.

In a review of the first thirty years the Grants found that natural selection on the medium ground finch's body and beak occurred frequently, sometimes pushing one way and sometimes swinging back and forth. Beak and body traits are highly heritable, so the populations tracked the selection. Their conclusion was that the shape of the birds at the end of the study could not have been predicted from the start. Evolution was predictable one generation ahead and unpredictable over thirty years, because the weather that steered it was.

## Closer to Home

The finches are the cleanest case because every bird was measured, but the process is not confined to remote islands. The house cat carries one of the more familiar examples: [domestic cat meows are shorter and higher than the calls of their wildcat ancestors](/blog/cats-invented-the-meow-for-humans/), reshaped over thousands of years by the one species that answers them.

It also cuts the other way, against the idea that ancient-looking animals have stopped changing. Crocodiles are the stock example of a living fossil, and the fossil record shows [a lineage that kept producing marine, running and plant-eating forms](/blog/crocodiles-survived-what-killed-the-dinosaurs/) long after its body plan seemed settled. Sharks have been at it for roughly 450 million years, [longer than there have been trees](/blog/sharks-are-older-than-trees-and-9-other-facts-that-will-blow-your-mind/), and they are not the same sharks.

The medium ground finches of Daphne Major are still there, and their beaks still move with the rain. The process Darwin described took one dry year to show up in them, and one generation to be passed on.
