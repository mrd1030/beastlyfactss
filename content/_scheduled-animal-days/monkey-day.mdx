Living primates run from Madame Berthe's mouse lemur, at about an ounce, to a male gorilla at around 440 pounds. A 2017 assessment counted 504 species in 79 genera, found them in the wild in 90 countries, and put about 60 percent of them at risk of extinction.

Monkey Day takes its name from the best known of them, though its founders meant the whole order.

## Two Artists and a Random Date

Monkey Day falls on December 14th. It was created in 2000 by the illustrator Casey Sorrow and the artist Eric Millikin. Sorrow has described how: he scribbled "Monkey Day" onto a friend's calendar on a random date, without the friend knowing, and when the day arrived the group went to the pub to celebrate it.

So the date means nothing. It is whatever square Sorrow's pen landed on, and it has stayed there.

It remains an unofficial holiday, kept by pranksters and primate lovers. Over the years it shifted from a party toward fundraising for primate sanctuaries. The founders once held a charity art show for Chimps Inc, a sanctuary that sent along two paintings made by its resident chimpanzees.

## The Founder's Position on Pet Monkeys

Sorrow is direct about the thing the day's cartoon monkeys tend to sell. "I believe, along with most reputable primate organisations, that monkeys (and primates) should not be kept as pets," he has said. Many pet monkeys, in his account, end up in sanctuaries once they lose their cute phase or become unmanageable.

The welfare literature agrees with him. A 2009 review by Carl Soulsbury and colleagues at the University of Bristol went through primate pet keeping in the UK, including complaint data from the RSPCA, and concluded there is strong evidence that primates are not suitable pets. Their welfare, the authors found, is unlikely to be adequately met in a normal household, and they recommended that the practice end.

## What Counts as a Monkey

The day says monkey, but primates are a wider group. The quick field test is the tail: almost all monkeys have one, and apes, the gibbons and great apes, do not. A chimpanzee on a Monkey Day card is an ape.

Lemurs, lorises and tarsiers belong to the order as well, and none of them is a monkey or an ape. Every primate native to Madagascar is a lemur, 103 species in the 2017 count. The slow loris is a loris, and [the only venomous primate](/blog/slow-loris-the-only-venomous-primate/), mixing secretion from a gland on its arm into its saliva. Lorises are also a pet trade species: the 2017 assessment notes their trade in Sri Lanka, Cambodia and Indonesia for traditional medicine and as pets.

## Sixty Percent and Falling

The 2017 assessment, led by Alejandro Estrada and Paul Garber with 29 co-authors, measured how much trouble the order is in. Primates live in four regions: the Neotropics with 171 species, mainland Africa with 111, Madagascar with 103 and Asia with 119. Two thirds of all species occur in just four countries: Brazil, Madagascar, Indonesia and the Democratic Republic of Congo.

The threat is spread unevenly. In Madagascar 87 percent of primate species are threatened, against 73 percent in Asia, 37 percent in mainland Africa and 36 percent in the Neotropics. Across the order, about 75 percent of species have declining populations.

Agriculture is the largest driver, affecting 76 percent of species through lost habitat, followed by logging and by hunting and trapping at 60 percent each. The illegal pet trade is named among the pressures as well, and it carries a disease problem of its own: primates that escape or are released from the pet trade or sanctuaries can carry pathogens into wild populations.

Sorrow's advice and the assessment point the same way. The monkey worth celebrating on December 14th is the one still in forest in Brazil, Indonesia or the Congo basin, where most of the order lives and most of it is losing ground. Its cousins the [lemurs of Madagascar](/blog/world-lemur-day/), the most threatened primates of any region, are losing the same ground.
