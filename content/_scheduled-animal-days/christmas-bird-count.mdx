North America has about 2.9 billion fewer breeding birds than it did in 1970, a net loss of 29 percent across the 529 species that a 2019 study could measure. Grassland birds lost the most proportionally, 53 percent, and three quarters of grassland species are still declining.

That figure was assembled from long-term monitoring, and some of the monitoring is a winter tradition that started as a direct answer to shooting birds for fun at Christmas.

## The Side Hunt

Before 1900, a holiday tradition among North American hunters was the Christmas Side Hunt. Hunters went out, and whoever came back with the biggest pile of feathered and furred quarry won.

Frank M. Chapman proposed something else. He had left banking in 1888 for a poorly paid assistant curator job at the American Museum of Natural History, where he rose to Curator of Birds, and he founded and edited the magazine Bird-Lore for bird lovers. He was also an early officer of the Audubon Society. His proposal was a "Christmas Bird Census": go out on the holiday, and count the birds instead of killing them.

On Christmas Day 1900, 27 birders ran 25 counts. They stretched from Toronto, Ontario to Pacific Grove, California, with most of them near the cities of the northeast. Between them they recorded about 90 species.

## How a Count Works

The count now runs every year between December 14th and January 5th. It is organized as circles: each local count covers a fixed circle, picks one day in the window, and does all its counting within that 24-hour period. The National Audubon Society runs the count, and a compiler leads each circle and gathers the results. Taking part is free and open to any level of birder.

Field counters survey an area during daylight, alone or in groups, and record every bird they see or hear. Feeder counters stay put and log what turns up at a stationary feeder in a backyard or local park, so a northern cardinal at a kitchen window can go into a dataset that runs back to 1900.

Telling those birds apart at speed is the skill a count rewards, and it comes from [the four keys field courses teach](/blog/ultimate-birdwatching-guide-best-places-times-tips-2026/), working from size and shape, then broad color pattern, behavior and habitat, before fine field marks. Some birds make it harder than others. A winter count along open water is where [bald eagles](/beastlypedia/bald-eagle/) gather, and the young ones stay mottled brown with dark heads for five or six years, which makes them among the most misidentified raptors on the continent.

## A Century of Winter Data

A single count is a list. More than a century of counts on the same circles is a dataset, and that is what the Christmas Bird Count became. Combined with the Breeding Bird Survey, which counts birds in the breeding season, its records have let researchers follow long-term population trends across North America.

Audubon's 2014 climate report used count data to model how climate change could shift the ranges of 588 species, and projected that 314 of them would lose more than half of their current climatic range by 2080. The Environmental Protection Agency's 2012 climate indicators report included the count-based work as one of 26 indicators.

The 2019 study, "Decline of the North American avifauna" by Kenneth Rosenberg and ten co-authors in Science, drew on many independent monitoring programs, and its authors credit an analysis of Christmas Bird Count trends among them. It found declines in 303 of the 529 species it measured. Across 67 bird families, 38 showed a net loss. More than 90 percent of the total loss came from just 12 families, among them sparrows, warblers, blackbirds and finches.

The winter side of the picture is the part a Christmas count sees directly. Species that spend the winter in temperate regions took the largest net loss of any group in the study, about 1.4 billion birds. Among native species, the 419 migratory ones lost 2.5 billion between them, while 100 resident species, the birds that stay put all year, showed a small net gain of 26 million. Wetland birds were the one habitat group to grow overall, by 13 percent, carried by a 56 percent rise in waterfowl.

A second line of evidence pointed the same way. A network of 143 weather radars, tracking the mass of birds moving through the night sky during migration, showed a similarly steep drop over a recent ten-year period.

The common birds are the point of the count. A chickadee or a house finch tallied on the same circle every winter for decades is the kind of record that catches a slow loss before a species is rare, and the people who catch it are volunteers standing in the cold with a clipboard.
