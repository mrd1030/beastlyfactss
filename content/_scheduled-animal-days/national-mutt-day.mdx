Nearly half the dogs in the largest genetic study of dog behavior yet published were mutts, and most of them were thoroughly mixed. About 70% had no single breed contributing even 45% of their ancestry. The most common breed in their family trees, the American pit bull terrier, came in at 9.9%.

Those are the dogs National Mutt Day exists for. They are also the dogs for which a breed label, whether guessed at an intake desk or printed on a DNA report, says the least about what living with them will be like.

## Two Dates and No Signature

National Mutt Day falls on 2 December, with a second edition on 31 July. Both dates appear on the day's own page, which also calls it National Mixed Breed Dog Day and closes with a plea to spay and neuter.

What that page does not give is a founder or a year. Calendar sites supply both, without pointing to anything the day publishes about itself. The date is settled. Who started it is not documented by the people who run it, and a founding story copied often enough starts to look like a fact.

## What 18,385 Dogs Showed

In 2022 Kathleen Morrill, Elinor Karlsson and colleagues published "Ancestry-inclusive dog genomics challenges popular breed stereotypes" in Science. Owners of 18,385 dogs answered more than a hundred questions about behavior, and 2,155 of the dogs were genotyped. About half were purebred.

Most behavioral traits turned out to be heritable, with heritability above 25%. Breed explained about 9% of the variation in how individual dogs behaved. The two results fit together because a trait can run in families without running neatly in breeds: the spread inside any one breed is wide, and the breeds overlap heavily.

The paper's own example shows the size of the effect. The chance that an owner ranks a dog in the top quarter for human sociability rises from 22% for a mutt to 40% for a [golden retriever](/encyclopedia/animal/golden-retriever/). That is a meaningful shift, and it still leaves most golden retrievers outside the top quarter.

The authors' explanation is historical. Modern breeds are only about 160 years old, a Victorian invention selected mainly for looks. The behaviors people think of as breed-typical come from thousands of years of selection for hunting, guarding and herding that happened before anyone drew up a breed standard.

## Where Ancestry Shows Up

The effect is small, but it is not zero everywhere. Biddability, the paper's term for responsiveness to direction and commands, is among the more heritable and more breed-differentiated traits, and in mutts, border collie ancestry went with higher biddability. That suits what the breed was built for. A [border collie](/blog/border-collie-enrichment-guide/) carries a predator's hunting sequence edited down to the stalk and the fixed stare, with the kill removed and a handler directing the rest.

Other stereotypes failed. Labrador retriever ancestry had no detectable effect on human sociability in mutts, even though friendliness is the Labrador's entire reputation. Agonistic threshold, defined as how easily a dog is provoked by frightening or uncomfortable things, was less heritable, and for it the authors found breed almost uninformative.

That last result is the one an adopter should hold onto. How a dog reacts when it is startled, cornered or hurt is the trait people most want predicted before a dog comes home, and it is the one the breed mix predicts worst. The authors summed up the whole picture as "a weak but discernible genetic effect of breed on disposition."

## The Label Was Often a Guess

All of that assumes the mix is known. On a kennel card it usually is not. Victoria Voith and colleagues at Western University of Health Sciences compared adoption agencies' breed labels with DNA results for 20 adopted dogs of unknown parentage. Of the 16 that an agency had assigned specific breeds, DNA found a named breed among the predominant ones in 4. In 14 of the 16, at least one breed on the card did not show up at all.

A cheek swab is better evidence than a guess from across a kennel, but breed results vary between testing companies, and the health side of the same report is uneven too: [the disease variants a pet DNA panel lists](/blog/pet-dna-test-results-guide/) run from mutations shown to cause disease in a given breed to changes never shown to cause anything. Where ancestry does earn its keep is health risk. In the large veterinary datasets on [inherited and conformation-driven eye disease](/blog/inherited-eye-disease-dogs-cats-guide/), crossbred dogs are the baseline that breed risk is measured against, and the flat-faced breeds sit far above it.

## The Other 91%

None of this makes ancestry meaningless. It makes it a small input next to the dog itself. A mutt with a third of its genome from herding breeds may be more responsive to cues than average, or may not, and the only way to find out is to watch that dog respond to cues.

The 91% that breed leaves to other causes is the individual animal: the one in the shelter run, with its own threshold for being startled and its own interest in people. National Mutt Day asks people to go and meet that dog, and the genetics back the request: the breed mix will not say what the meeting will.
