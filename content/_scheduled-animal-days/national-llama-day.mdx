A llama that spits is usually settling something with another llama, most often who gets to eat first. Spitting is a herd's way of sorting out rank at the feed trough, warding off a pushy neighbor or turning down an unwanted suitor.

When a llama does aim at a person, it tends to be scared or hurting. Veterinarians stop it with a cotton towel draped over the nose and mouth, because llamas dislike the smell of their own spit and quickly learn not to spit under one. The kick is the bigger hazard: a llama can kick hard enough to break a human bone.

## 9 December, Unsigned

National Llama Day is 9 December. The date is consistent wherever the day is listed, and that is about all that can be said for its paperwork. No organization claims it, no founder is on record, and the founding stories that circulate online come from pages that cite nothing. The date is settled and the origin is not, and it is better left blank than filled in.

## Llama or Alpaca

The two are close relatives and are often confused, but they were bred for different jobs. An alpaca weighs around 150 pounds and is kept for its fleece, which is lighter and warmer than sheep's wool. A llama can reach 400 pounds and was bred to work: llamas commonly carry 25 to 30% of their body weight for five to eight miles, though nobody heavier than a child rides one.

Both spit, both are herd animals that do poorly kept alone, and both can be dangerous when stressed. Oklahoma's wildlife code opens its list of species exempt from import permits and breeder's licenses with alpacas and bison, the same list that puts a [coati](/blog/coati-legal-guide/) on the footing of a fennec fox.

The job researchers have measured belongs to the llama.

## The Guard Llama

Kelly Powell's 1993 Iowa State thesis, "The use of guard llamas to protect sheep from coyote predation", surveyed 145 sheep producers using guard llamas by telephone in 1991 and 1992. On 114 ranches, average annual losses to predators fell from 11% of the flock before the llama arrived to 1% after it. Of 97 producers who reported losses beforehand, 62% lost no sheep to predators afterward. Most guard llamas were gelded males, and flocks guarded by one llama did better than flocks with several, 1.3% losses against 7.0%. The worry ranchers voiced about multiple llamas was that they would group up and ignore the sheep.

When producers saw a llama meet a predator, the llama usually walked or ran straight at it. If it caught up, it kicked and trampled. One rancher found a trampled coyote in his field. It matches the wild ancestor, in which a male guanaco defends his group by chasing intruders and sounding the alarm. A guard llama is doing a different job from a herding dog: a [border collie](/blog/border-collie-enrichment-guide/) works sheep with a predator's stalk and stare and the kill bred out, while a llama is a prey animal that walks toward the coyote.

Survey answers are owners grading their own animals, so the controlled evidence matters. Laurie Meadows and Frederick Knowlton placed 20 llamas with Utah sheep producers and tracked their flocks against unguarded ones for 20 months. In the first summer, unguarded flocks lost significantly more lambs. In the second, losses in the unguarded flocks fell to the guarded flocks' level, and the authors suggested predation may need to pass a threshold before a llama makes a visible difference.

There are limits. Llamas charge and intimidate smaller predators but cannot defend against dog packs, cougars or bears. One rancher in the Iowa State survey lost a seven-month-old guard llama to a pack of coyotes.

## The Berserk Male Story

The best-known llama warning is berserk male syndrome, also called aberrant behavior syndrome: a llama or alpaca, usually an intact male, that turns aggressive toward people as it matures. The standard explanation is that a bottle-raised cria imprints on its handlers and, at sexual maturity, directs fighting and mating behavior at humans.

That explanation has never been tested properly. A 2015 Iowa State survey by Stephen Ball, Katherine Way, Jennifer Schleining and Suzanne Millman collected 22 cases from owners and veterinarians. Seventeen were intact males, four were geldings and one was a female, so the name is a tendency more than a rule. Most of the animals had been raised by their own mothers, with daily or weekly handling, and the authors found no obvious association with how often a young animal had been handled. Aggression appeared between one and three years of age and was aimed at people more than at other animals. Every treatment tried, including castration, retraining and moving the animal, failed more often than it worked, and most of the animals were euthanized.

Twenty-two cases cannot overturn the bottle-rearing idea, but they show how little is underneath it. The same hypothesis has better evidence in parrots, where [hand-reared birds were more prone to aggression](/blog/choosing-a-pet-bird-guide/) and to imprinting on a single person than parent-reared ones. For camelids, the authors' conclusion was that the cause and the treatment both still need research.

What is well established is the ordinary llama: a large, social grazer that settles its arguments at the trough with spit, and that, set down alone among sheep, will walk out to meet a coyote.
