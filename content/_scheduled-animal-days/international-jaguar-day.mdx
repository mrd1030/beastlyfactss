A jaguar often kills with one bite through the skull. The jaws behind it are strong enough to pierce the heads of capybaras, peccaries and caimans, and the cat hunts in water as readily as on land.

On paper the species is in reasonable shape. The IUCN lists the jaguar as Near Threatened, a single step from Least Concern. That grade is an average, and nearly all of the good news inside it comes from one place.

## Fourteen Countries Proposed It

International Jaguar Day is 29 November. It came out of the Jaguar 2030 Forum, held at United Nations headquarters in New York in March 2018, where 14 jaguar range countries endorsed the Jaguar 2030 Statement. One of its commitments was a proposal to create an international day for the species.

The plan behind the day went public that November in Sharm El-Sheikh, Egypt, at the 14th meeting of the parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity. Range governments, UNDP, WWF, the Wildlife Conservation Society and Panthera launched the Jaguar 2030 Conservation Roadmap for the Americas there, with a target of securing 30 priority jaguar landscapes by 2030.

So the day has founders, and there are fourteen of them: it belongs to the range countries collectively. The date has no published explanation. Neither the forum documents nor the day's own site says why 29 November was picked.

## One Population Carries the Average

The jaguar ranges across 18 countries, from Mexico to Argentina. Its Red List assessment divides that range into 34 sub-populations, and 33 of them are Endangered or Critically Endangered. The one exception is the Amazon population, which on its own would be Least Concern. It is large enough to lift the whole species to Near Threatened.

The total is not small. A 2018 study by Włodzimierz Jędrzejewski and colleagues in PLOS ONE combined camera trap density surveys with satellite habitat data and estimated the world population at about 173,000 jaguars, most of them concentrated in the Amazon Basin. Brazil alone may hold about half. Outside the basin, the same model found populations that were small and fragmented.

That is a large number for a big cat. Set beside the tiger, [whose entire wild population would fit in one small town](/blog/every-wild-tiger-would-fit-in-a-small-town/), it can make the jaguar sound secure. Split by place, it reads very differently. The species has disappeared from more than three quarters of its historic range in Central America, and its numbers fell by an estimated 20 to 25 percent over two decades.

A jaguar in the forests of Belize or the dry scrub of northern Mexico belongs to a population that is, by the Red List's own criteria, in trouble. It does not share in the Amazon's numbers. The 30 landscapes in the 2030 roadmap are an attempt to stop the rest of the range from going the same way as its northern edge.

## Gone From Two Countries

El Salvador and Uruguay have lost their jaguars entirely. In the United States, lone males still disperse north from Mexico now and then. No female and no breeding population has been documented north of the border for nearly 100 years.

Jaguars in American captivity are a separate story. The jaguar is one of eight cats named in the Big Cat Public Safety Act, and [the federal ban on breeding or privately possessing a big cat since December 2022](/blog/big-cat-legal-guide/) covers it exactly as it covers a tiger.

International protection tightened in February 2020, when parties to the Convention on Migratory Species adopted a proposal led by Costa Rica to list the jaguar on both of the convention's appendices. Appendix I commits range states to the strictest protection, which prohibits taking, hunting or capturing the animal. A cat whose populations straddle national borders needs that kind of agreement, because a corridor protected on one side of a frontier and cleared on the other does not function as a corridor.

## Built for the River

The jaguar's paws are large and rounded, and they work as well in water as on the ground. In flooded Amazon forest, jaguars swim from tree to tree to reach monkeys and sloths in the canopy.

That comfort in water shapes the menu. Prey runs from peccaries to the capybara, [the largest living rodent, whose usual answer to danger is to head straight for the river](/beastlypedia/capybara/). For a jaguar, the river is no refuge. The cat also hunts large aquatic reptiles: turtles, crocodilians and even the [green anaconda, the heaviest snake alive](/beastlypedia/green-anaconda/).

Like nearly every wild cat, a jaguar lives and hunts alone, meeting others mainly to mate. A solitary predator this size needs a lot of ground per animal, and it needs that ground to connect: forest to river, river to the next patch of forest. In the Amazon it still does. In 33 places across the rest of the range, it is coming apart.
