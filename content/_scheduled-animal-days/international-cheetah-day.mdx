A cheetah at full stretch runs at 60 to 70 miles per hour, and it can hold that speed for about 300 yards. A full sprint lasts around 20 seconds. The fastest land mammal is a specialist in one short burst, and almost everything unusual about the animal follows from that.

International Cheetah Day is December 4th, and the date was chosen for a single cheetah.

## A Birthday From Oregon

The day was created in 2010 by Laurie Marker, founder and executive director of the Cheetah Conservation Fund. She designated it through the IUCN, the International Union for Conservation of Nature, the global body whose Red List rates how close each species is to extinction. She named it in remembrance of Khayam, a cheetah she had raised from a cub at Wildlife Safari in Winston, Oregon, and chose his birthday for the date. The day exists to raise awareness of the cheetah's decline and to rally people in every country to help conserve it.

Khayam was more than a mascot. In 1977 Marker took him to Namibia to test whether a cheetah born in captivity could be taught to hunt, and the pair later returned to Oregon.

So the date is personal, and it is documented by the organization that set it.

## Four Things the Fastest Cat Cannot Do

1. **It cannot run for long.** The burst is the whole strategy. A cheetah can go from a standstill to 45 miles per hour in about 2.5 seconds, but it cannot hold its top speed past a short sprint. The usual explanation is that the cat overheats and has to quit. A 2013 study in Biology Letters by Robyn Hetem and colleagues tested that on four free-living cheetahs fitted with body temperature loggers, and found the cats abandoned hunts without overheating. Body temperature averaged about 101°F when a chase was called off. The bigger rise came after a successful hunt, about 2.3°F against 0.9°F after a failed one, which the authors read as stress after the kill.

2. **It cannot roar.** A cheetah purrs much like a house cat, sometimes for minutes at a stretch. Over distance it uses a bird-like chirp that carries about a mile. Like the snow leopard, it has no roar at all.

3. **It cannot fully retract its claws.** They are blunt and semi-retractable, and they stay partly out to grip the ground like cleats. The long tail works as a rudder, swinging to counter the body's weight in a fast turn. Both are fittings for a sprinter.

4. **It cannot keep its food.** About half of a cheetah's chases end in a kill, and it then loses roughly half of what it catches to lions, hyenas and sometimes groups of vultures. A cat built light for speed is in no position to defend a carcass.

## Ninety-Five Percent the Same

The cheetah's other weakness is in its genome, and it is old.

In 2015 a team led by Pavel Dobrynin and Stephen O'Brien, with Laurie Marker among the authors, published the genome of wild-born African cheetahs. Cheetah genomes turned out to be on average 95% homozygous. The outbred domestic cat sits at about 24%, the Virunga mountain gorilla at about 78%, and an inbred Abyssinian cat breed at about 63%. The cheetah is more uniform than all of them.

The team traced that to two ancient population crashes. The first happened more than 100,000 years ago, when cheetahs migrated out of the Americas into Eurasia and Africa. The second came between about 11,000 and 12,600 years ago in Africa, at the time of the late Pleistocene large mammal extinctions.

The consequences show up in living animals. Cheetahs have lost much of the immune gene diversity that lets a body tell self from stranger, which explains why unrelated cheetahs accept each other's skin grafts. More than 80% of a male's sperm are malformed, and the study linked that to damaging mutations fixed in a gene that controls sperm development. A species this uniform has less variation to draw on when a new disease arrives.

## About 7,100

The most thorough count is a 2017 assessment in PNAS led by Sarah Durant, which put the global population at about 7,100 cheetahs, confined to 9% of the species' historical range. A century ago there were an estimated 100,000.

That is a little above the snow leopard, [whose wild count runs from about 3,920 to 6,390](/blog/international-snow-leopard-day/), and far below the jaguar, [estimated at around 173,000 in 2018](/blog/international-jaguar-day/). The cheetah's problem is where it lives. Durant's team found that 77% of the cheetah's remaining range lies outside protected areas, where the species faces most of its threats. Protected parks alone cannot hold a species that wide-ranging and sparse, and the authors argued the collapse justified moving the cheetah up to Endangered on the IUCN Red List.

Asia is further gone. Cheetahs have vanished from 98% of their historical Asian range, and the Asiatic subspecies now survives only in Iran, as fewer than 50 animals across three core areas.

The animal itself keeps one odd social habit. Females live alone, but young males often stay together for life in a coalition, usually two or three littermates. It is the closest any cat comes to [the lion's permanent groups](/blog/lions-are-the-only-social-cat/). Everything else about the cheetah it does on its own: a light, lone hunter staking each meal on twenty seconds, about 7,100 times over.
