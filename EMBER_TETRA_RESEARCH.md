## 1. Identity

1.1 Scientific name and authority
- "Hyphessobrycon amandae Géry and Uj, 1987". Status "valid", record credibility "verified - standards met". Source: ITIS (S3). https://itis.gov/servlet/SingleRpt/SingleRpt?search_topic=TSN&search_value=639893
- Seriously Fish gives the same authority: "Géry & Uj, 1987". (S1) https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/hyphessobrycon-amandae/
- FishBase: authority Géry & Uj, 1987. (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376

1.2 Naming story
- ETYFish: "in honor of Amanda Bleher (1910-1991)". The entry adds (paraphrased by the fetch tool) that she was the mother of ornamental fish wholesaler Heiko Bleher, who collected the holotype, and that the name recognizes her interest in and knowledge of Brazil's freshwater fauna and flora. (S4) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- FishBase eponymy: the species honors Amanda Bleher, a German limnologist, whose son Heiko Bleher collected the holotype. Search-result text from the same FishBase page: "Amanda Flora HIlda Bleher née Kiel (1910-1991) was a German limnologist and adventurer". (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Fishkeeper (UK): "are named in honour of Heiko's mother, hence the 'ae' ending rather than 'i.'" (S19) https://www.fishkeeper.co.uk/stories/Heiko-Bleher. Note: this page does not itself name her as Amanda; S2 and S4 do.
- Genus meaning. FishBase: "Greek, hyphesson, -on, -on = a little smaller + Greek, bryko = to bite". (S2). ETYFish: "hyphḗssōn (ὑφήσσων), smaller, allusion not explained, probably referring to their size (described at 26-42 mm)" and the "-brycon" element from brýchō, "to bite, gnash teeth or eat greedily" (paraphrased). (S4)

1.3 Family (sources disagree: this is a recent reclassification, not an error)
- FishBase: "Acestrorhamphidae (American tetras)", subfamily Hyphessobryconinae. (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Wikipedia, citing Catalog of Fishes: "belonging to the family Acestrorhamphidae, the American characins." (S14)
- ITIS: Characidae. (S3). Seriously Fish: "Characidae". (S1). GBIF: Characidae. (S5)
- Reading: older and slower-updating databases still say Characidae; FishBase and the Catalog of Fishes (via Wikipedia) now place it in Acestrorhamphidae. Owner to decide which the site uses; the existing neon/cardinal entries should be checked for consistency.

1.4 Original description
- GBIF "publishedIn": "Géry, J.; Uj, A. (1987). The ember tetra: a new pygmy characid tetra from the Rio das Mortes, Brazil, Hyphessobrycon amandae sp. n. (Pisces, Characoidei). Tropical Fish Hobbyist. 35 (5)(Jan.): 58-61, 65. [Possibly published in December 1986.]" (S5, read via API, see note above)

1.5 Trade names
- "This species is also traded under the names 'Amanda's', 'dwarf red', 'red', and 'fire' tetra." (S1)

## 2. Origin and wild habitat

- Distribution: "South America: Araguaia River basin." (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Type locality and river: "Rio das Mortes, some 100 km before its confluence with the Braço Maior of the Rio Araguaia". Country: Brazil. Type locality given as the Rio das Mortes in Mato Grosso (paraphrase of the page). (S1)
- Habitat type: "presumably inhabits minor tributaries, backwaters and oxbow lakes rather than main river channels". Water: "soft, weakly acidic water". Seriously Fish opens that section with "Little information appears to exist". (S1)
- Field account, Araguaia basin near Aruanã: "Aruanã sits on the banks of the mighty Araguaia River in the state of Goiás." "Currently, there is a dearth of information regarding the habitats of the ember tetra (Hyphessobrycon amandae)." (S6) https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/finding-ember-tetras-in-brazil/
- Water clarity and light at that site: "The water was clear, and there was little overhead cover meaning plenty of light hit the water." The author "slipped into the warm water". No temperature, pH or hardness readings were taken. (S6)
- Plants at that site (paraphrase): Eichhornia diversifolia, Cabomba furcata, Ludwigia inclinata, Ludwigia sedoides, flooded grasses; central channel mostly plant-free with leaf litter over a yellow clay-like substrate mixed with fine gravel and sand. (S6)
- Seasonal drying: "She also explained that in the summer, the water pretty much vanishes." (S6)
- Basin richness: "The Araguaia River is home to over 800 species of fish." (S6)
- DISAGREEMENT on water color: AquaInfo says the water is often very dark from tannins with tree-lined banks (paraphrase). (S12) https://aquainfo.nl/en/article/hyphessobrycon-amandae-ember-tetra/. The one first-hand field account (S6) found clear water. Seriously Fish (S1) says only "soft, weakly acidic". Both may be true across the basin; nothing measured.
- Error to ignore: The Shrimp Farm's header lists origin as "Southern China", but its body says "Ember tetras are naturally found in Brazil, specifically in the Araguaia River Basin." (S10)

## 3. Size, sexing, lifespan

3.1 Adult size
- "20mm SL" (standard length, snout to base of tail, so excludes the tail fin). (S1)
- "Max length : 2.0 cm SL male/unsexed." (S2)
- Aquarium Co-Op: "This species only grows up to 0.8 inch (2 cm)". (S8)
- Fishkeeper: "2cm (0.8")". (S9). Practical Fishkeeping: "2cm/0.8"". (S7)
- AquaInfo: "grows to no more than about 2 to 3 centimeters, including the tail." (S12). Wikipedia (uncited): about 2 cm (0.8 in) to 3 cm (1.2 in). (S14)
- Flowgrow: males to 2 cm, females to 3 cm ("Länge der Männchen: 0 - 2 cm", "Länge der Weibchen: 0 - 3 cm"). (S13)
- Summary for writers: 2 cm (0.8 in) standard length is the referenced maximum; total length with tail runs to about 2.5 to 3 cm (1 to 1.2 in) in the lower-tier sources.

3.2 Sexing
- "Adult males are more intensely-coloured, especially when in spawning condition"; females noticeably rounder-bodied (paraphrase). (S1)
- "Males are slender in profile, while females have rounder bodies, especially when viewed from above." (S8)

3.3 Lifespan in captivity (no authoritative source found; all figures are hobby-tier)
- Aquarium Source: "The typical Ember Tetra lifespan is between 2 and 4 years." It also says reports of up to ten years are "widely considered to be inaccurate." (S11)
- AquaInfo: "can live for a maximum of about 2 to 3 years" in a well-kept aquarium. (S12)
- Wikipedia: "have been known to live ten years or more", with no citation. (S14). Do not use.
- Seriously Fish, FishBase, Aquarium Co-Op and Fishkeeper give no lifespan.

3.4 Wild lifespan: no source found.

5.1 Minimum tank size and footprint

- Practical Fishkeeping: "45x25x25cm (18x10x10")". (S7)
- The Shrimp Farm: "a 10-gallon (long) aquarium should be enough for a small school". (S10)
- Aquarium Source: "The ideal tank size for Ember Tetras is 10 gallons at a minimum." (S11)
- Aquarium Co-Op: "you have the flexibility to keep them in a 5-gallon nano tank with a smaller school". (S8)
- Flowgrow: tank length 40 to 180 cm, and "Nano-Aquarium geeignet?: Nein" (not suitable for nano tanks). (S13)
- AquaInfo is internally inconsistent: text says "at least 40 gallons and about 40 centimeters long", data box says minimum length 50 cm. (S12). Do not use.
- DISAGREEMENT: 5 gallons (Co-Op) vs a 45 cm (18 in) footprint, roughly a standard 10 gallon (Seriously Fish, PFK, Shrimp Farm, Aquarium Source). Flowgrow says not for nanos at all.

## 6. Diet

- Wild diet: "Probably a micropredator feeding on tiny invertebrates and other zooplankton in nature." Note the "probably": nobody has studied gut contents. (S1)
- AquaInfo repeats this: "a micropredator that feeds on small invertebrates and other zooplankton." (S12). Wikipedia (uncited) says small invertebrates and plants. (S14). Aquarium Co-Op calls them omnivores eating zooplankton, small invertebrates and plant matter (paraphrase). (S8)
- Captive staples: "dried foods of a suitable size" plus "should also be offered daily meals of small live and frozen fare", with Artemia nauplii and Moina named (paraphrase for the species list). (S1)
- Fishkeeper: "Flake, granules and frozen foods"; small frozen foods named as "cyclops, baby brineshrimp, and daphnia". (S9)
- The Shrimp Farm: "a high-quality daily microcarnivore staple", supplemented with frozen bloodworms and live Daphnia (paraphrase). (S10)
- Food size: "they do have little mouths that prefer to feed on tiny, slow-sinking foods." (S8)
- Feeding frequency: Aquarium Source: "Aquarists have had success feeding Ember Tetras anywhere from 2-4 times a day" (recommends three); its own FAQ says "once or twice daily". (S11). Seriously Fish implies at least daily live or frozen meals (S1). No stronger source found on frequency.

## 7. Behavior and tankmates

- Temperament: "Very peaceful and will not compete well with very boisterous or much larger tankmates." (S1)
- Good companions: "similarly-sized, peaceful characids", plus small catfish and calm surface dwellers (paraphrase), and "an ideal dither fish for Apistogramma spp. and other dwarf cichlids". (S1)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "only combine with other tiny fish and invertebrates". (S7)
- Aquarium Co-Op: they "do fine with any similar-sized, peaceful animals that won't eat them"; bettas and dwarf gouramis may be territorial (paraphrase). Also: "ember tetras are relatively outgoing, especially in large groups". (S8)
- Fishkeeper: "model citizens" that "can be kept alongside a range of community species including shrimps." (S9)
- Fin nipping: no source found that says embers nip fins, and none that says they do not. Record as no source found.
- Shrimp: Aquarium Co-Op: "While they will leave the adult shrimp alone, almost all fish will opportunistically go after baby shrimp". (S8). The Shrimp Farm: "yes, ember tetras make excellent tankmates for both Neocaridina and Caridina dwarf shrimp!", with dense plants protecting shrimplets (paraphrase). (S10)
- Nano tanks: see 5.1 for the disagreement.
- Swimming level: Wikipedia (uncited) says they "swim at the middle level of the aquarium". (S14). Not citable alone.
- Mouth-size rule: Aquarium Co-Op's "won't eat them" (S8) is the only direct statement. No source gives a numeric mouth-size rule for this species. The wild predator at the field site was the pike cichlid (S6).

## 8. Health

8.1 Common diseases
- Aquarium Source: "there aren't any species-specific diseases that plague the Ember Tetra", with poor diet and water quality raising general risk (paraphrase). (S11)
- No species-specific disease literature found.

8.2 Ich and heat treatment at 86°F (IMPORTANT for the shared ich guide link)
- VCA: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF." and "Not all fish can stand the heat, though." Parasite replication is faster in warmer water: "they replicate faster in warmer water." (S15) https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/fish-white-spot-disease-ich
- Ember tetra temperature ceilings: 28°C (82°F) in Seriously Fish, FishBase, Practical Fishkeeping, Fishkeeper and Aquarium Co-Op (S1, S2, S7, S8, S9); 84°F in Aquarium Source (S11); only Flowgrow reaches 30°C (86°F) (S13).
- No critical thermal maximum (CTmax) or lethal-temperature study for H. amandae was found. Unlike the cardinal tetra guide (which cites a measured 50% mortality near 33.7°C), there is no measured ceiling for this species.
- Plain reading for the owner: 86°F is 4°F above the top of every main reference's range. No source documents that embers tolerate 86°F. The ich heat method cannot be stated as safe for this species from the sources found; it should not be written the way the cardinal guide writes it.

8.3 Neon tetra disease (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis)
- Sanders, Watral and Kent (2012): the parasite "has been reported from many species of aquarium fishes in several families, including Danio rerio and Danio nigrofasciatus"; it is called neon tetra disease after its type host, the neon tetra Paracheirodon innesi (paraphrase). The paper does not mention H. amandae. (S16) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4117576
- Ember tetra susceptibility specifically: no source found.

8.4 Acclimation (general, see section 9)

8.5 Quarantine
- VCA: quarantine new fish "at least 30 days before introducing any new fish to their tank." (S15)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "Utilising a quarantine or hospital tank is always advisable no matter where you source your fish." (S17)
- LiveAquaria (retailer's own procedure page, not a product page; lower tier): "The minimum quarantine period for all new arrivals is 2 weeks". https://www.liveaquaria.com/blogs/fish-health-nutrition/liveaquaria%C2%AE-divers-den%C2%AE-wysiwyg-store-quarantine-procedure-for-freshwater-fish
- DISAGREEMENT: 30 days (VCA) vs a 2-week minimum (LiveAquaria). The site's cardinal guide already uses 30 days.

## 9. Handling: netting, acclimation, transport

None of these are ember-specific; all are general small-fish guidance.
- Lights: "the fish should be kept in fairly dark conditions, and not blinded by tank lighting, so switch these off!" (S17)
- Mixing water: "Repeat this every few minutes until the bag contains more water from your aquarium than the shop." Total time "could take anywhere from 30-60 minutes." (S17)
- Do not pour the bag in: "it is good practice not to pour the contents of the whole bag into your aquarium." Bag water may carry waste and pest snail eggs (paraphrase). (S17)
- Check for strays (relevant for a 2 cm fish): "double check the bag, the floor and the net to ensure no fish have got stuck or jumped out along the way." (S17)
- Long trips: "Ammonia levels can increase due to fish waste and pH can drop due to increased CO2." (S17)
- Drip acclimation: "allows for a more gradual change in water chemistry", about two hours, aimed at sensitive species such as Caridina shrimp (paraphrase). (S17)
- Bagging ratio: "Aim to have a one-to three ratio: one part water, three parts air." because "it's the air that contains the essential oxygen". (S18)
- Transport: place bags in "a dark container, or wrap them in a towel or some newspapers." (S18)
- Netting technique: the article warns against having "a friend hold a half-filled bag open" while you "tip fish in from a net"; float a partly filled bag on the surface and open it as you net (paraphrase). (S18)
- LiveAquaria's no-net practice: "we practice a "no-net" technique to ensure that no additional stress or pathogenic transmission (from nets) can occur." (retailer procedure page, lower tier, URL in 8.5)

## 10. Breeding basics

- "An egg-scattering free spawner exhibiting no parental care." (S1)
- Setup (paraphrase of S1): separate breeding tank, dim light, mesh or marbles on the base to protect eggs, slightly acidic to neutral water, gentle aeration.
- Fry food: "Initial food should be Paramecium or a proprietary dry food of sufficiently small (5-50 micron) grade". (S1)
- Egg predation: "As egg scatterers, they display no parental care and will happily predate on their own eggs and newborn fry." (S8)
- Fishkeeper: spawning "happens in typical tetra fashion, with high temperatures and acidic water giving the best results." (S9)
- Clutch and hatch (lower tier, S12): "A female can lay between 30 and 60 eggs per spawning cycle." Eggs "hatch after about 24-30 hours." Fry "begin to swim freely about three days after hatching."
- Flowgrow calls it a "Bodenlaicher" (substrate spawner) (S13); this agrees in practice with "egg scatterer" (eggs fall to the bottom).
- Aquatic Arts lists its stock as tank-bred: "Tank-bred, but indigenous to South American Araguaia system." (retailer listing, observation only, not a source)

## 11. "Did you know?" candidates

A. Named for a scientist mother. The species honors Amanda Bleher (1910-1991), a German limnologist, whose son, explorer and fish exporter Heiko Bleher, collected the type specimen. The "-ae" ending marks a woman honoree.
- ETYFish: "in honor of Amanda Bleher (1910-1991)" (S4) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- FishBase eponymy, limnologist (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Fishkeeper: "hence the 'ae' ending rather than 'i.'" (S19) https://www.fishkeeper.co.uk/stories/Heiko-Bleher

B. Brazilians call them "little fires". At the Araguaia field site, the local guide shouted "Foguinhos, here, here", and the article states "This was the local name for ember tetras." (S6) https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/finding-ember-tetras-in-brazil/. "Foguinho" is the "diminutive of fogo" (fire). (S20) https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/foguinho

C. Bright orange, but camouflaged from above. In the wild, "From above they were hard to see." When clouds covered the sun, "their dark backs helped them fade into the gloom." The shoal "performed this mini-relocation every few minutes", and the author then saw why: "Slinking about in the tall stands of Eichhornia were pike cichlids." (S6)

D. (Backup) Described in a hobby magazine, not a scientific journal: the original description ran in Tropical Fish Hobbyist, January 1987, possibly published December 1986. (S5, read via the GBIF API: verify on the desktop before using)
