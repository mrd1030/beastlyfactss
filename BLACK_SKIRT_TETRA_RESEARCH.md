## 1. Identity and naming

1.1 Scientific name and authority
- ITIS: "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi (Boulenger, 1895)", TSN 162891, "Synonym(s): Tetragonopterus ternetzi Boulenger, 1895", "Current Standing: valid", "Record Credibility Rating: verified - standards met". (S15)
- FishBase: "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi ( Boulenger , 1895 )", common name "Black tetra". (S17)
- TFH: "Originally described as Tetragonopterus ternetzi". (S22)
- DFO: "first described in 1895 by Belgian-British zoologist George Albert Boulenger as Tetragonopterus ternetzi based on several specimens from Descalavados, Brazil" (DFO's spelling); "Carl Eigenamnn shifted T. ternetzi into Gymnocorymbus in 1910" (DFO's typo for Eigenmann). (S14)
- Seriously Fish lists the authority as "not recorded". (S21)

1.2 Common names
- ITIS: "black tetra", "black widow". (S15)
- Seriously Fish: "It goes by several common names, including 'butterfly tetra', 'black skirt tetra' and simply 'black tetra'." Page title: "Black Widow Tetra". (S21)
- TFH: "Black skirt tetra, black tetra, black widow tetra, white skirt tetra, blueberry tetra, strawberry tetra, mixed berry tetra" (the berry names are dyed fish, see 11). (S22)
- DFO: "the common names Black Tetra, Black Skirt Tetra, Black Widow Tetra, Petticoat Tetra, White Tetra, White Skirt Tetra, Black Long-Fin Tetra and White Long-Fin Tetra are all accepted as synonymous and morphs of the Black Tetra". (S14)
- Aquarium Source adds "high-fin black skirt tetra", "black fin tetra" and "blackamoor". (S32, lower tier)

1.3 Naming story
- Species name. ETYFish: "in honor of Swiss-born ichthyologist and naturalist Carl Ternetz (1870-1928), who "formed" the collection that contained holotype". (S19) FishBase eponymy: "Dr Carl Ternetz (1870-1928) was a Swiss-born ichthyologist and naturalist who first collected near Basel (1892) and subsequently collected in South America over a period of more than thirty years". (S17)
- Genus. ETYFish: "gymnós (γυμνός), bare or naked; kórymbos (κόρυμβος), summit, referring to scaleless (and humped) predorsal area of G. thayeri". (S19) FishBase: "Greek, gymnos = naked + Greek,korymb = summit, cluster of fruits and flowers". (S17)

1.4 Family (sources disagree: this is a recent reclassification, the same split as the ember tetra file)
- FishBase: "Acestrorhamphidae (American tetras) > Pristellinae". (S17)
- ETYFish lists Gymnocorymbus under Family Acestrorhamphidae, Subfamily Pristellinae. (S19)
- Wikipedia infobox: Family Acestrorhamphidae. (S35)
- ITIS: "Family Characidae". (S15). Seriously Fish: "Characidae". (S21). GBIF species match: Characidae. (S37). DFO: "the Characidae". (S14). Benine et al. 2015 title: "(Teleostei, Characiformes, Characidae)". (S3)
- Benine et al. 2015 also: "Our results support previous hypotheses of the alignment of Gymnocorymbus close to the subfamily Stethaprioninae". (S3)
- Owner decision, consistent with whatever the neon, cardinal and ember entries use.

1.5 The genus
- Benine et al. 2015: "Of the six previously known nominal species of Gymnocorymbus, three are considered valid herein: Gymnocorymbus bondi from the Río Orinoco basin, Gymnocorymbus ternetzi from the Rio Paraguay basin, and Gymnocorymbus thayeri ... A fourth species, Gymnocorymbus flaviolimai sp. n., is described from the Rio Madeira". It also supports "the sister relationship between G. ternetzi and G. thayeri". Lectotypes "are designated for G. ternetzi and G. thayeri." (S3)

## 2. Origin and wild habitat

2.1 Native range
- Seriously Fish: "Rio Paraguay and Guaporé basins in Brazil, Bolivia and Argentina." (S21)
- FishBase: "South America: Paraguay and Guaporé River basins to Argentina". Coordinates "11°S - 30°S, 64°W - 48°W". (S17)
- TFH: range "Paraguay and GuaporÉ River drainages in South America" (sic); type locality "Descalvados, Mato Grosso, Brazil". (S22)
- Benine et al. 2015: "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi from the Rio Paraguay basin". (S3)
- DFO: "The known native range of G. ternetzi is within the Rio Paraguay river basin in South America. Nearby populations in the upper Rio Parana and Rio Paraiba do Sol systems (Benine et al. 2015), as well as the Amazon River delta including the Paraquai and Rio Negro systems (Brysiewcz et al. 2009), are thought to be introduced and not included in native distributions." (S14)
- DISAGREEMENT: Gonzalez et al. 1997 calls it "native to ion-poor acidic waters of the Amazon River" (title: "a fish native to the Amazon River"). (S4) The taxonomic revision (S3) and DFO (S14) give the Rio Paraguay basin and treat Amazon populations as introduced. Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH add the Guaporé. Use Paraguay basin (plus Guaporé per the husbandry references); do not write "Amazon".

2.2 Habitat type
- Seriously Fish: "Small, slow-moving creeks, tributaries and streams, usually with dense overhanging vegetation." (S21)
- FishBase: "Freshwater; pelagic". "Adults occur in the middle and upper water layers." (S17)
- DFO: "Juveniles and adults have been found together in macrophytes of oxbow lakes of floodplains in Brazil, with higher abundance in the dry season than wet season" (citing Meschiatti et al. 2000). (S14)
- DFO quoting the GloFish company's notification (secondhand, citing Sakurai et al. 1992): "In its native habitat, this species inhabits relatively cool (22-24°C) slow-flowing freshwaters that are brown, but clear and slightly acidic (pH 5.5-6.3)". (S14) This is the only wild water chemistry found, and it is a company quote of a 1992 book, relayed by DFO.
- DFO on predators in the basin: "potential predators such as various piranha species, wolf fish, and golden dorado". (S14)
- DFO on how little is known: "Much of the information available on the Black Tetra is from the ornamental aquarium trade, and primarily involves anecdotal information ... rather than scientific studies on habitat or other requirements in the wild." (S14)

2.3 Introduced populations
- DFO: "reported to be established from released ornamental fish in Colombia (Welcomme 1992), temporarily established in a Colorado hot spring, and isolated occurrences have been reported in Florida and Louisiana ... but no occurrences of establishment in temperate climates (US and Europe ...) have been reported over the 70 or more years of its use". (S14)

## 3. Adult size, sexing, lifespan

3.1 Adult size
- Seriously Fish: "60 mm SL" (2.4 in, standard length, tail fin excluded). (S21)
- FishBase: "Max length : 7.5 cm SL male/unsexed". (S17)
- TFH: "6 cm TL (2.5 inches)" (total length, tail included). (S22)
- DFO: "up to 7.5 cm in length, but usually only to 6 cm in aquaria"; "Its normal length in an aquarium setting is approximately 4-6 cm, while they can reportedly grow to a maximum length of 7.5 cm in the wild". (S14)
- Banu et al. 2025 measured market GloFish tetras at "mean length 5.0 ± 0.3 cm" and "mean weight of 1.8 ± 0.5 g". (S5)
- Aquarium Source: "around 3 inches long at full maturity". (S32, lower tier)
- Summary for writers: about 2 to 2.5 in (5 to 6 cm) in a home tank; 7.5 cm (3 in) is the referenced maximum.

3.2 Sexing
- Seriously Fish: "Males are noticeably slimmer and a little smaller than females. The male also has more pointed dorsal and anal fins than the female." (S21)
- DFO: male has "a broader anal fin, a dorsal fin that is narrower and more pointed than females, as well as a caudal fin that sometimes has white spots" (relaying hobby sources), and from the taxonomic revision, "a pair of small hooks on the first 5-8 anal fin rays in the males that are absent in the females." (S14, citing Benine et al. 2015)

3.3 Color change with age
- Seriously Fish: "The intense black colouration of juveniles does fade as they grow". (S21)
- DFO: "Juveniles display the most intensive colouration and will become duller as they age, fading to a silvery grey". (S14)
- TFH: the black "can fade or darken depending on the fish's mood." (S22)

3.4 Lifespan in captivity (no authoritative source found; all figures trace to hobby tier)
- DFO: "The lifespan of the Black Tetra is between 3 and 6 years but could be extended in an aquarium setting if ideal conditions for survival are maintained" (citing Aqua-Fish Net 2005, a hobby site). (S14)
- Aquarium Source: "the average Black Skirt Tetra lifespan is between 3 and 5 years in captivity." (S32)
- Wikipedia (uncited in the text read): "3 to 5 years, though with the right conditions, can even exceed 6 or more". (S35). Do not use.
- Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH give no lifespan. Seriously Fish says dyeing "often shortens their lifespan considerably" (see 11). (S21)

3.5 Age at maturity (sources disagree)
- DFO: "many reports suggesting maturity ranges anywhere from 1-2 years old" (hobby sources), but "Mazzoni et al. (2015) found first gonadal maturation was between 150 to 180 dpf (5-6 months), and GloFish LLC states Tetra brood stock reach sexual maturity in 4-5 months". (S14)
- Wikipedia: "about two years of age" (uncited). (S35)
- Use the study figure: about 5 to 6 months (S14 citing S8's authors).

3.6 Wild lifespan: no source found.

## 4. Conservation status

- FishBase: "Least Concern (LC) ; Date assessed: 04 March 2021" (IUCN Red List version 2025-2). (S17)
- GBIF API: "category":"LEAST_CONCERN", "iucnTaxonID":"49829723". (S37)
- Wikipedia cites "Lima, F. (2023). Gymnocorymbus ternetzi. IUCN Red List of Threatened Species. 2023 e.T49829723A197221365." (S35)
- DISAGREEMENT on date only: assessed 2021 (FishBase) vs a 2023 Red List publication (Wikipedia's citation). These may be the assessment date and the publication year of one assessment. The IUCN page returned 403. Category agrees everywhere.
- FishBase: "Fisheries: highly commercial; aquarium: highly commercial". Resilience "High, minimum population doubling time less than 15 months". (S17)
- Trade source: Seriously Fish: "All the fish sold in the trade have been captive bred and as such are unfussy regarding water chemistry and diet." (S21). DFO: "bred for the aquarium trade since at least 1950 (Innes 1950)", and "Teletchea (2016) listed the Black Tetra as being at the highest level of domestication for aquarium fish." (S14)

## 5. Tank

5.1 Minimum tank size and footprint
- Seriously Fish: base "75 × 30 cm" (30 × 12 in), volume "~68 litres ~18 US gal", and "A group of these can be kept in a standard 30" x 12" (75cm x 30cm) - 20g long/70 litre tank." (S21)
- FishBase: "minimum aquarium size 60 cm". (S17)
- TFH: no size; "should have plenty of open swimming space but appreciates plant thickets to which it can retire if it feels threatened." (S22)
- Aquarium Source: "a minimum of 15 gallons", and "If possible though, go for a 20-gallon tank for good measure." (S32)
- LiveAquaria product page spec (retailer, observation only, not a source): "Minimum Tank Size: 10 Gallons".
- DISAGREEMENT: 60 cm (24 in, FishBase) vs 75 cm (30 in, a 20 gallon long, Seriously Fish) vs 15 to 20 gallons (Aquarium Source) vs 10 gallons (a retailer). The best references point to a 20 gallon long; a 10 gallon is only a retailer figure.

5.2 Group size and shoaling research
- FishBase: "in groups of 5 or more individuals". (S17)
- Aquarium Source: "At the very least, you need a group of 5 fish to keep them happy." (S32)
- Seriously Fish: "a small shoal of at least 12 specimens" (in the context of curbing nipping, see 7). (S21)
- TFH: "an active, schooling species that should always be kept in groups", "especially dramatic when kept in large schools of a dozen or more fish." (S22)
- GloFish product page: "you may keep them in larger schools". (S30, manufacturer, no number)
- DISAGREEMENT: 5 (FishBase, Aquarium Source) vs 12 (Seriously Fish, TFH). The nipping guidance in section 7 is the reason to write the higher number.
- Research on schooling: Rahman et al. 2020 studied black tetra schools under startle stimuli and found "information propagates through the group as a wave, indicating that each fish is not reacting individually to the stimulus", "the time taken for information to transfer across the group is independent of group density", and "information propagates across large groups faster than would be expected if the fish were simply responding to the motion of their nearest neighbor." (S9) This is about how schools move, not how many fish to keep.
- Group-size welfare study, NOT READ: Priestley, Stevenson and Alexander (2006), "Growth rate and body condition in relation to group size in Black Widow Tetras (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi) and Common Goldfish (Carassius auratus)", Journal of Nutrition 136(7 Suppl):2078S-2080S, DOI 10.1093/jn/136.7.2078S, WALTHAM Centre for Pet Nutrition. PubMed carries no abstract and the publisher PDF could not be retrieved. Its findings are unknown here; a search snippet from a hobby site paraphrased it, but that is not a source. Open on the desktop before citing anything from it.

5.3 Temperature
- Seriously Fish: 20-26°C (68-79°F). (S21)
- FishBase: "Subtropical; 20°C - 26°C". (S17)
- TFH: "Subtropical, 20° to 26°C (68° to 78°F)". (S22)
- DFO: "There are no known formal studies on the minimum and maximum temperature requirements of the Black Tetra." Hobby literature gives "20-26°C" with narrower suggestions of 23-25, 23-26 and 22-24°C; elsewhere "Suggested optimal temperature for health and activity of Black Tetras for ornamental aquarium culture varies from 20-29°C". (S14)
- Aquarium Source: "70°F to 85°F (aim for the middle of this range)". (S32)
- GloFish product page: "GloFish® fish enjoy tropical temperatures of 76-80F." (S30, manufacturer spec for its own fish)
- LiveAquaria spec (retailer observation only): "72 - 82° F".
- Cold limits (measured, useful for "does it need a heater"): DFO reports white skirt tetras lowered 1°C per day from 20°C "lost equilibrium at 9.95 ± 0.03°C", and "decreased feeding level and activity at 17°C, stopped feeding when temperatures approached 12°C and stopped activity just above 10°C". (S14)
- Kuhn et al. 2023 held black tetras "at four different temperatures for 2 weeks (20°C, 24°C, 28°C, 32°C)" and found "food intake increased with increasing temperature". (S7) The abstract does not report survival or mortality at 32°C.
- DISAGREEMENT: the main references top out at 26°C (79°F); DFO's compiled range, Aquarium Source and the retailers go to 28 to 29°C (82 to 85°F). Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH all call it subtropical.

5.4 pH
- Seriously Fish: 6.0-7.0. (S21)
- FishBase: "pH range: 6.0 - 8.0". (S17)
- TFH: "wide range of hardness and pH." (S22)
- DFO: "it has been noted that these fish are able to tolerate a pH that ranges between 5.0 and 8.5" (relaying hobby sources). (S14)
- Lab result, Gonzalez et al. 1997: tetras "possess abilities to resist the disruptive effects of moderately low pH but cannot survive in waters with a pH of 4.0 or less". (S4)
- Aquarium Source: "6.0 to 7.5". (S32)
- Wild, secondhand: pH 5.5-6.3 (see 2.2). (S14)

5.5 Hardness
- Seriously Fish: "5-20 dGH". (S21)
- FishBase: "dH range: 5 - 19". (S17)
- Practical Fishkeeping, for breeding only: "soft, acidic side (pH about 6.0, GH not more than 8)". (S23)
- Aquarium Source: "Water hardness: 4 to 8 dKH". (S32)
- LiveAquaria spec (retailer observation only): "dKH: 4 - 8".
- KH: only lower-tier and retailer figures (4 to 8 dKH).

5.6 Lighting
- Seriously Fish: "It will show its best colours when there are some dense areas of planting, with some floating vegetation added to dim the light entering the tank." (S21)
- GloFish versions only: "the more blue light you have the more your fish will shine!" (S30, manufacturer). GloFish FAQ: "To make you're GloFish fluorescent fish stand out add blue lights." (sic) (S29)
- No lighting intensity or photoperiod source.

5.7 Planting, decor and space
- Seriously Fish: "Fairly unfussy with regard to decor. It's an active species and prefers plenty of open swimming space, along with some quieter areas." "In truth it will thrive in most environments, though." (S21)
- DFO: "they are a small fish native to predatory waters and are therefore hiders by nature" (relaying a hobby site). (S14)

5.8 Flow

## 6. Diet

- Wild diet: FishBase: "They feed on worms, small crustaceans and insects." (S17). DFO: "Black Tetras exhibit a primarily carnivorous lifestyle, feeding on readily available worms, small crustaceans and insects (Galib 2011). However, it is not a particularly selective species, ingesting smaller amounts of algae and plant matter". (S14)
- Classification in a lab study: Butt et al. 2019 used "the omnivore black widow tetra" as their omnivore model. (S13)
- TFH: "A micropredator. Relishes live or frozen foods, will readily take all regular aquarium fare." (S22)
- Seriously Fish: "A most unfussy, omnivorous species. Flakes and granules, along with regular feedings of small live and frozen foods such as Daphnia and bloodworm are recommended." (S21)
- DFO: "Black Tetras are not known to be voracious eaters, and do not overeat (Frank 1980)." (S14)
- Temperature and appetite: "In all species, food intake increased with increasing temperature." (S7)
- Feeding frequency: GloFish product page: "Feed two to three times daily, in small amounts, only as much as your fish can eat in a minute or so." (S30, manufacturer). No independent source on frequency found.
- Fry: see 10.

## 7. Behavior and tankmates

7.1 Temperament
- Seriously Fish: "One of the better tetras for the 'general' community tank. The black widow is generally peaceful and lively". (S21)
- TFH: "The black tetra is a peaceful candidate for community tanks, but it may be a bit nippy toward slow-moving or long-finned tankmates." (S22)
- DFO: "Wild-type G. ternetzi is a small, non-aggressive fish". "generally not known to be aggressive to or highly competitive with other aquarium fish although may prey on much smaller fish (Frank 1980)." (S14)
- Leggatt and Devlin 2020 measured aggressive behavior in adult and juvenile tetras in a foraging competition; transgenesis "did not affect the foraging success or aggressive behaviour". (S1) This compares GloFish to white tetras; it does not measure nipping of other species.

7.2 Fin nipping (sourced carefully)
- Seriously Fish: "Although it has a reputation as a bit of a fin nipper, this behaviour can usually be rectified by keeping it in a small shoal of at least 12 specimens. When maintained in these kind of numbers any squabbling is generally contained within the group." (S21)
- TFH: "it may be a bit nippy toward slow-moving or long-finned tankmates. When kept in groups, as it should be, the fishes' energy is usually contained within the group, where it remains harmless." (S22)
- Aquarium Source: "this species tends to nip when they have the opportunity as well. For that reason, we recommend avoiding any long-finned fish species." Also: "this species is prone to nipping at flowy fins of betta fish or angelfish." (S32)
- No peer-reviewed study of fin nipping in this species was found. Both strong husbandry sources (S21, S22) agree on the fix: a group of about a dozen keeps the energy inside the school.

7.3 Good tankmates
- Seriously Fish: "It can be kept with most livebearers, danios, rasboras, other tetras and peaceful bottom dwellers such as Corydoras or smaller Loricariids. It also makes a suitable companion for more robust dwarf cichlids such as kribensis, and due to its somewhat rounded shape is unlikely to end up on the menu if combined with larger species such as angel fish." (S21)

7.4 Tankmates to avoid
- Long-finned and slow fish: TFH (S22), Aquarium Source (S32).
- DISAGREEMENT on angelfish: Seriously Fish lists angelfish as a workable companion because the tetra is too deep-bodied to be eaten (S21); Aquarium Source warns the tetra nips angelfish fins (S32); TFH warns against long-finned tankmates generally (S22). Reading: both can be true (the tetra is safe from the angelfish, the angelfish's fins are not safe from a small group of tetras). Write it as "only with a large school, and watch the fins".
- Long-fin black skirts themselves: Aquarium Source: "Black Skirt Tetras can become targets due to their passive nature. Plus, their long fins are the target of fin nippers." (S32)
- Much smaller fish: DFO, "may prey on much smaller fish". (S14)

7.5 Swimming level
- FishBase: "Adults occur in the middle and upper water layers." (S17)

## 8. Health

8.1 Common diseases
- FishBase disease list for this species: "Fin-rot Disease (late stage)", "White spot Disease", "Dactylogyrus Gill Flukes Disease", "Fin Rot (early stage)". (S18)
- Ich, documented outbreak: Banu et al. 2025, market GloFish tetras in India "found severely infected with white spot disease", "with clinical signs of white spot, loss of fin, complete loss of gill filaments, and anorexia." "The infected fish were dark in color, remain isolated, slow-moving, and stop feeding. Mortality was within 48 h unset of symptom" (sic). "The water temperature at the time of mortality was 23 °C and pH 7.5." (S5)
- Tetra disseminated microsporidiosis (new disease, 2021): "A novel microsporidial disease was documented in two ornamental fish species, black tetra Gymnocorymbus ternetzi Boulenger 1895 and cardinal tetra Paracheirodon axelrodi". "The occurrence of TDM in black tetra was associated with chronic mortality in a domestic farmed population". The parasite "occurred diffusely in most internal organs and the gill". (S6) Note: this is not neon tetra disease; it is a new genus, Fusasporis.
- Cancer case report (title only, not read beyond PubMed listing): "Squamous cell carcinoma in a black tetra, Gymnocorymbus ternetzi (Boulenger)", J Fish Dis 2014. Not needed for the guide.
- Canada: "Black Tetra is not included on the susceptible species list, nor are any other Tetra species" for diseases of concern listed by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. (S14)

8.2 Ich and the 86°F heat treatment (IMPORTANT for the shared ich guide link)
- VCA: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF, so slowly raising the temperature of the tank is enough to get rid of the parasite. Not all fish can stand the heat, though. Even for fish that can handle warm water, adding oxygen to the water is essential for this treatment method." Parasites "replicate faster in warmer water." (S27)
- Banu et al. 2025: "repeated chemical treatment, and elevations in water temperature are found to be successful disease control strategies" (general, not tested on these fish). Its outbreak table indicates "the preferred temperature for the outbreak remain < 30°C". (S5)
- Species heat evidence, such as it is:
  - Seriously Fish's breeding method runs the spawning tank at "around 28 - 30 °C (82 - 86 °F)", short term, for a single pair. (S21)
  - Kuhn et al. 2023 kept black tetras at 32°C (89.6°F) for 2 weeks in a feeding study; the abstract reports changes in appetite, not survival. (S7) Full text not read, so mortality at 32°C is unknown here.
  - DFO: "There are no known formal studies on the minimum and maximum temperature requirements". (S14)
- No CTmax or upper lethal temperature study for this species was found.
- Plain reading for the owner: 86°F (30°C) is 7°F above the 26°C (79°F) ceiling given by Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH. It sits at the very top of Seriously Fish's short-term spawning range, and one lab group kept the species at 32°C for two weeks. That makes 86°F more plausible for this species than for the ember tetra, but no source says black skirts tolerate a sustained 86°F ich treatment. Do not write it as safe the way the cardinal guide does; at most say the species has been held that warm briefly for breeding, with extra aeration (S27), and that a vet or a medication is the alternative. Owner to decide.

8.3 Neon tetra disease (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis)
- Sanders, Watral and Kent 2012: "This microsporidium has been described in a broad range of fish hosts and has been reported from many species of aquarium fishes in several families, including Danio rerio and Danio nigrofasciatus". The paper does not name G. ternetzi. (S34)
- Fish Pathogens database: hosts "Characins, Cichlids, Cyprinids (i.e., danios), and all the carp-like fishes"; "There are no known cures or treatments for Pleistophora." Its references are a hobby site and an info sheet, so it is weak. (S33)
- FishBase's disease list for this species does not include it. (S18)
- Black skirt tetra specifically: no source found naming it as a host. The documented microsporidian in this species is the new TDM parasite (S6), not Pleistophora.

8.4 GloFish health
- DFO, relaying company-supplied veterinary statements: "no evidence for increased susceptibility to, or transmission of, water-borne pathogens, or additional health impediments of CGT2016 or any other fluorescent line relative to non-transgenic counterparts". (S14) This is the company's evidence as summarized by DFO, not an independent study.

8.5 Quarantine
- VCA: "you should quarantine infected fish for at least 30 days before introducing any new fish to their tank." (S27)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "Utilising a quarantine or hospital tank is always advisable no matter where you source your fish." (S25)
- Relevance: the TDM outbreak in cardinals "occurred in moribund fish while in quarantine at a public aquarium". (S6)

## 9. Handling: netting, acclimation, transport

None of this is species-specific except the GloFish manufacturer's method; all is general small-fish guidance (same sources as the ember tetra file, reopened today).
- Lights off: "the fish should be kept in fairly dark conditions, and not blinded by tank lighting, so switch these off!" (S25)
- Mixing water: "Repeat this every few minutes until the bag contains more water from your aquarium than the shop. This could take anywhere from 30-60 minutes." (S25)
- Do not pour the bag in: "it is good practice not to pour the contents of the whole bag into your aquarium." (S25)
- Strays: "double check the bag, the floor and the net to ensure no fish have got stuck or jumped out along the way." (S25)
- Bagging ratio: "Aim to have a one-to three ratio: one part water, three parts air." (S26)
- Transport: "Place the fish bags into a dark container, or wrap them in a towel or some newspapers." (S26)
- GloFish's own method (manufacturer, shorter): "Acclimate your GloFish® fluorescent fish using this 25-minute method: Put the bag with your fish in your aquarium for 15 minutes." It also tells buyers to "remove any heating or cooling packs" and to watch for "droopy or flat fins" as a water quality sign. (S31)
- DISAGREEMENT: 30 to 60 minutes (PFK) vs a 25-minute method (GloFish).
- Cold shipping relevance: DFO's cold data (feeding drops at 17°C, activity stops near 10°C, S14) is why winter shipping matters; GloFish says orders "may be temporarily held to help reduce transit risk" in cold or hot weather (S30).

## 10. Breeding basics

- Spawning type: TFH: "An easily bred egg scatterer. Parents are voracious predators on eggs and fry." (S22). Domínguez-Castanedo et al. 2024 describe it as an "iteroparous batch spawner", where batch spawners "spawn in multiple events within each breeding cycle". (S12)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "They are relatively hardy and can tolerate a wide range of conditions". Water for breeding "on the soft, acidic side (pH about 6.0, GH not more than 8)", "temperatures around 24-26°C/75-79°F". "The breeding tank should be bare, with two or more spawning mops and a sponge filter." (S23, read via fetch tool)
- Seriously Fish setup: a separate tank "around 18" x 12" x 12"", "dimly lit and contain clumps of fine-leaved plants such as java moss or spawning mops, to give the fish somewhere to deposit their adhesive eggs", or mesh on the base sized "so that the eggs can fall through it, but small enough so that the adults cannot reach them." Group spawning: "half a dozen specimens of each sex being a good number." Pair method: condition at "24 - 25.5 °C (75 - 78 °F)", spawning tank "around 28 - 30 °C (82 - 86 °F), with a pH on the acidic side of neutral". "The pair should spawn the following morning." (S21)
- Egg eating: Seriously Fish: "the adults will eat the eggs, given the chance and should be removed at the first opportunity ie. as soon as eggs are noticed." (S21). PFK: "tetras are notorious egg eaters and parents must be removed as soon as spawning is complete, typically in the morning." (S23)
- Hatching (sources disagree slightly):
  - Seriously Fish: "These will hatch in 18-36 hours, with the fry becoming free swimming a few days later." (S21)
  - PFK: "The eggs typically hatch in about 24 hours, with the fry becoming free swimming after five or six days." (S23)
  - DFO: "Hatching generally occurs after 20-24 hours", embryos then "spend the next 5-6 days attached to the walls of the tank before filling their swim bladders". (S14)
- Light: Seriously Fish: "The eggs and fry are light sensitive in the early stages of life and the tank should be kept as dimly lit as possible." (S21). PFK: "the tank should be shielded for five to six days." (S23)
- Fry food: Seriously Fish: "an infusoria-type food for the first few days, until they are large enough to accept microworm or brine shrimp nauplii." (S21). PFK: "infusoria or similar fry food, graduating to brineshrimp nauplii after another 48 hours." (S23)
- Egg numbers: DFO: "the number of laid eggs can total up to 2000" (relaying hobby sources, AC Tropical Fish 2016 and others). (S14) Hobby tier only.
- Development speed: DFO, citing Pan et al. 2008: embryonic development "occurs approximately twice as fast up to the hatching stage with the first cell division occurring after only 30 minutes" compared with zebrafish. (S14)
- Salt and fry: DFO, citing Rothen et al. 2002: "extreme mortality in Black Tetra yolk sac larvae when exposed to 1 and 3 ppt salt treatment but very low mortality at the free-swimming larval stage". (S14) Useful for the health guide's salt advice.
- GloFish breeding is prohibited by license (see 11.4).

11.4 GloFish tetras: yes, the skirt tetra GloFish are this species

- Timeline: DFO: "CGT2016 was first approved and marketed in the US excluding California in 2012 as GloFish® Electric Green® Tetra, followed by GloFish® Long-fin Electric Green® Tetra (also CGT2016) and additional fluorescent colour variants in the Tetra." (S14)

The product URL for that line reads "pristella-maxilaris", i.e. the X-ray tetra, a different species. The only basis here for the species is that URL slug and Wikipedia's GloFish infobox (S36), so treat it as a lead. - Care: GloFish FAQ: "They need the same care as other fish". (S29). Its stated temperature (76-80°F, S30) is warmer than the 68-79°F of the wild-type references (S21, S17, S22).
- Breeding prohibited: "Intentional breeding and/or any sale, barter, or trade, of any offspring of GloFish® fluorescent ornamental fish is strictly prohibited." An exception covers "educational use by teachers and students in bona fide educational institutions". (S28)

## 12. "Did you know?" candidates

A. Every black skirt tetra starts out with ovaries. Mazzoni, Grier and Quagio-Grassiotto 2015: "During gonadal development in G. ternetzi, all individuals first developed ovarian tissue." In presumptive males, "Apoptotic oocytes were observed and afterward degenerated", and "the testis was organized on an ovary previously developed". (S8) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26386207/. DFO restates it: "All juveniles of the Black Tetra go through a female stage of development". (S14). Scope carefully: this is juvenile development, not adult sex change.

B. A black skirt school reacts faster than any one fish could. Startled schools pass the alarm "as a wave", and across large groups it travels "faster than would be expected if the fish were simply responding to the motion of their nearest neighbor." The authors were engineers at Utah State University and the Naval Undersea Warfare Center, publishing in Bioinspiration and Biomimetics. (S9) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32629435/

C. Its closest relatives have spy-novel connections. A sister species in the same genus, Gymnocorymbus bondi, is named for Francis "Frank" E. Bond, and ETYFish notes he "is the father of ornithologist James Bond, whose name Ian Fleming used for his 007 spy hero". (S19) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/. G. ternetzi itself honors Carl Ternetz, a Swiss naturalist who collected in South America "over a period of more than thirty years" (S17). Scope: the Bond link is the genus, not this species.

D. (Backup) Two genes decide whether a skirt tetra is black or white: dominance "at both loci" is needed for the dark, banded look. (S11) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15220394/

E. (Backup) The green GloFish tetra carries a gene from a Zoanthus reef coral (S16), and the whole Electric Green line descends from "a single G0 founding individual" (S14).
