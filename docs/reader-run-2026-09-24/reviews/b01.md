## aquatic-inverts-smallmammal-shared-1

**1. Article by article**
- Amphibian Quarantine, Acclimation, and Water Hardness: finish it. Names a real, unresolved gap in axolotl water-hardness advice instead of faking a number. Grade A.
- Tubbing, Cooling, and Salt Baths for Sick Axolotls: finish it. Traces the cooling and salt figures to one named conference paper and is upfront about how thin that sourcing actually is. Grade A.
- How to Cycle a Fish Tank, Start to Finish: finish it. Real ammonia thresholds, a 2025 study cited by name, and an honest note that the textbook bacteria name is probably wrong. Grade A.
- Aquarium Filtration: How It Actually Works and How to Maintain It: finish it. The turnover math table alone is worth bookmarking. Grade A.
- Treating Ich: Heat, Salt or Medication, and the Temperature Ceiling That Decides: finish it. Opens by naming its own reason for existing (two other guides on this site contradicted each other), then actually resolves it with a species table. Grade A.

**2. For articles graded C or lower**
None. Every article in this bundle clears a B+ at worst, so there's nothing to triage here.

**3. What the title promises that the article doesn't fully deliver**
- The quarantine article's title promises "amphibian" water hardness broadly, but the actual hardness section is axolotl-only; a reader who came for fire-bellied toad or tiger salamander water chemistry gets quarantine timing for their species but no hardness guidance at all, general or specific.
- The ich article's species table covers about a dozen named fish; a reader with a species not on the list (a rainbowfish, an African cichlid, a rasbora) has to guess which row is the nearest analog, since the article doesn't say how to place an unlisted species.
- Otherwise each title is answered in full: the quarantine piece covers quarantine, acclimation, and hardness in that order; the tubbing piece covers tubbing, cooling, and salt baths; the cycling piece genuinely runs fishless and fish-in start to finish; the filtration piece explains the mechanism and gives a maintenance routine; the ich piece delivers exactly the temperature-ceiling table its subtitle promises.

**4. Overlap and conflict**
Axolotls are the connective tissue running through four of the five articles (all but the ich piece), and the overlap is handled well: each article adds a different layer rather than repeating the last one. The quarantine article sets water safety and hardness, the tubbing article covers what to do with a sick animal, the cycling article covers the bacterial infrastructure an axolotl tank needs before the animal goes in, and the filtration article explains why a sponge filter specifically suits an axolotl's gills. No numbers between them disagree: the tubbing article's note that axolotls are a cold-water species matches the cycling article's stated 60-68°F housing range, and both treat the 5-15°C figure in the tubbing article as a treatment-specific range, not a baseline, so there's no contradiction between "kept at 60-68°F" and "cooled to 41-59°F during illness."
The cycling and filtration articles overlap by design; each explicitly defers the other's topic (cycling is "assumed" in the filtration piece, which points back to the cycling guide for the biology) rather than re-explaining it, so the repetition that does show up (biological filtration, chlorine killing bacteria, don't rinse media under the tap) is intentional reinforcement, not padding.
The ich article stands alone from the other four with no topic overlap, though it's worth noting it directly and openly resolves a conflict between two other guides not included in this bundle (a bristlenose pleco guide and a zebra danio guide), which is a strong internal-consistency signal for the site as a whole even though those source articles aren't here to check.

**5. Trust**
This bundle earns more trust than almost any other batch of pet content reads like it should, because it repeatedly does the opposite of what search-optimized pet sites usually do: it says when a popular number can't be sourced instead of repeating it anyway. The quarantine article's honest admission that no axolotl GH/KH figure traces back to a real source, despite that figure circulating everywhere, is the standout moment. The tubbing article does the same thing with the salt-bath dose, tracing it to one WSAVA proceedings paper and pointing out that the paper never even says what condition the dose treats. The cycling article corrects the standard "Nitrobacter" answer with a 2025 peer-reviewed study. Every article names its sources specifically (Merck, AZA, USGS, VCA, University of Florida, NC State's SRAC, a named author's WSAVA paper) rather than gesturing at "experts say."
The single most convincing sentence in the bundle: "Having gone looking for where those specific figures actually originate, from a peer-reviewed source, a veterinary publication that cites its own numbers, or an academic husbandry manual, they don't trace back cleanly to one. The pages repeating them present the ranges as given, without pointing to a study." That's a site describing its own research process, not just its conclusions, and it's the kind of sentence a content mill has no incentive to write.

**6. Grade: A.** Five for five at A-level, source-named throughout, and the bundle's defining trait, naming what isn't actually known rather than padding the gap with a plausible-sounding number, shows up more than once, not as a one-off flourish. The two changes worth making: give the quarantine article a short, even if similarly honest, water-chemistry note for the non-axolotl amphibians it names in its title (fire-bellied toad, tiger salamander, Pacman frog, White's tree frog) rather than letting the whole hardness section resolve to axolotls only; and add one line to the ich article's table explaining how to place a species that isn't listed, since the table's usefulness depends on a reader being able to find their fish on it.
