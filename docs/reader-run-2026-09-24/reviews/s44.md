## garter-snake

**1. Page by page**
- Hub: would finish, leads with "many will not eat mice" instead of burying the single most important expectation-setter. Grade A.
- Encyclopedia: would finish, the tetrodotoxin arms race and genome-sequencing history genuinely add something. Grade A.
- Cost guide: would finish, the "free snake still costs the same setup" framing is honest rather than a sales pitch. Grade A.
- Handling guide: would finish, the 2014 musking study and the 1985 bite case report are both real citations doing real work. Grade A.
- Health issues guide: would finish, correctly identifies thiamine deficiency and parasite load as this species' own risks rather than reusing a generic snake list. Grade A.
- Tank setup guide: would finish, the swimming-basin section is the best-argued single section in the set. Grade A.
- Feeding guide: would finish, the dietary-specialist framing and the red-wiggler warning are both things a generic snake guide would miss entirely. Grade A.
- Enrichment guide: would finish, and it is the most trust-building page of the whole set for reasons covered below. Grade A.

Nothing here read as thin or padded.

**2. Hub and encyclopedia**
Both earn their place. The hub compresses the deep dives into first-week numbers and an emergency card, and the encyclopedia supplies wild range, taxonomy, and the genus's research history, none of which overlap with the deep dives at length. I found no numeric disagreement between the hub and any deep dive: enclosure size (36x18x18 or 40-gallon breeder, 55-gallon for a pair), temperature (85 to 90°F warm side, up to 95°F, 70 to 75°F cool side), feeding frequency by diet type, and the thiaminase guidance all match word for word across the hub, tank setup, health, and feeding pages.

**3. Could I keep this animal**
Yes, and unusually completely for a beginner-labeled species. I could size and secure an enclosure, build a real swimming feature instead of a dish, source the right fish and worms, avoid the thiaminase trap, and recognize every emergency sign. One thing the set raises without closing the loop on: the hub and the "what to buy" list both mention "a specialist may refuse mice for life," which is a real budgeting problem, but nothing in the cost guide's monthly numbers actually shows what happens to the $195 to $360 annual figure if a snake turns out to be a pure fish-and-worm eater versus a mouse-eater, even though the feeding guide makes clear those are functionally different diets with different sourcing effort. The set tells the reader to expect the split but never prices the two branches separately.

**4. Overlap and conflict across the deep dives**
The thiamine deficiency mechanism, symptoms, and fix are given in full, near-identically, on the hub, the health issues guide, and the feeding guide. That's the one piece of real repetition, though it's repetition of a genuinely important warning rather than filler. The dietary-specialist framing (fish/amphibian specialists, worm/slug specialists, generalists) also appears twice, in the cost guide and the feeding guide, in nearly the same words.

I found no numeric contradictions across the deep dives. The set is unusually careful about saying when something is "not universally treated as mandatory" (UVB) versus a hard rule (the thermostat, the locking lid), which kept me from finding the kind of soft-versus-hard tension the other species sets had.

**5. Trust**
This is the strongest trust signal of the four sets I reviewed today. The enrichment guide opens by admitting the site's own past mistake: "This guide starts with a correction, because the mistake is common and we made it ourselves before checking," then explains that the enrichment study usually credited to garter snakes was actually done on ratsnakes, because of an old genus rename. A content mill does not admit to having been wrong; it just quietly fixes the page. That single admission did more to convince me a real person runs this site than any statistic elsewhere in the set. The 2014 musking study, the 1985 bite case report, and the named veterinary reference on thiamine deficiency in piscivorous snakes all back up specific claims rather than floating unsourced.

**6. Grade**
A. Internally consistent, correctly treats diet as the organizing fact of this species' care rather than an afterthought, and it is the only set of the four willing to publicly correct its own past error. The two changes I'd make: split the ongoing-cost table by diet type (fish/worm versus mouse) so the budget answer matches the "many will not eat mice" warning the hub leads with, and trim the thiamine explanation to one full telling with a link from the other two mentions instead of repeating the mechanism three times.

**Not covered anywhere**
- A cost breakdown for a fish-and-worm-only diet versus a mouse-eating one, despite the set repeatedly warning that a keeper cannot assume which kind of snake they'll get.
- None beyond that.
