1. One line per article
- Brumation in Pet Snakes: Would finish it. Learned pet-only keepers usually don't need to brumate and what the abort signs are. Grade B+ (thin) — the breeding protocol table is useful but short compared to the tortoise brumation piece in the same bundle.
- Sexing, Growth, and Body Condition in Pet Snakes: Would finish it. Learned probing/popping are vet jobs, weight beats length for tracking growth, and the cross-section body-condition test. Grade A.
- How Snakes Actually Shed (humidity myth): Would finish it. Learned the lymph-fluid mechanism, why blue eyes happen, and that humidity only matters at the final peel stage. Grade A.
- T5 vs Compact UVB: Would finish it. Learned why tube fixtures beat coil bulbs for anything larger than a small enclosure, with concrete product/price examples. Grade B+ (leans promotional with named products and prices).
- Tortoise Brumation: Would finish it. Learned which species should never brumate, the fast-then-cool sequence, and the weight-loss ceilings by species. Grade A.
- Tortoise Sexing, Eggs, and Egg Binding: Would finish it. Learned a female needs no male to lay, and the single behavioral tell (bright/active vs. lethargic) that separates normal gravidity from dystocia. Grade A.
- Tortoise Soaking: Would finish it. Learned the age/species soak schedule, correct depth and temperature, and that a dry tub at soak's end is itself the warning sign. Grade A.

2. Articles graded C or lower
None graded C or below.

3. Title-versus-content gaps
- Brumation in Pet Snakes: doesn't say what a natural, non-breeding seasonal appetite dip should look like day to day, so a keeper trying to distinguish normal fall slowdown from illness (the situation most pet-only readers actually have) gets less than the tortoise brumation article gives for tortoises.
- T5 vs Compact UVB: the title frames it as T5 vs compact broadly, but the "how much UVB does my species need" section only gives three example groups (desert baskers, forest species, crepuscular geckos) rather than a fuller species list, so a reader with, say, a snake or a chelonian gets no guidance here at all.

4. Overlap and conflict
The two brumation articles (snake and tortoise) don't conflict since they're explicitly scoped to different animals and the snake article even says its numbers don't transfer. The T5 vs Compact UVB article and the (separately-bundled) UVB lighting complete guide overlap heavily in subject but are consistent on the core facts (bulb decay before visible dimming, glass blocking UVB, gradient importance). No numeric disagreements found within this directory.

5. Trust
The snake shedding article is the most convincing piece because it corrects a widely repeated claim (that shedding needs high humidity) with a specific mechanism and a clean counterexample: "The rosy boa is the clearest case... kept at a dry baseline well under 60 percent for most of the year... and it completes normal sheds on that." That single sentence does more real work than most "myth-busting" content, because it gives a testable, named example rather than just asserting the myth is wrong. The T5 vs Compact UVB article is the one place trust wavers slightly, since it reads close to a buyer's guide with specific bulb brands and price ranges rather than a purely clinical piece, though nothing in it appears factually wrong.

6. Bundle grade: A-. Reason: strong, mechanism-first writing throughout, with only the UVB buying-guide piece leaning commercial enough to feel like it's selling as much as explaining.
Two changes first: expand the T5 vs Compact UVB species table beyond three examples, and add a short "normal seasonal slowdown vs. illness" checklist to the snake brumation article to match what the tortoise piece already does.

## reptiles-shared-4

1. One line per article
- UVB Lighting: Why It Matters More Than You Think: Would finish it. Learned Ferguson Zones, that birds and amphibians face the same D3 problem as reptiles, and the six actual mistakes that cause UVB failures. Grade A.
- Why Bearded Dragons Need UVB Lighting (and why skipping it is deadly): Would finish it, though it reads as more generic and promotional than its companion piece. Grade B (filler) — much of it restates the complete UVB guide's points in more dramatic, less sourced language.

2. Articles graded C or lower
None graded C or below, though the bearded dragon UVB piece is the weaker of the two by a wide margin.

3. Title-versus-content gaps
- Why Bearded Dragons Need UVB Lighting: no gap versus its own title, but relative to its cross-linked companion (the complete UVB guide), it never cites a single named source (no Merck, no VCA, no study), where the general guide cites several, so a reader comparing the two gets much weaker evidence for the species this site treats as its flagship.

4. Overlap and conflict
These two are meant to be read together, and mostly they agree: both cover the same D3/calcium chain, glass blocking UVB, and bulb replacement schedules. One inconsistency worth flagging: the general UVB guide says to replace bulbs "every 12 months" for reptiles as a flat rule, while the bearded-dragon-specific article gives a range, "every 6 to 12 months" for T5 HO and "every 6 months" for T8, and separately lists CFL bulb lifespan as "6 months" in its own product table while calling CFL only "supplemental use" rather than a primary source. These aren't a hard contradiction but the numbers don't line up cleanly if a reader is trying to get one firm answer.

5. Trust
The general UVB guide is the more convincing of the two, and its most convincing sentence is the direct Merck quote: "Secondary nutritional hyperparathyroidism is the most common bone disease encountered in reptile practice." It's specific, attributed, and consistent with how the reptile shedding and thermostat articles elsewhere on this site describe the same disease. The bearded-dragon-specific article reads less trustworthy by comparison: claims like "research consistently shows that UVB-synthesized D3 is more bioavailable... than oral supplements alone" appear with no citation at all, which is a genuine gap given how carefully sourced the sibling article and the rest of the site's reptile content are.

6. Bundle grade: B+. Reason: one excellent, well-sourced overview dragged down by a companion piece covering the same ground with more urgency and less evidence.
Two changes first: add real citations to the bearded-dragon-specific UVB article to match its own companion piece, and reconcile the bulb-replacement-interval numbers between the two so they don't read as two different answers to the same question.
