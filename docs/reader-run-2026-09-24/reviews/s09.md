## leaf-tailed-gecko

**1. Page by page**
- 00 Hub: A. Would finish it. Everything you need in the first week, with sources for every number.
- 01 Encyclopedia: B. Would finish it. Good taxonomy and CITES history, but nothing actionable beyond background.
- 02 Cost guide: A. Clear per-species pricing, honest table caveat, real vet numbers.
- 03 Handling guide: A. Tells you almost never to handle and explains exactly why, with a concrete stress checklist.
- 04 Health issues guide: A. Sorts every problem into home-manageable versus vet-now, no hedging.
- 05 Tank setup guide: A. The strongest page in the set. Corrects a genus-level myth (the 80 to 84F basking figure) instead of repeating it.
- 06 Feeding guide: A. Covers schedule, sizing, gut-loading, and a genuinely useful list of reasons a gecko stops eating.
- 07 Enrichment guide: A. Says plainly there is no research on this genus, then still gives a usable test (can you always find the gecko instantly).

**2. Hub and encyclopedia**
Both earn their place. The encyclopedia stays in its lane (wild biology, taxonomy, conservation) and never duplicates husbandry numbers. The hub is a genuine index with real numbers of its own, not a teaser. No disagreement found between the hub and any deep dive: enclosure sizes, temperatures, humidity ranges, and the budget figures all match word for word between the hub and the cost/tank-setup guides. The one thing the hub simplifies is the encyclopedia's wild lifespan ("5-10 years") versus the cost guide's captive figure ("5 to 15 years in captivity"), but that's two different populations, not a contradiction.

**3. The set as a whole**
Yes, mostly, for the satanic leaf-tailed gecko specifically, which is the species the tank setup and feeding guides default to when giving exact numbers. For any other species in the genus (Henkel's, mossy, giant) you're told "needs more room" or "costs less" without a real number to plan against. Two things raised but never answered in this set:
- How do you tell the ~15 species apart, or confirm you're actually buying the one whose care sheet you just read? The cost guide prices species separately; nothing tells you how to verify identification before purchase.
- How do you find or vet a captive-bred source? The cost guide and hub both insist captive-bred matters (health, price, lifespan) but never say how to confirm an animal actually is captive-bred rather than wild-caught relabeled as such.

The health issues guide references "the feeding guide has the light every-feeding dusting that covers the calcium half" without a link, but the feeding guide does answer it, so that one resolves inside the set even without a hyperlink.

**4. Overlap and conflict across the deep dives**
Read twice, sometimes three times: the "sheds in one clean piece, no sunken eyes" dehydration check appears near-verbatim in the hub, the tank setup guide, and the health issues guide. The "dehydration is a fast killer" line from specialist breeders is quoted in both the hub and the health issues guide. The satanic leaf-tail's tail-never-regrows fact appears in the handling guide and the health issues guide, consistently and cross-linked both times. No numeric conflicts turned up: temperature (68-75F, nights 64-68F), humidity (60-80%, 90-100% overnight for satanic), enclosure size (12x12x18 up to 18x18x24), and cost figures ($250-800 gecko, $300-600 setup, $20-40/month) all match everywhere they're repeated.

**5. Trust**
Nothing here reads as filler or exaggerated. The line that most convinced me a real keeper wrote this: "If you can always find the gecko instantly, it is too sparse." That's a field test, not marketing copy. The enrichment guide's admission that it's borrowing findings from tokay, crested, and leopard gecko studies that "transfer poorly" to this species, rather than dressing up thin evidence as solid, is the other thing that built confidence rather than eroded it.

**6. Grade and two changes**
Grade: A-. Comprehensive, internally consistent, and unusually honest about what isn't known, but it treats a 15-species genus as one animal in a few too many places.
1. Add a short species-identification and sourcing section (or table) so a buyer can confirm what they're actually purchasing and whether it's really captive-bred.
2. Cut the three-times-repeated shed/dehydration check down to one canonical spot (the health issues guide) and link to it from the hub and tank setup guide instead of restating it.

**7. Not covered anywhere**
- How to identify which Uroplatus species you're buying, or verify it's genuinely captive-bred: no page in this set or the Health and More list (quarantine, shedding, salmonella, heating, emergency plan, stool guide) answers this.
- Specific care numbers (enclosure size, humidity) for species other than the satanic leaf-tail and vague "larger species need more" guidance for the rest: not answered anywhere in the set.

## pacman-frog

**1. Page by page**
- 00 Hub: A. Would finish it. Dense, well-sourced first-week rundown with a clear emergency card.
- 01 Encyclopedia: B. Would finish it. Genuinely interesting history (Darwin's voyage, the fantasy frog hybrid) but no care value.
- 02 Cost guide: A. Solid pricing by morph, honest about the real financial risk being impaction, not routine cost.
- 03 Handling guide: A. Clear on why not to handle, how to if you must, and useful sexing detail.
- 04 Health issues guide: A. Sorts obesity, impaction, MBD, and red-leg cleanly by cause and urgency.
- 05 Tank setup guide: A. The substrate warning is specific and well-argued, not generic.
- 06 Feeding guide: A. The eight reasons a frog stops eating, with a "when to worry" section that actually gives numbers, is the best single section in the set.
- 07 Enrichment guide: A. The strongest page overall. Cites real, dated research (the 2023 anuran welfare review, the mountain chicken frog body-condition scale) instead of vague reassurance.

**2. Hub and encyclopedia**
The encyclopedia earns its place; it never repeats husbandry numbers and adds real content (the two-species taxonomy, the fantasy frog hybrid) the deep dives don't touch. The hub mostly earns its place too, but it disagrees with itself: it lists "$15 to $100 for the frog, $100 to $225 for the setup, about $225 all in." Added together, frog plus setup can run well past $225, yet "$225 all in" implies a single combined total. The cost guide's own line, "a complete first setup, frog included, commonly totals around $225," matches the hub's "all in" figure but not the two ranges sitting right next to it on the same page. This is a real internal inconsistency, quoted directly from the hub itself.

**3. The set as a whole**
Yes. This is the more complete of the two sets: setup, feeding, handling, and health issues all give a first-timer enough to act on immediately, and the enrichment guide's honesty about what "happy" even means for this species is unusually useful. One real gap: the feeding guide and health issues guide both mention aestivation as a real captive risk and are specific that a frog under a year old going dormant needs a vet, but neither says what a keeper should actually do if a healthy adult starts forming a cocoon. Wait it out? Intervene? That question is raised and never answered.

**4. Overlap and conflict across the deep dives**
Read three times almost verbatim: the "bite force compared to predatory dinosaurs" fun fact, in the cost guide, the handling guide, and the tank setup guide. It's accurate each time but reads as copy-paste padding by the third appearance. The impaction description (loose substrate, sand-coated feeders, prey too large) is also repeated across the health issues, tank setup, and feeding guides, but each repetition adds a slightly different angle (cause, prevention, symptom), so that one earns its space. No numeric conflicts: temperature (75-85F day, 65-75F night, 83F basking), humidity (60-80%), feeding frequency (juveniles daily to every 1-2 days, adults 1-3 times weekly), and lifespan (6-10 years, 10-15 achievable) all match everywhere they appear, including the encyclopedia's "about 6 years wild, 14.7 years captive record."

**5. Trust**
The line that most convinced me a real keeper wrote this: "A pacman frog sitting buried and motionless for days is not under-stimulated, it is hunting." It's a direct correction of bad advice a beginner would otherwise follow, and it's the actual thesis of the enrichment guide, not a throwaway line. What nudged trust down slightly was the identical dinosaur bite-force fact appearing three separate times word for word, which reads more like a marketing asset getting reused than three people separately checking the same fact.

**6. Grade and two changes**
Grade: A-. Thorough, consistent on every number that matters, let down only by one self-contradicting budget line and one unresolved question about adult aestivation.
1. Fix the hub's budget section so the frog range, the setup range, and the "all in" total actually reconcile with each other.
2. Add a line to the feeding or health guide on what to do (or not do) if a healthy adult frog begins aestivating, since only the under-a-year danger case is currently covered.

**7. Not covered anywhere**
- What to actually do if a healthy adult pacman frog begins aestivating in captivity: neither the feeding guide, the health issues guide, nor the Health and More list (salmonella, axolotl tubbing/cooling, amphibian quarantine, the four-pets comparison) answers this.
