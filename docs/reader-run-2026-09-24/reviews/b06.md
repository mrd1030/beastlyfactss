## birds-shared-1

**1. Would I finish it, what I learned, grade**

Avian Gastric Yeast (Megabacteriosis): The Silent Weight-Loss Disease. Yes. Learned the core danger, a bird can eat normally or even more while wasting away, the gram-scale habit for catching it early, and the specific 30-day amphotericin B protocol with the failure-if-shortened caveat. Grade A.

Avian Polyomavirus: Budgerigar Fledgling Disease and French Molt. Yes. The two-outcomes-from-one-virus framing (acute death in unweaned chicks versus permanent French molt in survivors) is a genuinely clarifying structure, and the PBFD comparison table answers the exact question an owner with a feather-damaged bird would actually have. Grade A.

Reading Bird Body Language: Fluffed Content vs. Fluffed Sick. Yes. The fluffed-relaxed-versus-fluffed-sick comparison table is immediately usable, and the article is unusually honest about where the evidence is thin (eye pinning mechanism studied in pigeons and cuckoos, not parrots). Grade A.

Chronic Egg Laying and Egg Binding in Budgies, Cockatiels, and Lovebirds. Yes. The full trigger list, including the under-known one about petting a hen's back being read as mating behavior, and the counterintuitive "don't pull the clutch" advice with the biological reasoning behind it, make this genuinely actionable. Grade A.

Colony and Aviary Keeping: Budgies, Canaries, and Finches in Groups. Yes. Refreshingly honest that no verified space-per-bird or sex-ratio number exists rather than inventing one, while still giving real, actionable principles (more feeding stations than birds, nest-site removal as the main lever). Grade A.

This is the strongest bundle so far. Every article is specific, well sourced, and cross-references the others correctly.

**4. Overlap and conflict**

This bundle is tightly cross-referenced and the pieces reinforce each other without repeating content. The polyomavirus article's point that a recovered bird can become an asymptomatic intermittent shedder is picked up directly and correctly by the colony guide's disease section, applied to the different context of group risk. The body-language article's "weigh your bird" recommendation matches the AGY article's identical advice almost word for word (a gram scale catching weight loss before visible signs), and both attribute it to the same underlying logic (feathers hide weight loss) without one copying the other's phrasing.

The chronic egg laying guide and the colony guide both touch nest-site removal as a management lever, but for different problems, a single laying hen versus preventing group breeding, and neither repeats the other's specifics.

No numeric or factual conflicts turned up. Where two articles cite the same source on the same point (VCA on avian polyomavirus carriers, cited independently in the polyomavirus article and the colony article), the description of the finding matches exactly.

**5. Trust**

This bundle cites primary and clinical sources unusually specifically: named Merck Veterinary Manual pages by exact topic, VCA Animal Hospitals, a peer-reviewed PMC case series, a named Australian avian-only practice (Melbourne Bird Vet), a 2021 USDA vaccine product summary quoted directly with dosing language, and a 2021 Current Biology study named by author and institution. The body-language article goes out of its way to flag the limits of that last citation (the eye-pinning mechanism was studied in pigeons and guira cuckoos, not parrots, and the summary is the Max Planck Institute's own account rather than a read of the paywalled paper), which is exactly the kind of caveat that builds trust rather than overselling a finding.

The AGY article is similarly careful with its mortality figure, presenting Merck's 10 to 80 percent range as a genuinely wide range rather than rounding it to one falsely precise number, and explicitly warning that "any source that hands you one flat figure is oversimplifying."

The most convincing sentence in the bundle is from the AGY article: "Documented case findings describe affected birds showing normal appetite or even increased appetite... right alongside the weight loss." It's counterintuitive, specific, and immediately actionable, exactly the kind of claim that changes what a reader does next (start weighing the bird) rather than just informing them.

**6. Bundle grade and first two changes**

Grade: A. Five well-sourced, tightly cross-referenced articles that are honest about the edges of their evidence and consistently reinforce rather than repeat each other.

First two changes: none of real substance turned up in this bundle. If pressed, the body-language article's subtitle promise could note upfront (as the intro paragraph already does) that feather condition and plucking live in a separate guide, so a reader skimming only the title doesn't wonder why those topics are absent. Second, the AGY article's "at least one peer-reviewed case series" citation could name the sample size or species split directly in the body rather than only in the Sources list, since that detail (cockatiels affected more often than budgies in that particular series) is exactly the kind of nuance the article otherwise handles well elsewhere.
