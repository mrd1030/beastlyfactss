## dogs-cats-general-2

1. Bloat in Dogs: The Emergency Every Large-Breed Owner Should Know: would finish it, gives a clear, memorizable list of warning signs and correctly debunks the raised-bowl myth with a named study. Grade A.
2. Dog Enrichment: Let Them Sniff, and the Dog Gets More Optimistic: would finish it, one well-explained study drives real, actionable advice, and it is honest that enrichment does not replace exercise or company. Grade A.
3. Kidney Disease in Cats: What the Numbers Actually Mean: would finish it, and reread it. The most rigorous article of the batch, it repeatedly flags where its own cited studies are weak rather than only where they are strong. Grade A.
4. French Bulldog Enrichment: Build the Whole Plan Around the Airway: would finish it, tailors general enrichment advice sensibly around a real physical constraint. Grade A-.
5. Golden Retriever vs. German Shepherd: Which Family Dog Fits Your Life: would finish it, a clear, decisive comparison table that actually answers the "which one" question. Grade B.
6. Hip and Elbow Dysplasia: What the Grades Actually Mean: would finish it, the OFA grading breakdown is genuinely useful and the Maine Coon extension is a nice surprise. Grade A.

Nothing here drops to a C, so there is no formal fix list, but the golden-versus-shepherd piece is the one furthest from an A and the closest to needing work. It would improve by naming its cancer-risk source instead of the vague "breed health foundation studies," and by including bloat risk in the German Shepherd health row, since that breed is deep-chested and this same site's own bloat article names it as a top risk breed.

What the golden-versus-shepherd title promises but the article does not fully deliver: it frames itself as settling "which family dog fits your life," but it never mentions cost of ownership, and its health comparison table leaves out bloat entirely for the shepherd side even though the breed is explicitly deep-chested. A reader who reads only this article would not know that risk exists for the breed they may be about to choose.

Overlap and conflict: the six articles interlock well and mostly repeat each other only where that is useful, the bloat guide and the golden-vs-shepherd guide both flag German Shepherds as deep-chested without contradicting each other on the fact itself. The one real gap is an omission rather than a numeric disagreement: the bloat article states plainly, in both its body and its FAQ, that "deep-chested large and giant breeds top the list: Great Danes, Standard Poodles, German Shepherds, Weimaraners, Saint Bernards, and Doberman Pinschers," but the health table in the golden-vs-shepherd comparison lists only "Hip/elbow dysplasia, degenerative myelopathy" as the German Shepherd's health concerns and never mentions bloat at all. A reader relying on the comparison piece alone would miss a risk the site's own bloat article treats as a top concern for that exact breed. No numbers actually contradict each other between any two articles; where breeds, prevalences, and grading scales are repeated (hip dysplasia mentioned in both the bloat and dysplasia articles, for instance) they match.

Trust: five of the six articles cite specific named studies with sample sizes, which sets a high bar, Duranton and Horowitz by name and year in the enrichment piece, Elliott, Plantinga, and Ross by name in the kidney disease piece, the OFA's own seven-grade system quoted directly in the dysplasia piece. Against that standard, the golden-vs-shepherd piece's "cited around 60% in breed health foundation studies" for cancer risk reads noticeably softer, no named foundation, no named study, in a bundle where its neighbors do better. Nothing else raised a flag. The most convincing sentence in the batch is from the kidney disease guide, which turns its skepticism on its own best evidence rather than only on the parts it wants to dismiss: "Effect sizes that large in observational cohorts usually signal confounding rather than a huge true effect, and the authors say as much themselves." That is a health article willing to undercut a dramatic-sounding number it could easily have just repeated.

Bundle grade: A-. Reason: four genuinely excellent, carefully sourced health and enrichment guides, one solid breed-specific enrichment piece, and one comparison article that is useful but noticeably less rigorously sourced and leaves out a risk its own sibling article names. Two changes first: add a named source for the golden retriever cancer-risk figure in the comparison piece, and add bloat to the German Shepherd health row in that same article so it does not contradict what the bloat guide already says about that breed.
