2. Grade C or lower

None.

3. Title versus content gaps

None significant. Each article does close to exactly what its title promises. The quarantine guide's title doesn't flag that it also covers the single-bird household case, which some readers might skip past assuming it's multi-bird only, but the FAQ catches that gap.

4. Overlap and conflict

The photoperiod guide and the pellet-conversion guide both reference budgie/cockatiel/lovebird hormonal and health issues without contradicting each other. No numeric disagreements found across the five: the quarantine windows (30/30-45/90 days) are presented as a tiered scale rather than competing claims, each attributed to a different level of household risk, which is coherent rather than conflicting.

One place worth flagging as a near-overlap rather than a conflict: the pellet-conversion guide's "12 hours for a small bird, 24 for larger" no-food rule and the household-hazards guide's general caution about a bird's small body mass and fast metabolism describe the same underlying vulnerability from two different angles, without linking to each other.

5. Trust

Most convincing sentence: the direct quote from the Merck Veterinary Manual that Lugol's iodine is used "until conversion to a pellet or fortified seed diet is accomplished and clinical signs have subsided," because it's specific, attributable, and reframes the whole article's stakes in one line.

Nothing sounded invented or overstated. The one place I'd want more rigor: the sexing article's claim that "more than half of the world's bird species show no distinct sexually dimorphic traits" is attributed to a single 2023 study, and the article leans on that one paper fairly heavily for a broad claim; it's disclosed as a single source rather than a consensus, which is honest, but it's the thinnest sourcing moment in the bundle.

6. Bundle grade: A, one line: five well-sourced guides that each correct a specific, common owner misconception with a citable mechanism rather than a vague warning.

Two changes first: cross-link the pellet-conversion starvation-risk numbers with the household-hazards guide's small-body-mass point, since they're really the same fact serving two articles, and give the sexing article a second source for the "more than half of bird species" claim so it doesn't rest on one paper alone.
