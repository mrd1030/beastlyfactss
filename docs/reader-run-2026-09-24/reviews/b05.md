## aquatic-inverts-smallmammal-shared-5

**1. Would I finish it, what I learned, grade**

Heat Stress and Cold in Small Mammals: Temperature Ranges and Emergency Signs. Yes. The 80°F ceiling across species, the chinchilla "150 rule" (temperature plus humidity), and the ordered symptom sequence are all specific and actionable, and the power-outage section usefully overturns the reptile-owner instinct to stop feeding a cold animal. Grade A.

Getting a Small Mammal to the Vet: Carriers, Car Travel, and Anesthesia. Yes. The anesthesia section alone is worth the read: it explains, with direct Merck quotes, why the standard dog-and-cat overnight fast is actively wrong for a rabbit or guinea pig. Grade A.

How to Tell If Your Fish Is Sick: Waste, Behavior, and Body Signs. Yes. Leads with "test the water first," which reframes almost every other symptom in the article correctly, and is honest that stringy white feces is a nonspecific sign rather than a diagnosis. Grade A.

All three articles are thorough, well organized, and free of filler or padding.

**2. Improvements for C-or-lower articles**

None graded C or below.

**3. Title-vs-content gaps**

Heat Stress and Cold in Small Mammals: none. The FAQ even extends the scope honestly to gerbils, degus, hedgehogs, and sugar gliders, flagging that hedgehogs and sugar gliders don't fit the room-temperature premise at all rather than silently ignoring the mismatch.

Getting a Small Mammal to the Vet: none, it delivers carrier, car travel, and anesthesia exactly as titled.

How to Tell If Your Fish Is Sick: none, it delivers waste, behavior, and body signs as promised, and clearly hands off treatment to a separate named guide rather than pretending to cover that too.

**4. Overlap and conflict**

The two small mammal articles are a matched pair and cross-reference each other correctly: the vet-visit guide defers power-outage heat questions to the temperature guide, and the temperature guide's power-outage section reinforces the vet-visit guide's core anesthesia point (never withhold food from these species) without repeating its content. Both articles state, consistently, that rabbits and guinea pigs cannot vomit and that this changes standard practice; the vet guide applies it to anesthesia fasting, the temperature guide applies it to feeding through an outage. No contradiction between them, and the shared fact is used two different ways rather than pasted twice.

The fish sickness guide doesn't overlap with the two small mammal pieces at all, being a different animal class, but it shares the bundle's general pattern of leading with an environmental cause (water quality, temperature) before jumping to a specific diagnosis. That's a stylistic echo, not a content overlap or conflict.

One small numeric wrinkle inside the temperature guide: the danger threshold is stated as "80°F" in the key takeaway box and the table, but the RSPCA figure quoted in the same table says guinea pig hyperthermia risk "can start as low as 75°F (24°C)." The article handles this reasonably by presenting it as a range rather than picking one number, but a reader skimming just the bolded 80°F takeaway could come away thinking 75-79°F is automatically safe for a guinea pig, when the article's own sourced detail says otherwise.

**5. Trust**

All three articles are heavily and specifically sourced, with direct quotes attributed to named documents (Merck Veterinary Manual pages named by topic, RSPCA Knowledgebase, VCA Animal Hospitals, UF/IFAS, LafeberVet with a named veterinary author, PDSA). The vet-visit guide is particularly careful to attribute the "why" behind a counterintuitive rule (no long fast because rabbits cannot vomit and their stomach is never empty) rather than just stating the rule. The temperature guide is honest where evidence is soft, explicitly stating "we couldn't verify a specific degu temperature range from a veterinary source" rather than inventing one.

The most convincing sentence in the bundle is from the fish sickness guide: "A large share of what looks like disease is actually a water quality problem, and testing the water is the first move, not the last resort." It's a strong, falsifiable claim that the rest of the article actually backs up point by point (ammonia, nitrite, and low oxygen all mimicking classic disease symptoms), rather than a vague generalization.

Nothing in this directory reads as unsourced or overstated. The closest to a soft spot is the temperature guide's subtitle framing ("verified against veterinary sources rather than the round numbers that circulate on forums"), which is a claim about competing content the article doesn't need to make since its own sourcing already demonstrates it.

**6. Bundle grade and first two changes**

Grade: A. Three well-sourced, non-overlapping, non-repetitive articles that each lead with the right first step (test the room, test the water, ask the right question before anesthesia) rather than jumping straight to treatment.

First two changes: in the temperature guide, either lower the memorized "80°F" number in the key takeaway box to match the RSPCA's 75°F guinea pig risk floor, or add one clause noting the true floor is species-dependent, so the boldest, most-skimmed number in the article doesn't undersell the guinea pig-specific risk. Second, cut the temperature guide's subtitle dig at "round numbers that circulate on forums," since the article's sourcing already makes that case without needing to state it.
