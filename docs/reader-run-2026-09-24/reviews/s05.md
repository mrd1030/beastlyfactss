## whites-tree-frog

**1. Page by page**
- 00 Hub: A. Numbers up front, links to source, a real emergency card. Would finish and act on it.
- 01 Encyclopedia: A. The John White/Hunterian Museum backstory is genuinely interesting, not filler.
- 02 Cost guide: A. Clear tables, dated prices, an honest "budget for the runway not the setup" close.
- 03 Handling guide: A. Answers the one real question (why gloves over soap) with a mechanism, not just a rule.
- 04 Health issues guide: A. Names the one big risk early and gives a testable line (tympanum/armpit fat) instead of vague warnings.
- 05 Tank setup guide: A. Flags where sources disagree on humidity and says which side it picked and why.
- 06 Feeding guide: A. The ridge test is the best single thing in this set, an at-a-glance body-condition check.
- 07 Enrichment guide: A. Honest about weak evidence (a red-eyed tree frog study, not this species) instead of dressing it up as species-specific.

**2. Hub and encyclopedia**
Both earn their place. The hub is a router with numbers and links, the encyclopedia is pure natural history (etymology, the 1790 description, the destroyed type specimen) that appears nowhere else. No numbers disagree between them: adult size, lifespan (16 avg, 21 record, 10-15+ conservative), and links to the same six deep dives are identical word for word in places, which reads more like shared structured data than duplicated writing effort.

**3. Could I keep one after reading all of this?**
Yes, close to fully. Enclosure size, gradient, humidity behavior, substrate, UVB, feeding amount and schedule, supplement frequency, and the obesity test are all specific and consistent. What's still missing: how to actually sex a frog or know what you're buying beyond "standard vs. designer morph," and any guidance on general household chemical exposure (air fresheners, cleaning sprays, candles) beyond hand hygiene before touching the frog, which matters a lot for skin that absorbs everything.

Two questions get raised on one page and answered on another without the text saying so directly, though a link is always there: the tank setup guide tells you to use dechlorinated water but the actual "why distilled water kills them" explanation lives in the quarantine/water guide (linked, so this is a soft miss, not a real gap). Similarly, the enrichment guide tells you to watch for obesity but the actual diagnostic (the ridge test) is in the feeding guide, not restated here, just cross-linked.

**4. Overlap and conflict across deep dives**
The lifespan figures, temperature/humidity numbers, and the "3-4 insects, 2-3x/week" feeding line repeat near-verbatim across the hub, cost guide, health guide, and feeding guide. That's expected reference data, not padding, since each page needs it standalone. I checked all of it for drift and found no numeric conflicts: enclosure size, temp, humidity, feeding schedule, and lifespan all match exactly everywhere they appear. The one place with an acknowledged disagreement is humidity itself: the tank setup guide says "sources genuinely differ here, some recommend a higher constant range" against the site's own chosen 50-60% baseline, which is disclosed rather than hidden, so it isn't really a trust problem.

**5. Trust**
Nothing here made me doubt the writer. The line that most convinced me a real keeper wrote this: "a moderate fat roll over the shoulders is actually normal for the species rather than a sign of overfeeding," paired with the specific tympanum/armpit test. That's the kind of distinction you only make after having second-guessed your own frog's weight. The dated price-check line ("Prices last checked September 2026 at Josh's Frogs and PetSmart") is also a small, unusual honesty signal most care sites skip.

**6. Grade: A.** Consistent numbers, an honest treatment of a genuinely uncertain point (humidity), and a feeding test you can use without a vet.
Two changes: add a line on general household chemical/scent exposure risk to amphibian skin, not just hand hygiene, and give buyers a way to tell morphs and sex apart before purchase.

**7. Not covered anywhere**
- How to sex or otherwise judge what you're actually buying beyond "standard vs. designer morph."
- General household chemical/scent exposure risk (candles, air fresheners, sprays) beyond hand-washing before handling.
- Breeding or egg-laying basics, if an owner ends up with a pair.

## jumping-spider

**1. Page by page**
- 00 Hub: A. Dense but organized, and the abdomen-as-gauge idea is repeated here exactly as it appears later, so it's reinforcement, not filler.
- 01 Encyclopedia: A. The Lucas the Spider/2017 pop-culture pivot is a genuinely useful piece of context most care sites wouldn't bother including.
- 02 Cost guide: A. Specific, dated, and honest that vet care basically doesn't apply to this animal.
- 03 Handling guide: A. Short, but the material genuinely is short: eyesight, fall risk, done. Not (thin), because nothing needed is missing.
- 04 Health issues guide: A. Tight cause-and-effect structure (humidity to dehydration to failed molt) that a reader can act on immediately.
- 05 Tank setup guide: A. The "leave room near the top for the retreat, put the door low" detail is the kind of thing that actually prevents a mistake.
- 06 Feeding guide: A. The most thorough page in either set; life-stage schedule, safe/unsafe prey, and seven distinct reasons a spider stops eating.
- 07 Enrichment guide: A. Cites actual named papers (a 2024 eLife individual-recognition study) rather than gesturing at "enrichment matters."

**2. Hub and encyclopedia**
Both earn their place. The hub is the numbers-and-links router; the encyclopedia carries the species' actual history and popularity story, which doesn't appear on the hub at all. I did not find a hard numeric disagreement, but there is a soft tension worth quoting: the encyclopedia states "Wild Lifespan: 1-2 years," while the hub and cost guide both say "Expect 1 to 3 years overall" for captive animals and break it out by sex (males 8-12 months post-maturity, females 1.5-3 years). These aren't technically contradictory, wild and captive lifespans can differ, but the page never says that's why the numbers don't match, so a careful reader has to work that out themselves.

**3. Could I keep one after reading all of this?**
Yes, and this set is even more complete than the frog's: enclosure size, temperature, humidity, misting frequency, substrate, feeding schedule by life stage, a full safe/unsafe prey list, and clear signs of molting or dehydration are all there and consistent. One soft tension: the cost guide says "exotic vets rarely treat spiders," effectively "don't expect vet care to be an option," while the hub's emergency card and the feeding guide both say a stuck molt or prolonged refusal "warrants an exotic-animal vet." Both can be true (vets who treat spiders exist but are rare), but the pages don't reconcile the "there's basically no vet care" framing with "go to the vet" advice sitting one click away.

**4. Overlap and conflict across deep dives**
The "never feed ants" warning and the abdomen-as-gauge advice repeat across the hub, feeding guide, and feeding FAQ almost verbatim, again reference data rather than padding. I found no numeric conflicts: enclosure size (4x4x7 min), humidity (50-60%), misting interval (every 2-3 days), and feeding windows by life stage match everywhere. The feeding guide's premolt fasting window ("a few days for spiderlings up to a few weeks for larger juveniles") and the health issues guide's humidity-causes-failed-molt explanation complement rather than repeat each other, which is the set working as intended.

**5. Trust**
Nothing raised a red flag. The line that most convinced me a real keeper wrote this: "Males in particular are sometimes fed as infrequently as every 10 days," a specific, slightly counterintuitive detail that reads like lived observation rather than a template fact. The explicit "this is hobbyist-reported, not veterinary-established" disclaimer on the no-food timelines is also a real trust signal, most sites would just state those numbers as fact.

**6. Grade: A.** Thorough, internally consistent, and honest about where its advice is anecdotal versus established.
Two changes: reconcile the "vets rarely treat spiders" framing with the emergency-card vet advice so a reader isn't left wondering if a vet visit is realistic at all, and explicitly note that captive lifespan can exceed the stated wild lifespan so the two numbers don't read as contradictory.

**7. Not covered anywhere**
- How to tell a spider's sex before maturity, relevant since female sub-adults cost more specifically for their longer lifespan.
- What "tropical genera need more" humidity actually means in a number, for anyone buying a related Phidippus species.
- Practical guidance for finding a vet who actually treats spiders, given the cost guide's own "exotic vets rarely treat spiders" line.
