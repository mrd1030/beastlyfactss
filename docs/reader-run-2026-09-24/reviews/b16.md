1. Article ratings, all twelve are the same "10 facts" template and all deliver:

Argentine Tegu: finish it, learned tegus can self-heat overnight from muscle mitochondria, a genuinely novel finding. Grade A.
Ball Python: finish it, learned brooding females shiver to warm eggs and why a balled-up snake isn't being difficult. Grade A.
Bearded Dragon: finish it, learned about the parietal eye and UV vision, casual tone but accurate. Grade B.
Betta Fish: finish it, learned the labyrinth organ and that wild bettas are drab olive, not the pet-store colors. Grade A.
Cockatoo: finish it, learned about Snowball the dancing cockatoo and tool-using Goffin's cockatoos. Grade A.
Emperor Scorpion: finish it, learned the sting is roughly bee-sting strength and mothers carry scorplings on their back. Grade A.
Fire-Bellied Toad: finish it, learned the toxin acts like digitalis at the cellular level, useful and specific. Grade A.
Hedgehog: finish it, learned self-anointing and that pet hedgehogs don't truly hibernate. Grade A.
Hermit Crab: finish it, learned about the size-ordered shell-swapping queue, one of the better single facts in the whole bundle. Grade A.
Hissing Cockroach: finish it, learned the hiss comes from abdominal spiracles, not the mouth, and there are four distinct hiss types. Grade A.
Leopard Gecko: finish it, learned they blink (most geckos can't) and squeak when startled. Grade A.
Praying Mantis: finish it, learned about the single bat-detecting ultrasonic ear. Grade A.

2. No article graded C or lower in this directory.

3. Title versus content gaps: none of the twelve leaves an expected gap; each fact list matches its title and the linked care/deep-dive guides cover anything the list only touches briefly (UVB for bearded dragons, venom for fire-bellied toads, handling for hermit crabs).

4. Overlap and conflicts: the tegu article and the emperor scorpion article both cite the platypus fluorescing or laying eggs as an aside, and both link to the same platypus article, which is fine as reuse but means a reader who opens both facts pages inside one session sees the identical platypus trivia twice. No numeric claims actually conflict between articles in this set.

5. Trust: the tegu claim that its heat-generation "rewrote a piece of" the endotherm/ectotherm textbook definition is a strong claim for a blog aside, though the mechanism (muscle mitochondria, 2024 research) is specific enough to sound genuine rather than invented. The leopard gecko squeak theory ("may function similarly to other prey species' alarm calls") is appropriately hedged as speculative. Most convincing sentence: the hermit crab shell-queue description, because it is oddly specific and testable, not vague color-and-mood language. Nothing else raised real doubt.

6. Directory grade: A-. Reason: a tight, consistent template executed well across twelve species, real specific facts rather than filler, and genuine internal linking to deeper guides rather than to nowhere. First two changes: vary the recurring platypus aside so it does not appear near-identically in two lists a reader might read back to back, and add one line distinguishing "still-unexplained" facts (scorpion UV glow, mantis molt behavior) from settled ones so casual readers do not treat speculation as confirmed science.

## fun-facts-2

1. Article ratings:

Tarantula: finish it, learned about urticating hairs and that falls, not bites, are the real danger to the animal. Grade A, matches the polish of fun-facts-1.
Axolotl: finish it, but the piece is noticeably thinner than the reptile/invert lists, nine facts of prose with no care guide link and no deep-dive sidebar at all. Grade B (thin).
Boa Constrictor: finish it, learned constriction cuts circulation rather than causing slow suffocation, a genuinely corrective fact. Grade B (thin), same missing sidebar infrastructure as the rest of this batch.
Cuttlefish: finish it, learned about the W-shaped pupil and the internal cuttlebone for buoyancy. Grade B (thin).
Humpback Whale: finish it, learned about bubble-net feeding and individually unique tail flukes. Grade B (thin).
Octopus: finish it, learned about the nine-brain arrangement and the coconut octopus's two-arm walk. Grade B (thin).
Rabbit: finish it, learned the "binky" and that rabbits release heat through their ears rather than sweating. Grade B (thin).

2. No article graded C or lower, but six of the seven (everything but tarantula) would benefit from the one fix worth naming: add a care guide link and populate the Deep Dive and Health and More sidebars the way every other species page in the bundle has. As written, these six read like an earlier or outsourced batch that was never wired into the rest of the site.

3. Title versus content gaps: the axolotl piece promises "real-life superheroes" and covers regeneration well, but its aside about captive leucistic axolotls glowing "from green fluorescent protein, a gene borrowed from a glowing jellyfish" implies any leucistic axolotl glows, when that trait only appears in axolotls that have actually been genetically modified with a jellyfish GFP gene, not leucistic axolotls generally. That is a gap between what the fact claims and what is biologically true, not just a missing topic.

4. Overlap and conflicts: cuttlefish and octopus both explain the "three hearts, one stops when swimming" fact and both attribute it correctly to cephalopods generally, so this is consistent, but a reader who has just read the octopus list gets the identical heart fact restated almost verbatim in the cuttlefish list minutes later. No numbers directly contradict each other across this set.

5. Trust: the axolotl GFP-glow claim above is the one that made me pause, since it conflates a lab tool (a jellyfish gene inserted into research axolotls) with an ordinary pet-store leucistic axolotl's natural color. The boa constrictor's heartbeat-syncing claim is well specified enough (matching each pulse, unconsciousness within seconds) to read as sourced from an actual study rather than folklore, and it is the most convincing sentence in this directory because it corrects a specific, common misconception rather than just restating one.

6. Directory grade: B. Reason: the writing quality and fact selection are consistent with fun-facts-1, but six of seven articles are missing the site's own linking infrastructure (no care guide, empty sidebars) and one fact (axolotl GFP glow) overstates what is true for an ordinary pet. First two changes: fix or hedge the axolotl fluorescence claim so it is clear that only genetically modified axolotls glow, and give the six thin articles the same care-guide link and populated Deep Dive and Health and More lists the tarantula article and the whole fun-facts-1 bundle already have.
