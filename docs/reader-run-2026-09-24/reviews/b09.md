Wing Clipping (Flighted vs. Clipped): finish it. Genuinely even-handed on a contested topic, and the blood-feather emergency section is the most important paragraph in the piece. A.

Choosing a Pet Bird: finish it. The lifespan table and the hand-raised-versus-parent-raised welfare tradeoff, backed by a named 2006 study, are both the kind of thing a buyer needs before money changes hands. A.

Parrot Training (Behavior Ladder): finish it. The six-rung ladder table is clear and actually teachable, and the point that training is welfare maintenance rather than a bonus trick is well argued. A.

Rehomed Parrot guide (Adopting a Rescue Parrot): finish it. The refusal to speculate about grief or emotion beyond what sources actually document is the strongest editorial choice in the whole bundle. A.

2. Grade C or lower

None.

3. Title versus content gaps

Choosing a Pet Bird: the title implies species-general guidance, but the worked lifespan table only covers cockatiel and cockatoo; a reader considering a budgie, lovebird, or African grey gets the framework but not their own species' numbers in the table itself.

Wing Clipping guide: it honestly answers "does clipping guarantee no flight" (most owners assume yes), which is a positive surprise rather than a gap, but the "case for and against" framing never actually recommends a default for a first-time owner, which some readers will want and won't get, deliberately.

4. Overlap and conflict

No factual conflicts. The choosing-a-bird guide and the rehomed-parrot guide directly complement rather than duplicate: one explains hand-raised-vs-parent-raised tameness costs for a new bird, the other explains why an adult rescue bird's socialization history is closed and unverifiable, citing different research without overlapping claims. The parrot-training guide and the rehomed-parrot guide agree that trained behavior often doesn't transfer between people, stated consistently in both places (training is person-specific, needs rebuilding with a new handler), though neither article links to the other's version of the same point.

Worth calling out as a consistency win rather than a conflict: the choosing-a-bird guide says it looked for a decibel figure for cockatoo screaming and found none worth citing, and no other article in this set attempts a number either, so the restraint holds across the bundle.

5. Trust

Most convincing sentence: the choosing-a-bird guide's explicit statement that it searched for a sourced decibel figure for cockatoo noise and couldn't find one, rather than repeating the commonly circulated number. That's the single strongest trust signal in the bundle.

Nothing else raised doubt. The wing-clipping article's willingness to quote VCA's pro-clipping behavioral claim and then directly say "this is, word for word, the thing critics object to" is unusually candid for a care-guide site and reads as more trustworthy for showing its own tension rather than resolving it artificially.

6. Bundle grade: A, one line: four articles that handle genuinely contested or evidence-thin topics (clipping, hand-rearing welfare, rescue-bird psychology) with real intellectual honesty instead of manufactured certainty.

Two changes first: extend the choosing-a-bird lifespan table beyond cockatiel and cockatoo to cover more of the species the site otherwise writes about, and add an explicit cross-link between the training guide's "behavior doesn't transfer between handlers" point and the rehomed-parrot guide's "regression in handling" section, since right now two articles make the same point independently without ever pointing at each other.
