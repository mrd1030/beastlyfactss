## overviews-1

1. Article ratings. All twelve share one template (three or four short species sections plus a "common thread" wrap-up) and it works well across the set:

Four Animals, Four First Impressions: finish it, learned angelfish turn predatory around 3 inches even toward tankmates they grew up with. Grade A.
Four Pets, Four Different Right Numbers: finish it, learned the one-handler-per-3-to-4-feet rule for boas and that neon tetras need six as a floor, not a suggestion. Grade A.
Three Shrimp, Three Different Jobs: finish it, learned Amano shrimp are almost never home-bred because larvae need saltwater. Grade A.
Two Pets Where You're Really Buying a Pair: finish it, learned degus have a specific diabetes risk that makes fruit genuinely dangerous. Grade A.
Five Reptiles That Actually Make Sense for Beginners: finish it, learned crested gecko tails never regrow while leopard gecko tails do. Grade A.
Four Pets That Break the Usual Mold: finish it, learned axolotls need cold water with no heater and goldfish need a real 20-gallon tank. Grade A.
Three Geckos, Three Very Different Pets: finish it, learned mourning geckos are all female and reproduce without males. Grade A.
Four Pets Whose Size Tells You the Wrong Thing: finish it, learned green anoles are more fragile than far larger lizards and tiger salamanders can live past 25 years. Grade A.
Four Pets With One Rule Each Worth Knowing First: finish it, learned overheated non-stick cookware fumes are rapidly fatal to birds. Grade A.
Four Pets, Four Different Lessons: finish it, learned Northern versus Indonesian blue-tongued skinks differ enough in origin and parasite risk to matter at purchase. Grade A.
Three Pets That Are a Bigger Deal Than They Look: finish it, learned an estimated 70% of captive green iguanas die in their first year from inadequate care. Grade A.
Four Pets Content to Stay Out of the Spotlight: finish it, learned Jackson's chameleons give live birth while most chameleons lay eggs. Grade A.

2. No article in this directory graded C or lower.

3. Title versus content gaps: essentially none. Each piece promises a short, specific comparison and delivers exactly that, with cost, handling, health, and setup links backing every claim rather than leaving it unsupported. The one soft spot is "Three Pets That Are a Bigger Deal Than They Look," whose title implies three independent surprises, but the iguana section explicitly says it repeats "the same outgrows the setup story already told here by the savannah monitor and Argentine tegu," which is honest but means a reader who has already read the tegu or size-mismatch articles gets less that is new.

4. Overlap and conflicts: the Argentine tegu appears as a comparison animal in three separate articles (Four Animals, Four Pets Whose Size Tells You the Wrong Thing, and Three Pets That Are a Bigger Deal Than They Look), each time repeating the same core claims almost verbatim: dog-like tameness once socialized, seasonal brumation of 2 to 4 months even indoors. The claims never contradict each other, but a reader working through this directory in order sees the same tegu paragraph three times with only the wrapper changed. The hermit crab's lifespan also shifts slightly by context: "Four Pets That Break the Usual Mold" gives captive hermit crabs 10 to 20 years, which sits under the wild figure of 20 to 30 years given for the species elsewhere on the site, though since one is captive and one is wild this reads as a real distinction rather than an error. Tarantulas, boas, and ball pythons likewise reappear across several of these comparison pieces with consistent, non-conflicting facts each time.

5. Trust: nothing in this directory reads as invented or overstated. Every strong number, the 70% first-year iguana mortality, the 3-to-4-feet-per-handler boa rule, the Amano shrimp's saltwater larval requirement, is specific enough and consistently repeated across pages to read as sourced rather than guessed. The most convincing sentence in the bundle is the boa constrictor FAQ's citation of "exotic veterinary guidance uses roughly one handler per 3 to 4 feet of snake as a working rule of thumb," because it names an actual practical threshold rather than a vague safety warning.

6. Directory grade: A. Reason: twelve tightly written comparison pieces that consistently deliver a real, checkable surprise per species, link out to genuine deeper guides rather than dead ends, and never pad with filler. The only real inefficiency is structural, not factual: the Argentine tegu's dog-comparison story is told nearly identically three separate times. First two changes: vary the tegu write-up across its three appearances so repeat readers get new angles instead of the same paragraph, and add one line to "Three Pets That Are a Bigger Deal Than They Look" acknowledging its iguana section overlaps with the tegu and monitor size-mismatch pieces before, not just after, the reader gets there.
