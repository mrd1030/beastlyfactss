## aquatic-inverts-smallmammal-shared-4

**1. Would I finish it, what I learned, grade**

Invertebrate Housekeeping: Quarantining a New Arrival, Cleaning the Enclosure, and Finding an Escapee. Yes. Learned the three-month quarantine figure (versus 30 days for fish, a different animal group so not a conflict), the "clean too much" warning with a real schedule, and a workable escape-search method by species habit. Grade A.

Rehousing an Invertebrate: Method, Timing, and Urticating Hair First Aid. Yes, all the way through. The prep-versus-transfer split is a genuinely useful reframe, the species comparison table is specific rather than generic, and the urticating-hair first aid section cites an actual peer-reviewed case report. This is the most rigorously sourced article in the bundle. Grade A.

Shrimp Molting: What a Normal Molt Looks Like, and the White Ring That Means It Failed. Yes. Concrete GH numbers by species, a clear explanation of why the white ring happens, and honest that intervention rarely saves the shrimp. Grade A-.

Antibiotic-Associated Enterotoxemia. Yes, and I'd act on it (bring the drug table to a vet visit). The species-by-species death timeline is sobering and specific rather than vague. Grade A.

Grooming, Nails, and Molting in Small Mammals. Yes. The rabbit-can't-vomit mechanism connecting molting to GI stasis is the kind of thing that reframes a "grooming chore" as a health task, and the per-species bathing table is a nice compact reference. Grade A.

Nothing here felt thin or padded. Second strong bundle in a row.

None graded C or below.

**3. Title-vs-content gaps**

Invertebrate Housekeeping: none, it delivers quarantine, cleaning, and escapes exactly as titled.

Rehousing an Invertebrate: none.

Shrimp Molting: none.

Antibiotic-Associated Enterotoxemia: the subtitle promises the danger "most vets don't warn you about," which is a claim about vet behavior the article doesn't actually support with any evidence about what vets do or don't say. It's a framing device rather than a real gap in content, but a skeptical reader could notice the subtitle overreaches slightly beyond what's proven inside.

Grooming, Nails, and Molting in Small Mammals: none, it covers exactly the species and topics promised.

**4. Overlap and conflict**

The housekeeping guide and the rehousing guide are clearly built as a matched pair (housekeeping explicitly defers the actual enclosure move to the rehousing guide), and they don't repeat each other. The rehousing guide's species table and the housekeeping guide's escape-search-by-habit section both use the tarantula-versus-arboreal-versus-scorpion distinction, but from different angles (moving it safely versus finding it after it got loose), so this reads as reinforcement rather than filler.

The enterotoxemia guide and the grooming guide both belong to the small mammal set and both point to the rabbit GI stasis guide, but for different reasons: enterotoxemia as a drug-triggered emergency, grooming as a molt-triggered one. No overlap in the actual content, and no conflicting claims between them.

No numeric or factual conflicts turned up within this directory. The one place two sources give different numbers for the "same" thing, cherry shrimp GH range, only appears once here (6 to 12, with 7 to 8 optimal) and isn't contradicted anywhere else in this set.

**5. Trust**

This directory is unusually well sourced. The rehousing guide is the standout: it cites a specific 2012 case report by name ("Tarantula hair keratitis," Mangat and Newman, New Zealand Medical Journal) with a real clinical detail (barbed hairs migrating through the corneal epithelium into the stroma), which is a level of citation specificity well above typical pet-care content. The enterotoxemia guide is similarly careful, naming exact drug classes with a source column per row, and explicitly flagging that the degu extension is "an extrapolation from a documented mechanism, not a degu-specific study," rather than overstating certainty it doesn't have.

The weakest citation in the bundle is the housekeeping guide's throwaway legal claim: "Releasing any non-native invertebrate or its eggs is prohibited." That's presented as flat, universal law with no source and no jurisdiction named, in an article that otherwise attributes almost every claim to a named hobbyist or vet source. It's plausible but is the one unsupported absolute statement in the set.

The most convincing sentence in the bundle is from the enterotoxemia article: "The Merck Veterinary Manual describes it plainly for lincosamide drugs in these species: the reaction 'can be lethal' and is 'very serious in herbivores.'" It's a direct quote from a named clinical reference backing up a claim that would otherwise sound like hobbyist exaggeration (a normal antibiotic killing a pet), and the specificity of the quote is what makes it land.

**6. Bundle grade and first two changes**

Grade: A. Every article is specific, well attributed, and honest about the edges of its own evidence, and the rehousing guide in particular is a model for how to cite a real case report without overclaiming from it.

First two changes: attach a source to the housekeeping guide's "releasing any non-native invertebrate is prohibited" line, or soften it to note that rules vary by species and state the way the emergency-plan and legal-status articles elsewhere in this set already do. Second, dial back the enterotoxemia article's subtitle claim about what vets do or don't warn about, since the body never actually supports that specific claim about vet behavior, only the biological mechanism itself.
