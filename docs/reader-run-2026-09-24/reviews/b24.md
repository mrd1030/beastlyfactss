## wild-animals-and-other-3

1. Article ratings

International Wombat Day: would finish it. Learned the date's origin is unverified folklore and got real numbers on the northern hairy-nosed wombat's recovery from 35 to 400 animals. Grade A.

Lions are the only social cat: would finish it. Learned pride structure is female-owned territory, not male-led, and that territory quality (not hunting efficiency) drives group living. Grade A.

Mantis shrimp: would finish it, but the tone is thin gruel next to the rest of the bundle. Learned the 16-photoreceptor claim is more nuanced than advertised and the club-strike physics. Grade C (filler).

Mike the headless chicken: would finish it. Learned the specific anatomy (brain stem survival) that explains an incredible true story, plus how he actually died. Grade A.

Naked mole rat: would finish it. Learned the queen-suppression pheromone, the contested cancer and aging claims, and the fructose-metabolism trick for surviving without oxygen. Grade A.

Narwhal facts: would finish it. Learned the tusk is a sensory organ (not just decoration), the Elizabeth I story is unverified legend, and the "150-year-old narwhal" figure is one outlier. Grade A.

Pangolins: would finish it. Learned the keratin-armor angle, the honest range of trafficking estimates, and that not all eight species are critically endangered despite popular claims. Grade A.

Penguins do not mate for life: would finish it. Learned fidelity tracks nest-site stability, not devotion, with real numbers by species. Grade A.

Seahorse male pregnancy: would finish it. Learned the pouch is modified skin, not a placenta by descent, and the immune system the male had to sacrifice to tolerate the embryos. Grade A.

Sharks are older than trees: would finish it, though it is the most listicle-shaped piece in the bundle. Learned several corrected numbers (tooth-shedding range, Fastskin's real effect, sleep uncertainty in swimming sharks). Grade B (some items still read as filler bullet points, e.g. items 7 and 9 add little beyond a definition).

2. Improving the C-or-lower article

Mantis shrimp: cut the exclamation-heavy opener ("buckle up," "yes, really") and cross-check the 16-color-vision claim harder; the article already flags that mantis shrimp are bad at discriminating similar colors but still leads with "16-color vision" in the title, which undersells its own correction. Also verify or drop the "mates for life" claim in the opener, since it is never substantiated anywhere in the body.

3. Title versus content gaps

Mantis shrimp: the title promises "16-color vision" as a headline feature, but the body's own reporting undercuts it (they are worse than humans at telling similar colors apart), so the title oversells the very fact the article complicates.

No other clear title/content gaps: the rest deliver what their headlines promise.

4. Overlap and conflicts

Naked mole rat piece and penguin piece both reference each other ("Compare it with... Penguins do not mate for life either") and are consistent.

Narwhal article and sharks article both cite the same eye-lens-based aging method (aspartic acid racemization / radiocarbon) for long-lived animals (narwhal, Greenland shark) with no conflict, just reinforcing cross-links.

Pangolin article states rhino horn is "the same protein" (keratin) as pangolin scales, which is factually consistent with general knowledge and not contradicted elsewhere.

No numeric conflicts found between articles in this bundle.

5. Trust

Most convincing sentence: "Those are not four competing versions of one number. They measure different things: documented international trafficking, customs seizures, and local hunting pressure." (pangolin article), because it resolves an apparent contradiction in trafficking statistics by explaining methodology rather than picking a favorite number.

Doubtful moments: the mantis shrimp piece's claim that some species "mate for life" is asserted in the opener and never revisited or sourced anywhere in the body, unlike everything else in this bundle which sources or hedges its claims carefully. The Narwhal piece is unusually good about flagging Elizabeth I stories as "traditional accounts rather than documented transactions," which raises overall trust.

6. Bundle grade

Grade A-minus. Nine of ten articles are precise, self-correcting, well-sourced, and consistently flag when a popular claim outruns the evidence. The mantis shrimp piece is a clear outlier in tone and rigor, reading like an older, unedited draft next to the rest.

Two changes: rewrite the mantis shrimp article to match the bundle's citation and hedging standard (it currently reads like unsourced enthusiasm), and tighten the sharks listicle by cutting or merging the weaker numbered items (7 and 9) that add no new information beyond a one-line definition.

## wild-animals-and-other-4

1. Article ratings

Shima enaga: would finish it. Learned the white face is a real taxonomic marker unique to Hokkaido birds, and how the nest's spider-silk elasticity works. Grade A.

Slow loris venom: would finish it. Learned the venom is two-part (gland secretion plus saliva), mostly used against other lorises, and how that connects to the pet trade's tooth-clipping cruelty. Grade A.

Tardigrades: would finish it. Learned the difference between cryobiotic and desiccated survival claims, that Dsup binds chromatin rather than "wrapping" DNA, and that the Beresheet crash survival claim is unresolved. Grade A.

Axolotl: would not finish it satisfied; it is short, has an affiliate disclosure up front, and reads like a truncated care-guide excerpt rather than a full article. Grade D (thin).

Octopus three hearts: would finish it. Learned the swimming/heart-stopping mechanism, corrected chromatophore-speed and "nine brains" myths, and the real design behind Otago's grudge-holding octopus story. Grade A.

Platypus: would finish it. Learned the venom genes are convergent rather than unique, the biofluorescence finding has since been reframed as a byproduct, and the near-absent stomach is a derived loss, not an ancestral trait. Grade A.

2. Improving the C-or-lower article

Axolotl: this is really an intro paragraph and two care-tip fragments, not a finished article. It needs the sections a reader expects from an animal profile: how it regenerates limbs (mentioned only in a bullet with no explanation), its conservation status beyond one adjective, and its life cycle. As it stands it reads like the top of a care guide with the rest cut off.

3. Title versus content gaps

Axolotl: the title promises coverage of "nature's smiling salamander" broadly, but the body only delivers a habitat sentence and water-quality tips; regeneration, arguably the animal's single most famous trait, is asserted in a bullet and never explained.

No other clear gaps: the other five articles deliver on their titles.

4. Overlap and conflicts

Platypus article and seahorse/octopus articles cross-reference the "no true stomach" trait consistently (platypus, seahorse, and 15 percent of teleost fish) with no conflict, reinforcing a real pattern across the bundle.

Pangolin trade reference in the slow loris piece (from bundle 3) is consistent with the slow loris piece here describing "the same shape of problem as the pangolin trade."

No factual conflicts found within this directory.

5. Trust

Most convincing sentence: "It binds to chromatin, preferring DNA already packaged around nucleosomes over bare strands... Part of it closely resembles a domain found in vertebrate HMGN proteins, so it is less an exotic invention than a familiar tool put to an unusual use" (tardigrade article), because it corrects a common oversimplification with a specific mechanistic detail rather than just asserting it is wrong.

Doubtful moment: the axolotl article's affiliate disclosure sitting directly under the headline, before any content, reads as commercially motivated rather than editorial, and the tank-size "convention rather than requirement" caveat is good but arrives in an otherwise thin piece that does not earn the trust the rest of the bundle builds.

6. Bundle grade

Grade A-minus. Five of six articles are dense, well-corrected, and specific. The axolotl piece drags the average down hard: it is unfinished-feeling and leads with affiliate language.

Two changes: expand the axolotl article into a full profile covering regeneration and lifecycle before publishing it, and add Deep Dive/Health and More sidebar entries across this directory's pages, since every one of the six shows "none" for both despite clear opportunities to cross-link (axolotl to a full care guide, platypus to venom pieces, etc).
