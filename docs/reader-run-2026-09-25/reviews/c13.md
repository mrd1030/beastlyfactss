## ferret

1. Page by page.
Care guide hub: A, would finish it, everything a first-week owner needs is here with working links to the numbers behind it.
Encyclopedia: B+, short, but the 1933 influenza history is real substance, not filler, even though there is little to act on.
How Much Does a Ferret Really Cost?: A, clear tables, honest about the lifespan drop, actionable sourcing advice.
How to Handle a Ferret: A, corrects the "pop the nose" myth and explains why, practical.
Common Ferret Health Issues: A, gives the wide, disputed prevalence range for adrenal disease instead of picking one number.
Ferret Cage Setup: A, size, temperature, and the ferret-proofing that actually causes GI blockages.
Ferret Feeding: A, the fast gut transit and the same-day urgency for appetite loss are the most useful facts in the set.
Ferret Enrichment: A, built around an actual observed behavior (lying awake, eyes open) instead of vague boredom advice.
Ferret Adrenal Disease: A, a real treatment comparison table with costs and timelines.

2. Conflicts.
The feeding guide and hub both give ferret kibble protein as "roughly 32 to 40%." The sidebar overview "Three Pets Whose Drama Isn't the Real Story" (sidebar/03-veiled-chameleon-ferret-hognose-overview.txt) instead says ferrets "need 35 to 40% protein, with carbohydrate under 25% and fiber under 2.5%." Same fact, two different lower bounds, and the second version drops the health issues guide's own honesty about the fat percentage being disputed too.

3. Gaps (after the sidebar check).
No guidance anywhere in the set or the linked sidebar articles for quarantining or introducing a new ferret into an existing pair, despite the hub recommending pairs from the start. Grepping the sidebar for quarantine and introduction terms turned up nothing.
No ferret-specific vet-visit or anesthesia guidance. The shared "Getting a Small Mammal to the Vet" article in the sidebar is written entirely around rabbits and guinea pigs, who cannot vomit, so its fasting rules do not transfer to a ferret, who can. Nothing fills that gap for this species.
No mention of pet-sitting or boarding for a trip.

4. Trust.
Named commercial products appear inside health content rather than a buying list: "Dietary management often starts with a solid nutritional baseline, a high-protein food like Marshall Premium Ferret Diet" sits inside the insulinoma section of the health issues guide. It reads more like product placement than a symptom-and-treatment discussion, even with the affiliate disclosure at the top.
The sidebar overview flattens a genuinely debated statistic into certainty: "a documented majority of US pet ferrets develop adrenal disease," where the species' own health guide gives a range from under 1% to 70% and calls the number disputed.

5. Set grade: A minus. The species-specific pages are thorough, well sourced, and honest about disputed numbers, which is rare. The minus is for the two things above: the protein conflict and the missing anesthesia and introduction guidance. First change: standardize the protein percentage across the feeding guide, hub, and sidebar overview. Second change: add a short ferret-specific vet-visit note (ferrets can vomit, standard fasting applies, unlike the rabbit and guinea pig guide linked from this page) and a paragraph on quarantining and introducing a second ferret.

## hedgehog

1. Page by page.
Care guide hub: A, the legal-first framing and the myth-busting on Maine and Wisconsin are useful and specific.
Encyclopedia: B+, short, but the Salmonella outbreak and the 1991 import ban are real content.
How Much Does a Hedgehog Really Cost?: A, leads with the legal check before money, which is the right order for this species.
How to Handle a Hedgehog: A, explains huffing, self-anointing, and sexing clearly.
Common Hedgehog Health Issues: A, corrects the old 10% Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome figure down to roughly 3% with a cited study.
Hedgehog Enclosure Setup: A, temperature framed correctly as the one non-negotiable item.
Hedgehog Feeding: A, the 24-hour appetite-loss rule is specific and actionable.
Hedgehog Enrichment: A, survey data (84% no hide, 68% under half a square meter, 17% no wheel) makes this concrete rather than generic.

2. Conflicts.
Legal status is the serious one. The hub and cost guide (00-care-guide-hub.txt, 02-cost-guide.txt) state plainly: "Six places will not let you keep one: California, Georgia, Hawaii, Pennsylvania, Washington DC and New York City," with New Jersey the only permit state, and add "Maine and Wisconsin are not permit states, whatever the internet says." The sidebar overview "Four Pets Decided Before You Ever Set Up the Tank" (sidebar/03-sulcata-hedgehog-lovebird-guppy-overview.txt) contradicts this on three points at once: "banned outright in California, Georgia, Hawaii, and Washington D.C., restricted in Pennsylvania and Maine, and require a permit in New Jersey and Wisconsin." That calls Pennsylvania merely restricted rather than banned, and claims Maine and Wisconsin need permits, which is the exact internet myth the hedgehog's own pages debunk.
Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome prevalence gets the same treatment. The health issues guide corrects the old figure to "closer to 3%." The same sidebar overview instead repeats "roughly 10% of captive African hedgehogs in North America," the outdated number.
Minor: lifespan is "3 to 6 years typically, some to 8 to 10" on the hub and cost guide, versus "a mean lifespan of 3 to 5 years" in the sidebar overview.

3. Gaps.
No hedgehog-specific nail-trim instructions. The hub's buy list includes nail clippers, but the shared grooming sidebar article covers only rabbits and guinea pigs.
No hedgehog-specific note on keeping a carrier warm on a vet trip, even though this set treats cold as the single biggest danger to this species, and the shared vet-visit sidebar article never mentions hedgehogs.

4. Trust.
The sidebar's confidently wrong legal and WHS numbers, quoted above, are the standout trust problem: a reader who lands on that page instead of the hedgehog's own guide could believe Pennsylvania is merely restricted or that Wisconsin requires a permit it does not.

5. Set grade: B+. The species pages are excellent and self-correcting, but the sidebar piece meant to reinforce them actively spreads the wrong legal information for a pet that is illegal in several states, which is a real-world risk, not a style quibble. First change: fix the state list and the WHS percentage in the "Four Pets Decided" sidebar article to match the hedgehog's own guide. Second change: add a nail-trim paragraph specific to hedgehogs.

## sugar-glider

1. Page by page.
Care guide hub: A, the legal check, the pair requirement, and the calcium ratio are all foregrounded correctly.
Encyclopedia: A minus, short, but the 2019 genetic tracing of the US pet population to Indonesian stock is genuinely interesting and specific.
How Much Does Sugar Glider Ownership Really Cost?: A, clear on the doubled cost of the mandatory pair.
How to Handle a Sugar Glider: A, the six-step introduction protocol for a second glider is unusually concrete.
Common Sugar Glider Health Issues: A, explicitly warns against the intuitive "just add more calcium" mistake.
Sugar Glider Cage Setup: A, the wheel-axle injury risk and the toxic-branch list are specific and actionable.
Sugar Glider Feeding: A, the thirds framework and the 2:1 calcium ratio are clear and repeatable.
Sugar Glider Enrichment: A, unusually honest: it states the self-mutilation claim outruns its evidence and explains why, without abandoning the pair recommendation.

2. Conflicts.
The encyclopedia (01-encyclopedia.txt) states wild gliders "live in groups of 10-15 individuals." The sidebar overview "Four Pets, Four Different Right Numbers" (sidebar/03-boa-glider-mantis-tetra-overview.txt) instead says "they live in groups of six to ten." Neither page is the species' actual behavior guide, but they disagree on the same natural-history fact.

3. Gaps.
No coverage of common infectious or parasitic illness beyond the first-exam fecal test mentioned in the cost guide. The health issues guide covers only diet-driven conditions (bone disease, obesity, self-mutilation, dental disease) and nothing on GI infection or parasites, and the sidebar has nothing on it either.

4. Trust.
Nothing sounds overstated or like an ad here. The set is notably careful about hedging claims it cannot fully support, which is the opposite of a trust problem.

5. Set grade: A minus. This is the most self-aware set of the four, including flagging its own weakest evidence. First change: fix the wild group-size number so the encyclopedia and the sidebar overview agree. Second change: add a short section on common non-dietary illness (parasites, GI upset) to the health issues guide.

## flying-squirrel

1. Page by page.
Care guide hub: A, correctly frames this as a wildlife-law question rather than a standard exotic-pet purchase.
Encyclopedia: A minus, short, but the 2019 Florida trafficking case is a strong, specific piece of history.
How Much Does a Pet Flying Squirrel Really Cost?: A, clearly explains why the legal picture differs from a sugar glider's.
How to Handle a Flying Squirrel: A, the age-window emphasis (6 to 8 weeks) and the loose-squirrel search steps are concrete.
Common Flying Squirrel Health Issues: A, includes real epidemic typhus case numbers rather than a vague zoonosis warning.
Flying Squirrel Cage Setup: A, clearly states how this differs from a sugar glider's setup (cooler temperature, light requirement) rather than assuming the two are interchangeable.
Flying Squirrel Feeding: A, the 80/20 rodent-block-to-produce split and the D3 toxicity warning are specific.
Flying Squirrel Enrichment: A, unusually transparent that no dedicated research exists and explains why borrowing from sugar glider care is defensible anyway.

2. Conflicts. None found.

3. Gaps.
No specific quarantine duration for introducing a new flying squirrel into an existing group. The health guide only says a new squirrel "lives in its own cage until an exotics vet has checked it," with no day count, while the sugar glider set (a close comparison this same set draws repeatedly) gives 30 days.
No real nail or tooth-length check guidance despite the health issues guide linking to the shared grooming article for exactly that ("a look for overgrowth"). That grooming article covers only rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, and chinchillas, and never mentions flying squirrels, rodent incisors, or rodent nails at all, so the link does not actually answer the question it is offered for.

4. Trust. Nothing sounds overstated or promotional. The set is careful to flag where evidence is thin (enrichment) and where a claim needs a vet's judgment (vitamin D3 dosing).

5. Set grade: A minus. Strong, well-sourced, and honest about what is and is not known for this species specifically. First change: give a specific quarantine period for a new flying squirrel, matching the specificity used for sugar gliders. Second change: either write real flying-squirrel nail and incisor guidance or stop linking to a grooming article that does not cover it.
