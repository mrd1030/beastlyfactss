## 1. What "tetra" means

**It is a common name, not one scientific group.**
- FishBase titles the Characidae family page "Family Characidae - Characins; tetras" and says: "The African tetras are recognized in the separate family Alestiidae, following Eschmeyer 1998 (Ref. 26282)." (S2) https://www.fishbase.net.br/summary/FamilySummary.php?ID=102. So even FishBase spreads "tetras" across at least two families. (Spelling note: this page has "Alestiidae"; the FishBase Congo tetra page uses "Alestidae". Use "Alestidae".)
- Merriam-Webster defines it as "any of numerous small often brightly colored South American characin fishes often bred in tropical aquariums". (S6) https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/tetra. This is narrower than the real usage (it leaves out African and Central American tetras); use it for the etymology only.

**The family reclassification (record of what each authority says):**
- Melo et al. 2024 split the old Characidae. Abstract: the data are "allowing us to propose a new classification of former Characidae into four families." The four are Spintherobolidae, Stevardiidae, Characidae and Acestrorhamphidae, and "The family Acestrorhamphidae congregates 15 subfamilies" (fetch tool). Hyphessobryconinae, Megalamphodinae and Pristellinae are among the Acestrorhamphidae subfamilies; Tetragonopterinae stays in Characidae. Per the Results (fetch tool summary), Acestrorhamphidae holds about 685 species against 203 left in Characidae. (S1) https://academic.oup.com/zoolinnean/article/202/1/zlae101/7739515
- Acestrorhamphidae is a revived name, not a new one: the family authority is Eigenmann 1907.
- FishBase has adopted it. Neon tetra: family "Acestrorhamphidae" (American tetras), subfamily Megalamphodinae. Mexican tetra: family "Acestrorhamphidae" ("American tetras"), subfamily "Acestrorhamphinae". (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Paracheirodon-innesi.html , https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Astyanax-mexicanus.html
- FishBase's older Characidae family page has not been updated: "Genera : 38 | Species : 207", "A comprehensive phylogenetic study is needed," and no mention of Acestrorhamphidae. (S2) https://www.fishbase.net.br/summary/FamilySummary.php?ID=102
- ITIS still places Hyphessobrycon amandae in family "Characidae", order "Characiformes". (S11) https://itis.gov/servlet/SingleRpt/SingleRpt?search_topic=TSN&search_value=639893
- Seriously Fish still calls the neon tetra "a small characid from the Amazon basin in Colombia, Brazil and Peru". (S10) https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/paracheirodon-innesi/
- Gonzalez et al. 2024 also still refer to the neon tetra as a member of Characidae (fetch tool summary). (S3)
- Dictionary.com: "any of several tropical, freshwater fishes of the family Characidae, often kept in aquariums." (S7) https://www.dictionary.com/browse/tetra
- Safe wording for the intro: tetras are small characins (order Characiformes) spread across several families; most American tetras now sit in Acestrorhamphidae or Characidae, African tetras in Alestidae.

**Where the word comes from.**
- Merriam-Webster: "by shortening from New Latin Tetragonopterus, former genus name, from Late Latin tetragonum quadrangle + Greek pteron wing". First known use: "1931". (S6) https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/tetra
- Dictionary.com: dated 1930 to 35 (the original writes the range with a dash), then "shortening of New Latin Tetragonopterus former genus name." (S7) https://www.dictionary.com/browse/tetra
- Agreement: both say "tetra" is short for the old genus name Tetragonopterus. The genus still exists (Tetragonopterinae is a Characidae subfamily in S1).
- Do NOT use the "four unpaired fins" explanation of the name. It appears in Wikipedia and hobby pages but no dictionary supports it; both dictionaries derive the word from the genus name.

## 2. Where tetras come from

- Grzimek's: Characiformes "are widely distributed in Central America, South America, and Africa" (over 1,300 species in South America, at least 176 in Africa, per the fetch tool). (S9) https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/characiformes-characins
- FishBase Characidae range: "Southwestern United States to northern Patagonia in Argentina". (S2) https://www.fishbase.net.br/summary/FamilySummary.php?ID=102
- Mexican tetra distribution: "North America: Mexico." (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Astyanax-mexicanus.html
- Congo tetra: English name "Congo tetra", family "Alestidae (African tetras)", distribution "Africa: lower and middle Congo River basin in Democratic Republic of the Congo and Republic of Congo." (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Phenacogrammus-interruptus.html
- Habitat, neon tetra: "Blackwater or clearwater stream tributaries of the Solimões River." (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Paracheirodon-innesi.html
- Seriously Fish (neon): water "stained brownish due to the presence of humic substances", fish also collected in "transparent, unstained clearwater", water "typically acidic, of negligible carbonate hardness and conductivity". (S10) https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/paracheirodon-innesi/
- Rio Negro: "a naturally acidic, blackwater tributary of the Amazon River"; "the waters have exceptionally low concentrations of ions"; organic acids "acidify the river and produce the characteristic tea color in the water." Characiforms "are the most species-rich group in the river", "which represents 39% of all species in the river". (S3) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11486802

## 3. Why they school

- Neon tetra, predator context: with a robotic predator present, "fish preferred to locate in the middle of the aggregation to favour dilution effects." With no stimulus, fish stayed toward the back to "exploit the increased likelihood of predator detection by conspecifics swimming ahead." Species described as "a highly social fish species." (S4) https://iris.santannapisa.it/handle/11382/536590
- Neon tetra, predator vs food: "The longest shoaling duration was observed when a biomimetic predator was present" and "the shortest shoaling duration was recorded in presence of food"; conclusion: "heterogeneous shoals are promoted by the anti-predator benefits, and reduced by competition". Study species: "the neon tetra Paracheirodon innesi was selected as ideal model organism". (S8) https://iris.santannapisa.it/handle/11382/545491
- Takeaway for the intro: in neon tetras the school is chiefly a predator defense (safety in the middle, more eyes ahead). In this work food did not hold the school together; it shortened shoaling because of competition. So do not claim tetras school "to find food" on this evidence.
- Grzimek's: "Many species travel in schools under certain conditions." (S9)
- Seriously Fish keeping advice: "a mixed-sex group of at least 8-10 specimens"; other schooling fishes "provide security". (S10) FishBase: groups of five or more for neon and Congo tetras (fetch tool). (S2)

## 4. Why they want soft, acidic water

- Neon tetra water in FishBase: "pH range: 5.0 - 7.0; dH range: 1 - 2." (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Paracheirodon-innesi.html. Seriously Fish gives a wider aquarium range, pH 4.0 to 7.5, 1 to 12 dGH, and for breeding "soft and acidic in the range pH 5.5-6.5, gH 1-5". (S10)
- Native water is acidic, ion-poor and tea-colored (S3, quoted in topic 2).
- Physiology: in neon tetras, "Na+ uptake was completely unaffected by transfer to low pH water (down to pH 3.25" (Gonzalez and Preest 1999, as reported in the review). The review's abstract: "pH-insensitive transport was a trait shared by many, if not most, species in the Order Characiformes". (S3) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11486802
- Careful framing: S3 shows these fish are built to tolerate soft, acidic water and keep their salts balanced in it. It does not show they need very low pH in an aquarium. Keep the intro claim to "they come from soft, acidic water and do best in water that resembles it".
- Exception worth knowing: the Congo tetra's FishBase range is "pH range: 6.0 - 8.0; dH range: 5 - 19". (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Phenacogrammus-interruptus.html. "Soft and acidic" fits the South American blackwater species, not every tetra. Scope the sentence ("most South American tetras").

## 5. Did you know? candidates

**A. The blind Mexican cave tetra is a tetra.** (best candidate)
- FishBase: Astyanax mexicanus, English name "Mexican tetra", family "Acestrorhamphidae" ("American tetras"). (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Astyanax-mexicanus.html
- Jeffery 2009: "The teleost Astyanax mexicanus is a single species consisting of two radically different forms." "At least 30 different populations of Astyanax cavefish are present in limestone caverns in Mexico." "cavefish and surface fish are completely interfertile." "Although functional eyes are lacking in adults, cavefish embryos begin to develop eye primordia" which "subsequently degenerate." (S5) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3594791
- FAU 2021 (Sifuentes-Romero et al., Journal of Experimental Zoology Part B): "at least 29 populations of a blind, cave-dwelling form"; the data "support the hypothesis that these two cavefish populations evolved eye loss independently." (S12) https://www.fau.edu/research/fau-research-daily/2021/eye-loss-in-mexican-cavefish/index.php
- Disagreement: population count "at least 30" (S5, 2009) vs "at least 29" (S12, 2021). Say "about 30" or "dozens of caves".
- Draft line: "The blind cavefish sold as a curiosity is a tetra too: the Mexican tetra, whose cave populations hatch with eyes that then degenerate."

**B. Neon tetras keep their salts balanced in water as acidic as pH 3.25.** (S3, quoted in topic 4). Strong but more technical; fits better on a water-chemistry page than in a two-sentence intro.

**C. The adipose fin.** Grzimek's: "Most species have an adipose fin, a short fin between the dorsal and caudal fins." (S9). FishBase Characidae: "Adipose fin may be present or lacking." (S2). Usable as "most tetras carry a small extra fin on the back near the tail", scoped with "most". Not tied to the word's origin (see topic 1).

## Gaps

- **Foraging benefit of schooling in small characins:** no opened source tests it in tetras. The classic shoal-size foraging study (Pitcher, Magurran and Winfield 1982, "Fish in larger shoals find food faster", Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology 10: 149 to 151, DOI 10.1007/BF00300175) is on minnows and goldfish, and its publisher page would not open, so it is NOT verified and must not be cited. The neon tetra work (S8) points the other way: food shortened shoaling. Drop the foraging reason from the intro.
- **Eschmeyer's Catalog of Fishes position on Acestrorhamphidae:** the classification and species-by-family pages returned 403. Several 2025 species descriptions indexed by the catalog place Hyphessobrycon, Moenkhausia and Priocharax in Acestrorhamphidae (seen in search results only), which suggests acceptance, but the catalog's own classification page was not opened. Not recorded as verified.
- **Encyclopaedia Britannica** ("tetra" and "characin" entries) returned 403 both times; nothing taken from it.
- **Why tetras are colorful / the neon stripe** (Ikeda and Kohshima 2009 "mirror-image decoy" hypothesis): seen only in a search summary, not opened. Not used.
- **Exact family split of all fish called "tetra"** (Lebiasinidae, Stevardiidae and others): Wikipedia lists them, but Wikipedia is not citable and the claim was not confirmed in S1's opened text beyond the four-family split. If the intro needs more than "several families", this needs another source.
- **"Tetras have four unpaired fins, hence the name":** unsupported folk etymology. Dropped.
