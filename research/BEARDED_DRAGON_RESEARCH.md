## 1. Identity and size

1.1 Species and common name
- Australian Museum: "Central Bearded Dragon". (B4)
- Baines et al. 2016: the species table lists Pogona vitticeps. (B1)

1.2 The beard
- Australian Museum: the beard is the lower jaw (the skin under it), "puffed out" when alarmed; mature males blacken it to signal dominance. (B4) Behavior use is in 6.1.

1.3 Adult size and age at adulthood
- No reliable source found for adult sizing. Owner (2026-11-06): no reliable source found; the cost guide FAQ's "reach adult size within a year or so" is kept as written (see "Kept without a source"). The site's growth and weight guide puts adult at "18 months and up".
- The ReptiFiles 2020 care sheet PDF gives adults from 12 months (set aside, see 4.9).

## 2. Wild habitat and trade

2.1 Range and habitat
- Australian Museum: habitat "temperate to tropical arid to semi-arid woodland, shrubland and hummock grassland" across the semi-arid interior of eastern Australia; "an adept climber, and can usually be found on tree stumps, tree branches, boulders and fence posts"; basks from the elevated position. (B4, verbatim page read 2026-11-06; merged with the 2026-11-06 FunFact pass, which recorded the same climbing line and "woodland and shrubland across the semi-arid interior of eastern Australia, basking from the elevated position")

2.2 Wild-caught vs captive-bred, and the export ban
- VCA Owning Bearded Dragons (verbatim page read): "Choose a young, captive-bred animal when possible, and ask the seller about the dragon's age, diet, and health history." and "Older, wild-caught animals may be harder to tame, may harbor parasites, and may suffer from the stress of captivity." (B12)
- Australian Veterinary Association, Exotic pet and wildlife trade policy (verbatim page read): "the ban on the export of live native Australian mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians for commercial purposes", under the EPBC Act 1999. (B3) So a US buyer does not meet a wild-caught dragon.
- History (2026-11-06): the cost guide said "An older, wild-caught animal is harder to tame, more likely to carry intestinal parasites, and often stressed by captivity" (VCA Owning says this of older wild-caught animals). Owner (2026-11-06): the cost guide keeps the money angle (an older adult with no health history is the costly one); the wild-caught comparison stays on the site in the reptile quarantine guide; the health guide's parasite section gained one sentence on adults with no health history. The export ban stays off the care guides; if used, it belongs in the encyclopedia entry, sourced to the Australian government's EPBC Act page.

2.3 Wild sun and shade (search summaries only, not opened, not citable)
- Australian summer midday UV index regularly above 12 to 14 (a 2024 Frontiers review).
- Wild central bearded dragons move between sunny spots and shade (EurekAlert release on a tracking study).
- What the opened source says on wild UV exposure is in 4.3 (Baines 2016).

## 4. Enclosure, heat and UVB

4.1 Enclosure size
- ReptiFiles 2020 care sheet PDF: 6x2x2 minimum (set aside; the site keeps 4x2x2 ft, see 4.9).
- No opened source in this file states the guides' 4x2x2 ft adult minimum or the "three times nose-to-vent length" rule (see Gaps).

4.2 Basking temperature and basking surface
- ReptiFiles Temperatures and UVB (verbatim page read): basking temperatures should "cover an area at least as large as the dragon's entire body" (including tail); "a light-colored stone basking surface should be provided". It gives no platform dimensions. (B24)
- RSPCA (verbatim page read): "Place a natural stone in the hot end"; the dragon's back no closer than "25 to 30cm from the lamp". (B21)
- VCA Bearded Dragons Housing (fetch summary, not verbatim): "Large rocks that are easy enough to climb on and around allow for basking". (B12)
- ReptiFiles 2020 care sheet PDF: 105 to 115F basking (set aside, see 4.9).
- Owner firsthand (2026-11-06): Dex has arthritis and basks on a low slate on a riser with a stronger bulb. The owner chose to keep that out of the guides.

4.3 UVB zone (Ferguson zones) and wild exposure
- Baines et al. 2016, Table 1 (PDF text read): Zone 3 "Open or partial sun basker", zone range UVI 1.0 to 2.6, maximum 2.9 to 7.4; Zone 4 "Mid-day sun basker", 2.6 to 3.5, maximum 4.5 to 9.5. The species table lists Pogona vitticeps as zone 3 to 4. (B1)
- History (2026-11-06, FunFact pass): the tank setup FunFact's "full, direct desert sun" was contradicted by this table and rewritten to the 3 to 9.5 maximum range.
- Open: the site's UVB lighting guide lists bearded dragons under Zone 3 only.

4.4 UVB decay and replacement
- Baines et al. 2016, lamp tests (PDF text read): UV-B "decays with use, primarily due to solarisation of the glass envelope"; seven Arcadia and Zoo Med tubes lost a mean 39.9% of UVI from new after 4000 hours (about a year), range 30 to 48%; one other brand lost 64% after 1000 hours (about three months). (B1)
- VCA Bearded Dragons Housing (fetch summary, not verbatim): UV output "decreases with age, so they should be replaced every six months or as directed by the manufacturer, even if they still produce visible light". (B12) Desktop check needed for the visible-light line.
- History (2026-11-06, FunFact pass): the cost guide's UVB FunFact said a tube can glow "for months after it's stopped producing usable UV output". Rewritten to the Baines figures. "Even while it still lights up" rests on the VCA Housing line above, read from a fetch summary, not verbatim; confirm on the desktop.

4.5 UVB through glass or plastic
- VCA Bearded Dragons Housing (fetch summary, not verbatim): UVB must reach the pet without glass or plastic between. (B12) This is the tank setup guide's sourced line that the enrichment guide's window glass note rests on (see 6.4).

4.6 T5 tube lengths and wattages
- Zoo Med ReptiSun 5.0 and 10.0 T5 HO product pages (search summary only): 12 in 15 W, 22 in 24 W, 34 in 39 W, 46 in 54 W. (B25)

4.7 Water dish
- RSPCA (verbatim page read): "you should provide a large, shallow dish with clean, fresh water at all times in the cool end", replaced "at least daily". (B21) Added to the tank setup guide's Sources 2026-11-06.
- ReptiFiles Temperatures and UVB (verbatim page read): "it's good practice to keep a shallow water dish". (B24)
- VCA Bearded Dragons Housing (fetch summary, not verbatim): fresh water in a sturdy dish that will not tip. (B12)

4.8 Humidity and substrate
- VCA Owning Bearded Dragons does not mention impaction or substrate. (B12)
- ReptiFiles 2020 care sheet PDF: 30 to 60% humidity, sand substrate (set aside, see 4.9).
- No opened source in this file states the guides' 30 to 40% humidity or the loose-substrate impaction line (see Gaps).
- Bioactive floor: owner firsthand (cost guide): in his dragon's 4 by 2 foot enclosure, two bags of topsoil were enough. Play sand bag volume is a price observation (section 10).

4.9 ReptiFiles 2020 care sheet PDF (owner upload, read): reviewed and set aside
- Owner (2026-11-06): do nothing with it and keep the site's own figures. Recorded only so it is not re-raised. Main differences from the site: 6x2x2 minimum (site 4x2x2 ft), 105 to 115F basking (site 95 to 110°F for adults), 30 to 60% humidity (site 30 to 40%), sand substrate (site avoids loose sand), adults from 12 months (site 18 months and up).

## 5. Diet and feeding

5.1 Feeding frequency by age
- NC State (B8): "<1 month old; Feed 2-3x daily", "1 - 4 months old: Feed 2x daily", "4 months old - adulthood: Feed once daily", "Adult dragons can be fed everyday or every other day".
- LafeberVet (B11), same tiers: "<1 month old: Feed 2-3 times daily", "1-4 months old: Feed twice daily", "4 months to adult: Feed once daily", "Adult: Feed every 1-2 days".
- VCA Bearded Dragons Feeding (B12, live text): "Most young, bearded dragons should eat once or twice daily", "Adult dragons may only eat every 24 to 72 hours".
- Avian & Exotic Animal Hospital of Louisiana (B15): "Bearded dragons need to eat daily for the first 10-12 months of life", "After 10 months of age, many bearded dragons convert from eating a meal every day to eating every 48-72 hours", "As long as your adult bearded dragon remains active and eats every 2-3 days he/she should be ok." Not in Sources (cap); supports the day-or-two-is-normal line.
- Chicago Exotics (B17): hatchlings "two to three times a day" up to two months, no brumation.
- PetMD "What Do Bearded Dragons Eat?" (Sean Perry DVM): hatchlings "two to three times daily", fruit "no more than 5%"; left out as a Chewy property.
- Hatchlings: NC State, LafeberVet, Chicago Exotics and PetMD all give 2 to 3 feedings a day, so the guide's 2 to 3 is sourced; the old "3 to 5 feedings a day" was removed on the first pass (owner call stands, see "Kept without a source").
- History (2026-11-06, sweep): the feeding guide's schedule contradicted itself (juveniles "once daily, or once to twice daily" then a salad "every day" and "every other day is the minimum"; adults "once daily or every other day, roughly every 24 to 72 hours" next to "greens daily"). Now: juveniles insects once or twice daily plus a daily salad (every other day at the least); adults a salad daily and insects a few times a week. The adult "every 24 to 72 hours" figure came out with the contradiction; FAQ 1 and the hub's Feeding schedule row follow the guide.

5.2 Diet proportions and fruit
- NC State (B8): diet "50% dark leafy greens, 20% grated or chopped vegetables, 25% animal matter" and "a maximum of 5% fruits"; "Fruits should be limited in the diet, as they are high in sugar."
- LafeberVet (B11): "Fruit should make up no more than 5% of the diet".
- VCA Bearded Dragons Feeding (B12): "about 50% plant-based and 50% animal-based (insect) material", "Fruit should comprise only 10% to 20% of the plants fed to bearded dragons." With plants at about half the diet that is 5 to 10% of the whole diet, so the guide's old "5 to 10%" was not wrong; it now says aim for 5% or less and never past 10%, which matches VCA, NC State and LafeberVet together.
- Whyte Ridge (B13) says "Fruits should be avoided" outright (see "Possible contradictions").
- PetMD: fruit "no more than 5%" (not citable, see 5.1).

5.3 Feeder insects: mealworms and superworms
- NC State (B8) lists an "occasional super/mealworm" from 1 to 4 months and super/mealworms in the 4 months to adulthood diet.
- Owner (2026-11-03): follow NC State, and note superworms are high in fat. The feeding guide says occasional from about a month old, never daily; the safe foods guide's rows match it (third pass, 2026-11-03), with "fatty" dropped from the mealworm row (owner, 2026-11-03) and superworms keeping "high in fat".

5.4 Rodents (no mice)
- VCA Bearded Dragons Feeding (verbatim page read, 2026-11-06): "Pinkie mice or young 'fuzzy' mice may be fed to larger bearded dragons, but sparingly". (B12) NC State's page returned 503 when checked (2026-11-06).
- Owner (2026-11-06): no mice for bearded dragons anyway; the site does not suggest mice for bearded dragons; snakes and larger reptiles are fine. The feeding guide's pinky line came out, and the safe foods guide now says pinky mice and other rodents stay off the menu, with insects covering protein at every age. A house rule, not a sourced claim.

5.5 Toxic foods
- Merck Veterinary Manual, Avocado (Persea spp) Toxicosis in Animals: persin. (B5)
- Knight and colleagues 1999, Firefly Toxicosis in Lizards: fireflies. (B2)
- LafeberVet (B11): "Avoid lightning bugs, as they can be toxic."

5.6 Food refusal: how long before a vet
- NC State (B8): seek an exotic vet if the dragon becomes lethargic or "stops eating for more than 1-2 days (adults only)". That line is the origin of the feeding guide's old "more than a day or two" threshold, which the owner overruled on 2026-11-03 (a 1 to 2 day refusal is normal).
- Texas Avian & Exotic Hospital (B14): "Not every case of a bearded dragon not eating is cause for alarm" and, for a vet visit, when it "persists for more than a week (or sooner for younger dragons)". Brumation is listed as a normal reason for appetite change, no figures.
- South Pasadena Animal Hospital (B16): "give it a few days to a week before panicking", vet visit at "two or more weeks without eating when it's not brumation season". Not in Sources (cap).
- Avian & Exotic Animal Hospital of Louisiana (B15): adults eating "every 2-3 days" should be ok (5.1).
- Owner decision (2026-11-03): the feeding guide matches the health guide and the emergency card, not the other way around. `bearded-dragon-health-issues-guide.mdx` and the hub's emergency card (`src/lib/data/guides/lizards.js`) call an adult refusing food for 1 to 2 weeks outside brumation a call-now sign. Texas Avian ("more than a week") is the low end and South Pasadena ("two or more weeks") the high end of that 1 to 2 week threshold.
- Babies and juveniles: the guides say 2 to 3 days at most; no vet page gives 2 to 3 days (see "Kept without a source").

## 6. Behavior and handling

6.1 Beard and arm-waving
- Australian Museum (verbatim page read, 2026-11-06): the beard is "puffed out" when alarmed and blackened by mature males to signal dominance; arm-waving: males show submission to a dominant male, which "generally defuses the confrontation", and females show responsiveness to a male with a slow head bob. (B4)
- History (2026-11-06, FunFact pass): the handling guide's arm-wave FunFact said "Nobody is entirely sure why"; the Australian Museum states it, so it was rewritten. The health guide's beard FunFact and the enrichment guide's climbing FunFact also cite the Australian Museum.

6.2 Taming and choosing a dragon
- VCA Owning Bearded Dragons (verbatim page read): "Choose a young, captive-bred animal when possible, and ask the seller about the dragon's age, diet, and health history."; "Older, wild-caught animals may be harder to tame". (B12; full quotes in 2.2)

6.3 Handling session length
- The enrichment guide said free-roam sessions build "toward 15 to 30 minutes for some adults"; the handling guide caps sessions near 10 to 15 minutes because a dragon cools down. Owner (2026-11-06): session length is set by heat.
- Owner firsthand (2026-11-06): roams his dragons for an hour or so in window sun under a heat lamp, checked with a temp gun; they go back when they wander off to poop or sleep. The enrichment guide now carries that as its firsthand beat (15 to 30 minutes removed), with the note that window glass blocks UVB (the tank setup guide's sourced line, 4.5) and that time out does no harm with a good setup to return to. The handling guide says 10 to 15 minutes in an ordinary room, longer with a heat lamp.

6.4 Claw trimming
- Owner firsthand (2026-11-06): trims Dex and Cera with baby clippers (in the handling guide).
- The rest of the claw section has no source since The Vet Desk came out (see "Kept without a source").

6.5 Glass surfing
- Hepper (B27, hobby) is the only source behind the enrichment guide's cause list: undersized enclosure, wrong temperatures, reflections, boredom, hunger, a gravid female. Kept under the under-3 exception (RULES, no hobby sites; owner 2026-11-06): removing it and BeardedDragon.org leaves RSPCA alone. A ReptiFiles page would replace it if it states the same causes (desktop check). It comes out the day a non-hobby source backs its claims and the count stays at 3.

## 7. Health

7.1 Atadenovirus (ADV)
- VCA Bearded Dragons Diseases (verbatim page read, 2026-11-06): "Atadenovirus infection is very common in young bearded dragons but may affect dragons of any age."; "It typically causes fatal hepatitis (infection of the liver) and gastrointestinal infection."; "Affected animals are weak, do not eat, and often die acutely suddenly." The page does not cover carriers. (B12)
- ReptiFiles Atadenovirus (ADV) (verbatim page read, 2026-11-06): "no symptoms (carrier)"; "it's best to assume that yours is at least a carrier, which means it will have mild to no symptoms". (B23) Backs the health guide's "typically" fatal and carrier lines (changed 2026-11-06).
- The Merck page for reptile infectious diseases returned 404. (B5)

7.2 Yellow fungus disease
- WSU Veterinary Teaching Hospital (verbatim page read; dated January 1, 2026, by Communications staff, College of Veterinary Medicine): "The fungus can spread between reptiles."; signs "Yellow to brown crusted skin", "Thickened or swollen scales"; "Prognosis is often guarded to grave."; "Treatment often requires aggressive and prolonged therapy." It names Nannizziopsis guarroi and never uses "CANV". (B7)
- Open item: the health guide heading still says "Yellow Fungus Disease (CANV)".

7.3 Internal parasites and the first vet visit
- VCA Owning Bearded Dragons (verbatim page read, 2026-11-06): "Many bearded dragons, including those bred in captivity, may have pinworms or other intestinal parasites."; a health guarantee "may require an examination within 48 hours"; "A microscopic examination of a fecal sample may be recommended". (B12)
- Health guide parasite section gained one sentence on adults with no health history (owner, 2026-11-06; see 2.2).

7.4 Quarantine
- Merck (MSD) Veterinary Manual (search summary only, not opened): reptile quarantine 3 to 6 months. (B5)
- WikiVet (search summary only, not opened): 90 to 180 days. (B6)
- 30 to 90 days appears only on hobby pages, as a minimum.
- Backs the cost guide's "three to six month" quarantine line (the handling and health guides carry the same line, linked to the reptile quarantine guide). Neither page was opened, so neither is citable yet.

7.5 Pre-brumation vet visit
- Health guide (added 2026-11-06 so the hub's Vet row matches its source): cited to VCA "Brumation in Bearded Dragons", added to the guide's VCA entry. Needs a desktop check that the page states a pre-brumation exam. VCA's brumation page had no figures when read on 2026-11-03 (8.1).

7.6 Food refusal thresholds in the health guide
- See 5.6. The health guide's vet list: a juvenile refusing food for 2 to 3 days; an adult refusing food for 1 to 2 weeks outside brumation.

## 8. Brumation

8.1 Duration and monitoring
- Whyte Ridge Veterinary Hospital (B13): "Brumation usually lasts around 2 months", "Weigh them with a gram scale once a week- they should not lose a significant amount of weight while brumating", and the vet-visit list: "losing 10% or more of their pre-brumation body weight, brumation lasting longer than 4 months, and no return of appetite when they start moving around consistently." Added to the feeding guide's Sources (2026-11-03) and the brumation guide's.
- VCA "Brumation in Bearded Dragons" (live text read 2026-11-03): no figures. (B12)
