## 1. Identity

1.1 Scientific name and authority
- "Hyphessobrycon amandae Géry and Uj, 1987". Status "valid", record credibility "verified - standards met". Source: ITIS (S3). https://itis.gov/servlet/SingleRpt/SingleRpt?search_topic=TSN&search_value=639893
- Seriously Fish gives the same authority: "Géry & Uj, 1987". (S1) https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/hyphessobrycon-amandae/
- FishBase: authority Géry & Uj, 1987. (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376

1.2 Naming story
- ETYFish: "in honor of Amanda Bleher (1910-1991)". The entry adds (paraphrased by the fetch tool) that she was the mother of ornamental fish wholesaler Heiko Bleher, who collected the holotype, and that the name recognizes her interest in and knowledge of Brazil's freshwater fauna and flora. (S4) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- FishBase eponymy: the species honors Amanda Bleher, a German limnologist, whose son Heiko Bleher collected the holotype. Search-result text from the same FishBase page: "Amanda Flora HIlda Bleher née Kiel (1910-1991) was a German limnologist and adventurer". (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Fishkeeper (UK): "are named in honour of Heiko's mother, hence the 'ae' ending rather than 'i.'" (S19) https://www.fishkeeper.co.uk/stories/Heiko-Bleher. Note: this page does not itself name her as Amanda; S2 and S4 do.
- Genus meaning. FishBase: "Greek, hyphesson, -on, -on = a little smaller + Greek, bryko = to bite". (S2). ETYFish: "hyphḗssōn (ὑφήσσων), smaller, allusion not explained, probably referring to their size (described at 26-42 mm)" and the "-brycon" element from brýchō, "to bite, gnash teeth or eat greedily" (paraphrased). (S4)

1.3 Family (sources disagree: this is a recent reclassification, not an error)
- FishBase: "Acestrorhamphidae (American tetras)", subfamily Hyphessobryconinae. (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Wikipedia, citing Catalog of Fishes: "belonging to the family Acestrorhamphidae, the American characins." (S14)
- ITIS: Characidae. (S3). Seriously Fish: "Characidae". (S1). GBIF: Characidae. (S5)
- Reading: older and slower-updating databases still say Characidae; FishBase and the Catalog of Fishes (via Wikipedia) now place it in Acestrorhamphidae. Owner to decide which the site uses; the existing neon/cardinal entries should be checked for consistency.

1.4 Original description
- GBIF "publishedIn": "Géry, J.; Uj, A. (1987). The ember tetra: a new pygmy characid tetra from the Rio das Mortes, Brazil, Hyphessobrycon amandae sp. n. (Pisces, Characoidei). Tropical Fish Hobbyist. 35 (5)(Jan.): 58-61, 65. [Possibly published in December 1986.]" (S5, read via API, see note above)

1.5 Trade names
- "This species is also traded under the names 'Amanda's', 'dwarf red', 'red', and 'fire' tetra." (S1)

## 2. Origin and wild habitat

- Distribution: "South America: Araguaia River basin." (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Type locality and river: "Rio das Mortes, some 100 km before its confluence with the Braço Maior of the Rio Araguaia". Country: Brazil. Type locality given as the Rio das Mortes in Mato Grosso (paraphrase of the page). (S1)
- Habitat type: "presumably inhabits minor tributaries, backwaters and oxbow lakes rather than main river channels". Water: "soft, weakly acidic water". Seriously Fish opens that section with "Little information appears to exist". (S1)
- Field account, Araguaia basin near Aruanã: "Aruanã sits on the banks of the mighty Araguaia River in the state of Goiás." "Currently, there is a dearth of information regarding the habitats of the ember tetra (Hyphessobrycon amandae)." (S6) https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/finding-ember-tetras-in-brazil/
- Water clarity and light at that site: "The water was clear, and there was little overhead cover meaning plenty of light hit the water." The author "slipped into the warm water". No temperature, pH or hardness readings were taken. (S6)
- Plants at that site (paraphrase): Eichhornia diversifolia, Cabomba furcata, Ludwigia inclinata, Ludwigia sedoides, flooded grasses; central channel mostly plant-free with leaf litter over a yellow clay-like substrate mixed with fine gravel and sand. (S6)
- Seasonal drying: "She also explained that in the summer, the water pretty much vanishes." (S6)
- Basin richness: "The Araguaia River is home to over 800 species of fish." (S6)
- DISAGREEMENT on water color: AquaInfo says the water is often very dark from tannins with tree-lined banks (paraphrase). (S12) https://aquainfo.nl/en/article/hyphessobrycon-amandae-ember-tetra/. The one first-hand field account (S6) found clear water. Seriously Fish (S1) says only "soft, weakly acidic". Both may be true across the basin; nothing measured.
- Error to ignore: The Shrimp Farm's header lists origin as "Southern China", but its body says "Ember tetras are naturally found in Brazil, specifically in the Araguaia River Basin." (S10)

## 3. Size, sexing, lifespan

3.1 Adult size
- "20mm SL" (standard length, snout to base of tail, so excludes the tail fin). (S1)
- "Max length : 2.0 cm SL male/unsexed." (S2)
- Aquarium Co-Op: "This species only grows up to 0.8 inch (2 cm)". (S8)
- Fishkeeper: "2cm (0.8")". (S9). Practical Fishkeeping: "2cm/0.8"". (S7)
- AquaInfo: "grows to no more than about 2 to 3 centimeters, including the tail." (S12). Wikipedia (uncited): about 2 cm (0.8 in) to 3 cm (1.2 in). (S14)
- Flowgrow: males to 2 cm, females to 3 cm ("Länge der Männchen: 0 - 2 cm", "Länge der Weibchen: 0 - 3 cm"). (S13)
- Summary for writers: 2 cm (0.8 in) standard length is the referenced maximum; total length with tail runs to about 2.5 to 3 cm (1 to 1.2 in) in the lower-tier sources.

3.2 Sexing
- "Adult males are more intensely-coloured, especially when in spawning condition"; females noticeably rounder-bodied (paraphrase). (S1)
- "Males are slender in profile, while females have rounder bodies, especially when viewed from above." (S8)

3.3 Lifespan in captivity (no authoritative source found; all figures are hobby-tier)
- Aquarium Source: "The typical Ember Tetra lifespan is between 2 and 4 years." It also says reports of up to ten years are "widely considered to be inaccurate." (S11)
- AquaInfo: "can live for a maximum of about 2 to 3 years" in a well-kept aquarium. (S12)
- Wikipedia: "have been known to live ten years or more", with no citation. (S14). Do not use.
- Seriously Fish, FishBase, Aquarium Co-Op and Fishkeeper give no lifespan.

3.4 Wild lifespan: no source found.

- The Shrimp Farm: "a 10-gallon (long) aquarium should be enough for a small school". (S10)
- Aquarium Source: "The ideal tank size for Ember Tetras is 10 gallons at a minimum." (S11)
- Aquarium Co-Op: "you have the flexibility to keep them in a 5-gallon nano tank with a smaller school". (S8)

## 6. Diet

- Wild diet: "Probably a micropredator feeding on tiny invertebrates and other zooplankton in nature." Note the "probably": nobody has studied gut contents. (S1)
- AquaInfo repeats this: "a micropredator that feeds on small invertebrates and other zooplankton." (S12). Wikipedia (uncited) says small invertebrates and plants. (S14). Aquarium Co-Op calls them omnivores eating zooplankton, small invertebrates and plant matter (paraphrase). (S8)
- Captive staples: "dried foods of a suitable size" plus "should also be offered daily meals of small live and frozen fare", with Artemia nauplii and Moina named (paraphrase for the species list). (S1)
- Fishkeeper: "Flake, granules and frozen foods"; small frozen foods named as "cyclops, baby brineshrimp, and daphnia". (S9)
- The Shrimp Farm: "a high-quality daily microcarnivore staple", supplemented with frozen bloodworms and live Daphnia (paraphrase). (S10)
- Food size: "they do have little mouths that prefer to feed on tiny, slow-sinking foods." (S8)
- Feeding frequency: Aquarium Source: "Aquarists have had success feeding Ember Tetras anywhere from 2-4 times a day" (recommends three); its own FAQ says "once or twice daily". (S11). Seriously Fish implies at least daily live or frozen meals (S1). No stronger source found on frequency.

## 7. Behavior and tankmates

- Temperament: "Very peaceful and will not compete well with very boisterous or much larger tankmates." (S1)
- Good companions: "similarly-sized, peaceful characids", plus small catfish and calm surface dwellers (paraphrase), and "an ideal dither fish for Apistogramma spp. and other dwarf cichlids". (S1)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "only combine with other tiny fish and invertebrates". (S7)
- Aquarium Co-Op: they "do fine with any similar-sized, peaceful animals that won't eat them"; bettas and dwarf gouramis may be territorial (paraphrase). Also: "ember tetras are relatively outgoing, especially in large groups". (S8)
- Fishkeeper: "model citizens" that "can be kept alongside a range of community species including shrimps." (S9)
- Fin nipping: no source found that says embers nip fins, and none that says they do not. Record as no source found.
- Shrimp: Aquarium Co-Op: "While they will leave the adult shrimp alone, almost all fish will opportunistically go after baby shrimp". (S8). The Shrimp Farm: "yes, ember tetras make excellent tankmates for both Neocaridina and Caridina dwarf shrimp!", with dense plants protecting shrimplets (paraphrase). (S10)
- Nano tanks: see 5.1 for the disagreement.
- Swimming level: Wikipedia (uncited) says they "swim at the middle level of the aquarium". (S14). Not citable alone.
- Mouth-size rule: Aquarium Co-Op's "won't eat them" (S8) is the only direct statement. No source gives a numeric mouth-size rule for this species. The wild predator at the field site was the pike cichlid (S6).

## 8. Health

8.1 Common diseases
- Aquarium Source: "there aren't any species-specific diseases that plague the Ember Tetra", with poor diet and water quality raising general risk (paraphrase). (S11)
- No species-specific disease literature found.

8.2 Ich and heat treatment at 86°F (IMPORTANT for the shared ich guide link)
- VCA: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF." and "Not all fish can stand the heat, though." Parasite replication is faster in warmer water: "they replicate faster in warmer water." (S15) https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/fish-white-spot-disease-ich
- Ember tetra temperature ceilings: 28°C (82°F) in Seriously Fish, FishBase, Practical Fishkeeping, Fishkeeper and Aquarium Co-Op (S1, S2, S7, S8, S9); 84°F in Aquarium Source (S11); only Flowgrow reaches 30°C (86°F) (S13).
- No critical thermal maximum (CTmax) or lethal-temperature study for H. amandae was found. Unlike the cardinal tetra guide (which cites a measured 50% mortality near 33.7°C), there is no measured ceiling for this species.
- Plain reading for the owner: 86°F is 4°F above the top of every main reference's range. No source documents that embers tolerate 86°F. The ich heat method cannot be stated as safe for this species from the sources found; it should not be written the way the cardinal guide writes it.

8.3 Neon tetra disease (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis)
- Sanders, Watral and Kent (2012): the parasite "has been reported from many species of aquarium fishes in several families, including Danio rerio and Danio nigrofasciatus"; it is called neon tetra disease after its type host, the neon tetra Paracheirodon innesi (paraphrase). The paper does not mention H. amandae. (S16) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4117576
- Ember tetra susceptibility specifically: no source found.

8.4 Acclimation (general, see section 9)

8.5 Quarantine
- VCA: quarantine new fish "at least 30 days before introducing any new fish to their tank." (S15)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "Utilising a quarantine or hospital tank is always advisable no matter where you source your fish." (S17)
- LiveAquaria (retailer's own procedure page, not a product page; lower tier): "The minimum quarantine period for all new arrivals is 2 weeks". https://www.liveaquaria.com/blogs/fish-health-nutrition/liveaquaria%C2%AE-divers-den%C2%AE-wysiwyg-store-quarantine-procedure-for-freshwater-fish
- DISAGREEMENT: 30 days (VCA) vs a 2-week minimum (LiveAquaria). The site's cardinal guide already uses 30 days.

## 9. Handling: netting, acclimation, transport

None of these are ember-specific; all are general small-fish guidance.
- Lights: "the fish should be kept in fairly dark conditions, and not blinded by tank lighting, so switch these off!" (S17)
- Mixing water: "Repeat this every few minutes until the bag contains more water from your aquarium than the shop." Total time "could take anywhere from 30-60 minutes." (S17)
- Do not pour the bag in: "it is good practice not to pour the contents of the whole bag into your aquarium." Bag water may carry waste and pest snail eggs (paraphrase). (S17)
- Check for strays (relevant for a 2 cm fish): "double check the bag, the floor and the net to ensure no fish have got stuck or jumped out along the way." (S17)
- Long trips: "Ammonia levels can increase due to fish waste and pH can drop due to increased CO2." (S17)
- Drip acclimation: "allows for a more gradual change in water chemistry", about two hours, aimed at sensitive species such as Caridina shrimp (paraphrase). (S17)
- Bagging ratio: "Aim to have a one-to three ratio: one part water, three parts air." because "it's the air that contains the essential oxygen". (S18)
- Transport: place bags in "a dark container, or wrap them in a towel or some newspapers." (S18)
- Netting technique: the article warns against having "a friend hold a half-filled bag open" while you "tip fish in from a net"; float a partly filled bag on the surface and open it as you net (paraphrase). (S18)
- LiveAquaria's no-net practice: "we practice a "no-net" technique to ensure that no additional stress or pathogenic transmission (from nets) can occur." (retailer procedure page, lower tier, URL in 8.5)

## 10. Breeding basics

- "An egg-scattering free spawner exhibiting no parental care." (S1)
- Setup (paraphrase of S1): separate breeding tank, dim light, mesh or marbles on the base to protect eggs, slightly acidic to neutral water, gentle aeration.
- Fry food: "Initial food should be Paramecium or a proprietary dry food of sufficiently small (5-50 micron) grade". (S1)
- Egg predation: "As egg scatterers, they display no parental care and will happily predate on their own eggs and newborn fry." (S8)
- Fishkeeper: spawning "happens in typical tetra fashion, with high temperatures and acidic water giving the best results." (S9)
- Clutch and hatch (lower tier, S12): "A female can lay between 30 and 60 eggs per spawning cycle." Eggs "hatch after about 24-30 hours." Fry "begin to swim freely about three days after hatching."
- Flowgrow calls it a "Bodenlaicher" (substrate spawner) (S13); this agrees in practice with "egg scatterer" (eggs fall to the bottom).
- Aquatic Arts lists its stock as tank-bred: "Tank-bred, but indigenous to South American Araguaia system." (retailer listing, observation only, not a source)

## 11. "Did you know?" candidates

A. Named for a scientist mother. The species honors Amanda Bleher (1910-1991), a German limnologist, whose son, explorer and fish exporter Heiko Bleher, collected the type specimen. The "-ae" ending marks a woman honoree.
- ETYFish: "in honor of Amanda Bleher (1910-1991)" (S4) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- FishBase eponymy, limnologist (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/12376
- Fishkeeper: "hence the 'ae' ending rather than 'i.'" (S19) https://www.fishkeeper.co.uk/stories/Heiko-Bleher

B. Brazilians call them "little fires". At the Araguaia field site, the local guide shouted "Foguinhos, here, here", and the article states "This was the local name for ember tetras." (S6) https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/finding-ember-tetras-in-brazil/. "Foguinho" is the "diminutive of fogo" (fire). (S20) https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/foguinho

C. Bright orange, but camouflaged from above. In the wild, "From above they were hard to see." When clouds covered the sun, "their dark backs helped them fade into the gloom." The shoal "performed this mini-relocation every few minutes", and the author then saw why: "Slinking about in the tall stands of Eichhornia were pike cichlids." (S6)

D. (Backup) Described in a hobby magazine, not a scientific journal: the original description ran in Tropical Fish Hobbyist, January 1987, possibly published December 1986. (S5, read via the GBIF API: verify on the desktop before using)

### 13.1 Substrate (owner's top priority)

No source gives an ember-specific substrate specification beyond "dark". Everything below except 13.1.2 and 13.1.6 is general aquarium or planted-tank husbandry.

13.1.1 Do embers feed from or use the bottom?
- Ember-specific: Practical Fishkeeping places them in the middle of the water column: "A group of 10 of either of these would fill the mid-waters of your tank with activity and colour." (S51, about glowlights and embers)
- Ember-specific: "they do have little mouths that prefer to feed on tiny, slow-sinking foods." (S8)
- No source found that says embers sift or forage on the substrate. Seriously Fish's wild-habitat line is about the bottom, not the fish's use of it: "with the substrate covered by a layer of fallen leaves and branches." (S1)
- Reading for writers: the substrate matters to an ember for plants, chemistry and color, not for feeding. That reading is an inference; see 13.9.

13.1.2 Color: dark vs light
- Ember-specific: "Should ideally be kept in a heavily-planted set-up, preferably with a dark substrate." (S1)
- Ember-specific: "We find that ember tetras tend to show brighter colors in planted tanks with a dark substrate and background." (S8). This is an observation by a retailer, not a measurement.
- Ember-specific (weak): store conditions dull them: "pet stores are stressful places that can dull a fish considerably." and "They'll likely brighten up once they've settled into a more appropriate environment." (S10)
- Mechanism, other tetras (peer-reviewed): background matching in small characins runs through pigment cells. In the sailfin tetra (Crenuchus spilurus, a different family), "Populations inserted into blackwaters (sandy soil) showed higher rates of color darkening" on a dark leaf-litter bottom; background matching "occurs through the expansion or retraction of melanophores". Fish were tested over 10 minutes on leaf litter only. (S23). Not tested on embers; no light-background trial.
- No study found on substrate color and stress in any Hyphessobrycon. The coho salmon and aquaculture tank-color literature surfaced in search but was not opened and is not a tetra.

13.1.3 Sand vs gravel and grain size
- General: "with gravel the norm is to go for a deeper layer of say 5cm/2" or more." and "In freshwater tanks, generally only a thin layer of sand is used". (S43)
- General: grain size and dirt: "more dirt may get trapped in the substrate if you use a larger grain size." and "very fine substrates, such as sand, don't allow much dirt to be trapped". (S43)
- General, for beginners: inert "aquarium gravel or coarse sand" (Co-Op's wording seen in search results for S54; the opened page confirms "we recommend that beginners start with inert substrates that contain no nutrients"). (S54)
- Grain-size numbers (1 to 3 mm for planted tanks) appeared only on low-tier retailer and aggregator blogs in search results. Not citable; listed in 13.9.
- Ember-specific: The Shrimp Farm's biotope suggestion uses sand: "All you need is a sandy substrate, some branches and rocks, a layer of leaf litter and seed pods". (S10). The wild site had leaf litter "over a yellow clay-like substrate mixed with fine gravel and sand" (section 2, S6).

13.1.4 Depth
- General planted tank: "Planted tanks usually require at least 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) of substrate." (S54)
- General: gravel 5 cm (2 in) or more; sand usually a thin layer (S43, quoted in 13.1.3).
- A bare-bottom layer: the existing guides already use a bare tank for quarantine (cost and tank guides). No source found that a bare floor suits a display ember tank, and S1's "dark substrate" implies one is used.

13.1.5 Planted options: aquasoil, capped soil, inert plus root tabs
- Inert plus root tabs (general): "insert root tab fertilizers into the ground if you are using inert substrate" for root-feeding plants such as Cryptocoryne and swords (plant examples from the search snippet of the same page). (S54)
- Capped soil (general): "only adding a 1/8 to 1/4-inch of dirt (just enough to barely cover the tank bottom)" then "putting at least a 2-inch cap of gravel to prevent the dirt from leaking into the water column." (S55)
- Why gravel, not sand, as the cap: "gravel allows the gases to escape more easily." and "With sand, escaping gases cause the dirt and sand to explode to the surface, making the water very cloudy and muddy." (S55)
- Dirt drawbacks: "Makes a huge cloudy mess if you move plants or have bottom dwellers that dig" and "Can lead to toxic ammonia spikes or unsightly algae growth". (S55)
- Aquasoil (weak, retailer wiki for one brand, ADA Amazonia): "ADA Amazonia is originally acidic, with a pH level of 5.0-6.0, and it makes water more acidic." Ammonia at the start: "immediately after it is laid in aquarium, a large amount of ammonium (NH4) leaches into water." Stocking delay: "It is advised that adding fish and shrimp to aquarium should be two to three weeks after planting of aquatic plants." Lifespan: "You can use substrate made of ADA Amazonia for two to three years." (S58)
- Planted substrate and vacuuming: "If you use a gravel cleaner in a tank containing a costly planting substrate, such as laterite, you risk sucking it out." (S43)

13.1.6 Effect on water chemistry, and how it fits the ember's range
- The ember's range (section 5): pH 5.0 to 7.0, 1 to 10 dGH (S1). Ember-specific: "while not quite so finicky, will certainly look their best in soft, acidic conditions." (S48, Neale Monks)
- Calcareous substrate (general): "Inert substrates are of particular importance to those keeping fishes that like soft water, such as Discus." and "Even standard pea gravel contains sufficient calcium to boost the pH and hardness". (S43)
- Coral sand (general): "you should only use it in situations where you want the pH and hardness to be kept high." (S43)
- Coated gravel (general): some colored gravels "particularly the rough textured angular ones with a powdery colour coating" raise pH and hardness, so "it wouldn't be a great choice at all if you want to keep fish that like neutral or soft water - you'll need an inert substrate instead." (S43)
- Rock (general): "Limestone and other calcareous rocks should be avoided by keepers of softwater aquaria." and "Like seashells, these can leach calcium carbonate into the water and have an undesirable effect on hardness and pH." (S44)
- Vinegar test (general): "Test for calcium carbonate by applying acid to the suspected rock to look for any fizzing." "Vinegar is often used for this purpose". Dragon stone is shown as inert. "Soft water aquarists would also do well to avoid rocks with veins." (S44)
- Aquasoil lowers pH and KH (weak, S58, quoted in 13.1.5). How that fits the ember: an acid-buffering soil pushes toward the ember's range; a crushed coral, aragonite or limestone bed pushes away from it. That pairing is an inference from S1 plus S43, S44 and S58.
- pH chemicals (ember-relevant): "by themselves, pH buffers are not a solution to hard water", they "should never be used without lowering the mineral levels in your water by adding RO or rainwater", and "this approach tends to be unpredictable and unstable." (S48). Ember-specific, weak: "Don't start fiddling with harsh products like pH Down, though!" and embers "can react badly to sudden water parameter swings." (S10)
- Crushed coral dissolving faster at low pH: retailer product copy only (Aquarium Co-Op crushed coral listing, seen in search results). Not cited.

13.1.7 Leaf litter and botanicals on the substrate
- Ember-specific: "Floating plants are a useful addition as are driftwood branches and dried leaf litter," with leaf litter "driving establishment of microbe colonies as decomposition occurs," and "the tannins and other chemicals released by the decaying leaves are also thought beneficial." (S1). Note "thought": no evidence given.
- Ember-specific: some keepers add "catappa leaves, driftwood, and other interesting botanicals"; catappa can "create biofilm for the fry to eat and slightly acidify the water if you have higher pH." (S8)
- Ember-specific, weak: "Fallen branches and a layer of leaves bring down the pH and keep the water moderately soft, though usually not enough to turn it into blackwater." (S10)
- Wood (general): "Bogwood and Mopani are notorious for leaching tannic acid into water." (S44)
- Catappa antibacterial claims: one peer-reviewed abstract. Catappa leaf extract was tested against Aeromonas hydrophila in patin catfish; fish were "injected intramusculary with TC at the dosages of 60 g/l for the prevention and 120 g/l for curative efficacy", and the authors concluded "TC were better and more effective as preventive than curative". (S26). This was injected extract in a food fish, not leaves soaking in a tank. It does not show that a leaf on the substrate treats or prevents disease. Do not write it that way.
- Leaf amount and pH drop numbers: only low-tier blogs, and they disagree (one says to about pH 5 in soft water, another says 0.2 to 0.5 units). Not citable. See 13.9.

13.1.8 Cleaning
- General: "The easiest way to keep the gravel clean is to regularly use a gravel cleaner." and "you should be able to vacuum most of the tank floor while only removing about 20-30% of the aquarium water". (S43)
- General, sand: "Once it's in the tank it's a doddle to keep clean." (S43)
- Pre-rinse (general): "most substrates are very dusty and need to be cleaned thoroughly before they're used, otherwise the tank will turn extremely cloudy." "Powder-coated coloured gravels are a particular irritation here". (S43)
- Planted beds: capped soil "Makes a huge cloudy mess if you move plants" (S55); vacuuming a planting substrate risks "sucking it out" (S43).
- Hovering the vacuum about an inch above sand, cleaning a third at a time, and staying 2 to 3 inches from plant stems: only retailer blogs and forums in search results. Not citable. See 13.9.

13.1.9 What to avoid
- Coated, angular colored gravel in a soft-water tank (S43). Calcareous rock, shells, veined rock (S44). Coral sand (S43).
- Sharp gravel: the only sources found are about bottom-sifting fish (Corydoras barbels), mostly forums, plus a sera setup guide seen in search results ("grubbing fish such as armored catfish otherwise get injured by sharp-edged chippings"). Relevant only if the ember tank holds Corydoras, which S8 suggests as tankmates. Weak.
- Black sand sharpness (Tahitian Moon type): forum anecdotes only, split both ways. Not citable.

### 13.2 Tank setup: lighting and tankmates

13.2.1 Lighting
- Ember-specific: no source gives an intensity or a photoperiod. S1 mentions light only for spawning: "This should be very dimly lit". The Shrimp Farm (weak): "Use live (floating) plants and plenty of natural decor to dim the light and help the fish feel at ease." (S10). The field site (S6, section 2) had "plenty of light".
- Planted-tank photoperiod (general, two pages from the same retailer that disagree with each other): start "for only 5-6 hours per day at first" then "Slowly increase the amount of lighting" (S54); versus "starting with only 6 to 8 hours a day" then "slowly increase the lighting up to 8 to 12 hours a day" (S56, written about the retailer's own light; en dashes replaced). Both: "Start with low amounts of fertilizer and lighting at first to avoid algae growth." (S54). "If algae starts getting out of control, then decrease the duration again." "Timers are much more reliable than people." (S56)
- Light and color in a related tetra (peer-reviewed): cardinal tetras held at 0, 250, 500, 1,200 and 2,700 lux for up to 72 hours. "Loss of skin color was observed when cardinal tetra was exposed to intense light." The melanic and neon stripes paled, while "the color of the red stripe was intensified due to the proliferation of erythrophores." "At low light levels (0 to 250 lux), the melanophores ... proliferate in the black and neon stripes resulting in a more vibrant skin color." (S22). Cardinal, not ember; the ember's orange is not a melanin or neon stripe, so this does not say bright light fades an ember. Use only as "light level changes tetra color" with the cardinal named.
- Sudden lights-on (general): TFH reports from the literature that "when the lights went on suddenly, some fish sank or dove to the bottom; dashed around frantically," "sometimes bumping into objects or jumping out of the aquarium; or seemed to fade in color." Counterpoint in the same article (Thom Demas): "Lots of fish do just fine with the lights popping on or off regardless of the light level of the room they are in." (S52)
- Acclimation (already section 9): lights off for new arrivals (S17).

13.2.2 Tankmates in more depth
- Good (ember-specific): "similarly-sized, peaceful characids, smaller callichthyid or loricariid catfishes"; "an ideal dither fish for Apistogramma spp. and other dwarf cichlids"; it "does not normally hunt fry"; in a general community "it can be combined with smaller rasboras, barbs, anabantoids, etc." (S1)
- Good (ember-specific): "other nano schooling fish such as rasboras, tetras, and danios"; "bottom-dwelling corydoras catfish and surface-dwelling hatchetfish or pencilfish"; "otocinclus catfish and dwarf shrimp". (S8)
- Group size and security (ember-specific, closes the Neale Monks lead in Gaps): "larger groups of Embers and hatchets will feel more secure and look more peaceful". (S49). The tank in that answer was about 90 liters, not a 5 gallon nano.
- Bad, large fish (ember-specific, weak): "avoid bigger species (like angelfish) that will try to make a meal out of them." (S10). Seriously Fish on angelfish: "may eat small fish such as tetras"; adults "reach 15 cm in body length". (S42)
- Bad, fin nippers and boisterous fish: tiger barbs are "notoriously aggressive with a reputation for biting the fins of other fishes", worst "when insufficient numbers are purchased or space is limited", and the species "does not make an ideal companion for timid, slow-moving, or long-finned species." (S40). Serpae tetras have "a reputation for nipping the fins of tankmates" and are "notorious as an aggressive community inhabitant". (S41). PFK on serpaes: "renowned for fin-nipping behaviour" and they "will often dominate and bully less robust species". (S50). Ember-specific, weak: avoid "overly boisterous fish, such as tiger barbs, that might stress out the embers". (S10)
- Why it matters for an ember (ember-specific): "Very peaceful and will not compete well with very boisterous or much larger tankmates." (S1, already section 7)
- Embers themselves (fills the fin-nipping gap, weakly): "Ember tetras are peaceful and not known to be nippy." (S10). PFK suggests embers as a replacement for serpaes because they "are not aggressive like the Serpaes." (S50)
- Bettas and dwarf gouramis: Co-Op both warns and recommends: "some bettas or dwarf gouramis can be quite territorial" and "A blue betta fish or powder blue dwarf gourami would look amazing as a centerpiece fish". (S8). See 13.10.
- Shrimp (ember-specific, adds to section 7): The Shrimp Farm says "ember tetras would struggle to eat anything but newly hatched shrimp" and shrimplets "will spend the first few weeks of their lives hiding in dense plants". (S10)

Frequency: "Most aquarium fish should be fed at least daily." and "adult fish should be fed 3% of their body weight daily for maintenance." (S31, general) |

13.3.2 Storage (general, useful for the cost guide too)
- Dry food containers kept at room temperature should be discarded "after 2 months of opening"; commercial feeds in the freezer "after 6 months"; frozen feeds such as brine shrimp and bloodworms "after 1 year". "Thawed feeds should not be refrozen" (paraphrase of the page). (S31)

13.3.3 Foods to avoid or handle with care
- Live tubifex (general): of 7 shop samples, "6 contained infected oligochaetes; waterborne spores were present in 5 samples at the time of purchase", and the worms sold as tubifex were several species. "The authors believe that their use has the potential to spread disease to new areas." (S46, reporting Hallett et al. 2005)
- Live crustaceans as intermediate hosts (general): "Cyclops and Daphnia spp are common intermediate hosts for Camallanus spp." and "Aquarium and cultured pond fish may be heavily infected if crustacean intermediate hosts are present." (S33). Note: the feeding guide names cyclops and daphnia as foods. Frozen and cultured sources are the usual workaround, but no source found states that freezing removes the Camallanus risk. See 13.9.
- Oversized food (ember-specific): "little mouths that prefer to feed on tiny, slow-sinking foods" (S8). The Shrimp Farm suggests "frozen foods like bloodworms" (S10); no source checks whether a whole bloodworm fits a 2 cm fish. See 13.9.
- Diet composition (general): hepatic lipidosis "is a common problem in captive fish", with causes including "a high percentage of carbohydrates in the diet" and a high lipid amount. "Inadequate nutrition can result in poor growth, deformities, a depressed immune system." (S31)

13.3.4 Why embers stop eating, and when to worry
- No ember-specific source. General causes from veterinary sources:
- Water quality: in new tank syndrome, "the fish suddenly become lethargic, anorectic, and frequently die", with ammonia or nitrite "high enough to result in toxicity". (S32)
- Parasites: some "cause problems such as weight loss and loss of appetite." (S35). Spironucleus: weight loss and anorexia (S33). Fin rot: "Affected fish become anorexic, lethargic and dark in colour." (S53)
- Sick fish and medicated food: "Palatability can be a problem when feeding medicated food to sick fish, which may have a poor appetite." (S32)
- Competition (ember-specific): embers "will not compete well with very boisterous or much larger tankmates" (S1).
- New arrivals: stress of transport (S17, S18, section 9). No source gives a normal number of days a new ember may refuse food.
- When to worry: the existing health guide's action list already covers it. Sources for escalation: weight loss plus lethargy with "white, stringy feces" is a listed internal parasite sign (S35, written for cichlids); several fish affected at once points to water (S32).

### 13.4 Health

13.4.1 Fin rot (general, no ember-specific source)
- Cause: "The most common bacteria involved are usually Aeromonas, Pseudomonas and Flavobacterium." "It can be caused by inappropriate aquarium conditions which may increase susceptibility to infections." (S53)
- Signs: "The tip of the fin becomes greyish, and then it becomes eroded and hemorrhagic." "Mortality increases significantly once bacteria enter the circulatory system." (S53). FishBase: "Beginning with white margin of fins, when all fins are erroded, tail turns red." Causative agent "Aeromonas sp." (S39). Merck lists ragged fins among Aeromonas signs (S35).
- Prevention: "Immediate treatment of fins or tails of fishes weakened by mechanical damage." and "Disinfection of tanks or any culture containers." (S39). Fin damage often starts with fights between tankmates (S53, paraphrase), which links to the fin-nipper tankmates in 13.2.2.
- Treatment basics: "Many bacterial infections can be treated with antibiotics," but lab testing picks the drug (S35). "Rapidly changing antibiotics or 'shotgunning' fish with many different drugs is a dangerous practice and can result in creation of resistant bacterial strains." (S35). FishBase lists antibiotics but says "Seek advise from a veterinarian or your local aquarium shop." (S39). ANGFA: "the use of antibiotics should be considered carefully before any application." (S53). In the US, antibiotics for pet fish generally need a vet; that legal point is unsourced here (see 13.9).
- Columnaris (look-alike): "skin lesions with slimy or cotton-like excretions"; "Early treatment with potassium permanganate can help," otherwise "antibiotic treatment is generally needed". (S35)

13.4.2 Fungal infections (general)
- Saprolegnia: "grayish-white, cotton-like growths on the skin, gills, eyes, or fins." "Prevention consists of removing all potential sources of the fungus." "a single treatment of potassium permanganate can often control external Saprolegnia" when the environment is clean. (S35)
- Fungal fin rot "looks like creamy or whitish, hairy-like growths," while bacterial fin rot is "whitish or gray and less prominent". (S53)
- That water molds mostly invade already damaged tissue appeared only in low-tier blogs in search results. See 13.9.

13.4.3 Internal parasites (general)
- Camallanus: "These blood-red worms can be seen in the intestine and protruding from the anus of affected fish (red worm disease)." Treatment table: "Fenbendazole, levamisole". Intermediate hosts Cyclops and Daphnia (13.3.3). (S33). Doses differ between Merck pages and hobby sources; do not print a dose without the owner choosing a source.
- Spironucleus (intestinal flagellate): "The only treatment available for spironucleosis is metronidazole (use only in ornamental species)". (S33). Owner-version signs: "lethargy, weight loss, and white, stringy feces" (S35, written for cichlids).
- Tapeworms: "The anthelmintic of choice is praziquantel (35 to 125 mg/kg, in feed, every 24 hours for three days)." (S33, en dash replaced). "Heavily infested fish may lose weight and appear lethargic." (S35)
- Ivermectin: the older MSD edition seen in search results says it "can be highly toxic to aquarium fish". Search result only; not cited.
- Diagnosis: "Examination of fresh smears or biopsies that contain living parasites is often diagnostic." (S33). That is a vet procedure, which supports the vet section.

13.4.4 Medications: invertebrate-safe vs harmful
- Copper and invertebrates (strong): "Most invertebrates are highly sensitive to copper and will not survive a copper treatment." Invertebrates "should be moved and not returned until Cu2+ concentrations are 0.01 mg/L or less, but ideally zero". Copper bound to substrate "may be released if pH drops" (paraphrase of the same document). (S30, written for marine systems; the invertebrate point is general)
- Copper in freshwater (strong, and important for a soft-water fish): "the toxicity of copper to fish increases as the total alkalinity decreases." "If the total alkalinity is less than 50 ppm, copper treatments are not recommended because of the high risk of killing fish." "Copper is also toxic to invertebrates, such as snails, and also most of the zooplankton". (S29). 50 ppm total alkalinity is about 2.8 dKH (conversion, unsourced arithmetic). An ember tank run at 1 to 10 dGH with low KH can sit below that line. See conflict 13.10.1.
- Formalin: "Formalin is toxic; it should be used with caution and by following the instructions for proper use." (S35)
- Shrimp-safe list (praziquantel safe; formalin, malachite green, salt risky): only hobby blogs and retailer pages in search results, and they disagree on malachite green. Not citable. See 13.9. The guides' current rule (shrimp come out before medication goes in) is the only safe statement the sources support.

13.4.5 Finding a fish vet, and what to bring
- AAFV map (search result only, 403 to fetch): the tool lets owners zoom to their location, "this map only supports fish vets located in North America", and if no vet appears, a local vet can contact AAFV "to be connected with a fish vet to collaborate on the case." AAFV "does not diagnose or prescribe". (S38, verify on desktop)
- Fish vet's advice (S37, Jessie Sanders DVM, DABVP Fish Practice): licenses "cannot transfer across state lines"; "ANY veterinarian can consult with an aquatic veterinarian in ANY STATE to assist with cases."; "All you have to do is ask your local exotic/small/large animal vet if they are willing to help consult on a case." Directories named: AAFV (by state or country) and WAVMA (international). Online diagnosis without an exam is limited and not legal for treatment plans (paraphrase).
- WAVMA: its member directory is members-only (WAVMA.org Member Resources page title seen in search results: "a members-only tool"). Not opened; a public WAVMA vet finder could not be confirmed. Recommend AAFV as the US route.
- What to bring (veterinary): "a separate water sample should be provided in a plastic bag or clean, plastic water bottle." "A minimum of 1 quart of tank water should be requested for regular analysis." For municipal water, "a second smaller sample (< 100 mL) in a glass container for chlorine testing." Samples "capped under the surface of the water without any gas bubbles visible." If a visit is not possible, "video and/or photos of the system (including filtration) and the fish, both affected and unaffected." History: system volume, water source, filtration, new additions and quarantine, previous medications, number and species of animals (paraphrase). (S34). The other Merck page asks for "A minimum of 500 mL of tank water" (S32); 1 quart is about 946 mL, so the two agree that a pint or more is needed.
- Fish necropsy: "A veterinary clinic or diagnostic facility familiar with fish necropsy protocols and aquatic microbiology should be used." "Water samples should be submitted with necropsy specimens." (S32)

13.4.6 What a healthy ember looks like
- General veterinary: "Healthy fish generally have smooth, unblemished scales, fins, and skin surfaces." "Skin and fins should be intact, not bloody or ragged." "Eyes should be bright, not cloudy." "Fish should be swimming normally and steadily around the aquarium." "Diseased fish often swim erratically (circling or drifting around the tank) or appear listless." (S36)
- Ember-specific: "Adult males are more intensely-coloured, especially when in spawning condition" (S1, section 3.2). Brighter color in a planted tank over a dark floor (S8). Color returns after the stress of the store (S10, weak).
- Ember-specific: "ember tetras are relatively outgoing, especially in large groups" (S8, section 7); embers in larger groups "feel more secure and look more peaceful" (S49).

### 13.5 Handling: fish tank granuloma and first-timer mistakes

13.5.1 Fish tank granuloma (Mycobacterium marinum)
- Cause and route: the bacterium lives in fresh and salt water, unchlorinated pools and aquarium tanks, and enters through a "break in the skin, such as a cut". (S27)
- Signs: a reddish-to-purple bump that slowly becomes a painful nodule, most often on elbows, fingers and backs of the hands (paraphrase). Signs usually appear "about 2 to 3 weeks after exposure," sometimes a month or longer. Treatment often takes several months and may combine antibiotics (paraphrase). (S27)
- Prevention: wear rubber gloves when cleaning aquariums and wash hands and arms thoroughly afterward (paraphrase). (S27). French public health bulletin: keepers should "wear gloves when they clean out tanks or handle fish," and "use antiseptic to treat skin cuts and grazes." 63 cases in France between 1996 and 1998. (S47)
- Case reports: one aquarist cleaned an injured knee "with a brush usually used to clean the aquarium"; the other was injured "during aquarium clearing". "Delayed diagnosis is the main cause of the patients' serious side effect." One patient was first misdiagnosed and treated with steroids for three months; the other needed "an eight-month-long treatment". "Microbiologists should be alerted to the possibility of M. marinum if the injury originated from an aquarium." (S24). Practical line for writers: tell the doctor about the aquarium.
- Higher-risk people: "Mycobacterium can infect people, causing skin infection that can progress to more severe illness." "People with immune system disorders should not be allowed contact with a Mycobacterium-positive aquarium." (S35)
- In fish: "These bacteria are generally resistant to antibiotic treatment in fish," and "Infected fish are usually destroyed." Mycobacteria "cannot be detected with nonlethal tests." (S35, S32)
- Mouth-siphoning a gravel vacuum: no source found. See 13.9.

13.5.2 Common first-timer mistakes (no reputable single list found; each item sourced separately)
- Adding fish to an uncycled tank: new tank syndrome, ammonia or nitrite toxicity, "Monitoring of ammonia and nitrite until measurable concentrations subside is recommended." (S32). Ember-specific, weak: "They should always be kept in a fully cycled aquarium," (S10)
- Chasing pH with chemicals: S48 and S10 (13.1.6). Ember-specific, weak: "Any changes or improvements should be made over the course of weeks or months, not hours or days." (S10)
- Buying too few: tiger barb and serpae nipping gets worse with too few fish (S40, S41); embers do better in bigger groups (S49).
- Skipping quarantine: "A 30-day period is the minimum time for quarantine, but longer periods may be necessary." (S32)
- Mixing with fin nippers or big fish: 13.2.2.
- Calcareous gravel or rock in a soft-water tank: 13.1.6.
- Bare hands with cuts in tank water: 13.5.1.
- Pouring bag water into the tank, and losing fish in the net: already section 9 (S17, S18).
- Overfeeding: no reputable ember-specific source; the general "overfeeding can lead to health problems" line appeared on the Merck owner page in search results only.
- Lists of "beginner mistakes" found in search were retailer and aggregator blogs; not cited.

### 13.7 Enrichment: clear water vs tannin-stained (blackwater)

The disagreement, with what each side has:

Clear water side
- The only first-hand account of a wild ember site found clear water: "The water was clear, and there was little overhead cover meaning plenty of light hit the water." (S6, section 2)
- River type (peer-reviewed, indirect): under Sioli's original classification the Tocantins, Tapajós and Xingu "are considered clearwater rivers, with low concentrations of sediments and solutes", and "The Tocantins basin naturally has clear waters, with a low concentration of suspended sediment, low turbidity, visibility limits greater than 4 m deep". The Araguaia is named as the Tocantins's "main tributary and longest (1,670 km) channel". (S25). This classifies the basin, not every ember backwater.
- Plants: blackwater habitats have sparse plants: "Aquatic plants can be sparse, and would definitely need to have low lighting tolerance." (S45). The ember guides call for heavy planting (S1), and tannins cut the light plants get (that last point came only from low-tier blogs; see 13.9).
- Stability: "Even a simple 20% weekly water change has the potential to disrupt or raise the pH and dilute the colouration." Activated carbon strips tannins (paraphrase). (S45)

Tannin side
- Ember-specific: "the tannins and other chemicals released by the decaying leaves are also thought beneficial." (S1). AquaInfo (S12, lower tier, section 2) describes dark tannin-stained water at ember sites.
- Ember-specific: catappa leaves and botanicals are commonly added (S8); leaf litter lowers pH a little, "though usually not enough to turn it into blackwater" (S10, weak).
- Evidence for a benefit (peer-reviewed, not an ember and not a tetra): Rio Negro dissolved organic carbon "provided almost perfect protection against ionoregulatory disturbances" in zebrafish exposed to pH 4.0 in ion-poor water; it "reduced diffusive losses of Na+ and Cl-" and protection held after acclimation "even if the DOC was no longer present." (S21). The fish were zebrafish from a pet store; the paper did not test characins.
- What PFK says (S45): blackwater is "soft, acidic water with a deep brown colouration"; humic and fulvic acids "along with the elevated DOC, are the main source of colouration"; blackwater "has long been associated" with anti-fungal and healing properties, better color and breeding, which the article presents as long-standing association rather than tested findings (paraphrase). It names catappa: "catappa, guava and magnolia will all work".
- Antibacterial claim for catappa: S26 only, injected extract in catfish (13.1.7). Not evidence for leaves in a tank.

Where the two sides agree
- Both keep the water soft and acidic to neutral (S1, S48). Neither side has a measured benefit or harm for embers. Light leaf litter is supported on both sides as cover and a biofilm source (S1, S8); a dark blackwater tank is not required by any source.

## Substrate: A Dark Floor That Leaves the Water Soft

The one substrate detail tied to this species is color: embers are best kept in a heavily planted tank, preferably over a dark substrate. Keepers report that embers show brighter color in planted tanks with a dark substrate and background, an observation from the hobby that nobody has measured. A <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0018CLX3C?tag=beastlyfacts-20" product="Seachem Flourite Black Sand, 7 kg (15.4 lb)">black sand</AffiliateLink> or a dark, small-grained gravel meets it. At the one wild ember site described firsthand, near Aruanã on the Araguaia, the bottom was leaf litter over a yellow, clay-like floor mixed with fine gravel and sand.

**Sand or gravel.** Both work, and they go in differently. Gravel is normally laid 2 inches (5 cm) deep or more, while freshwater tanks generally get only a thin layer of sand. Grain size decides how much dirt the bed holds: very fine sand lets little dirt sink into it, so it stays easy to clean, and the larger the grain, the more gets trapped. For a first planted tank, start with an inert substrate that carries no nutrients of its own, either aquarium gravel or sand.

**Depth.** A tank planted into the substrate needs at least 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7.5 cm) of it. A thin layer of sand is enough where little is rooted in the floor.

**Feeding the plants.** An inert bed leaves the water alone and feeds nothing, so a planted tank adds nutrients one of three ways.

**What the substrate does to the water.** Whatever goes on the floor has to leave the water soft. Inert substrates matter most for soft-water fish, and even standard pea gravel contains enough calcium to raise pH and hardness. Coral sand belongs only in a tank meant to stay hard and alkaline. Rough, angular colored gravel with a powdery coating can raise both figures too, so a soft-water tank needs an inert substrate in its place. An acidic aquasoil pushes the other way, toward the ember's range.

**Rock follows the same rule.** Limestone and other calcareous rocks, and seashells, leach calcium carbonate into the water. To check a stone, put a few drops of vinegar on it: fizzing means calcium carbonate. Dragon stone passes, and rock with visible veins is best left out of a soft-water tank.

**Leaf litter on the bed.** Leaves are optional, and they sit on top of the substrate. Dried leaf litter and driftwood branches both suit this species. As the leaves decay, colonies of microbes establish on them, and the tannins and other compounds they release are thought to be beneficial, which is a belief among keepers more than a tested result. Catappa leaves are the common choice: they grow a biofilm that fry graze on, and they slightly acidify water whose pH runs high. Bogwood and mopani leach tannic acid as well. None of it softens hard water. Leaves and branches bring pH down a little and keep the water moderately soft, usually not enough to turn it into blackwater, and in hard tap water the hardness still has to come down through the RO blend first. Catappa's reputation against disease traces to a study in which leaf extract was injected into farmed catfish. That says nothing about a leaf lying on the floor of a tank, so a leaf is cover and biofilm, not a treatment.

**Cleaning.** Rinse new substrate thoroughly before it goes in: most of it is dusty enough to cloud the tank, and powder-coated colored gravel is the worst. Once the tank is running, a gravel cleaner keeps the bed clean, and a careful pass can reach most of the floor while drawing off only about 20 to 30% of the water. A planted bed is the exception. A gravel cleaner can suck a planting substrate out, and capped soil clouds the tank when its plants are moved, so the cleaner works the open patches and the rooted areas are left alone.
