## 1. How a fish makes its color

1.1 Four kinds of pigment cell
- Ligon and McCartney (fetch tool, quoted): the four major chromatophore types are "melanophores, erythrophores, xanthophores, and iridophores". "Melanophores are capable of synthesizing their own melanin." Erythrophores and xanthophores get their yellow-to-red from "pteridines and carotenoids" in varying amounts, with xanthophores named for yellow pteridine content and erythrophores for red or orange carotenoids. Iridophores hold stacked guanine platelets and "do not contain an actual 'pigment' molecule": "The organization of the guanine reflecting platelets into stacks causes iridophores to provide a highly reflective, often iridescent surface in the dermis of the skin". The dermal chromatophore unit, from the surface down a melanophore's processes, a layer of xanthophores or erythrophores, a layer of iridophores, then the melanophore body, is "the fundamental unit of rapid, physiological color change in vertebrates." (F1)
- Zebra danio: Patterson and Parichy (abstract): adult stripes result "from the precise arrangement of three main classes of pigment cells: black melanophores, yellow xanthophores, and iridescent iridophores." (F9)
- Goldfish: Elshafey et al. (verbatim): "body color can be classified into two categories: pigment color and structural color. Fish color is ultimately determined by the mixture of these two pigment patterns." (F16)
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. For the guide: black is melanin the fish makes; yellow, orange and red are carotenoids (from food) and pteridines (made by the fish) in the same cells; silver, blue and iridescent green are reflected light from guanine crystals.

1.2 Structural color: the neon and cardinal stripe
- Gur et al. (abstract, verbatim): the neon tetra changes "the structural color of its lateral stripe in response to a change in the light conditions, from blue-green in the light-adapted state to indigo in the dark-adapted state. The colors are produced by constructive interference of light reflected from stacks of intracellular guanine crystals, forming tunable photonic crystal arrays." Micro X-ray diffraction showed "reversible variations in crystal tilt within individual arrays are responsible for the light-induced color variations", settling the debate for the "Venetian blinds" model over the "accordion" model. (F6)
- Yoshioka et al. (abstract, verbatim): the neon's structural color "can change in response to the light intensity of the surrounding environment"; an iridophore "contains a few stacks of periodically arranged light-reflecting platelets"; in the Venetian blind model "the light-reflecting platelets are assumed to be tilted during colour change, resulting in a variation in the spacing between the platelets"; their measurements "quantitatively verify the model." (F7)
- Seriously Fish on the neon (NEON N12, verified there): "duller with a violet lateral stripe at night" and lighter "in bright conditions with pale substrate or background and clear water." (F23)
- Cardinal: Linhares et al. (CARDINAL C4, verified there): under intense light "The melanic and neon stripes became pale due to a reduction in melanophores densities." The red stripe intensified. (F22)
- Verdict: SUPPORTED for the neon (two peer-reviewed papers, one husbandry source). The cardinal's blue stripe is the same kind of structure by every description, but only the neon was studied in F6 and F7; the cardinal evidence (F22) is about light fading the stripes, not the mechanism. Write the mechanism for the neon and say the cardinal stripe is the same kind of structure "by every description" only if the owner accepts that; otherwise keep it to the neon.

## 2. Why fish must eat their reds, oranges and yellows

- Nakano and Wiegertjes (verbatim): "Most animals cannot produce carotenoids de novo." "On the contrary, photosynthetic organisms, such as plants, can produce carotenoids de novo." "Salmonids cannot synthesize ketocarotenoids, such as ASX". Carotenoids divide into "carotenes (e.g., α-carotene, β-carotene, and lycopene)" and "xanthophylls (e.g., zeaxanthin, lutein, canthaxanthin 'CAX', and ASX)". "The amount of carotenoids deposited in fish muscle depends on the level of carotenoids in the feed". (F2)
- Maoka (verbatim): "In general, animals do not synthesize carotenoids de novo, and so those found in animals are either directly accumulated from food or partly modified through metabolic reactions". Fish differ in what they can convert: "Cyprinidae fish, which inhabit fresh water, can synthesize (3S,3′S)-astaxanthin (8a) from zeaxanthin (70) by oxidative metabolic conversion"; "On the other hand, Perciformes and Salmonidae fish cannot synthesize astaxanthin from other carotenoids"; "Therefore, astaxanthin present in these fish originates from dietary crustacean zooplankton." Applied: "Synthetic and natural astaxanthin from Phaffia yeast and Haematococcus algae is widely used for the pigmentation of salmon, trout, and red sea bream. Lutein from marigold is also used as a yellow coloration ... Zeaxanthin from spirulina is used as a red coloration for goldfish and ornamental carp." Also: "The principal carotenoid in crustaceans is astaxanthin". (F3)
- Grether, Hudon and Endler (abstract, verbatim): "Animals cannot synthesize carotenoid pigments and ultimately depend on dietary sources." "Unlike carotenoids, pteridines are synthesized de novo by animals." (F4)
- Elshafey et al. (verbatim): "Like other animals, fish are incapable of synthesizing carotenoids, thus, they must obtain them from their food." (F16)
- Verdict: SUPPORTED by four peer-reviewed sources. Species note from Maoka: goldfish and koi (Cyprinidae) can turn zeaxanthin (spirulina, marigold-type pigments) into astaxanthin, which is why spirulina works as a red source for them; cichlids (angelfish, discus, oscar) and other perch-like fish cannot make astaxanthin from other carotenoids, so their reds depend on astaxanthin in the food (krill, shrimp, algae-derived or synthetic astaxanthin). Where tetras, livebearers, bettas and danios sit on that conversion was not found; do not generalize beyond "Cyprinidae can, Perciformes cannot".

For those species the guide can say only that the general rule applies (section 2) and that the red of an ember or the nose of a rummy-nose is a carotenoid-and-pteridine color by cell type (F1), which is an inference the writer owns, not a species result.

## 4. What diet cannot change

4.1 Black
- Melanin is made by the fish: "Melanophores are capable of synthesizing their own melanin." (F1) Black-pattern genes in platies are inherited macromelanophore patterns (PLATY P7). Verdict: SUPPORTED. No food darkens black; stress and background do (section 6).

4.2 Blues, silvers, iridescent greens
- Iridophores reflect light from guanine crystals and carry no pigment (F1); the neon stripe is interference color that shifts with light (F6, F7). Verdict: SUPPORTED that these colors are structural, not dietary. One nuance for the owner: a 2026 koi study (Europe PMC record, Gao et al., Animals 16(14):2235, seen in a search batch, abstract not read in full) tested fish scale meal, which carries guanine, against iridophore density and brightness; not opened, so nothing from it goes on the site.

4.3 Pteridines
- The fish makes them (F1, F4). In guppies "males used more, not less, drosopterins in streams with higher carotenoid availability." (F4) Verdict: SUPPORTED; part of a guppy's orange and a betta's red is self-made and diet cannot move it.

4.4 Genetics, strain, sex
- Platies: extreme wild polymorphism, inherited patterns, sex-linked pigment genes (PLATY P6, P7). Guppy: the mate preference and its sensory bias are "genetically linked" (Grether 2005 record, seen in a search batch, not used). Betta: pigmentation gene expression differs by variety (F21). Females duller: Seriously Fish on the guppy, females "generally exhibit dull colouration" (GUPPY G22; F23). Verdict: SUPPORTED in kind: diet changes how strong an inherited red, orange or yellow is; it cannot add one.

4.5 Age
- Black skirt tetra: "the intense black of a young fish slowly fades as it grows older" is already on the site and verified in BLACK_SKIRT_TETRA_RESEARCH.md. Neon: juveniles "colour up at about four weeks" (NEON N14, Practical Fishkeeping, verified there). No cross-species source on age and color was opened. Verdict: PARTLY, two species only.

## 5. Color-enhancing foods: what they are and where the claims stop

- What is in them: the industry sources are "synthetic ASX ... Haematococcus pluvialis, red yeast Xanthophyllomyces dendrorhous ... Paracoccus carotinifaciens, krill oil and meal, crayfish oil" and "The level of ASX in red yeast, krill, and crayfish is low." (F2) Marigold (lutein), red pepper (capsanthin) and krill or crustacean meal (astaxanthin) (F12). Spirulina as a zeaxanthin source for goldfish and koi (F3). Brine shrimp are a carrier, not a source: enriched Artemia worked in the platy and goldfish trials (F11, F16), and Elshafey calls Artemia "an ideal biological carrier for bioencapsulating (live food enrichment) critical nutrients".
- Doses: "Generally, ASX has been recommended to use in the level of 50 to 100 mg/kg diet to enhance muscle pigmentation in fish" (F2, the source writes the range with a dash). Ornamental trials used 100 to 200 mg/kg (F14, F15). US law: astaxanthin in salmonid feed "shall not exceed 80 milligrams per kilogram (72 grams per ton) of finished feed", and it "is used to enhance the pink to orange-red color of the flesh of salmonid fish" (F18, verbatim). The regulation covers salmon and trout flesh, not aquarium fish; cite it only as the one legal ceiling that exists.
- Limits, verbatim: "Higher-than-optimal doses of carotenoids could counteract the potentially beneficial effects of these same free radicals released during early inflammatory responses to attack bacterial membranes." (F2) In the 2008 swordtail trial every marigold diet grew fish slower than the control and the gelatin diet shrank them (F12). Eşanu et al.: color foods "contain natural dyes, such as beta carotene, and are not harmful to fish, but the effect is temporary, because once they are no longer fed the treated food, fish eventually lose their color" (F20; its citation for that line is a hobby page, so treat it as a weak statement). Koi under blue LED colored up in 30 to 60 days but "chronic or excessive BL irradiation exacerbated dermal loosening and vacuolation, decreased a* and b* values" (F17), which is a light result, not a food result.
- Benefits beyond color, for the "is it worth it" question: carotenoids "are considered semi-essential nutrients that promote optimal survival and growth at relatively low dietary-inclusion levels" (F2); the oscar trial cut post-challenge mortality from 76.7 to 50 or 56.7 percent (F14); the platy trial raised mucus lysozyme and immunoglobulin (F11).
- Verdict: SUPPORTED that color foods are carotenoid foods, that they work on reds and oranges at the tested doses over weeks, and that the effect is maintained only while fed. "Overfeeding a color food poisons the fish" is UNSUPPORTED; what is supported is a plateau (more past the optimum does nothing useful, F2), a growth cost when the pigment source crowds out the diet (F12), and the general Merck rule that variety is how needs get met (PLATY P14). The guide should not quote a hobby-food dose; labels do not state one.

## 6. What changes color that is not food

6.1 Stress
- Höglund et al. (abstract, verbatim): "social subordination resulted in a significant skin darkening"; "Skin darkness following social interaction showed a significant positive correlation with plasma levels of alpha-MSH"; "social stress induces skin darkening in Arctic charr and that this effect could be mediated by a stress-induced increase in the levels of alpha-MSH in the circulation." (F8) Ligon and McCartney: alpha-MSH disperses melanosomes (darkening) in fish; MCH and melatonin generally aggregate them (paling); "The sympathetic nervous system is responsible for the flight-or-fight response to stressful stimuli." and epinephrine acts on melanophores through alpha and beta receptors with opposite effects, low levels darkening and high levels lightening in the anole example. (F1) Rummy-nose: "if the fish are not in good condition the red colour can fade considerably" (F23, RUMMY_NOSE S21, S22).
- Verdict: SUPPORTED that stress changes color fast through hormones and nerves, in both directions depending on species and context. The charr darkened; the rummy-nose pales. Write "stress can darken a fish or wash it out" and name the examples, not a universal direction.

6.2 Light, time of day, background
- Neon: blue-green stripe in light, indigo in dark (F6); at night "duller with a violet lateral stripe" (F23). Cardinal: intense light pales the stripes (F22). Background: zebrafish agouti protein "appears to underlie the background matching" (F1); Seriously Fish on neon, cardinal, glowlight and zebra danio: lighter over pale substrate and in clear bright water, better color in a planted tank with a dark substrate (F23). Pinto et al. 2020 on the sailfin tetra, darker on dark leaf litter (EMBER S23, verified there, not reopened). Koi: blue LED raised lightness and skin lutein and beta carotene for 30 to 60 days, too long harmed the skin (F17).
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. The practical reading: a fish that is pale first thing in the morning, or under a bright light over white sand, is often showing a normal response, not illness.

6.3 Water quality and illness
- Merck (fetch tool, quoted): skin protozoa, "Signs include dulled coloration, a light gray-white covering of mucus on the body of the fish"; larger parasites, "Fish become pale and can have skin sores"; mycobacteriosis lists "paleness"; gill infections, "Fish become pale, and their breathing becomes rapid and shallow."; anemia, "The most obvious sign that a fish is anemic is very pale gills." Intro: "Many of the disorders and diseases that are known to occur in fish are the result of stress, poor water quality..." (F19) Rummy-nose: faded "if kept in alkaline conditions" (F23). Glowlight: "more colourful when kept in acidic conditions" (F23). Ich: "lethargy, clamped fins, and dark coloration" (Merck Parasitic Diseases, verified in MOLLY M8, not reopened).
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. Loss of color is a sign in Merck's lists, never a diagnosis on its own; pale gills point to anemia or gill disease; dark color with clamped fins and white spots points to ich.

6.4 Breeding dress
- Malawi golden cichlid: dominant males "completely invert this pattern with the yellow and white regions becoming black, and the black stripes becoming white to iridescent blue", with changes in "pigment cell (chromatophore) number, intracellular dispersal of pigments, and tilting of reflective platelets (iridosomes) within iridophores" (F10). Ember: "Adult males are more intensely-coloured, especially when in spawning condition" (F23, EMBER S1). Guppy: carotenoid color is the male display and females prefer more of it (F5).
- Verdict: SUPPORTED in two species plus the guppy. Nothing on angelfish, discus or betta breeding color was opened.

## 7. Dyed, painted, tattooed and injected fish

- Eşanu et al. (verbatim): "Painted, dyed or tattooed fish are terms for a new variety of pet fish whose scales have been transformed from monochrome silver into vivid pigmentations using artificial colors or lasers". Injection: "The practice of dying live fish started in the late 1970s in Asian fish farms, with glasfish - Parambassis ranga"; "needles are used to inject dye under the skin by many punctures"; "The same needle is used to inject several fish." Dipping: "the fish being dipped in a caustic solution which removes the fishes mucus coat. Then the fish is dipped in a solution with the dye". Tattooing: "a special 'low intensity laser' which leaves a permanent mark" on parrot cichlids, livebearers and goldfish. Hormones: "fish are 'juiced' with hormones in order to obtain more intensive colours ... the treatment will also make the fish sterile, the intense colour only lasts for a few weeks". Outcomes: "The dyeing process cause a high mortality in fish"; "the dye eventually fades within six to ten months"; "using the same needle for a large number of fish greatly increases the risk of disease transmission"; the dip "removes the fishes' first line of protection whcih is the mucus layer". GloFish are distinguished: "genetically modified to fluoresce" and "just as hardy and healthy as the regular varieties". (F20) Every one of those outcome sentences cites a hobby page, a blog or a 1998 magazine feature, so the paper is a description of the trade, not evidence of rates.
- Seriously Fish on the black skirt (BLACK_SKIRT S21, verified there): dyed specimens "have been injected with garishly coloured dyes of various colours. The process is very stressful to the fish, and often shortens their lifespan considerably." (F23)
- Practical Fishkeeping campaign page (BLACK_SKIRT S24): exists, retailer pledge; not Sources-block material under the hobby-site rule (no byline, a news item).
- NOT FOUND at a citable tier: a mortality rate, the 1998 lymphocystis survey (search extract only), any legal ban.
- Verdict: the methods and the welfare direction are SUPPORTED (F20 for the methods, Seriously Fish for stress and shortened life); no figure is. Write what is done to the fish, that the color fades, that the process strips the slime coat or punctures the skin, and that no number for survival exists in the sources; do not print the 80 percent or the 40 percent lymphocystis figures.

### Angelfish
- Kouba et al. 2013 (OpenAlex record, verbatim): a microalgae product "characterised by a high content (approximately 5% w/w) of the carotenoid astaxanthin was included in a basal diet at 0.25, 0.50, 1.00 and 2.00 g per 100 g as a substitute for wheat flour", fed "at 3% of live weight to three replicates of 10 fish each for a period of 28 days". ### 1. Diet

- Clotfelter et al. (IDEAS record, verbatim): "We found that male redness is underlain by carotenoids and that females preferred to associate with red males over blue males". "Redder fish given supplemental carotenoids increased in both immune response (to a phytohemagglutination challenge) and redness compared with controls. In contrast, bluer fish given supplemental carotenoids did not become more red but instead benefited immunologically more so than either control or redder supplemented fish." Also: "Because vertebrates cannot synthesize carotenoids, limited dietary availability may impose a trade-off between maintaining ornamental coloration and health." (F33)
- Kattakdad et al. (abstract, verbatim): diets of "control feed, 5 % orange sweet potato (OrSP), 5 % yellow sweet potato (YeSP) and 5 % purple sweet potato (PuSP)", total carotenoids "15.63, 42.61, 38.79 and 32.32 mg/kg, respectively"; "Two-month male fishes" from a commercial farm; "After the 8 weeks rearing period", growth and FCR "were not significantly different between treatment groups (p > 0.05)"; "The lightness (L*) of fish fed with the control feed showed the highest value"; "The redness (a*) of fish fed with 5 % OrSP and 5 % YeSP was higher than in the other treatments (p ≤ 0.05)"; "the fish fed with 5% OrSP showed the highest carotenoid accumulation in skin and scale, dorsal fin, anal fin and caudal fin". The abstract does not name the color variety of the males. (F34)
- Already in this file: Dorce et al. 2025, annatto oil extract (an orange-red plant pigment) in red females for 28 days, skin color unchanged (F27); Gauer et al. 2025, pitaya bark extract in blue males for 21 days, color unchanged (F28).
- Seriously Fish (curl, verbatim): captive fish "should be offered plenty of small live or frozen foods such as Daphnia, Artemia or chironomid larvae (bloodworm) regularly to ensure development of optimal colour and condition." (F38)
- Varieties: Tang et al. 2022 compared "mono-color (Red, Blue, Black, White and Yellow), bi-color (Cambodian) and multi-color (Marble) varieties"; "A C > T2520 SNP in BsFzd4 and 10 of 11 SNPs in BsTyrp1a were found only in Black", with tyrosinase and Tyrp1a expression differing among varieties (F37). Tyrosinase and Tyrp1 are melanin-pathway genes, so a Black betta's black is set by its genes and its own melanin (F1: "Melanophores are capable of synthesizing their own melanin").
- Verdict: SUPPORTED, betta-specific, two studies: carotenoids in the food make a red betta redder (F33, controlled experiment, peer-reviewed, the strongest betta result found), and a carotenoid-bearing feed (5 percent orange or yellow sweet potato) raised redness and fin and skin carotenoid in young males over 8 weeks with no growth cost (F34). Blue bettas on the same supplement did not redden (F33). Not every pigment source works: annatto (F27) and pitaya bark (F28) changed nothing. Dose and duration for F33 were NOT READ (paywall). Which varieties respond: red, by F33; orange and yellow are carotenoid colors by the general rule (F1, F2) but no betta trial tested those varieties by name. Which cannot: blue, by F33; metallic, green and steel sheens are structural by the general rule (F1), not tested in a betta; black is melanin under genetic control (F37, F1). Astaxanthin, spirulina, marigold, paprika, shrimp or krill meal in a betta trial: NOT FOUND at a citable tier (search extracts only, above).

What reliably brings out color and condition is the small live and frozen food on the menu above, with daphnia, brine shrimp and bloodworms offered regularly.
