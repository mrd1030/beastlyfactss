# Molly (Poecilia sphenops, P. latipinna and trade forms): Research Notes

Researched 2026-11-01. Research only: no article copy here. Every claim below comes from a page opened and read in this session unless it is listed under "Seen but not opened". Quotes are short extracts as returned from the page or, for PDFs and HTML downloaded with curl, read directly. Where a source used an en dash in a range, the range is written here with "to", because this file carries no em or en dashes. Quoted text keeps the source's own spelling (Seriously Fish, Practical Fishkeeping and Fishkeeper are British).

Purpose: check every factual claim and figure in the molly pages so a later pass can correct them in one go. Builds on LIVEBEARER_GROUP_RESEARCH.md (its ids S1 to S11 are cited where reused; molly ids here are M1 onward). Format follows NEON_TETRA_RESEARCH.md and CARDINAL_TETRA_RESEARCH.md.

Pages checked: content/guides/molly-{cost,tank-setup,feeding,handling,health-issues,enrichment}-guide.mdx (no other molly-*.mdx exists), the molly hub in src/lib/data/guides/fish.js (id "molly", from line 1072; the Tank, Temperature, Water and Size comparison row at line 47), the encyclopedia entry in src/lib/data/encyclopedia/fish.js (line 143), and the molly lines of content/guides/platy-swordtail-molly-overview.mdx (lines 36 to 65; the overview has no Sources block).

Verdict labels: SUPPORTED, PARTLY (supported with a caveat or only for one of the two species), CONTRADICTED, UNSUPPORTED (no source found that states it).

A scoping fact that matters for every number: "molly" on these pages covers two wild species (the short-finned or sphenops molly and the sailfin molly), plus trade forms that are hybrids of several species. FishBase, Seriously Fish and Practical Fishkeeping give different numbers for the two species, and the guides use one set of numbers for all. Where the species differ, both values are given.

## 1. Identity, size, distribution, status

- Short-finned molly size. FishBase: "Max length : 7.5 cm SL male/unsexed"; common length 4.0 cm TL; maturity about 3.5 cm. (M12) Seriously Fish: "80mm SL" (3.1 in SL). (M14) Fishkeeper: "Maximum of 10cm (4")". (M19) Practical Fishkeeping: farmed mollies are usually smaller than wild ones. (M16) The unsigned TFH black molly page gives "6 to 15 cm (3½ to 6 inches)" for the trade forms as a whole. (M21)
- Sailfin size. FishBase: "15.0 cm TL male/unsexed" and "10.0 cm TL (female)". (M13) Seriously Fish: 125 mm SL (5 in SL). (M15) Florida Museum: maximum recorded 150 mm TL; males at one year "15-51 mm SL". (M10) Practical Fishkeeping: giant sailfin females "about 15 cm (5.9")", "such fish seem rare in aquaria". (M16)
- Distribution, short-finned. FishBase: "Central and South America: Mexico to Colombia." (M12) Seriously Fish: "native range runs north from Venezuela and Colombia to Mexico." (M14)
- Distribution, sailfin. FishBase: "Cape Fear drainage in North Carolina, USA to Veracruz, Mexico"; "Introduced to many countries." (M13) NEMESIS: established outside its range in California, Hawaii, Guam, the Philippines, Queensland, the Bahamas, Thailand, the Northern Marianas, New Zealand's Lake Taupo, and near Athens, Greece. (M6) Florida Museum: introduced California fish "have also reduced populations of the desert pupfish". (M10)
- IUCN status. FishBase reports Least Concern for P. sphenops (assessed 22 March 2018) and for P. latipinna (assessed 3 February 2019). FishBase also lists the sailfin molly as a "Potential pest". (M12, M13) Aquarium trade: "highly commercial" for both.
- Authority and etymology. P. sphenops Valenciennes, 1846 (FishBase, Seriously Fish). ETYFish: "Subgenus Mollienesia Lesueur 1821 ... -esia, adjectival suffix: in honor of Nicholas Francois, Count Mollien (1758-1850), one of Napoleon's finance ministers"; the original spellings "Mollinesia" and "Molienisia" are "considered typographical or copyist errors". P. latipinna: "latus, broad; pinna, fin, referring to enlarged dorsal fin of male". (M11)
- Genus change. Secondary sources (search results) say Rosen and Bailey placed Mollienesia in synonymy under Poecilia, citing 1959 for the priority point and 1963 for the monograph. ETYFish treats Mollienesia today as a subgenus of Poecilia. I could not open the 1963 monograph, so the exact year of the sinking is unverified.
- Trade forms. USGS NAS: black molly is "none; hybrid", "Likely aquarium release or escape from tropical fish farms"; "There are many hybrid varieties of several different Poecilia species" collectively "known as 'black mollies'". (M9, and S10 in the group file lists the four species as P. latipinna, P. mexicana, P. sphenops and P. velifera.) Seriously Fish: "Many aquarium-bred colour morphs of this species are available, including the ubiquitous black molly", some hybrids with P. latipinna, "the vast majority of the ones on sale being mass-produced in the Far East"; of the balloon form: "can cause swimbladder and digestive problems and may bring about premature death." (M14) TFH: the black molly is "Not a natural species"; domesticated mollies are hybrids of several natural species. (M21)

## 2. Tank size and footprint

- Seriously Fish short-finned: aquarium base 90 x 30 cm (36 x 12 in), "~81 litres ~21 US gal"; 90 x 30 x 30 cm. (M14) Seriously Fish sailfin: base 76 x 38 cm (30 x 15 in), "~87 litres ~23 US gal". (M15) FishBase short-finned: "minimum aquarium size of 60 cm". (M12)
- Practical Fishkeeping biotope article: "90x30x30cm (36x12x12")". (M17) TFH (Coletti): a 20-gallon long is the author's "bare minimum" for a trio of large sailfins; Norton used 30-gallon tanks for small batches. (M20) TFH black molly page: a 20-gallon tank is "the absolute smallest acceptable aquarium". (M21) PetMD: "10+ gallons, depending on species", but "A sailfin molly needs at least 30 gallons". (M22) Aquarium Co-Op: "at least 20 gallons of water", with 29 to 55 gallons better for larger species. (M26)
- Verdict on the guides: "20 gallons is the minimum for a small group" is SUPPORTED (Seriously Fish 21 gal for the short-fin; TFH; Aquarium Co-Op). "Sailfin varieties need 30 gallons or more" is PARTLY supported: PetMD says 30 gallons; Seriously Fish's own sailfin figure is 23 US gallons; TFH calls 20 long the minimum for a trio of large sailfins. The 30-gallon floor is a vet-site figure, not a measured requirement. Go "long and rectangular rather than tall" is SUPPORTED by every footprint above (all are 36 x 12 or 30 x 15 bases).
- "Mollies are capable jumpers" / "use a lid": no source found. UNSUPPORTED.

## 3. Temperature

- Seriously Fish short-finned: 21 to 28°C (70 to 82°F). (M14) Seriously Fish sailfin: 21 to 26°C (70 to 79°F). (M15) FishBase short-finned: "Tropical; 18°C - 28°C" (64 to 82°F). (M12) FishBase sailfin: "Subtropical; 20°C - 28°C". (M13) Practical Fishkeeping biotope: "22-28°C (72-82°F)". (M17) PetMD: "72 to 82 F" and no more than a 2 degree swing in 24 hours. (M22) NEMESIS sailfin: preferred range about 20 to 30°C; critical limits about 7.7°C to 41°C; "high temperature requirement for reproduction (22 C)". (M6) Fishkeeper short-finned: 18 to 28°C. (M19)
- Aquarium Co-Op: "raise the temperature to 75-80°F (24-27°C)" (for sick fish). (M26) TFH (Coletti): starts new arrivals around 80°F, then 70°F for wild species and 78°F for fancy strains. (M20)
- Verdict: "72 to 82°F" is SUPPORTED for the short-fin (Seriously Fish, FishBase top end 82, Practical Fishkeeping, PetMD) and PARTLY for the sailfin: Seriously Fish's sailfin range stops at 79°F, FishBase's at 82°F. The "no more than about 2°F swing" comes from PetMD only (vet-authored, no mechanism given); treat as PARTLY.
- Cold: no sailfin or short-fin cold tolerance study is cited by the guides. For reference, M3 found the critical thermal minimum of P. sphenops was 10°C (20 to 29°C cycle) or 11.7°C (26 to 35°C cycle), so cold alone at 70°F (21°C) is nowhere near lethal. The "cold water triggers shimmy" claim is not backed by any source (see section 10).

## 4. Water chemistry, hardness and salt

- pH. Seriously Fish: 7.0 to 8.5 (both species). (M14, M15) FishBase short-finned: pH 7.5 to 8.2. (M12) Practical Fishkeeping: pH 7.5 to 8.5. (M16, M17) PetMD: 7.5 to 8.5. (M22) Verdict: guide's "7.5 to 8.5" is SUPPORTED (Seriously Fish's floor is 7.0).
- Hardness. Seriously Fish: short-finned 15 to 30 dGH, sailfin 15 to 35 dGH. (M14, M15) FishBase short-finned dH 11 to 30. (M12) Practical Fishkeeping: "Aim to provide general hardness levels of 20°DH or higher and ensure the carbonate hardness is high as well, 10°KH or more." (M16) Practical Fishkeeping biotope: "moderate hardness (10-25°H)". (M17) Fishkeeper: dH 8 to 25. (M19) Practical Fishkeeping livebearers: poeciliids generally "moderately hard to hard water of about 10-30 GH". (M18)
- Seriously Fish on the cause: "this species must be maintained in moderately hard or harder water, with a basic pH. When kept in soft or acidic water, the fish weakens fairly rapidly, frequently indicated by shimmying, fungus, and/or clamped fins. Salt is not necessary, as it is the "hard" minerals (calcium, magnesium) that are crucial to the long-term health of this species." (M14, M15, same wording)
- Verdict on the guides: "medium-hard to hard" is SUPPORTED but gives the reader no number; the sources agree on roughly 15 dGH and up (Practical Fishkeeping asks for 20 dGH and KH 10). The enrichment guide's "hard, alkaline, mineral-rich water, not necessarily salt" is SUPPORTED by Seriously Fish almost word for word.
- Brackish. Seriously Fish short-finned: "all the morphs do better in slightly brackish conditions and are much more prone to disease when maintained in freshwater that is not hard and alkaline" (cited in the group file as S5; this is the page-top summary). Seriously Fish sailfin: "particularly associated with brackish habitats"; "often referred to as a brackish species but can live perfectly well in freshwater provided it is not soft and/or acidic" (group file S5). FishBase: short-finned "Freshwater; brackish", sailfin "Marine; freshwater; brackish". (M12, M13) Practical Fishkeeping: mollies "can range from fresh to brackish, although they can even be found in marine conditions near the coast"; "Salt reduces the toxicity of nitrate (and nitrite)"; tonic salt about 1 to 2 teaspoons per 10 L; brackish planted tank 4 to 6 g/L marine mix (SG 1.002 to 1.003). (M16, M17) Practical Fishkeeping livebearers: "Mollies may require salt as populations are known from brackish waters." (M18) Fishkeeper: short-finned "Does best in slightly brackish water"; salt required only for sailfin hybrid colour varieties. (M19) TFH (Coletti): the idea that mollies are brackish fish needing salt is "[w]rong!"; salt is "definitely not required for fancy mollies" and can hinder live plants. (M20) PetMD: "Many species benefit from freshwater salt" at "specific gravity 1.004". (M22)
- Hyper-salinity (Gonzalez 2005, M1), abstract verbatim in parts: "salinity range 35-95 ppt"; "Mollies were able to tightly control plasma Na(+) and Cl(-) concentrations and tissue water levels up to 65 ppt, but at higher salinities plasma ion levels began to rise and muscle water content dropped." "At 80 ppt, a salinity where plasma ion levels just begin to rise ..." "even at the highest salinity (90 ppt) plasma Na(+) and Cl(-) levels were only 32% and 39%, respectively, above levels at 35 ppt." Also: "the rate of O(2) consumption ... rose with salinity" and "growth was unaffected by hyper-salinity." Study species: P. latipinna, "an inhabitant of salt marshes along the gulf coast". NEMESIS: "a very wide salinity range, from freshwater to hypersaline conditions, 0-80 PSU." (M6)
- Verdict on the guides' salt story: "tightly regulate ... up to roughly 65 parts per thousand, well beyond typical seawater" is SUPPORTED for the sailfin molly (seawater is about 35 ppt; the paper's own baseline). Two caveats for the wording: (a) the paper is about P. latipinna in salt-marsh stock, not the short-fin or the trade hybrids, and (b) "without major physiological stress" is the guides' gloss: the abstract reports oxygen consumption rose with salinity (a metabolic cost) even though growth held. Safer: "regulate tightly up to about 65 ppt, with a higher metabolic cost". The overview's "tolerate salt concentrations well past normal seawater" is SUPPORTED for P. latipinna. Overview and hub "a rare-for-freshwater trait" is a framing, not a checked fact. Also: UF/IFAS says "Most freshwater fish can tolerate 5 ppt salinity for several weeks and many can live in 3 ppt permanently" (M7), so "unlike most freshwater fish" is overstated; the difference is degree.
- "Salt is disputed" is SUPPORTED: Seriously Fish and Coletti say not necessary; Practical Fishkeeping, Fishkeeper and PetMD lean toward benefit. The guides' framing (a real dispute) matches the sources.
- Salt and plants. UF/IFAS: "Plants in an aquarium or small ornamental pond may not tolerate increased salinity." (M7) SUPPORTED for plants. Salt and snails: no source opened. UNSUPPORTED.
- Salt type (aquarium salt vs marine mix vs table salt, iodine, anti-caking): no strong source opened; the guides cite Tankarium. UNSUPPORTED by anything I could replace it with (Gap). Practical Fishkeeping does say marine salt mix is better than tonic salt for a brackish tank (M16), which is the opposite emphasis from the guides' "never a marine or reef salt mix". Flag: the guides say a marine mix "can harm freshwater fish"; Practical Fishkeeping recommends a marine mix (4 to 6 g/L) for a brackish molly tank. That is a brackish tank, not a freshwater one, so the guides are not contradicted, but the "never" is stronger than the source.

## 5. Group size, sex ratio, temperament, tankmates

- Seriously Fish short-finned: "several females should be kept to every male"; "Peaceful but should only be kept with fish that can tolerate the same water conditions"; "Good tankmates include other poecilia, some rainbowfish and hard water tolerant barbs and tetras." (M14) Seriously Fish sailfin: "trios of 2 females to a single male as males can be quite vigorous in their pursuit of mates"; "may pick on smaller fish as it matures"; "Males can be aggressive to one another, particularly when spawning"; tankmates "other livebearers, some catfish". (M15) Practical Fishkeeping: "A ratio of one male to two or three females is ideal." (M18) Fishkeeper: "at least two females per male"; "Hardy and adaptable"; males may harass other species without enough of their own kind. (M19) Practical Fishkeeping A to Z: "Mollies are best kept alone. If mixing, choose fish with similar water needs"; liberty mollies "can be nippy"; balloon mollies "don't compete well with standard mollies or boisterous fish." (M16)
- Verdicts: "one male to two or three females" is SUPPORTED (Practical Fishkeeping, Seriously Fish). "Male mollies squabble among themselves" is SUPPORTED for the sailfin (Seriously Fish). "More than male guppies do" is UNSUPPORTED. "Peaceful" is PARTLY: Seriously Fish says peaceful for the short-fin but the sailfin "may pick on smaller fish" and Practical Fishkeeping says best kept alone; the tank guide's tankmate list ("cory catfish, danios, loaches, barbs and tetras that share its water") is PARTLY supported ("hard water tolerant barbs and tetras", "some catfish"). Danios and loaches have no source here.
- Hardy vs delicate. The handling guide says mollies are "widely considered one of the more forgiving species", the cost guide says "a species this hardy", and the hub says "shrugs at water". The enrichment guide says their "reputation for being delicate mostly traces back to being kept in conditions suited to a guppy". Sources: Seriously Fish, "years of inbreeding and over-production have left many of them weak and prone to disease", and fish in soft or acidic water "weaken fairly rapidly"; Practical Fishkeeping, giant sailfin females are "somewhat delicate" and prone to stunting and bacterial infections in suboptimal conditions, fancy forms best in mature tanks; TFH: "often recommended for beginners but ... also considered delicate"; "fancy mollies have a very low tolerance for ammonia" (Coletti). Fishkeeper says "Hardy and adaptable". Verdict: the pages contradict each other, and the sources lean toward "tough only in the right water, fragile otherwise". See Contradictions.

## 6. Diet and feeding

- Short-finned wild diet. Seriously Fish: "Omnivorous by nature, feeding on a variety of zoobenthos and detritus in the wild"; "a good proportion of the diet should be composed of vegetable matter". (M14) FishBase: "Adults feed on worms, crustaceans, insects, plant matter"; trophic level 3.0; eats green algae in aquaria. (M12)
- Sailfin wild diet. Seriously Fish: "Wild fish are almost exclusively herbivorous, feeding on algae and other plant material"; "If insufficient greenstuff is provided male dorsal fin development may be impaired." (M15) FishBase: "feed mainly on algae" and also rotifers, small crustaceans and aquatic insects. (M13) Florida Museum: adults mainly eat "algae and other plant materials", also aquatic invertebrates such as mosquito larvae. (M10) Florida Springs Institute: "Algae, crustaceans, and insects." (M24)
- Captive diet. Practical Fishkeeping A to Z: "Mostly plant-based. Spirulina flake and pellets are staples. Blanched lettuce and cooked spinach are popular. Bloodworms should be limited to 'once or twice a week at most.'" (M16) Fishkeeper: alternate flake with a green vegetable flake plus frozen foods; they graze on algae. (M19)
- Fry. Seriously Fish: fry "will accept brine shrimp nauplii or powdered flake from birth" (short-finned; sailfin adds microworm); "The adult fish will predate on the young". (M14, M15) Merck: "Fry and fingerlings can receive up to 5%" of body weight; adults "3% of their body weight daily"; "Most aquarium fish should be fed at least daily"; "Flaked feeds may be too large for a small-mouthed fish ... can be crushed". (M8, Nutritional page)
- Verdicts. "Omnivores that lean heavily herbivorous" / "primarily algae and plant material": SUPPORTED for the sailfin, PARTLY for the short-fin (omnivore, trophic level 3.0, worms and insects included). "Veggies several times a week" and zucchini, spinach, cucumber, lettuce, peas: SUPPORTED in kind (spinach, lettuce), the frequency (two to three times a week) is UNSUPPORTED. "Protein as a supplement": SUPPORTED. Bloodworms "around three feedings a week": CONTRADICTED by Practical Fishkeeping ("once or twice a week at most"); the guide hedges with "some sources". Fry on crushed flake or baby brine shrimp: SUPPORTED (Seriously Fish). Fry "at least 3 to 5 feedings" a day: UNSUPPORTED. Adults "once or twice a day": UNSUPPORTED as a molly figure (Merck: at least daily). "Pellets keep nutrition better than flake": UNSUPPORTED (Merck only says flakes are lighter than pellets). "An all-protein diet is tied to digestive and bloating problems": PARTLY (Seriously Fish: "a good proportion" vegetable; Practical Fishkeeping: tougher in plant-based diets; the bloating mechanism is not stated). "A pregnant female may eat less before giving birth": UNSUPPORTED. "Overfeeding drives constipation and swim bladder problems": UNSUPPORTED by an opened source; Seriously Fish ties swim bladder trouble to the balloon body shape instead.

## 7. Breeding: gestation, brood size, interval, sperm storage, fry

- Gestation. FishBase: P. sphenops "Produces 20 to 150 young after 28 days gestation."; P. latipinna "After 28 days gestation, this species produces 10 to 100 young." (M12, M13) NEMESIS: gestation about 28 days; "10-141 young"; newborns about 12 mm. (M6) Florida Museum: "approximately 3-4 weeks," varying with temperature; "broods of 10-140 live young." (M10) Fishkeeper: "Gestation is 28 days", 20 to 150 young. (M19) Seriously Fish: short-finned "Gestation takes around 8 weeks" with "up to 120 fry"; sailfin "taking around 2 months" with "up to 50 young". (M14, M15) Aquarium Co-Op: "30 to 60 days for the baby fish to arrive"; "more than 50 offspring". (M26) Practical Fishkeeping: "Livebearers typically take about four to seven weeks to produce a brood." (M18)
- Interval between broods (M2): "Sailfin mollies generally produce a brood every 28-64 days, according to earlier studies cited in the paper. In this study, stored-sperm females averaged 38.3 days (SD 14.17) between broods, and mated females averaged 34.5 days (SD 9.88)." (Snelson et al. 1986 as seen in a search snippet: 26 to 50 days, mode 33.) Seriously Fish's "8 weeks / 2 months" is most likely the whole cycle, not time carried; no opened source reconciles the gap.
- Brood size in the lab (M2): mated females mean 34.87 young (SD 9.52, n=15); stored-sperm females mean 21.33 (SD 13.90, n=12). Of 16 stored-sperm females, 4 produced no brood in 90 days.
- Sperm storage. Florida Museum: "females may store sperm long after the demise of their relatively short-lived mates." (M10) M2: "Stored sperm may remain viable for over a year after insemination" (a general statement from earlier poeciliid work, not measured in this study); the study tested only the first brood after isolation. No source I opened gives a measured number of broods from one mating for P. latipinna or P. sphenops. For the guppy, Gasparini 2018 (S6) gives up to eight monthly cycles; do not transfer that to the molly.
- Verdicts. "A single mating can keep a female producing fry": SUPPORTED, with the caveat that M2 found stored-sperm broods were smaller (21 against 35). "Gestation roughly 4 to 6 weeks" (handling guide, hub): PARTLY; FishBase, NEMESIS and Florida Museum say about 28 days (3 to 4 weeks), so "about 4 weeks" is the supported figure, and 4 to 6 weeks fits the whole brood cycle (M2: means of 34 to 38 days). "A brood roughly every 4 to 6 weeks" (cost guide): SUPPORTED (M2 means of 34 to 38 days, but individual intervals run 28 to 64 days). "A typical brood runs 20 to 60": PARTLY; no source gives a "typical" figure; M2's means are 21 to 35, FishBase ranges are 10 to 100 (latipinna) and 20 to 150 (sphenops). "Documented range as wide as 10 to over 100": SUPPORTED (FishBase 20 to 150, Florida Museum 10 to 140, NEMESIS 10 to 141). "Mollies run larger broods than guppies": UNSUPPORTED here (I did not collect guppy brood figures; FishBase's guppy page is in S1).
- Parental cannibalism: SUPPORTED ("The adult fish will predate on the young"; "heavily planted if the fry are to survive predation by the parents and other fish"). Dense planting helps: SUPPORTED (Seriously Fish; Practical Fishkeeping biotope suggests hornwort as "a refuge for any fry").
- Superfetation: not found for molly. The guides do not claim it.

## 10. Health: shimmy, ich, velvet, fin rot, dropsy, swim bladder, quarantine

Shimmy
- Seriously Fish: "frequently indicated by shimmying, fungus, and/or clamped fins" when kept in soft or acidic water. (M14, M15) TFH Coletti (hypothesis, not data): shimmy has three parts: osmotic shock from moving fish out of salted farm water, ammonia buildup during shipping, and crowded or poor conditions in shops and home tanks; Clapsaddle says "even the smallest amount" of ammonia can cause clamping and shimmying; Coletti avoids salt and recommends biological filtration, an ammonia neutralizer, and 25 to 33% water changes weekly or biweekly. (M20) No veterinary, university or extension source on shimmy found; a search returned only forums and blogs.
- Verdicts. Shimmy as a symptom of soft or acidic water and poor water quality: SUPPORTED (Seriously Fish, Coletti on ammonia). "Not a disease, a sign of lost nerve and muscle control": UNSUPPORTED (no source states the nerve and muscle mechanism). "Low temperature" and "chlorine exposure" as triggers, "temperature instability a commonly cited trigger", and the fix "raise temperature toward 76 to 80°F": UNSUPPORTED. "Fancy mollies bred overseas in harder, more mineral-rich, brackish-leaning water, then sold into softer water, trigger shimmy": PARTLY (Seriously Fish: mass-produced in the Far East; Coletti: osmotic shock from salted farm water; "brackish-leaning" and "harder" are not stated). "1 tablespoon of aquarium salt per 5 gallons in a hospital tank, commonly cited for shimmy": UNSUPPORTED as a shimmy dose; that exact dose appears in VCA for ich (M23). Practical Fishkeeping's sailfin entry gives 5 to 30 g per 10 L as an optional salt level, which is 2 to 15 times stronger than a tablespoon per 5 gallons on my arithmetic (a level tablespoon is about 17 g, my figure, not from a source).

Ich
- UF/IFAS (M7): "One Ich organism will produce up to 1024 individuals in one generation" (the PDF text says "as many as 1024 new 'daughter' parasites (tomites)"); "At warm temperatures (75°F to 79°F), the life cycle is completed in about 3 to 6 days. At these temperatures, chemical treatments should be applied daily, and a minimum of 3 to 5 treatments are required." "at warmer water temperatures (75 to 79°F), use of 4 to 5 g/L (= 4 to 5 ppt) salt (sodium chloride) in a prolonged bath for 7 to 10 days is another effective treatment in smaller systems, provided the fish species can handle the increased salt concentration." Theronts are "intolerant to increased salinity levels of 3 to 5 ppt". Copper: "NEVER use copper sulfate without first testing the total alkalinity"; "Do not use copper sulfate if the total alkalinity is less than 50 mg/L"; "If you do not know the alkalinity of your water and cannot measure it, DO NOT USE COPPER SULFATE." Formalin "usually applied at a concentration of 25 mg/L". "a minimum 30-day quarantine period is recommended for new fish." UF/IFAS does not mention malachite green or a heat-only method.
- Merck (M8, Parasitic): signs "lethargy, clamped fins, and dark coloration; white dots are often visible"; "Formalin or copper are often drugs of choice"; "At warm temperatures typical of home aquaria (eg, >26°C), infected fish should be treated daily"; freshwater salinity option "5 g/L for one week at >=24°C (75°F)"; over-the-counter pet products "often contain formalin and malachite green". No heat-only method.
- VCA (M23): "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF." "Not all fish can stand the heat, though." Salt: "about a tablespoon of salt per 5 gallons of water", for scaled fish only; quarantine sentence is about infected fish, so do not use it for new-fish quarantine.
- Does the molly tolerate 86°F (30°C)? Yes by physiology, no by listed husbandry range. M3: P. sphenops had an upper incipient lethal temperature of 38.8 to 39.5°C and a critical thermal maximum of 40°C (20 to 29°C acclimation) or above 42°C (26 to 35°C acclimation), so 30°C is far below lethal. NEMESIS gives a critical maximum of 41°C and a preferred range of 20 to 30°C for the sailfin. Husbandry ranges stop lower: FishBase and Seriously Fish both top out at 28°C (82°F) for P. sphenops; Seriously Fish gives 26°C (79°F) for the sailfin. So a hold at 86°F is not dangerous to the fish on thermal grounds, but warm water holds less oxygen, which is why VCA stresses aeration. The guides' "warm slowly to about 82°F with an air stone" is SUPPORTED and conservative; it is below the 86°F that VCA says kills most strains. No source recommends a duration for the heat hold (Gap).
- Molly-specific salt: because mollies handle 65 ppt (M1), the UF/IFAS 4 to 5 g/L bath is well inside what they tolerate. The guides' "aquarium salt or an ich medication" is SUPPORTED but gives no concentration for the ich use; the tablespoon-per-5-gallons figure (about 1 g/L on my arithmetic) is one quarter to one fifth of UF/IFAS's 4 to 5 g/L, which is the stronger, evidence-based dose for a salt-tolerant fish.
- Copper "last pick because the dose depends on alkalinity": SUPPORTED by UF/IFAS (the alkalinity rule), though the guides say "dose depends" while UF/IFAS says never use without testing. Malachite green and formalin as medications: SUPPORTED (Merck; formalin dose in UF/IFAS).

Velvet, fin rot, dropsy, swim bladder
- Velvet (Piscinoodinium): Merck mentions it in a protistan table ("Copper sulfate, chloroquine (nonfood fish only)") and in the text as "frequently seen on zebrafish and some barbs"; no dedicated section. The guide's "gold or rust-colored dust, appetite loss, lethargy, scratching, high death rates if not caught early, contagious" is UNSUPPORTED by any page I could open (no veterinary page with that detail).
- Fin rot: "bacterial in origin, tied to poor water quality, ammonia, untreated injury": Merck "Bacterial Diseases of Fish" says Aeromonas outbreaks are frequently associated with poor water quality and does not mention fin rot by name. PARTLY (water-quality link only).
- Dropsy: Merck "Disorders and Diseases of Fish" lists "dropsy" and "pinecone disease" among signs and calls Aeromonas "the most common bacterial infection of freshwater aquarium fish"; also says many fish disorders "are the result of stress, poor water quality, overcrowding". SUPPORTED for the Aeromonas link and the water-quality pattern. "The hardest condition to treat, poor outlook": UNSUPPORTED (Merck says antimicrobials if fish are dying).
- Swim bladder: Seriously Fish ties it to the balloon body shape in mollies. The guides say "usually from overfeeding or constipation" and "short fast": UNSUPPORTED for mollies. The guides never mention the balloon form's built-in swim bladder risk, which two sources do (Seriously Fish, TFH-unsigned does not; Practical Fishkeeping says balloon forms need mature tanks).
- Quarantine at least 30 days: SUPPORTED (UF/IFAS, "minimum 30-day quarantine period is recommended for new fish").
- Stress, poor water quality, overcrowding as the common thread: SUPPORTED (Merck).
- "Mollies rarely see a vet; dedicated aquatic vets are uncommon": UNSUPPORTED (no source).

## 11. Behavior and welfare research (enrichment guide)

- Plath et al. 2008 (M5), summary: "To avoid unwanted copulations, surface females are more vigilant in the presence of a male than in the presence of a female, which detracts from the time spent feeding. Reduced sexual activity in cave mollies comes along with reduced vigilance in females, so cave molly females do not feed less when accompanied by a male." Species: Atlantic molly, Poecilia mexicana.
- Koehler et al. 2011 (M4): the record gives the title and keywords (sexual conflict, foraging, oxygen consumption, ventilation rate) but no abstract; a search summary of it said females' feeding times were "dramatically reduced when being pursued by a harassing male" and that the effect appeared with only visual contact. That summary is secondhand.
- Plath et al. 2007 (not opened) compares nine species including P. latipinna.
- Verdicts. "Females flee, are more vigilant, and have hampered feeding opportunities" in P. mexicana: SUPPORTED (M5; M4 by its title). "Males attempt to mate almost constantly while females are largely non-responsive": UNSUPPORTED in the wording; I could not read the text that carries it (probably the guppy-paper discussion the guide leans on, which names P. mexicana as "closely related"). The cave-form exception is not mentioned in the guides and is relevant: the finding applies to surface-dwelling Atlantic mollies. P. mexicana is a wild species related to the trade mollies (it is one of the four parents in the USGS list), not the sphenops or latipinna the guides are about. The "Borrowed evidence, partly from a molly" line in the Sources block is honest about that.
- "Mollies are the largest of the common livebearers" (enrichment FAQ): CONTRADICTED. The group file records FishBase giving swordtail females 16.0 cm (6.3 in) against the sailfin molly's 15.0 cm (male) and the short-fin's 7.5 cm SL.
- Space "mollies are big enough that overstocking hits water quality faster than it does with guppies": a reasonable inference from size; no source states it. UNSUPPORTED but low risk.
