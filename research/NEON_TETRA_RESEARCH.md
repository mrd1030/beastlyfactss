## 1. Identity, size, sexing

1.1 Adult size
- Seriously Fish: "30 mm SL (1.2 in SL)". (N12)
- FishBase: max length 2.5 cm SL. (N11)
- Fishkeeper: maximum "4cm (1.58")" (looks like total length; lower confidence). (N18)
- Summary: 3 cm standard length is the best-referenced maximum.

1.2 Sexing
- Seriously Fish: mature females are "normally noticeably rounder-bodied" and slightly larger than males. (N12)
- The guide's detail that a female's blue stripe looks "slightly bent" was not found in any opened source (see Gaps).

1.3 Authority
- FishBase: Myers, 1936. (N11)

## 2. Wild habitat, wild trade, conservation

2.1 Range and habitat
- Seriously Fish: Colombia, Brazil and Peru in the Amazon basin; the species "may be widespread" but its true extent "remains unclear." Habitat: "Mostly inhabits forest streams and minor tributaries rather than turbid (whitewater) main channels." (N12)
- FishBase: blackwater or clearwater stream tributaries of the Solimoes River. (N11)
- Practical Fishkeeping (Bleher): "only known from the western Amazon, Peru and parts of western Amazon state in Brazil." (N13)
- Measured wild water chemistry for neon tetra: none found in a citable source (see Gaps).

2.2 Wild-caught vs captive-bred (supports the cost guide's "almost entirely captive-bred")
- Practical Fishkeeping (Bleher, 2009): "Rarely do wild-caught specimens of this species reach the hobby", and it calls the neon "the world's most widely bred aquarium fish." (N13)
- Fishkeeper: "Most specimens in the aquatics trade are captive-bred." and wild imports "are very rarely seen." (N18)
- Seriously Fish (paraphrase): most aquarium fish are commercially produced and "are more adaptable than wild specimens." Years of commercial breeding have "somewhat affected the genetic vigour of the ornamental strain." (N12)
- No trade statistic (percent wild, volume) found in any opened source.
- The guides say commercial breeding left "deformities" widespread in farmed neons. Seriously Fish supports only the weaker "genetic vigour somewhat affected" wording. Deformities were not found in any opened source.

2.3 Conservation status
- FishBase: IUCN Least Concern (LC), assessed 04 March 2021; resilience "High, minimum population doubling time less than 15 months"; fisheries and aquarium trade both "highly commercial." (N11)
- The IUCN page itself returned 403. Treat the LC and 2021 date as "via FishBase."

## 3. Lifespan

- No authoritative source found for neon tetra lifespan, wild or captive. Seriously Fish, FishBase and Fishkeeper give none. The Animal Diversity Web neon page exists but holds only taxonomy.
- The cost guide's "around 5 years achievable, often 2 to 3 years" has no supporting source in anything opened. Search results offered only hobby sites (not opened). Either soften to no number, or cut. See Gaps.

## 4. Tank

4.1 Tank size and footprint
- Seriously Fish: base "60 x 30 cm (24 x 12 in) or larger, with a volume of about 54 litres (14 US gal)." (N12)
- FishBase: kept in groups of 5 or more "in a tank at least 60 cm long." (N11)
- Practical Fishkeeping (cardinal profile, covers neon too): cardinal and neon tetra "no smaller than 60cm (24")". (From the cardinal profile, see cardinal notes C13.)
- Fishkeeper gives no litre figure. (N18)
- CONTRADICTION with the tank guide: it calls "10 gallons the practical minimum for a proper school of 6." Every opened reference sets about 60 cm (24 in) and about 54 L (14 US gal). A 10 gallon does not meet that (its standard 20 in length is general knowledge, not from a source here; verify the owner's 10 gallon dimensions before rewriting).

4.2 Temperature
- Seriously Fish: 21 to 25°C (70 to 77°F). (N12)
- FishBase: 20 to 26°C. (N11)
- Fishkeeper: "20-26°C". (N18)
- Practical Fishkeeping: neon tetras "a maximum of 25°C (77°F)". (cardinal profile, C13)
- Breeding temperature: Seriously Fish 26.5 to 29°C (80 to 84°F) (N12); Robertson's two successful spawnings ran 24°C (75°F) and 26°C (78°F) (N14).
- CONTRADICTION: the tank guide says "70 to 81°F works" and the health guide calls 81°F "the top of a neon's workable range." No opened source gives 81°F. The references stop at 77°F (Seriously Fish, PFK) or 26°C / 79°F (FishBase, Fishkeeper). The 72 to 78°F "solid target" sits inside the references.
- Heat tolerance, lab data: see 7.4 (N3).

4.3 pH
- Seriously Fish: 4.0 to 7.5. Breeding 5.5 to 6.5. (N12)
- Fishkeeper: "pH: 5.0-7.5". (N18). FishBase: 5.0 to 7.0. (N11)
- The guide's 6.0 to 7.0 sits inside every reference range (narrower than any of them).

4.4 Hardness
- Seriously Fish: 1 to 12 dGH; breeding gH 1 to 5. (N12)
- Fishkeeper: "dH: 1-12 degrees"; breeding "hardness <5 degrees dH". (N18)
- FishBase lists "dH 1 to 2" (an outlier; do not use).
- Robertson: spawning tanks ran "DH of less than 1." (N14)

4.5 School size
- Seriously Fish: "a mixed-sex group of at least 8-10 specimens." (N12)
- FishBase: "groups of 5 or more." (N11)
- Fishkeeper: "should be kept in large groups." (N18)
- Saxby et al. (N4): neon tetras "displayed reduced aggression and darting" and "spent more time shoaling in larger groups"; principal components analysis indicated "improved welfare in larger groups of neon tetras." The repository abstract does not state the group sizes. The Practical Fishkeeping news item (N17) states they tested "group sizes of one, two, five and 10 fish per tank" for neon tetras and white cloud mountain minnows, with "the lowest levels of aggression and darting in the larger group sizes" and "latency to feed decreasing with increased group size in Neon tetras."
- CONTRADICTION: the handling guide says "Six is the floor." Seriously Fish says at least 8 to 10; FishBase says 5 or more. The enrichment guide's "ten" is the best-supported number.
- Species caveat in the abstract: the effect was species-specific (angelfish showed no clear group size effect).

4.6 Lighting
- No neon-specific source for "8 to 10 hours" or "dim to moderate" found. Robertson says darkness is critical for spawning ("the darkness is" critical) and eggs and fry "are susceptible to light." (N14)
- For reference only: the Kasprzak trial ran neons on "a 10 h:14 h" light:dark photoperiod at 25 ± 0.5°C, pH about 6.4. It is an experimental protocol, not a husbandry recommendation. (N5)
- Cardinal-specific light work exists (see CARDINAL_TETRA_RESEARCH.md, Linhares et al. 2018) but must not be stated as neon fact.

## 5. Diet and feeding

5.1 Wild diet
- Seriously Fish: likely omnivorous, eating "small invertebrates, crustacea, filamentous algae, fallen fruit and suchlike." Note the "likely": no gut-content study of wild neons was found. (N12)
- FishBase: adults eat worms, small insects, crustaceans and plant matter; trophic level 2.9 (± 0.32 SE) "based on food items." (N11)

5.2 Captive diet
- Seriously Fish: dried foods can sustain them but "does best" on a varied menu including live and frozen bloodworm, mosquito larvae, Daphnia and Moina. (N12)
- Fishkeeper: "Offer a good quality flake food, supplemented with small frozen foods such as daphnia and cyclops." (N18)
- Merck: "Feeding a wide variety of feeds is one way to try to meet the nutritional requirements." (N9)

5.3 Food size
- Merck (general fish, not neon-specific): "Flaked feeds may be too large for a small-mouthed fish to easily ingest." and "Such feeds can be crushed to enable easy ingestion." "The feed should be in particles easily ingested by the fish." (N9)
- The guide's "mouths only about 1 to 2 mm across" has no source in anything opened. Cut the number or find a measurement (see Gaps).

5.4 Frequency and ration
- Merck: "Most aquarium fish should be fed at least daily"; "adult fish should be fed 3% of their body weight daily for maintenance." (General, not neon-specific.) (N9)
- Kasprzak 2021 trial (neon tetras about 2 cm total length): fed twice daily (09:00 and 16:00), "about 3-4% of body weight," with one starvation day per week; survival over 12 weeks was 70% pooled and 56% to 81% by diet group. This is an experimental protocol on sub-adults, not advice. (N5)
- The guide's "twice daily, what they finish in 2 to 3 minutes" and "fry 3 to 4 feedings" have no support in opened sources. Twice daily is consistent with the Kasprzak protocol and with Merck's "at least daily" minimum.

5.5 Diet quality finding (optional "did you know")
- Kasprzak 2021: neon tetras grew significantly only on the TetraMin flake diet (275 to 384 mg); there were "no differences in final body weight between groups"; authors note other work suggesting higher-protein (above 50%) diets. (N5) Small sample, 12 weeks, wholesaler-sourced fish of unstated origin; do not generalize.

## 6. Behavior and tankmates

- Seriously Fish: "Generally peaceful"; suitable companions include similarly sized characids, lebiasinids, small callichthyid or loricariid catfish, and non-predatory small-to-medium cichlids. (N12)
- Whether adult angelfish eat neons once past 3 inches (tank guide) was not found in an opened source.
- Fin-nipping tankmates disrupting schooling: no opened source.

## 7. Health

7.1 Neon tetra disease (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis)
- Merck: "Neon tetra disease is caused by Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, which infects the skeletal musculature"; affected fish listed include tetras, angelfish, rasboras, barbs and zebrafish. For microsporidia generally: "Microsporidia have a direct life cycle; therefore, horizontal transmission in an aquarium is likely." and "The spores are extremely resistant, and microsporidia are considered nontreatable." (N6)
- Sanders et al. 2010: the neon tetra is the primary and type host; the parasite infects "some 20 species of freshwater fishes in four orders" (families named: Characidae, Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Cichlidae). Spores were given orally by adding them to water: 7 of 10 fifteen-day-old zebrafish became infected. The authors assume infection starts when fish ingest spores and suggest spores released from dead fish are a likely source. Prevention advice: quarantine and PCR screening of brood fish, and "it would be prudent to not mix zebrafish used in research with other aquarium fishes." (N1) A chlorine-resistance statement appears in the PMC4155925 text as returned; the 2012 review (N2) says chlorine would "likely" kill it. They conflict, so do not use either.
- Sanders et al. 2012: the parasite is "very common in the aquarium trade, often resulting in considerable mortality"; it "has been described in a broad range of fish hosts"; infected fish can be "harbored by otherwise healthy-appearing fish"; transmission is "presumably per os", shown by experimental exposure for this parasite; cohabitation on a shared rack also spread it; "There are currently no known treatments for microsporidiosis in zebrafish" (general statement, not specific to neon tetra). Diagnosis: wet mounts of skeletal muscle from "opaque lesions," spores with a "very prominent posterior vacuole." "No PCR-based assays currently exist for the specific detection of P. hyphessobryconis" (as of 2012). (N2)
- Seriously Fish: NTD is "virtually untreatable once it reaches an advanced stage"; avoid fish showing restlessness, white skin patches or separating from the school. (N12)
- Cardinal tetra as a named NTD host: not named in any opened paper. Merck says "tetras" and the 2010 paper lists the family Characidae. The cardinal guide's cardinal-specific claim has only the family-level support.
- Claims not found in any opened source: "film forms under the skin, not on top"; spores "in muscle, liver and spleen" (opened sources say skeletal muscle, and for zebrafish also ovary and visceral macrophages in the search summary only); that aquatic specialists recommend euthanasia for confirmed cases.

7.2 Columnaris lookalike
- Seriously Fish: columnaris (Flavobacterium columnare) "can mimic NTD" and can be treated with antibacterial products, so isolate and treat symptomatic fish. (N12)
- The Merck bacterial diseases page (opened) names columnaris as most common in warmwater species but gave no signs or treatment I could quote; do not cite it for the lookalike claim.

7.3 Ich (white spot) and quarantine
- UF/IFAS CIR920: "At the warm water temperatures required for many aquarium fish, active disease will often become evident 1-3 weeks after shipping. For this reason, a minimum 30-day quarantine period is recommended for new fish." At 75 to 79°F the Ich life cycle completes in about 3 to 6 days; chemical treatments should be daily, "a minimum of 3 to 5 treatments"; "Treatments should never be discontinued until all mortality from Ich has stopped." Salt: at 75 to 79°F, 4 to 5 g/L (4 to 5 ppt) in a prolonged bath for 7 to 10 days "provided the fish species can handle the increased salt concentration." (N7)
- Merck: "At warm temperatures typical of home aquaria (eg, >26°C), infected fish should be treated daily"; "Three to seven treatments may be required." Protistan table: a freshwater salinity option "for one week at >=24°C (75°F)." Merck does not give a heat-only method. (N8)
- VCA: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF." and "Not all fish can stand the heat, though." Replication is faster in warmer water. Salt: "about a tablespoon of salt per 5 gallons" for scaled fish. Quarantine wording: "Quarantine infected fish for at least 30 days before introducing any new fish to their tank." This is ambiguous (it says infected fish). For the new-fish 30-day rule cite UF/IFAS (N7) instead. (N10)
- CONFLICT on salt dose: the health guide gives aquarium salt at "a tablespoon per 5 gallons" (matches VCA, N10). UF/IFAS gives 4 to 5 g/L (4 to 5 ppt) for 7 to 10 days (N7). Taking a level tablespoon as roughly 17 g (my arithmetic, not from a source), VCA's dose is about 1 g/L, so UF/IFAS is several times stronger. The guide should say which one it follows and that the UF/IFAS bath applies only where the fish species can handle the salt.

7.4 The 86°F question for neon tetras
- Cooper et al. 2019 (N3): neon tetras acclimated for about two weeks at constant 26, 29 and 31°C, then tested. CTMax rose with acclimation temperature; the chronic lethal thermal maximum for neon tetra was 33.5°C (92.3°F); the authors say chronic limits sit only 4 to 5°C above current average natural temperatures. Oxygen consumption did not differ significantly across the three acclimation temperatures; Q10 between 26 and 31°C was 1.92. (Search-result text also gave CTMax values of about 38.5 to 39.6°C; those figures were not on the opened page, so do not quote them.)
- Reading: neon tetras survive sustained 31°C (87.8°F) in a lab, so "86°F is past what this fish tolerates" overstates the data as a survival claim. But every husbandry reference caps the range at 77 to 79°F, and no study tests ich treatment on neon tetras. A safe wording: "86°F is well above the 70 to 77°F range keepers use, and no source tests holding neons there for an ich treatment." Source for tolerance limits: N3.
- Fish origin and welfare endpoints beyond survival were not read in the abstract; the full text was not opened.

7.5 Euthanasia
- TAMU IACUC guidance (restating AVMA 2020): clove oil and derivatives are "not FDA approved for use as an agent of euthanasia", described as extra-label in fish in the AVMA 2020 guidelines; "Fish should be left in the anesthetic solution for a minimum of 10 minutes after cessation of opercular movement"; "Rapid freezing of conscious fish is not considered humane." The text says 0.20 mL of clove oil has been recommended for "small tropical fishes" in studies but does not state the water volume, so no usable dose. (N19)
- Merck's mycotic page says nothing on euthanasia for NTD. No source opened recommends euthanasia specifically for confirmed NTD.

## 8. Handling and acclimation

General small-fish guidance, nothing neon-specific except where noted.
- Practical Fishkeeping (acclimatising): keep fish in "fairly dark conditions"; float the bag; "Repeat this every few minutes until the bag contains more water from your aquarium than the shop"; float acclimation "could take anywhere from 30-60 minutes"; drip acclimation "is likely to take a couple of hours" at "1-2 drips per second"; "it is good practice not to pour the contents of the whole bag into your aquarium"; leave lights off for an hour or two after. (N15)
- Practical Fishkeeping (bagging): "Aim to have a one-to three ratio: one part water, three parts air."; put bags "into a dark container, or wrap them in a towel or some newspapers"; do not have a friend hold a half-filled bag while you tip fish in from a net; float the bag open and net the fish straight into it. (N16)
- "Neons react to water chemistry shifts more than most community fish": no source found (see Gaps).

## 9. Breeding basics

- Seriously Fish: breeding pH 5.5 to 6.5, gH 1 to 5, 26.5 to 29°C. (N12)
- Robertson: spawning with a pH of 5.5 (tank one) and pH under 5 (tank two), DH under 1, 24°C and 26°C. "The eggs and fry are susceptible to light." Neons "colour up at about four weeks"; 12 weeks is "about the age of most we see for sale." (N14)
- FishBase: females lay a relatively small number of eggs that hatch in about 24 hours. (N11) The handling guide says 24 to 36 hours; swim-free "3 to 4 days" is in FishBase only for the cardinal tetra, not the neon.

### Substrate (owner's top priority)

S1. Do neons use the bottom?
- FishBase: "Freshwater; pelagic". (N11) A midwater fish: substrate is for plants, cover and chemistry, not for the fish to forage or dig in.
- Seriously Fish describes the wild bottom as "covered in fallen branches, tree roots and leaf litter". (N12)
- No source opened says neons feed from or sift the substrate. Food that sinks to the bottom and is left there is the feeding guide's existing point.

S2. Sand vs gravel and grain size
- Seriously Fish: the species "perhaps looks best in an arrangement comprising a sandy substrate plus some driftwood roots and branches." (N12) That is an aesthetic and biotope suggestion, not a health requirement.
- Tropica: "Different kinds and colours of gravel with a grain size from 0,8-4 mm will ensure a good water circulation" (about 0.03 to 0.16 in). (E2)
- PFK (Clarke): "very fine substrates, such as sand, don't allow much dirt to be trapped"; "more dirt may get trapped in the substrate if you use a larger grain size"; silver sand "tends to stagnate if used in a deep layer." (E1)
- Reading for the guide: either works. Sand keeps waste on the surface where it can be siphoned; gravel in the 1 to 4 mm range keeps water moving through the bed for roots.

S3. Color: dark vs light
- Seriously Fish: color is "becoming lighter in bright conditions with pale substrate or background and clear water," and the fish turn "duller with a violet lateral stripe at night." (N12) This is the first neon-specific source found for the tank guide's restored line "A darker color makes neon coloring pop" (see "Resolves earlier gaps" below).
- Mechanism: the stripe is structural color from iridophores whose platelet angle shifts; the stripe "can change from yellow to violet depending on the time of the day and the mood of the fish." (E8, reporting Yoshioka et al. 2010) The article does not tie the change to substrate.
- A pale or washed-out neon on a white or bright bed can be normal background response, not illness. A neon that is pale at night or first thing in the morning is showing its normal night coloring. (Both readings rest on N12.)
- No neon-specific study tying substrate color to stress or cortisol was found. Search results showed cross-species tank-color studies (clownfish, oscar, tilapia) with conflicting directions; none was opened, so nothing is cited from them.

S4. Depth: bare layer vs rooted plants
- PFK: "For sand, most people go for a depth of around 2.5cm/1"" (E1)
- Tropica (gravel over a nutrient base): gravel "sloping upwards and backwards with at least 3-4 cm in the front and 6-8 cm in the back" (about 1.2 to 1.6 in front, 2.4 to 3.1 in back), with "a layer of ½-1 cm substrate" (about 0.2 to 0.4 in) of nutrient base under the gravel, placed "in the places where plants with strong roots will be planted." (E2)
- Tropica Aquarium Soil used alone: their worked example uses an 8 cm (about 3.1 in) layer, deeper toward the back. (E3)
- Reading: a thin sand layer of about 1 in suits a lightly planted or epiphyte tank (anubias, java fern on wood); rooted plants want roughly 2 to 3 in.

S5. Planted options
- Aquasoil (fired soil granules): Tropica: "Aquarium Soil is also a substrate that lowers pH and slightly affects water chemistry"; grain "2-3 mm"; "we recommend changing 25-50% of the water at least twice a week during the first 4 weeks after setting up the aquarium." (E3) ADA Amazonia per the Aquasabi wiki (WEAK): the soil "is originally acidic, with a pH level of 5.0-6.0, and it makes water more acidic"; "a large amount of ammonium (NH4) leaches into water" early on, more dangerous above pH 7, so stock fish "two to three weeks after planting"; usable "for two to three years" before nutrients run down. (E5)
- Capped soil: Tropica's own method is a nutrient base (1/2 to 1 cm) under gravel (E2), which is the capped approach. No source opened for a garden-soil (Walstad style) capped tank.
- Inert substrate plus root tabs: Seachem says Flourite "will not alter the pH of the water"; "Gravel modifiers such as laterite are not necessary"; Flourish Tabs "can be useful and helpful, but are not necessities." (E4) No independent source was opened for root tab dosing intervals.
- PFK: laterite is "impossible to wash" and a gravel cleaner run over a planting substrate risks "sucking it out." (E1)

S6. Effect on water chemistry and fit with the neon's range
- The neon's reference ranges (already in this file): pH 4.0 to 7.5, 1 to 12 dGH (N12); wild water has negligible carbonate hardness (N12, habitat section).
- Lowering: aquasoils lower pH (E3, E5). Fits the neon's range; the cost is the early ammonium leach and the frequent water changes in month one (E3, E5).
- Raising: PFK: "Coral sand and gravel is calcareous"; use it only if you want "to make your water rock hard and increase the pH of your water to over 8.0"; "Even standard pea gravel contains sufficient calcium to boost the pH and hardness"; "Inert substrates and rocks don't alter the water chemistry, while non-inert, calcareous substrates do." (E1) A pH over 8 is above every neon reference range.
- Old tank pH crash: Merck describes old tanks with infrequent water changes and topping off seeing "either a gradual or dramatic drop in pH," and "If the pH decline is rapid, the result could be death for the resident fish." (E14) Relevant because a low-KH neon tank on acidic soil has little buffer. This ties to the tank guide's "steady readings" advice.
- Vinegar fizz test for limestone: no reputable source opened (only hobby and retailer pages in search results). Unsourced.

S7. Leaf litter and botanicals on the substrate
- Seriously Fish: "The addition of dried leaf litter would further emphasise the natural feel"; leaf litter releases humic substances and supports microbes that "can provide a valuable secondary food source for fry." (N12)
- Seriously Fish on peat: unnecessary, and its collection "is both unsustainable and environmentally-destructive." (N12)
- Dosing per gallon, boiling leaves, and how often to replace: only retailer and hobby pages found; unsourced.

S8. Cleaning
- PFK: "The easiest way to keep the gravel clean is to regularly use a gravel cleaner"; battery or air-powered cleaners "are invariably much less effective"; sand, "Once it's in the tank it's a doddle to keep clean." (E1)
- PFK: most substrates "are very dusty and need to be cleaned thoroughly before they're used," "otherwise the tank will turn extremely cloudy." (E1) Seachem says the same for Flourite: it "may become dusty in transit and require rinsing." (E4)
- Planted bed: PFK's warning that a gravel cleaner "risk[s] sucking" out a planting substrate (E1) supports skimming the surface over planted zones rather than plunging the siphon.

S9. What to avoid
- Calcareous gravel, coral sand and some pea gravels (E1), see S6.
- PFK on colored gravel: "Powder-coated coloured gravels are a particular irritation here," "the more you swirl them, the dirtier the water gets"; some rough, angular colored gravels are made from white dolomite, "a naturally occurring mineral rich in calcium and magnesium," so "it wouldn't be a great choice at all if you want to keep fish that like neutral or soft water." (E1)
- Tropica: "make sure that the ground layer does not liberate harmful substances into the water." (E2)
- Sharp gravel injuring a midwater fish: no source found; neons do not dig, so this is weak as a neon-specific risk. Unsourced.

The neon has an agastric larval stage, with gastric glands and pepsin appearing at 20 days after hatch. Survival to 32 days: live Artemia only 22.0%, weaning at 22 days 16.2%, microdiet only 0.8%. The practical point: fry fed only prepared food die.
- E11 (WEAK): larvae on green water had 80% survival over 13 days; early fry on mixed zooplankton had 95.23% survival over 30 days. Temperature in the trial 24 to 25°C (75 to 77°F).
- Robertson fed newly hatched brine shrimp once fry were found in the leaf litter "after about a month"; neons "colour up at about four weeks"; "large enough to sell at 12 weeks". (N14)
- The guide's "adults from about 6 months" has no source (see Unsourced).

F2. Why neons stop eating (causes found)
- New or immature tank: fish "suddenly become lethargic, anorectic, and frequently die," often from ammonia or nitrite. (E14)
- Rapid pH drop in an old, under-maintained tank. (E14)
- Internal parasites: Spironucleus gives "Weight loss (anorexia), mortality of fry and juveniles." (N8) External parasites also cause anorexia per Merck search-result text (not re-verified on the page this session).
- Food too large: Merck says flakes "may be too large for a small-mouthed fish to easily ingest" (already N9 in this file).
- Shipping and handling stress: "Stress weakens the immune system of the fish, resulting in increased susceptibility to disease." (E29) Shippers withhold food 1 to 3 days before bagging (E29), so a newly bought neon may not have eaten for days.
- Not eating is a listed sign of illness (E12), alongside lethargy, loss of color and erratic swimming.
- Night coloring is not illness: duller with a violet stripe at night (N12).

F3. When to worry
- Merck's sign list pairs not eating with lethargy, rapid breathing, color loss, weight loss and erratic swimming (E12). A neon that skips one meal after a move is expected (E29 context); one that refuses food while the school eats, loses condition, or hangs apart from the school matches Seriously Fish's NTD warning signs (restlessness, white patches, "setting themselves apart from the main school"). (N12)
- No source opened gives a number of days a neon can go without food. Unsourced.

F4. Live food risk (supports an existing unsourced line)
- Merck: "Cyclops and Daphnia spp are common intermediate hosts for Camallanus spp" (N8); tapeworm larvae come from eating infected invertebrates (N8); tubificid worms are hosts for myxozoans such as whirling disease (N8). This supports the feeding guide's "Avoid low-quality live foods from questionable sources, which can transmit disease." Frozen and freeze-dried foods were not addressed in any source opened.

### Health

H1. What a healthy neon looks like
- Merck (general fish): "smooth, unblemished scales, fins, and skin surfaces"; "Skin and fins should be intact, not bloody or ragged"; "Eyes should be bright, not cloudy"; "swimming normally and steadily." (E16)
- Neon-specific: in the school, not apart from it, not restless, no white skin patches (N12). Color dulls at night with a violet stripe, and lightens over pale substrate in bright, clear water (N12).

H2. Fungal infections (water molds)
- What it is: Saprolegnia and relatives are "fungus-like protists" (Oomycota), not true fungi. (N6)
- Looks like: "a cotton or wool-like white growth on fish skin." (E17) Fishkeeper: "fuzzy 'cotton wool' growths on body or fins, usually associated with injury or damage" (E18, WEAK).
- Causes: Merck: aquatic fungi "often are considered secondary tissue invaders that follow traumatic injuries" or "environmental insults such as poor water quality or low water temperatures." (N6) E17: stressors "such as temperature shocks, poor water quality, handling and high fish density have been associated with outbreaks."
- Treatment basics: E17 (aquaculture review): malachite green was the standard and "has been banned due to its carcinogenic and toxic effects" (in food fish production); alternatives include hydrogen peroxide, formalin, Bronopol, salt; "an effective, suitable and safe compound to treat Saprolegnia infection remains to be found." Fishkeeper (WEAK): fix the root cause, hospital tank, remove carbon before medicating, add aeration because some medications lower oxygen. (E18)
- The lookalike: some bacterial infections look like fungus ("so called 'false fungus'"). (E18) Columnaris is the known neon lookalike (N12, already in the guide).
- Neon angle: no source ties water mold to neon tetras specifically. It belongs in the health guide as a secondary infection after netting injury, a chill, or bad water.

H3. Internal parasites (signs, causes, treatment basics; all Merck N8 unless noted)
- Camallanus (nematode): "blood-red worms can be seen in the intestine and protruding from the anus"; intermediate hosts Cyclops and Daphnia; treated with fenbendazole or levamisole (no dose given for Camallanus).
- Capillaria: weight loss and pot belly; fenbendazole "25 mg/kg, PO, once a day for 3 days" (about 11 mg per pound), plus levamisole, ivermectin or emamectin listed.
- Spironucleus (intestinal flagellate): weight loss, anorexia, deaths in fry and juveniles; treat "If there is a loss of condition, or >15 organisms are seen per low-power field"; "metronidazole (use only in ornamental species), which should be given orally," or as a bath "if fish are anorectic."
- Tapeworms: "can inhibit growth, and cause coelomic distension and intestinal impactions"; many infected fish show no signs; praziquantel 35 to 125 mg/kg in feed every 24 hours for three days (about 16 to 57 mg per pound).
- Diagnosis is by wet mount and direct visualization, which means a vet or diagnostic lab (N8; E25).
- Neon angle: a thin neon that keeps eating is a parasite or mycobacteriosis suspect before it is an NTD suspect; see H5.

H4. Shrimp-safe vs invertebrate-harmful medications

**Owner decision (2026-11-01): the guides never list any medication as shrimp-safe.** They keep the rule that shrimp come out before any medication goes in, plus the sourced point that copper is toxic to invertebrates.
- Copper: Seachem says invertebrates "are extremely sensitive to copper and will not tolerate treatment with a copper-based medication"; freshwater target 0.25 mg/L. (E21) UF/IFAS: "Copper is also toxic to invertebrates, such as snails, and also most of the zooplankton." (E19) A 96 hour LC50 of 0.0313 mg/L copper for a freshwater shrimp, the most toxic of five metals tested. (E20) That LC50 sits below Seachem's own 0.25 mg/L freshwater dose (my comparison).
- Copper and soft water (neon-specific): UF/IFAS: "the toxicity of copper to fish increases as the total alkalinity decreases," and "If the total alkalinity is less than 50 ppm, copper treatments are not recommended because of the high risk of killing fish." (E19) 50 ppm alkalinity is about 2.8 dKH (my conversion at 17.86 ppm per dKH). A neon tank kept soft, on aquasoil, or with negligible carbonate hardness like the wild habitat (N12) will often sit below that line. Copper is a poor choice for a neon tank even with no shrimp in it. Seachem's Cupramine page instead says the product "is buffered and will not negatively influence pH or alkalinity" and makes no alkalinity warning (E21); UF/IFAS writes about copper sulfate, so note the product difference.
- Labeled invertebrate-harmful: Hikari IntestiPro (sodium chloride, metronidazole, praziquantel) "is harmful to invertebrates, including snails, crayfish, shrimp, etc." (E22)
- Unlabeled: Hikari PraziPro (praziquantel) "Treats flukes, tapeworms, flatworms, and turbellarians" and "Won't negatively impact biological filtration," but makes no statement about invertebrates. (E23) Do not call it shrimp-safe on that basis.
- Fenbendazole: only hobby and retailer pages say it kills many snails and spares shrimp; no reputable source opened. Unsourced.
- Practical rule supported by the sources: treat in a hospital tank with no invertebrates (consistent with the site's quarantine guide and E21, E22).

H5. Mycobacteriosis (new differential for the NTD section)
- Merck: signs "are variable and nonspecific" and "can include emaciation, ascites, skin ulceration and hemorrhages, exophthalmos, paleness, and skeletal deformities"; "There are no effective treatments that eliminate mycobacteria in fish"; predisposing conditions "low dissolved oxygen, low pH, and high organic load"; "An infected aquarium should be disinfected before other fish are added." (E15)
- CFSPH: infected fish "will have skin lesions or ulcers. The eyes may bulge and enlargement of the abdomen may be noted. Spine deformities can also occur, as can sudden death." (E28)
- Why it matters: paleness, wasting and spinal deformity overlap with the guide's NTD sign list. A neon that fails a columnaris course is not proven NTD; only a lab can separate the two (E25). See Conflicts.

H6. Finding a fish vet, and what to bring
- AAFV: the site's spotting-a-sick-fish guide already carries the AAFV Find a Fish Vet map and its fallback steps (North America only). The page returned 403 this session; that guide's own source line stands.
- WAVMA: the page lists a "WAVMA Find a Fish Vet Directory" and members choose whether to be listed; it does not say how the public searches it. WAVMA "was formed in 2006 as a not-for-profit professional association." (E24) Treat WAVMA as the option outside North America, with the caveat that listing is opt-in.
- What to bring (UF/IFAS diagnostic lab, written for lab submissions; the closest reputable guidance found):
  - Live, sick fish showing the signs: "Fish found dead in a pond or tank are rarely good candidates for evaluation." The lab asks for four to six live, sick fish from a group. (E25) For a home neon school, bringing one or two of the sickest live fish is my reading, not the lab's wording.
  - Before any treatment: "Submit fish as soon as a problem is noticed and before any treatments have been applied." (E25)
  - A water sample from the tank, not the transport bag: "Approximately one quart will suffice"; "The water from the fish transport container is not suitable for testing"; collect it "just before delivery"; keep it dark and cool if held more than two hours. (E25)
  - Fish travel in their own tank water with oxygen and nothing else added. (E25)
  - History: "stocking densities, water quality information, system set up, nutrition," and the "number of sick and/or dying fish, and previous treatment information." (E26)
- Merck: "More complicated diseases require veterinary treatment." (E13)

H7. Quarantine length (for reference)
- Merck management: "A 30-day period is the minimum time for quarantine, but longer periods may be necessary." (E14) Merck pet owner page: valuable fish "should be quarantined for at least 30 to 60 days," and adding a new fish restarts the clock. (E13) Consistent with the guides' 30 days as a floor.

### Cost

C1. Buying checklist (signs of healthy stock)
- Watch before buying: Seriously Fish recommends "observing the fish in the shop" and avoiding fish showing "restlessness, white patches on the skin or setting themselves apart from the main school"; if NTD signs are present "it's best to avoid purchase entirely." (N12)
- The whole tank, not one fish: "The aquarium itself should be clean, and other fish in the environment should also appear healthy." (E16) A store tank with one NTD-looking fish is a tank to skip, since NTD is "harbored by otherwise healthy-appearing fish" (N2, already in this file).
- Individual fish: intact fins and skin, bright eyes, normal steady swimming (E16); erratic circling, drifting or listlessness are red flags (E16).
- Buy the group at once: "a mixed-sex group of at least 8-10 specimens." (N12)
- Why stock quality costs money: imported neons in the UF/IFAS story had pre-sale losses that "can run as high as 50 percent." (E9) Trade-wide, transport and handling mortality runs "up to 73%" and "as low as 2%," mostly estimated rather than measured. (E30)
- Domestic farming context: Florida farms "are the only ones in the United States producing tetras in any quantity"; one farm sold 50,000 to 100,000 neons a month (2001 figures). (E9)

C2. Shipping live fish online (risks; prices are in the observations list)
- How fish ship: in "plastic bags containing oxygenated water," inside a polystyrene box inside a cardboard box; "Water quality in the bag will become degraded due to accumulation of carbon dioxide, ammonia, and other waste products during shipment"; heat or ice packs in summer and winter; fish are fasted 1 to 3 days first. (E29)
- Tropical fish ship best at 26 to 30°C (78.8 to 86°F) per FA-120's general table (E29); that sits above the neon's 70 to 77°F keeping range, so the table is general, not neon advice.
- Mortality range in trade transport: 2 to 73% (E30).
- Retailer guarantee terms are in the observations list (24 hour claim window, photos of the unopened bag, shipping not refunded).

C3. Monthly running cost
- Electricity price: US average residential price 18.16 cents per kWh in August 2026 (preliminary), up from 17.57 in August 2025. (E31)
- Formula for the guide: watts x hours on per day x 30 / 1000 x $0.18 = dollars per month. Worked example with ASSUMED wattages and duty cycle (unsourced): a 100 W heater that runs about 8 hours a day uses 24 kWh, about $4.36 a month; a 3 W air pump for a sponge filter running 24 hours uses about 2.2 kWh, about $0.39; a 15 W LED on 8 hours uses about 3.6 kWh, about $0.65. Total about $5.40 a month in electricity. The wattages and the 8 hour heater duty cycle are not from a source.
- Water testing: Merck pet owner page recommends testing "pH, total alkalinity (pH), ammonia levels, nitrate and nitrite levels" every two weeks. (E13) A liquid test kit is a recurring cost the guide's ongoing table leaves out.
- Food and conditioner: already in the guide's table; no new source.
- Aquasoil replacement: usable "for two to three years" (E5, WEAK); a recurring cost only for soil-based planted tanks.

C4. Quarantine tank and adding fish later
- Quarantine minimum 30 days, longer may be needed (E14); 30 to 60 days for valuable fish, and adding a fish restarts it (E13). Adding neons later means another full quarantine cycle before they join the school.
- Copper is a poor quarantine-tank default for neons in soft water (E19), so budget for non-copper medication.
- Prices: see observations.

### Handling

K1. Fish tank granuloma (Mycobacterium marinum)
- Cause and route: the bacterium lives in fresh and salt water and aquarium tanks and enters through broken skin; people who keep aquariums are at higher risk, "particularly if they have cuts or scrapes." (E27) Infection "most commonly occurs at sites of skin wounds or abrasions." (E28)
- Signs in people: usually 2 to 3 weeks after exposure; a "reddish to purple bump" that becomes a painful nodule and can open into a sore; elbows, fingers and backs of the hands most often. (E27) Lesions "may be single nodule or ulcer or a series of multiple nodules"; can heal alone or persist for months; rarely spreads to joints and bone; death is rare, most often in people with weakened immune systems. (E28)
- Treatment: "You may need several months of treatment with more than one antibiotic." (E27) See a provider if bumps do not clear. (E27)
- Prevention: "Wash your hands and arms thoroughly after cleaning aquariums, or wear rubber gloves." (E27) "wear gloves when contacting fish, fish water, or any associated equipment. Hand washing is essential after glove removal." (E28) Remove sick or dying fish from the tank. (E28)
- Equipment disinfection: CFSPH says clean and disinfect equipment with "dilute bleach solution" (E28); Merck says bleach is ineffective against mycobacteria and should only strip organic material, followed by 70% ethanol or phenolic compounds (E15). See Conflicts.
- Merck: "Mycobacteria can cause zoonotic infections," and "aquarists should be informed of potential risks." (E15)
- Neon angle: the handling guide's only hands-in-water job is acclimation and netting; this is the place for a short gloves-and-cuts paragraph.

### Enrichment: clear water vs tannin (blackwater)

B1. The case for tannins (blackwater side)
- Wild habitat: water "stained brownish due to the presence of humic substances released by decomposing organic matter." (N12) Fishkeeper: wild neons are "mostly found in blackwater habitats that generally contain large quantities of dead leaves and waterlogged wood." (N18)
- Color: Seriously Fish says the fish lightens in "bright conditions with pale substrate or background and clear water" and "seems to do best under fairly dim lighting." (N12)
- Physiology (not neon): Rio Negro dissolved organic carbon "provided almost perfect protection against ionoregulatory disturbances" at pH 4.0 in ion-poor water, in zebrafish; these blackwaters "support 8% of the world's ichthyofauna." (E6)
- Humic substances in fish keeping: "HS are able to increase the physiological condition of the individuals" and "detoxify heavy metals and organic pollutants." (E7)

B2. The case for clear water (clearwater side)
- The species is not blackwater-only: "it's also been collected from similar habitats containing transparent, unstained clearwater." (N12) FishBase: "Blackwater or clearwater stream tributaries." (N11)
- Farmed stock: "millions of them thrive in community tanks filled with local tap water"; soft, acidic conditions are advised for wild imports. (N18) Nearly all neons sold are farmed (N12, N13, N18).
- The evidence for humic benefits is mixed: reports are "diverse, if not inconsistent," and "HS are not real alternatives to strong traditional therapeutics." (E7) A commercial DOC product "actually exacerbated ionoregulatory failure" in one earlier study while protecting in another. (E6) The protective study used zebrafish, not neons (E6).
- Light for plants: tinted water cuts light reaching plants (search-result consensus from hobby and retailer pages only; WEAK, nothing opened).
- Fishkeeper lists lighting as "No special requirements" (N18), against Seriously Fish's "fairly dim lighting" (N12).
- Peat as a tannin source: Seriously Fish calls peat collection "unsustainable and environmentally-destructive." (N12)

B3. Reading for the guide (per RULES.md, state the range and the recommendation, do not stage the disagreement)
- Sources support: tannins are optional for farmed neons; a dark bed, dim light and some leaf litter bring out color; a light tint is consistent with both wild water types. No study measures neon welfare or color in tannin-stained vs clear water.

### Conflicts with current guides

1. neon-tetra-health-issues-guide.mdx, The Lookalike section: "A fish that keeps declining through a full course points back to NTD." Mycobacteriosis also causes paleness, emaciation and skeletal or spine deformities and has no effective treatment (E15, E28), so a fish that fails an antibacterial course could have either. The same applies to the FAQ "Treat a suspect fish as bacterial in a hospital tank first: one that improves did not have NTD" (fine as written) and to the NTD sign list's "a curved or deformed spine in advanced cases" (not unique to NTD).
2. neon-tetra-tank-setup-guide.mdx, excerpt and opener: "Neon tetras come from soft, dim, tannin-stained blackwater streams." Seriously Fish (N12) and FishBase (N11) say blackwater or clearwater. Partial conflict: true of some habitats, not all. The enrichment guide's "tints the water the way their habitat does" has the same issue.
3. neon-tetra-health-issues-guide.mdx, Ich section: "a standard commercial ich medication ... does the killing." Not wrong, but copper-based ich products carry an alkalinity warning that bites in soft neon water: UF/IFAS advises against copper below 50 ppm alkalinity (E19). The guide should steer to non-copper products for a soft-water neon tank.
4. neon-tetra-cost-guide.mdx, Ongoing Costs: the table lists only food and conditioner and the text says "a small amount of electricity." Merck recommends water tests every two weeks (E13), so test kit refills are a missing line; electricity can be stated with E31.
5. Source vs source (not a guide line): Fishkeeper "Lighting: No special requirements" (N18) vs Seriously Fish "fairly dim lighting" (N12). Fishkeeper "strongly acidic water makes life difficult for bacteria and parasites" (N18) vs Merck listing low pH among predisposing conditions for mycobacteriosis (E15). CFSPH "dilute bleach solution" for equipment (E28) vs Merck, which says bleach is ineffective against mycobacteria and should be followed by 70% ethanol or phenolic compounds (E15, paraphrase). For the handling guide, follow Merck.
