## 1. Identity and naming

1.1 Scientific name and authority
- ITIS: "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi (Boulenger, 1895)", TSN 162891, "Synonym(s): Tetragonopterus ternetzi Boulenger, 1895", "Current Standing: valid", "Record Credibility Rating: verified - standards met". (S15)
- FishBase: "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi ( Boulenger , 1895 )", common name "Black tetra". (S17)
- TFH: "Originally described as Tetragonopterus ternetzi". (S22)
- DFO: "first described in 1895 by Belgian-British zoologist George Albert Boulenger as Tetragonopterus ternetzi based on several specimens from Descalavados, Brazil" (DFO's spelling); "Carl Eigenamnn shifted T. ternetzi into Gymnocorymbus in 1910" (DFO's typo for Eigenmann). (S14)
- Seriously Fish lists the authority as "not recorded". (S21)

1.2 Common names
- ITIS: "black tetra", "black widow". (S15)
- Seriously Fish: "It goes by several common names, including 'butterfly tetra', 'black skirt tetra' and simply 'black tetra'." Page title: "Black Widow Tetra". (S21)
- TFH: "Black skirt tetra, black tetra, black widow tetra, white skirt tetra, blueberry tetra, strawberry tetra, mixed berry tetra" (the berry names are dyed fish, see 11). (S22)
- DFO: "the common names Black Tetra, Black Skirt Tetra, Black Widow Tetra, Petticoat Tetra, White Tetra, White Skirt Tetra, Black Long-Fin Tetra and White Long-Fin Tetra are all accepted as synonymous and morphs of the Black Tetra". (S14)
- Aquarium Source adds "high-fin black skirt tetra", "black fin tetra" and "blackamoor". (S32, lower tier)

1.3 Naming story
- Species name. ETYFish: "in honor of Swiss-born ichthyologist and naturalist Carl Ternetz (1870-1928), who "formed" the collection that contained holotype". (S19) FishBase eponymy: "Dr Carl Ternetz (1870-1928) was a Swiss-born ichthyologist and naturalist who first collected near Basel (1892) and subsequently collected in South America over a period of more than thirty years". (S17)
- Genus. ETYFish: "gymnós (γυμνός), bare or naked; kórymbos (κόρυμβος), summit, referring to scaleless (and humped) predorsal area of G. thayeri". (S19) FishBase: "Greek, gymnos = naked + Greek,korymb = summit, cluster of fruits and flowers". (S17)

1.4 Family (sources disagree: this is a recent reclassification, the same split as the ember tetra file)
- FishBase: "Acestrorhamphidae (American tetras) > Pristellinae". (S17)
- ETYFish lists Gymnocorymbus under Family Acestrorhamphidae, Subfamily Pristellinae. (S19)
- Wikipedia infobox: Family Acestrorhamphidae. (S35)
- ITIS: "Family Characidae". (S15). Seriously Fish: "Characidae". (S21). GBIF species match: Characidae. (S37). DFO: "the Characidae". (S14). Benine et al. 2015 title: "(Teleostei, Characiformes, Characidae)". (S3)
- Benine et al. 2015 also: "Our results support previous hypotheses of the alignment of Gymnocorymbus close to the subfamily Stethaprioninae". (S3)
- Owner decision, consistent with whatever the neon, cardinal and ember entries use.

1.5 The genus
- Benine et al. 2015: "Of the six previously known nominal species of Gymnocorymbus, three are considered valid herein: Gymnocorymbus bondi from the Río Orinoco basin, Gymnocorymbus ternetzi from the Rio Paraguay basin, and Gymnocorymbus thayeri ... A fourth species, Gymnocorymbus flaviolimai sp. n., is described from the Rio Madeira". It also supports "the sister relationship between G. ternetzi and G. thayeri". Lectotypes "are designated for G. ternetzi and G. thayeri." (S3)

## 2. Origin and wild habitat

2.1 Native range
- Seriously Fish: "Rio Paraguay and Guaporé basins in Brazil, Bolivia and Argentina." (S21)
- FishBase: "South America: Paraguay and Guaporé River basins to Argentina". Coordinates "11°S - 30°S, 64°W - 48°W". (S17)
- TFH: range "Paraguay and GuaporÉ River drainages in South America" (sic); type locality "Descalvados, Mato Grosso, Brazil". (S22)
- Benine et al. 2015: "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi from the Rio Paraguay basin". (S3)
- DFO: "The known native range of G. ternetzi is within the Rio Paraguay river basin in South America. Nearby populations in the upper Rio Parana and Rio Paraiba do Sol systems (Benine et al. 2015), as well as the Amazon River delta including the Paraquai and Rio Negro systems (Brysiewcz et al. 2009), are thought to be introduced and not included in native distributions." (S14)
- DISAGREEMENT: Gonzalez et al. 1997 calls it "native to ion-poor acidic waters of the Amazon River" (title: "a fish native to the Amazon River"). (S4) The taxonomic revision (S3) and DFO (S14) give the Rio Paraguay basin and treat Amazon populations as introduced. Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH add the Guaporé. Use Paraguay basin (plus Guaporé per the husbandry references); do not write "Amazon".

2.2 Habitat type
- Seriously Fish: "Small, slow-moving creeks, tributaries and streams, usually with dense overhanging vegetation." (S21)
- FishBase: "Freshwater; pelagic". "Adults occur in the middle and upper water layers." (S17)
- DFO: "Juveniles and adults have been found together in macrophytes of oxbow lakes of floodplains in Brazil, with higher abundance in the dry season than wet season" (citing Meschiatti et al. 2000). (S14)
- DFO quoting the GloFish company's notification (secondhand, citing Sakurai et al. 1992): "In its native habitat, this species inhabits relatively cool (22-24°C) slow-flowing freshwaters that are brown, but clear and slightly acidic (pH 5.5-6.3)". (S14) This is the only wild water chemistry found, and it is a company quote of a 1992 book, relayed by DFO.
- DFO on predators in the basin: "potential predators such as various piranha species, wolf fish, and golden dorado". (S14)
- DFO on how little is known: "Much of the information available on the Black Tetra is from the ornamental aquarium trade, and primarily involves anecdotal information ... rather than scientific studies on habitat or other requirements in the wild." (S14)

2.3 Introduced populations
- DFO: "reported to be established from released ornamental fish in Colombia (Welcomme 1992), temporarily established in a Colorado hot spring, and isolated occurrences have been reported in Florida and Louisiana ... but no occurrences of establishment in temperate climates (US and Europe ...) have been reported over the 70 or more years of its use". (S14)

## 3. Adult size, sexing, lifespan

3.1 Adult size
- Seriously Fish: "60 mm SL" (2.4 in, standard length, tail fin excluded). (S21)
- FishBase: "Max length : 7.5 cm SL male/unsexed". (S17)
- TFH: "6 cm TL (2.5 inches)" (total length, tail included). (S22)
- DFO: "up to 7.5 cm in length, but usually only to 6 cm in aquaria"; "Its normal length in an aquarium setting is approximately 4-6 cm, while they can reportedly grow to a maximum length of 7.5 cm in the wild". (S14)
- Banu et al. 2025 measured market GloFish tetras at "mean length 5.0 ± 0.3 cm" and "mean weight of 1.8 ± 0.5 g". (S5)
- Aquarium Source: "around 3 inches long at full maturity". (S32, lower tier)
- Summary for writers: about 2 to 2.5 in (5 to 6 cm) in a home tank; 7.5 cm (3 in) is the referenced maximum.

3.2 Sexing
- Seriously Fish: "Males are noticeably slimmer and a little smaller than females. The male also has more pointed dorsal and anal fins than the female." (S21)
- DFO: male has "a broader anal fin, a dorsal fin that is narrower and more pointed than females, as well as a caudal fin that sometimes has white spots" (relaying hobby sources), and from the taxonomic revision, "a pair of small hooks on the first 5-8 anal fin rays in the males that are absent in the females." (S14, citing Benine et al. 2015)

3.3 Color change with age
- Seriously Fish: "The intense black colouration of juveniles does fade as they grow". (S21)
- DFO: "Juveniles display the most intensive colouration and will become duller as they age, fading to a silvery grey". (S14)
- TFH: the black "can fade or darken depending on the fish's mood." (S22)

3.4 Lifespan in captivity (no authoritative source found; all figures trace to hobby tier)
- DFO: "The lifespan of the Black Tetra is between 3 and 6 years but could be extended in an aquarium setting if ideal conditions for survival are maintained" (citing Aqua-Fish Net 2005, a hobby site). (S14)
- Aquarium Source: "the average Black Skirt Tetra lifespan is between 3 and 5 years in captivity." (S32)
- Wikipedia (uncited in the text read): "3 to 5 years, though with the right conditions, can even exceed 6 or more". (S35). Do not use.
- Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH give no lifespan. Seriously Fish says dyeing "often shortens their lifespan considerably" (see 11). (S21)

3.5 Age at maturity (sources disagree)
- DFO: "many reports suggesting maturity ranges anywhere from 1-2 years old" (hobby sources), but "Mazzoni et al. (2015) found first gonadal maturation was between 150 to 180 dpf (5-6 months), and GloFish LLC states Tetra brood stock reach sexual maturity in 4-5 months". (S14)
- Wikipedia: "about two years of age" (uncited). (S35)
- Use the study figure: about 5 to 6 months (S14 citing S8's authors).

3.6 Wild lifespan: no source found.

## 4. Conservation status

- FishBase: "Least Concern (LC) ; Date assessed: 04 March 2021" (IUCN Red List version 2025-2). (S17)
- GBIF API: "category":"LEAST_CONCERN", "iucnTaxonID":"49829723". (S37)
- Wikipedia cites "Lima, F. (2023). Gymnocorymbus ternetzi. IUCN Red List of Threatened Species. 2023 e.T49829723A197221365." (S35)
- DISAGREEMENT on date only: assessed 2021 (FishBase) vs a 2023 Red List publication (Wikipedia's citation). These may be the assessment date and the publication year of one assessment. The IUCN page returned 403. Category agrees everywhere.
- FishBase: "Fisheries: highly commercial; aquarium: highly commercial". Resilience "High, minimum population doubling time less than 15 months". (S17)
- Trade source: Seriously Fish: "All the fish sold in the trade have been captive bred and as such are unfussy regarding water chemistry and diet." (S21). DFO: "bred for the aquarium trade since at least 1950 (Innes 1950)", and "Teletchea (2016) listed the Black Tetra as being at the highest level of domestication for aquarium fish." (S14)

## 5. Tank

5.1 Minimum tank size and footprint
- Seriously Fish: base "75 × 30 cm" (30 × 12 in), volume "~68 litres ~18 US gal", and "A group of these can be kept in a standard 30" x 12" (75cm x 30cm) - 20g long/70 litre tank." (S21)
- FishBase: "minimum aquarium size 60 cm". (S17)
- TFH: no size; "should have plenty of open swimming space but appreciates plant thickets to which it can retire if it feels threatened." (S22)
- Aquarium Source: "a minimum of 15 gallons", and "If possible though, go for a 20-gallon tank for good measure." (S32)
- LiveAquaria product page spec (retailer, observation only, not a source): "Minimum Tank Size: 10 Gallons".
- DISAGREEMENT: 60 cm (24 in, FishBase) vs 75 cm (30 in, a 20 gallon long, Seriously Fish) vs 15 to 20 gallons (Aquarium Source) vs 10 gallons (a retailer). The best references point to a 20 gallon long; a 10 gallon is only a retailer figure.

5.2 Group size and shoaling research
- FishBase: "in groups of 5 or more individuals". (S17)
- Aquarium Source: "At the very least, you need a group of 5 fish to keep them happy." (S32)
- Seriously Fish: "a small shoal of at least 12 specimens" (in the context of curbing nipping, see 7). (S21)
- TFH: "an active, schooling species that should always be kept in groups", "especially dramatic when kept in large schools of a dozen or more fish." (S22)
- GloFish product page: "you may keep them in larger schools". (S30, manufacturer, no number)
- DISAGREEMENT: 5 (FishBase, Aquarium Source) vs 12 (Seriously Fish, TFH). The nipping guidance in section 7 is the reason to write the higher number.
- Research on schooling: Rahman et al. 2020 studied black tetra schools under startle stimuli and found "information propagates through the group as a wave, indicating that each fish is not reacting individually to the stimulus", "the time taken for information to transfer across the group is independent of group density", and "information propagates across large groups faster than would be expected if the fish were simply responding to the motion of their nearest neighbor." (S9) This is about how schools move, not how many fish to keep.
- Group-size welfare study, NOT READ: Priestley, Stevenson and Alexander (2006), "Growth rate and body condition in relation to group size in Black Widow Tetras (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi) and Common Goldfish (Carassius auratus)", Journal of Nutrition 136(7 Suppl):2078S-2080S, DOI 10.1093/jn/136.7.2078S, WALTHAM Centre for Pet Nutrition. PubMed carries no abstract and the publisher PDF could not be retrieved. Its findings are unknown here; a search snippet from a hobby site paraphrased it, but that is not a source. Open on the desktop before citing anything from it.

5.3 Temperature
- Seriously Fish: 20-26°C (68-79°F). (S21)
- FishBase: "Subtropical; 20°C - 26°C". (S17)
- TFH: "Subtropical, 20° to 26°C (68° to 78°F)". (S22)
- DFO: "There are no known formal studies on the minimum and maximum temperature requirements of the Black Tetra." Hobby literature gives "20-26°C" with narrower suggestions of 23-25, 23-26 and 22-24°C; elsewhere "Suggested optimal temperature for health and activity of Black Tetras for ornamental aquarium culture varies from 20-29°C". (S14)
- Aquarium Source: "70°F to 85°F (aim for the middle of this range)". (S32)
- GloFish product page: "GloFish® fish enjoy tropical temperatures of 76-80F." (S30, manufacturer spec for its own fish)
- LiveAquaria spec (retailer observation only): "72 - 82° F".
- Cold limits (measured, useful for "does it need a heater"): DFO reports white skirt tetras lowered 1°C per day from 20°C "lost equilibrium at 9.95 ± 0.03°C", and "decreased feeding level and activity at 17°C, stopped feeding when temperatures approached 12°C and stopped activity just above 10°C". (S14)
- Kuhn et al. 2023 held black tetras "at four different temperatures for 2 weeks (20°C, 24°C, 28°C, 32°C)" and found "food intake increased with increasing temperature". (S7) The abstract does not report survival or mortality at 32°C.
- DISAGREEMENT: the main references top out at 26°C (79°F); DFO's compiled range, Aquarium Source and the retailers go to 28 to 29°C (82 to 85°F). Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH all call it subtropical.

5.4 pH
- Seriously Fish: 6.0-7.0. (S21)
- FishBase: "pH range: 6.0 - 8.0". (S17)
- TFH: "wide range of hardness and pH." (S22)
- DFO: "it has been noted that these fish are able to tolerate a pH that ranges between 5.0 and 8.5" (relaying hobby sources). (S14)
- Lab result, Gonzalez et al. 1997: tetras "possess abilities to resist the disruptive effects of moderately low pH but cannot survive in waters with a pH of 4.0 or less". (S4)
- Aquarium Source: "6.0 to 7.5". (S32)
- Wild, secondhand: pH 5.5-6.3 (see 2.2). (S14)

5.5 Hardness
- Seriously Fish: "5-20 dGH". (S21)
- FishBase: "dH range: 5 - 19". (S17)
- Practical Fishkeeping, for breeding only: "soft, acidic side (pH about 6.0, GH not more than 8)". (S23)
- Aquarium Source: "Water hardness: 4 to 8 dKH". (S32)
- LiveAquaria spec (retailer observation only): "dKH: 4 - 8".
- KH: only lower-tier and retailer figures (4 to 8 dKH).

5.6 Lighting
- Seriously Fish: "It will show its best colours when there are some dense areas of planting, with some floating vegetation added to dim the light entering the tank." (S21)
- GloFish versions only: "the more blue light you have the more your fish will shine!" (S30, manufacturer). GloFish FAQ: "To make you're GloFish fluorescent fish stand out add blue lights." (sic) (S29)
- No lighting intensity or photoperiod source.

5.7 Planting, decor and space

## 6. Diet

- Wild diet: FishBase: "They feed on worms, small crustaceans and insects." (S17). DFO: "Black Tetras exhibit a primarily carnivorous lifestyle, feeding on readily available worms, small crustaceans and insects (Galib 2011). However, it is not a particularly selective species, ingesting smaller amounts of algae and plant matter". (S14)
- Classification in a lab study: Butt et al. 2019 used "the omnivore black widow tetra" as their omnivore model. (S13)
- TFH: "A micropredator. Relishes live or frozen foods, will readily take all regular aquarium fare." (S22)

- Temperature and appetite: "In all species, food intake increased with increasing temperature." (S7)
- Feeding frequency: GloFish product page: "Feed two to three times daily, in small amounts, only as much as your fish can eat in a minute or so." (S30, manufacturer). No independent source on frequency found.

## 7. Behavior and tankmates

7.1 Temperament
- Seriously Fish: "One of the better tetras for the 'general' community tank. The black widow is generally peaceful and lively". (S21)
- TFH: "The black tetra is a peaceful candidate for community tanks, but it may be a bit nippy toward slow-moving or long-finned tankmates." (S22)
- DFO: "Wild-type G. ternetzi is a small, non-aggressive fish". "generally not known to be aggressive to or highly competitive with other aquarium fish although may prey on much smaller fish (Frank 1980)." (S14)
- Leggatt and Devlin 2020 measured aggressive behavior in adult and juvenile tetras in a foraging competition; transgenesis "did not affect the foraging success or aggressive behaviour". (S1) This compares GloFish to white tetras; it does not measure nipping of other species.

7.2 Fin nipping (sourced carefully)
- Seriously Fish: "Although it has a reputation as a bit of a fin nipper, this behaviour can usually be rectified by keeping it in a small shoal of at least 12 specimens. When maintained in these kind of numbers any squabbling is generally contained within the group." (S21)
- TFH: "it may be a bit nippy toward slow-moving or long-finned tankmates. When kept in groups, as it should be, the fishes' energy is usually contained within the group, where it remains harmless." (S22)
- Aquarium Source: "this species tends to nip when they have the opportunity as well. For that reason, we recommend avoiding any long-finned fish species." Also: "this species is prone to nipping at flowy fins of betta fish or angelfish." (S32)
- No peer-reviewed study of fin nipping in this species was found. Both strong husbandry sources (S21, S22) agree on the fix: a group of about a dozen keeps the energy inside the school.

7.3 Good tankmates
- Seriously Fish: "It can be kept with most livebearers, danios, rasboras, other tetras and peaceful bottom dwellers such as Corydoras or smaller Loricariids. It also makes a suitable companion for more robust dwarf cichlids such as kribensis, and due to its somewhat rounded shape is unlikely to end up on the menu if combined with larger species such as angel fish." (S21)

7.4 Tankmates to avoid
- Long-finned and slow fish: TFH (S22), Aquarium Source (S32).
- DISAGREEMENT on angelfish: Seriously Fish lists angelfish as a workable companion because the tetra is too deep-bodied to be eaten (S21); Aquarium Source warns the tetra nips angelfish fins (S32); TFH warns against long-finned tankmates generally (S22). Reading: both can be true (the tetra is safe from the angelfish, the angelfish's fins are not safe from a small group of tetras). Write it as "only with a large school, and watch the fins".
- Long-fin black skirts themselves: Aquarium Source: "Black Skirt Tetras can become targets due to their passive nature. Plus, their long fins are the target of fin nippers." (S32)
- Much smaller fish: DFO, "may prey on much smaller fish". (S14)

7.5 Swimming level
- FishBase: "Adults occur in the middle and upper water layers." (S17)

## 8. Health

8.1 Common diseases
- FishBase disease list for this species: "Fin-rot Disease (late stage)", "White spot Disease", "Dactylogyrus Gill Flukes Disease", "Fin Rot (early stage)". (S18)
- Ich, documented outbreak: Banu et al. 2025, market GloFish tetras in India "found severely infected with white spot disease", "with clinical signs of white spot, loss of fin, complete loss of gill filaments, and anorexia." "The infected fish were dark in color, remain isolated, slow-moving, and stop feeding. Mortality was within 48 h unset of symptom" (sic). "The water temperature at the time of mortality was 23 °C and pH 7.5." (S5)
- Tetra disseminated microsporidiosis (new disease, 2021): "A novel microsporidial disease was documented in two ornamental fish species, black tetra Gymnocorymbus ternetzi Boulenger 1895 and cardinal tetra Paracheirodon axelrodi". "The occurrence of TDM in black tetra was associated with chronic mortality in a domestic farmed population". The parasite "occurred diffusely in most internal organs and the gill". (S6) Note: this is not neon tetra disease; it is a new genus, Fusasporis.
- Cancer case report (title only, not read beyond PubMed listing): "Squamous cell carcinoma in a black tetra, Gymnocorymbus ternetzi (Boulenger)", J Fish Dis 2014. Not needed for the guide.
- Canada: "Black Tetra is not included on the susceptible species list, nor are any other Tetra species" for diseases of concern listed by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. (S14)

8.2 Ich and the 86°F heat treatment (IMPORTANT for the shared ich guide link)
- VCA: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF, so slowly raising the temperature of the tank is enough to get rid of the parasite. Not all fish can stand the heat, though. Even for fish that can handle warm water, adding oxygen to the water is essential for this treatment method." Parasites "replicate faster in warmer water." (S27)
- Banu et al. 2025: "repeated chemical treatment, and elevations in water temperature are found to be successful disease control strategies" (general, not tested on these fish). Its outbreak table indicates "the preferred temperature for the outbreak remain < 30°C". (S5)
- Species heat evidence, such as it is:
  - Seriously Fish's breeding method runs the spawning tank at "around 28 - 30 °C (82 - 86 °F)", short term, for a single pair. (S21)
  - Kuhn et al. 2023 kept black tetras at 32°C (89.6°F) for 2 weeks in a feeding study; the abstract reports changes in appetite, not survival. (S7) Full text not read, so mortality at 32°C is unknown here.
  - DFO: "There are no known formal studies on the minimum and maximum temperature requirements". (S14)
- No CTmax or upper lethal temperature study for this species was found.
- Plain reading for the owner: 86°F (30°C) is 7°F above the 26°C (79°F) ceiling given by Seriously Fish, FishBase and TFH. It sits at the very top of Seriously Fish's short-term spawning range, and one lab group kept the species at 32°C for two weeks. That makes 86°F more plausible for this species than for the ember tetra, but no source says black skirts tolerate a sustained 86°F ich treatment. Do not write it as safe the way the cardinal guide does; at most say the species has been held that warm briefly for breeding, with extra aeration (S27), and that a vet or a medication is the alternative. Owner to decide.

8.3 Neon tetra disease (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis)
- Sanders, Watral and Kent 2012: "This microsporidium has been described in a broad range of fish hosts and has been reported from many species of aquarium fishes in several families, including Danio rerio and Danio nigrofasciatus". The paper does not name G. ternetzi. (S34)
- Fish Pathogens database: hosts "Characins, Cichlids, Cyprinids (i.e., danios), and all the carp-like fishes"; "There are no known cures or treatments for Pleistophora." Its references are a hobby site and an info sheet, so it is weak. (S33)
- FishBase's disease list for this species does not include it. (S18)
- Black skirt tetra specifically: no source found naming it as a host. The documented microsporidian in this species is the new TDM parasite (S6), not Pleistophora.

8.4 GloFish health
- DFO, relaying company-supplied veterinary statements: "no evidence for increased susceptibility to, or transmission of, water-borne pathogens, or additional health impediments of CGT2016 or any other fluorescent line relative to non-transgenic counterparts". (S14) This is the company's evidence as summarized by DFO, not an independent study.

8.5 Quarantine
- VCA: "you should quarantine infected fish for at least 30 days before introducing any new fish to their tank." (S27)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "Utilising a quarantine or hospital tank is always advisable no matter where you source your fish." (S25)
- Relevance: the TDM outbreak in cardinals "occurred in moribund fish while in quarantine at a public aquarium". (S6)

## 9. Handling: netting, acclimation, transport

None of this is species-specific except the GloFish manufacturer's method; all is general small-fish guidance (same sources as the ember tetra file, reopened today).
- Lights off: "the fish should be kept in fairly dark conditions, and not blinded by tank lighting, so switch these off!" (S25)
- Mixing water: "Repeat this every few minutes until the bag contains more water from your aquarium than the shop. This could take anywhere from 30-60 minutes." (S25)
- Do not pour the bag in: "it is good practice not to pour the contents of the whole bag into your aquarium." (S25)
- Strays: "double check the bag, the floor and the net to ensure no fish have got stuck or jumped out along the way." (S25)
- Bagging ratio: "Aim to have a one-to three ratio: one part water, three parts air." (S26)
- Transport: "Place the fish bags into a dark container, or wrap them in a towel or some newspapers." (S26)
- GloFish's own method (manufacturer, shorter): "Acclimate your GloFish® fluorescent fish using this 25-minute method: Put the bag with your fish in your aquarium for 15 minutes." It also tells buyers to "remove any heating or cooling packs" and to watch for "droopy or flat fins" as a water quality sign. (S31)
- DISAGREEMENT: 30 to 60 minutes (PFK) vs a 25-minute method (GloFish).
- Cold shipping relevance: DFO's cold data (feeding drops at 17°C, activity stops near 10°C, S14) is why winter shipping matters; GloFish says orders "may be temporarily held to help reduce transit risk" in cold or hot weather (S30).

## 10. Breeding basics

- Spawning type: TFH: "An easily bred egg scatterer. Parents are voracious predators on eggs and fry." (S22). Domínguez-Castanedo et al. 2024 describe it as an "iteroparous batch spawner", where batch spawners "spawn in multiple events within each breeding cycle". (S12)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "They are relatively hardy and can tolerate a wide range of conditions". Water for breeding "on the soft, acidic side (pH about 6.0, GH not more than 8)", "temperatures around 24-26°C/75-79°F". "The breeding tank should be bare, with two or more spawning mops and a sponge filter." (S23, read via fetch tool)
- Seriously Fish setup: a separate tank "around 18" x 12" x 12"", "dimly lit and contain clumps of fine-leaved plants such as java moss or spawning mops, to give the fish somewhere to deposit their adhesive eggs", or mesh on the base sized "so that the eggs can fall through it, but small enough so that the adults cannot reach them." Group spawning: "half a dozen specimens of each sex being a good number." Pair method: condition at "24 - 25.5 °C (75 - 78 °F)", spawning tank "around 28 - 30 °C (82 - 86 °F), with a pH on the acidic side of neutral". "The pair should spawn the following morning." (S21)
- Egg eating: Seriously Fish: "the adults will eat the eggs, given the chance and should be removed at the first opportunity ie. as soon as eggs are noticed." (S21). PFK: "tetras are notorious egg eaters and parents must be removed as soon as spawning is complete, typically in the morning." (S23)
- Hatching (sources disagree slightly):
  - Seriously Fish: "These will hatch in 18-36 hours, with the fry becoming free swimming a few days later." (S21)
  - PFK: "The eggs typically hatch in about 24 hours, with the fry becoming free swimming after five or six days." (S23)
  - DFO: "Hatching generally occurs after 20-24 hours", embryos then "spend the next 5-6 days attached to the walls of the tank before filling their swim bladders". (S14)
- Light: Seriously Fish: "The eggs and fry are light sensitive in the early stages of life and the tank should be kept as dimly lit as possible." (S21). PFK: "the tank should be shielded for five to six days." (S23)
- Fry food: Seriously Fish: "an infusoria-type food for the first few days, until they are large enough to accept microworm or brine shrimp nauplii." (S21). PFK: "infusoria or similar fry food, graduating to brineshrimp nauplii after another 48 hours." (S23)
- Egg numbers: DFO: "the number of laid eggs can total up to 2000" (relaying hobby sources, AC Tropical Fish 2016 and others). (S14) Hobby tier only.
- Development speed: DFO, citing Pan et al. 2008: embryonic development "occurs approximately twice as fast up to the hatching stage with the first cell division occurring after only 30 minutes" compared with zebrafish. (S14)
- Salt and fry: DFO, citing Rothen et al. 2002: "extreme mortality in Black Tetra yolk sac larvae when exposed to 1 and 3 ppt salt treatment but very low mortality at the free-swimming larval stage". (S14) Useful for the health guide's salt advice.
- GloFish breeding is prohibited by license (see 11.4).

11.3 Dyed ("painted", "berry") tetras: welfare

Aside from the fact that the color fades with time and the fact that it subjects the fish to unnecessary distress". (S22)

- Practical Fishkeeping campaign: "Our global campaign against the sale of dyed fish is asking all aquatic retailers, wholesalers and importers to sign our pledge not to stock dyed fish." "Our 1996 Dyed Fish Campaign led to a dramatic decrease in the number of dyed fish on sale." It refers to fish "that are cruelly injected with dye". (S24)

- Not the same as GloFish: GloFish FAQ: "GloFish fluorescent fish are not dyed or altered in any way; their glow is inherited." (S29). Note the manufacturer's "not ... altered in any way" sits oddly beside the fish being genetically engineered (S14, S16); quote it only as the company's claim, if at all.

11.4 GloFish tetras: yes, the skirt tetra GloFish are this species
- Biosafety Clearing-House: name "GloFish® Electric Green® Tetra", organism "Gymnocorymbus ternetzi", developer "Yorktown Technologies", gene source "Zoanthus sp."; "The white tetra (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi) was modified to exhibit a permanent green coloration in natural light and fluorescent green colouration in blue or UV light." (S16, read via fetch tool)
- DFO: "CGT2016 is a genetically engineered White morph of the Black Tetra (G. ternetzi) possessing a single transgenic insert"; "All CGT2016 individuals are descendants of a single G0 founding individual with the transgene construct microinjected at the single cell stage." (S14)
- Hill et al. 2014: "Three species of transgenic fluorescent ornamental fish are commercially available to the public in the United States: Zebra Danio Danio rerio, Black Tetra Gymnocorymbus ternetzi, and Tiger Barb Systomus tetrazona" (source used em dashes; rewritten with a colon). (S2)
- OMIA (University of Sydney) lists "Fluorescent protein, transgenic in Gymnocorymbus ternetzi (black tetra)", "Species-specific name: GloFish". (S20)
- Origin of the idea in this species: Pan, Zhan and Gong 2008 put a red fluorescent gene into "a bona fide ornamental fish species, the white skirt tetra (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi)", and "the vivid red fluorescent color was prominently visible in adult transgenic founders under the normal daylight". (S10). Whether this "Gong Z" is the Gong Zhiyuan of the site's fact 303 was not checked (PubMed showed only the first author's affiliation); do not link them without a source.
- Timeline: DFO: "CGT2016 was first approved and marketed in the US excluding California in 2012 as GloFish® Electric Green® Tetra, followed by GloFish® Long-fin Electric Green® Tetra (also CGT2016) and additional fluorescent colour variants in the Tetra." (S14)
- Current colors (manufacturer): "Starfire Red®, Sunburst Orange®, Electric Green®, Cosmic Blue®, Moonrise Pink® and Galactic Purple®". (S30)
- CAUTION, two species: GloFish now also sells "Pristella Tetras" as a separate line. (S28, S30 site navigation). The product URL for that line reads "pristella-maxilaris", i.e. the X-ray tetra, a different species. The only basis here for the species is that URL slug and Wikipedia's GloFish infobox (S36), so treat it as a lead. Write "GloFish Skirt Tetras are black skirt tetras", not "GloFish tetras are black skirt tetras".
- Care: GloFish FAQ: "They need the same care as other fish". (S29). Its stated temperature (76-80°F, S30) is warmer than the 68-79°F of the wild-type references (S21, S17, S22).
- Breeding prohibited: "Intentional breeding and/or any sale, barter, or trade, of any offspring of GloFish® fluorescent ornamental fish is strictly prohibited." An exception covers "educational use by teachers and students in bona fide educational institutions". (S28)
- Reduced breeding success: DFO, company data: "CGT2016 had over 40% reduction in expected reproductive success in competition with wild-type siblings, but did not differ in juvenile survival." (S14)
- Behavior and cold: Leggatt and Devlin 2020: transgenesis "did not affect the foraging success or aggressive behaviour"; "adult green tetras had greatly diminished cold tolerance relative to unrelated adult white fish", but sibling juveniles were "not affected by FP transgenesis", so "background genetics, rearing history and/or life stage may play larger roles"; "both adult and juvenile white tetras were 3.8 times more likely to take refuge in shelters when temperature declined than green tetras." (S1)
- Legal, US: DFO: "Approval to sell GloFish® in California, including CGT2016 long and short-fin varieties, was granted in 2015 (California Fish and Game Commission 2015)." (S14). The Commission record itself was not opened. Hill et al. 2014 found the transgenic fish "represent a low risk of invasiveness. Any risk is limited to the warmer regions of the country." (S2)
- Legal, Canada: DFO's 2018 assessment concluded "CGT2016 poses Low Risk to Canadian environments under the proposed use for the ornamental aquarium trade." (S14). The final ECCC decision page for the tetra was not opened, so do not state that it is on sale in Canada from this source alone.
- Legal, EU and elsewhere: no source opened (Wikipedia says not permitted in the EU, S36; not citable alone).

## 12. "Did you know?" candidates

A. Every black skirt tetra starts out with ovaries. Mazzoni, Grier and Quagio-Grassiotto 2015: "During gonadal development in G. ternetzi, all individuals first developed ovarian tissue." In presumptive males, "Apoptotic oocytes were observed and afterward degenerated", and "the testis was organized on an ovary previously developed". (S8) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26386207/. DFO restates it: "All juveniles of the Black Tetra go through a female stage of development". (S14). Scope carefully: this is juvenile development, not adult sex change.

B. A black skirt school reacts faster than any one fish could. Startled schools pass the alarm "as a wave", and across large groups it travels "faster than would be expected if the fish were simply responding to the motion of their nearest neighbor." The authors were engineers at Utah State University and the Naval Undersea Warfare Center, publishing in Bioinspiration and Biomimetics. (S9) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32629435/

C. Its closest relatives have spy-novel connections. A sister species in the same genus, Gymnocorymbus bondi, is named for Francis "Frank" E. Bond, and ETYFish notes he "is the father of ornithologist James Bond, whose name Ian Fleming used for his 007 spy hero". (S19) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/. G. ternetzi itself honors Carl Ternetz, a Swiss naturalist who collected in South America "over a period of more than thirty years" (S17). Scope: the Bond link is the genus, not this species.

D. (Backup) Two genes decide whether a skirt tetra is black or white: dominance "at both loci" is needed for the dark, banded look. (S11) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15220394/

E. (Backup) The green GloFish tetra carries a gene from a Zoanthus reef coral (S16), and the whole Electric Green line descends from "a single G0 founding individual" (S14).

### X1. Tank setup

**X1.1 Tank size.** Nothing new beyond 5.1. Seriously Fish (S21, re-read) still gives "Aquarium base 75 × 30 cm 30 × 12 in, Volume ~68 litres ~18 US gal", and "A group of these can be kept in a standard 30" x 12" (75cm x 30cm) - 20g long/70 litre tank." Its 2026 revision adds a difficulty panel: "Space medium tank", "Water needs a steady pH", "Temp flexible", "Social needs to be in a group".

**X1.2 Temperature.** Nothing new on the upper limit (CTmax search repeated today, no study found; see Gaps). New detail from the DFO re-read (S14): the 20 to 29°C "optimal" band appears in the survival section as "the optimal temperature for Black Tetras may lie somewhere between 20 and 29°C"; for the GloFish line, "CGT2016 had slightly impaired cold tolerance when temperature was rapidly decreased (i.e., average LD50 was 8.11°C relative to 7.94°C for wildtype siblings)", and DFO concludes White Tetra and CGT2016 "cannot survive at temperatures below 7°C, and likely cannot survive long-term at temperatures lower than 9.5°C." See Conflict 7 on how the tank guide words GloFish cold tolerance.

**X1.3 Water chemistry.** Seriously Fish's 2026 page reads pH "6.0 to 7.0" and "5 to 20 dGH" (source used en dashes), and flags "Water needs a steady pH" (S21). FishBase 6.0 to 8.0 (S17). The guides use 6.0 to 7.5 (Aquarium Source S32 and a retailer spec). See Conflict 10. Lab limit restated from the full abstract (S4): the species shows "several specializations for uptake in these waters" (ion-poor water) yet "blackskirt tetras were not especially tolerant of low pH"; at pH 4.0 "they experienced a massive loss of Na+", and they "cannot survive in waters with a pH of 4.0 or less because of leaching of Ca2+ from branchial tight junctions".

**X1.4 Filtration.** No species-specific flow source (unchanged from 5.8). Habitat "Small, slow-moving creeks, tributaries and streams" (S21). Seriously Fish's auto-generated equipment box now prints a filter and heater size; it is an affiliate widget and is not cited (and heater wattage is excluded by owner rule).

**X1.5 Substrate (short, species-specific only).**
- Does the fish use the bottom? No. "Adults occur in the middle and upper water layers." (S17). The only bottom use in any source is breeding: mesh on the base "of a large enough grade so that the eggs can fall through it, but small enough so that the adults cannot reach them" (S21), and adhesive eggs scattered in fine-leaved plants such as java moss (S21; S14).
- Inert vs calcareous for this species' water: a calcareous floor is the wrong side of this fish's best-referenced range. PFK: "Inert substrates and rocks don't alter the water chemistry, while non-inert, calcareous substrates do. These contain calcium and other minerals that increase the hardness and pH"; coral sand will "make your water rock hard and increase the pH of your water to over 8.0" (S61, reused). Over pH 8 sits above Seriously Fish's 6.0 to 7.0 (S21) and at or past the top of FishBase's 6.0 to 8.0 (S17). Black skirts do not need soft water the way cardinals do, so the reason here is staying inside the range and holding pH "steady" (S21), not softening.
- Color effect: no source ties substrate color to black skirt color. The only color-and-setting line is Seriously Fish's "best colours when there are some dense areas of planting, with some floating vegetation added to dim the light" (S21). See Unsourced.
- Depth for a planted bed: general only. PFK: sand "around 2.5cm/1"" and gravel "5cm/2" or more" (S61, reused); a planted inert bed "7.5cm layer of any inert aquarium gravel or grit" with a complete liquid fertilizer (S62, reused).

**X1.6 Plants.** Seriously Fish: "dense areas of planting, with some floating vegetation" (S21); for breeding, "clumps of fine-leaved plants such as java moss or spawning mops" (S21). DFO: in the wild "associated with rooted plants in slow-moving freshwaters, like oxbow lakes", relaying the GloFish notification; "The striped pattern is thought to be advantageous in avoiding predation under these conditions (Frankel, 2004)" (S14, company text quoted by DFO). No source names specific plant species for display tanks.

**X1.7 Lighting.**
- Wild-type: dim the light with floating plants for best color (S21).
- GloFish: "To make you're GloFish fluorescent fish stand out add blue lights. The more the better!" and, new today, "Leave lighting on for no more than 12 hours a day." (S29, manufacturer; sic). This is the only photoperiod figure found for any form of this species. Lighting hours are on the owner's list of unsourced items the guides may carry anyway; this gives a manufacturer ceiling for the GloFish forms.
- Eggs and fry: "light sensitive in the early stages of life and the tank should be kept as dimly lit as possible." (S21)

**X1.8 Tankmates.**
- Unchanged list from Seriously Fish (S21): livebearers, danios, rasboras, other tetras, Corydoras, smaller Loricariids, robust dwarf cichlids such as kribensis; angelfish workable because of the tetra's "somewhat rounded shape" (see 7.4 for the angelfish disagreement).
- New, peer-reviewed but narrow: in a shared-environment test, zebrafish showed a fear response to three cichlids "and a lesser response to green Gymnocorymbus ternetzi"; zebrafish "mostly avoiding green G. ternetzi" when offered differently colored skirt tetras (S50, abstract). Read: a GloFish skirt tetra registers to zebrafish as less threatening than a cichlid but not as nothing. It does not measure nipping or injury. Optional detail for the danio pairing; do not overstate.
- Nipping consequence for tankmates: see X6.4.

**X1.9 Blackwater or clear water.** One home only, per the owner's layout decision (tank setup: how; enrichment: why). How, for this species: clear water is fine. All trade fish are captive bred and "unfussy regarding water chemistry" (S21). Tannins are optional, and pH should not be pushed low: the species tolerates moderately low pH but dies at 4.0 or below (S4). Wild water is described once, secondhand, as "brown, but clear and slightly acidic (pH 5.5-6.3)" (S14, GloFish notification citing Sakurai et al. 1992). Why: X6.1.

**X1.10 Cycle the tank first.** General sources, reused from the cardinal file (original URLs):
- "New tank syndrome is the most common cause of death for fish in new fish tanks"; "It can take between four to six weeks for your filtration to becoming fully established or mature"; stock in steps, "Start with a small percentage of the total number of fish you plan to keep." (S64, reused)
- Merck, re-read today: new tank syndrome "usually occurs within the first 6 weeks"; fish "suddenly become lethargic, anorectic, and frequently die" (S53). Owner version: "usually occurs within the 1 to 3 weeks after a new system is set up" (S56). The two Merck pages differ on timing; both say weeks, not days.
- Fishless cycling: dose ammonia-nitrogen to 2 to 3 mg/L, "Do not go above 5 mg/L"; done when a full dose turns to nitrate "with no trace of nitrite within 24 hours before adding fish" (S65, WEAK to moderate, reused).
- pH and the cycle: "Nitrification can occur at low pH, but it proceeds best at a pH near 8" (S66, reused); stalls "Usually this is due to a low pH value (less than 7.0)" (S65, reused). Species relevance: a black skirt tank run at pH 6.0 to 7.0 (S21) can cycle more slowly than a neutral one. Inference; see Unsourced.
- Ammonia toxicity rises with pH: un-ionized NH3 is "approximately 100 times more toxic" and its share "increases with increasing pH and/or temperature" (S67, reused).
- Twelve fish at once is a large first load (the guides' school size), which is why the stock-in-steps line (S64) and the quarantine rule (X3.11) pull against each other. Owner call on wording; see Unsourced.

Notes on the table:
- Lipscomb et al. (S44): "Larval Black Tetra Gymnocorymbus ternetzi and Neon Tetra Paracheirodon innesi exhibited poor survival and growth for MDs relative to Artemia"; both species have "an agastric, altricial larval stage"; "weaning did not result in statistically higher growth than Artemia." Practical point for the fry row: prepared fry food alone is not enough for newly free-swimming black skirt fry; live nauplii carry them until about two weeks. Survival figures are from a lab trial run to 33 days post hatch, not a home tank.
- Ration disagreement: UF/IFAS "For maintenance, 0.5 to 1.0% body weight per day is adequate" (S38) vs Merck "adult fish should be fed 3% of their body weight daily for maintenance" (S54). See Conflict 3.
- Flake volume trap (new, S38): flakes are light, "the volume required to meet the nutritional needs of the animals may be deceptively high. The author recommends weighing the diet to approximate the amount required and avoid accidental underfeeding."

**X2.2 What they eat.**
- Wild diet: unchanged (section 6): mostly worms, small crustaceans and insects, with small amounts of algae and plant matter (S17, S14). Omnivore model in a lab study (S13).
- Staple: flakes and granules (S21). Not a selective feeder (S14).
- Live and frozen, and their disease risks:
  - Daphnia, which both Seriously Fish (S21) and the current feeding and enrichment guides name, is a known parasite route: "Cyclops and Daphnia spp are common intermediate hosts for Camallanus spp., a nematode that is pathogenic for guppies and other aquarium fish." "Aquarium and cultured pond fish may be heavily infected if crustacean intermediate hosts are present." (S52)
  - "Wild-harvested live foods may harbor parasites or other harmful organisms that can cause disease in fish. You should purchase any live foods from a reputable source." (S56)
  - "Cultured live foods can provide a source of high quality nutrition, but care must be taken to avoid perpetuation of infectious disease. Use of wild caught food items is also risky because of the potential for disease introduction." (S38)
  - Live tubifex: reused from the ember file (S46 there, PFK reporting Hallett et al. 2005): 6 of 7 shop samples carried infected worms. Not re-opened today.
  - No source says freezing removes the Camallanus risk from frozen Daphnia. See Unsourced.
- Merck storage rule (S54, re-read today): "A feed container kept at room temperature should be discarded after 2 months of opening. Commercial feeds stored in the freezer should be discarded after 6 months. Commercial frozen feeds such as brine shrimp, bloodworms, mysids, or glassworms should be discarded after 1 year. If thawed, these feeds should not be refrozen."
- Moldy food: "Moldy feeds should always be discarded." Aflatoxin from Aspergillus flavus "causes rapid growth of tumors and a high death rate." (S56)

**X2.3 Foods to avoid.**
- Wild-collected live foods and live tubifex (above, S56, S38).
- Moldy or stale dry food past the 2-month mark (S54, S56).
- Diet balance: hepatic lipidosis "is a common problem in captive fish" from causes including "starvation, a high percentage of carbohydrates in the diet, a high amount of lipids, and rancidity" (S54).
- Nothing species-specific found (no black skirt food that harms it).

**X2.4 Why they stop eating.**
- Sick fish stop first: "sick fish often stop feeding and may appear lethargic. Healthy fish should eat aggressively if fed at regularly scheduled times." (S58). First step: "check the water quality" (S58).
- Cold, species-specific and measured: white skirts "decreased feeding level and activity at 17°C, stopped feeding when temperatures approached 12°C" (S14). Appetite rises with temperature across 20 to 32°C (S7). A failed heater is the species' clearest documented appetite stopper.
- New tank: anorexia is part of new tank syndrome (S53).
- Ich: "anorexia" was a clinical sign in the 2025 widow tetra outbreak (S5); Merck lists "lethargy, clamped fins, and dark coloration" for ich (S52).
- Mycobacteriosis, new for this species (X3.7): "loss of appetite, lethargy, staying alone in the corner of the aquarium" (S46).
- Internal parasites: Capillaria in "tetras, barbs, and other aquarium fish" shows as "Weight loss, pot belly" (S52); Spironucleus "Weight loss (anorexia)" (S52).
- New arrivals: PFK: "Don't be surprised if it takes a day or two for the new arrivals to begin to feed, but if they continue to not feed you may need to switch up the food you are offering." (S25, re-read). Owner rule check: this is a settling-in observation, not a fasting limit; quote it only if the owner accepts a day count here.
- Shippers withhold food before bagging (S76, reused from the neon file; days not repeated here by owner rule).

**X2.5 When to worry (well fed vs underfed).**
- Underfed: "Occasionally however, owners dramatically underfeed their fish." Starvation "is usually the result of poor husbandry"; keepers fighting water problems "may cut back on feed to the point where the animals are in a negative caloric balance and begin to lose weight". (S38)
- Signs list (owner version): "Not eating", "Weight loss", "Swelling or bloating", "Lethargy" among Merck's common signs of illness (S55).
- Overfed: "The most common mistake made by pet owners is over-feeding their fish, often with resulting degradation of water quality." (S38). Merck owner page daily routine: "Feed fish and remove any uneaten food" (S55). Overfeeding also feeds parasites: "Poor sanitation, crowding, or overfeeding can contribute to proliferation of the parasites" (S56).
- Species-specific: DFO relays that black skirts "do not overeat (Frank 1980)" (S14; hobby book via DFO), so leftovers are a sign of too much food, not of a sick fish, unless the fish also show the signs above. Inference from S14 plus S38.
- No source gives a body-shape description of a well-fed black skirt. See Unsourced.

**X2.6 When you go away.** No source found. The vacation steps are on the owner's allowed-unsourced list. The only sourced frequency line is "Fish should probably be fed at least 5 days per week" (S38); per owner rule it must not be turned into a number of days a fish can go without food. See Conflict 4.

### X3. Health

**X3.1 Signs that need action today.** Sources for sorting by urgency:
- Ich is fast: "Mortality can be rapid and catastrophic" (S52); widow tetras died "within 48 h" of symptoms at 23°C (S5).
- Several fish at once points to water: new tank syndrome, fish "suddenly become lethargic, anorectic, and frequently die" (S53).
- Gills: ich may show "no spots visible if on gills only; sudden high mortality event" (S52). Dactylogyrid gill flukes: "Piping, coughing, weight loss" (S52).
- Slow trickle of losses: tetra disseminated microsporidiosis gave "chronic mortality in a domestic farmed population" (S6); mycobacteriosis is chronic (X3.7).

**X3.2 What a healthy black skirt looks like.**
- General (S57): "smooth, unblemished scales, fins, and skin surfaces"; "Skin and fins should be intact, not bloody or ragged"; "Eyes should be bright, not cloudy"; "swimming normally and steadily"; diseased fish "swim erratically (circling or drifting around the tank) or appear listless". "The aquarium itself should be clean, and other fish in the environment should also appear healthy."
- Eats "aggressively" at regular times (S58).
- Species-specific: peaceful and "lively" (S21); juveniles blackest, adults fade toward silvery gray (S21, S14); color "can fade or darken depending on the fish's mood" (S22). Fading black with age is normal; GloFish lists "droopy or flat fins" as a water quality sign (S31).

**X3.3 Water quality.** Steady pH (S21). Ammonia and pH interplay (S67, reused). Merck: copper or other treatment can stall the biofilter, "a transient increase in ammonia and nitrite should be expected for weeks to months after treatment" (S53). Low pH below 6.0 named as a problem condition in Merck's owner page (context: a tank to break down and restart): "low alkalinity, low pH (below 6.0), and high total hardness" (S56).

**X3.4 Ich.**
- Ich signs (S52): "White spots visible on skin or fins; no spots visible if on gills only"; "lethargy, clamped fins, and dark coloration". Transmission "via direct exposure to infected fish or via fomites (nets, etc)"; survivors "may also serve as a source of infection to previously unexposed individuals".
- Treatment without high heat, which matters because this species' references stop at 79°F (section 8.2): Merck's table gives, for freshwater ich, "formalin, CuSO4, increase salinity to 5 g/L for one week at ≥24°C (75°F) if the fish species will tolerate it" (S52). 75°F is inside the black skirt's 68 to 79°F band, so the salt route does not need heat past the range. No source tests adult black skirt salt tolerance; the only species salt data is the yolk-sac fry kill at 1 and 3 ppt (S14). General: "Most freshwater fish should be able to tolerate a salinity of 3 g/L on an almost indefinite basis" (S53).
- Dosing rhythm: "Multiple chemical treatments (with intervals determined by water temperature) are required"; "At warm temperatures typical of home aquaria (eg, >26°C), infected fish should be treated daily. Three to seven treatments may be required." Encysted stages "are refractory to chemical treatment, but cysts can be removed by thorough cleaning and removal of debris from gravel substrates." (S52)
- Over-the-counter: "Over-the-counter medications for pet fish often contain formalin and malachite green and are effective" (S52).
- Copper in soft water: black skirts are not a soft-water fish, but a tank run at the low end (pH 6.0, soft tap) can fall under the copper line: "If the total alkalinity is less than 50 ppm, copper treatments are not recommended because of the high risk of killing fish" (S68, reused). Merck: "The total alkalinity of the water to be treated must be known" before copper is used in freshwater (S53). No KH figure for the species from any source above hobby tier (section 5.5), so whether a given black skirt tank is under 50 ppm is the keeper's test reading.
- Heat: section 8.2 stands. Merck's 86°F footnote ("Mortalities stop at 86°F (>30°C), but survivors remain carriers") belongs to a table of regulated diseases, not ich; do not cite it for ich.

**X3.5 Fin rot and fungus.** Reused general points from the ember and cardinal files stand (ANGFA, FishBase fin rot summary, UF/IFAS on water molds as secondary invaders). Species-specific: FishBase lists early and late fin rot for this species (S18). New link: fin damage is a listed sign of mycobacteriosis in tetras (S46), and nipping causes tears (section 7.2), so a ragged fin has three candidate causes in this species: nipping, fin rot, and a systemic disease. The guide already separates the first two.

**X3.6 Internal parasites.**
- Capillaria and Pseudocapillaria: intestine of "Angelfish, discus, zebrafish, tetras, barbs, and other aquarium fish"; "Weight loss, pot belly"; treatment table lists fenbendazole and levamisole (S52; doses exist in mg/kg on the page; RULES allow only that form if ever used).
- Camallanus: worms "protruding from anus", Daphnia and Cyclops as hosts (S52). This ties the feeding guide's daphnia to the health guide.
- Tapeworms: praziquantel "anthelmintic of choice" (S52).
- Characin warning: "Organophosphates should be avoided in systems containing elasmobranchs and some characins and cichlids." (S52)
- Gill flukes, species-specific (new): a dactylogyrid gill fluke, Diaphorocleidus armillatus, was described as a new species "from Gymnocorymbus ternetzi" among "introduced aquarium fishes" in India (S48), and found again on the gills of G. ternetzi there (S49). This adds peer-reviewed support to FishBase's "Dactylogyrus Gill Flukes Disease" listing (S18); note the genus named in the papers is Diaphorocleidus, not Dactylogyrus, and both are in the family Dactylogyridae. Merck: Dactylogyridae signs "Piping, coughing, weight loss, skin lesions", treatment "Praziquantel in nonfood fish, formalin"; "High numbers of any of these monogeneans can result in catastrophic mortality" (S52).

**X3.7 Neon tetra disease and lookalikes (mycobacteriosis).** New species-specific evidence:
- Black tetra is a documented mycobacteriosis host. Řehulka et al. (S46): "Jahnel (1940ab) described findings of mycobacteria in the following species: black tetra (Gymnocorymbus ternetzi)", and in their own 1995 to 2004 Czech casework, "Cases of fish symptoms of tuberculous nature occurred in the following fish species: ... cardinal tetra (Paracheirodon axelrodi), black tetra, guppy, neon tetra". Signs in their diseased fish: "loss of appetite, lethargy, staying alone in the corner of the aquarium, floating on the surface of the water, cachexia, depigmentation, severe exophthalmos, ascites, erection of scales, loss of scales, skin ulcers, and fin damage." Note: their cultured isolates list does not include black tetra; the black tetra appears among clinical cases, and in Jahnel's 1940 finding.
- Delghandi et al. (S45), Table 1 "Mycobacterium spp., aquatic host and zoonotic potential": M. smegmatis in "Poecilia sphenop, Poecilia reticulata, Gymnocorymbus ternetzi", fresh water, zoonotic potential "Yes". Outbreak factors: "stocking density and low water quality, notably low pH and low dissolved oxygen". Treatment: "prolong antibiotic therapy is generally required as well as depopulation of infected fish"; "there is currently no effective vaccine and infections react poorly to antibiotherapy."
- Merck owner page: "Fish may be infected by eating infected dead fish"; "Poor sanitation, low oxygen, and low pH create conditions that are favorable to these bacteria" (S56). Merck professional: mycobacteria "at this time cannot be detected with nonlethal tests" (S53).
- Lookalike problem for this guide: the health guide's "Start treatment today" line, "A fish that turns darker, keeps apart from the school and refuses food while the water tests clean", fits ich (S5) but also early mycobacteriosis (S46: lethargy, alone in a corner, off food). The difference a keeper can see: ich brings white spots within days and kills fast (S5, S52); mycobacteriosis is chronic, with wasting, scale loss, ulcers or popeye (S46).
- Neon tetra disease itself: still no source names G. ternetzi as a host of Pleistophora hyphessobryconis. Sanders et al. 2010 (S47): "The primary and type host is the neon tetra"; the parasite "infects some 20 species of freshwater fishes in four orders"; the paper does not name black tetra. So black skirts can be neither ruled in nor out. The documented microsporidian in this species is Fusasporis (TDM, S6).

**X3.8 Swim bladder.** The black skirt health guide has no swim bladder section, so none is needed (owner rule: kept only where a guide already has it). Nothing species-specific was found.

**X3.9 Copper, medication and shrimp.** Owner rule: no medication is ever called shrimp-safe; shrimp come out before medication goes in.
- "Copper is extremely toxic to invertebrates and plants, which must be removed before the water is treated." (S53, re-read today)
- "Most invertebrates are highly sensitive to copper and will not survive a copper treatment"; do not return them "until Cu2+ concentrations are 0.01 mg/L or less, but ideally zero" (S69, reused).
- Copper harms the biofilter: "a transient increase in ammonia and nitrite should be expected for weeks to months after treatment" (S53).

**X3.10 Species-specific conditions.**
- Long-finned forms: no welfare or health study found for long-fin black skirts (searched today; results were goldfish and betta work only). Only Aquarium Source's line that long fins are "the target of fin nippers" (S32, lower tier). DFO confirms long fins are a selected, naturally occurring trait (S14). See Gaps.
- Dyed ("berry", "painted") skirt tetras: TFH (S22, re-read): dyeing "requires removing the fish's slime coat with acids, followed by immersion in the dye", and "the color fades with time". Seriously Fish (S21, re-read): "injected with garishly coloured dyes", "often shortens their lifespan considerably". Lead only, NOT a source: a 1998 PFK survey ("Why it's cruel to dye", MacMahon and Burgess) is widely quoted as finding lymphocystis in over 40% of painted glassfish against about 10% of unpainted ones; the article itself was not found online, and the fish were glassfish, not skirt tetras. Do not use.
- GloFish health: company veterinary statements only, via DFO (section 8.4); DFO also says "No formal studies have compared potential disease susceptibility of CGT2016 and wildtype strains" (S14, re-read).
- Cold: X1.2 and section 5.3.

**X3.11 Quarantine.**
- "A 30-day period is the minimum time for quarantine, but longer periods may be necessary." Quarantined fish "should be handled only when contact with all other animals is finished for the day" and "should have their own designated equipment (nets, buckets, siphons, etc)". "Quarantine is not useful for some pathogens, but that does not mean it should not be done. Examples are mycobacteria, which at this time cannot be detected with nonlethal tests". (S53)
- Owner page: "Valuable pet fish should be quarantined for at least 30 to 60 days before being added to the general population." (S55)
- Ich transfers on nets (S52), which supports separate quarantine equipment.

**X3.12 When a fish vet is worth it, and how to find one.**
- Why not just medicate: "fish are often incorrectly treated with over-the-counter medications ... it usually does more harm than good." (S70, reused)
- Mycobacteriosis needs diagnosis and has poor treatment response (S45, S53); diagnosis of parasites is by "Examination of fresh smears or biopsies" (S52), a vet procedure.
- Finding one: AAFV "Find a Fish Vet" tool (S71, reused); "ANY veterinarian can consult with an aquatic veterinarian in ANY STATE" (S72, reused).
- Samples: Merck asks for "A minimum of 500 mL of tank water" with fish (S53, reused from ember via the same page); water "sampled as soon as the fish begin to act abnormally and before water changes" and fish "near death" rather than dead (S70, reused). Merck today: "Freshly dead fish should be placed in a sturdy plastic bag and submitted on ice. A water sample should always be submitted with the fish. An animal that has died and been placed in a freezer has limited diagnostic value". (S53)

### X4. Cost

**X4.1 Buying checklist.**
- Fish: Merck's selection points (X3.2, S57), including "other fish in the environment should also appear healthy."
- Species-specific: active and with the group (S21); fins held open, not "droopy or flat" (S31); no white spots (S52); skip dyed fish (S21, S22).
- GloFish: genuine GloFish come only from licensed producers: the trademark "cannot be used in connection with the promotion or sale of any ornamental fish other than authentic GloFish® fluorescent fish, which are exclusively produced by Segrest Farms, Inc. and 5-D Tropical, Inc." Fish are "intended solely for visual enjoyment as aquarium fish by end-users who have purchased these fish through authorized channels, and not for commercial reproduction." Resale tanks and bags "will be marked with either "GloFish® fluorescent fish", or "GloFish®", along with the name of the specific line". (S28, re-read). Breeding and any sale or trade of offspring "is strictly prohibited" (S28). Cost consequence: a GloFish school cannot be grown by breeding; every fish is bought.
- Captive-bred vs wild: not a buying choice for this species. "All the fish in the trade are captive bred" (S21); domesticated "at the highest level" (S14).

**X4.2 Shipping risks (transit only).**
- Cold: feeding drops at 17°C and activity stops near 10°C (S14), so a cold box is the main transit risk for this species.
- Bag chemistry: "Ammonia levels can increase due to fish waste and pH can drop due to increased CO2. This can create a ticking time bomb if you were to acclimate the fish slowly" (S25, re-read).
- Dead on arrival: "Online sellers often have a Dead On Arrival guarantee, should you see any issues take photos before opening the bag" (S25).
- GloFish's own process (manufacturer, S60): fish packed "in a special bag with water and oxygen, then seal and insulate them in foam coolers", "adjusting with heating or cooling packs as needed", "All packages ship overnight", no weekend or holiday delivery, and "Inclement weather may delay your order". It says a sealed package can wait in "your room temperature office" until the buyer gets home. "All orders are guaranteed to arrive alive and thrive for 14 days." (Guarantee terms are a seller policy; treat as an observation for the cost table.)

**X4.3 Ongoing costs (no electricity figures).**
- Food replacement cycle: discard opened dry food after 2 months at room temperature (S54), so buy small tubs. Frozen foods 1 year (S54).
- Water conditioner and test reagents: no new source.

**X4.4 Vet costs.** Prices are observations only (below). Sourced reasons the cost guide's "Seldom part of the budget" is too narrow: X3.12 (S70, S45, S53). Lab necropsy is an option for a keeper with a dying school: Merck's sample rules (S53) plus the university fee schedules observed below.

**X4.5 Quarantine tank and adding fish later.**
- Quarantine minimum 30 days, longer may be needed (S53); 30 to 60 days for valuable fish (S55). Separate nets and buckets (S53).
- Adding fish later means another quarantine run each time; mycobacteria-positive groups are kept closed (S73, reused) and the disease cannot be screened out with nonlethal tests (S53). For a 12-fish school this argues for buying the whole school in one batch, then closing the tank.
- GloFish additions: no breeding allowed (S28), so a GloFish school is topped up only by purchase, and each purchase is a new quarantine.

**X4.6 Captive-bred vs wild.** Not relevant beyond X4.1: there is no wild-caught trade to compare (S21, S14). GloFish licensing is the species' real price split (X4.1; prices in section 13 and F7).

### X5. Handling

**X5.1 Netting.** No source on small-fish netting technique (unchanged; owner rule keeps it out where unsourced). Sourced: check "the bag, the floor and the net" for strays (S25); quarantine nets are kept separate (S53); ich spreads on nets (S52).

**X5.2 The drive home.** One part water to three parts air, dark wrapped container (S26). Cold risk (S14). GloFish's sealed shipping box can wait at room temperature (S60, manufacturer).

**X5.3 Acclimation: float vs drip, and the shipped-fish catch.**
- Float and mix: "Repeat this every few minutes until the bag contains more water from your aquarium than the shop. This could take anywhere from 30-60 minutes"; do not pour the bag in, because transport water "could have waste, pest snail eggs and all sorts of other things in" (S25, re-read).
- Drip: for "sensitive species like Caridina shrimp, corals and some specialist fish"; "likely to take a couple of hours" (S25). Black skirts are not in that group by any source: captive bred, "unfussy regarding water chemistry" (S21). Float and mix is the fitting method for shop fish.
- Shipped fish: after long transport, the slow method risks rising ammonia as pH climbs, so "transfer the fish out of the bag into your tank immediately after temperature acclimation. Or, alternatively, you can add an ammonia detoxifier like SeaChem Prime to the bag and follow normal procedures." (S25). Mechanism: S67 (reused).
- GloFish's 25-minute method (S31, re-read) ends: "Finally, pour all the water and your fish from the bag into your aquarium." That is the opposite of PFK's rule (S25). See Conflict 8.

**X5.4 How many.** Twelve: Seriously Fish's "at least 12" (S21) and TFH's "a dozen or more" (S22) for nipping; FishBase "5 or more" (S17). Group-size study context (S51): Saxby et al. found larger groups lowered aggression and darting in neon tetras and white clouds, but in tiger barbs (a fin nipper) "there was no association with a decrease in aggression or darting behaviour" at the sizes tested (up to 8), and the authors "considered it possible that the group sizes used might have been too small for a decrease in aggression to take place." Not a black skirt study; it neither supports nor refutes the 12. It is a reason to keep "usually" in the nipping claim (see X6.4, Conflict 6).

**X5.5 Sexing.** Unchanged (3.2): slimmer, smaller males with more pointed dorsal and anal fins (S21); male anal fin hooks on the first 5 to 8 rays (S14 citing Benine et al. 2015). New behavioral detail: "Males will generally claim and guard a territory during spawning events" (S14, relaying hobby sources AC Tropical Fish, Brysiewcz and Jones).

**X5.6 Fish tank granuloma.**
- CDC lists "Mycobacteriosis (Fish Tank Granuloma, Swimming Pool Granuloma)" among diseases pet fish spread. "Wear gloves when cleaning or caring for your aquarium"; "Wash your wounds with soap and water immediately"; seek care if a wound turns red, painful, warm or swollen; "Don't let children younger than 5 years old clean or handle aquarium items"; "Dump tank water and clean aquarium items in a bathtub, laundry sink, or outside." (S59, read via fetch tool)
- Clinical picture: aquarium exposure in "the majority of cases (84%)", upper limb lesions "95%", incubation usually "less than four weeks", treatment median "3.5 months" (S74, reused). Signs about 2 to 3 weeks after exposure (S75, reused).
- Species tie, new: black tetra is a recorded mycobacteriosis host (S46, S45); S45 marks the mycobacterium found in G. ternetzi (M. smegmatis) as having zoonotic potential. No source measures human infection from black skirt tanks specifically. Safe framing: tetras, black skirts included, are among the fish recorded with mycobacteria, so the glove rule applies here as much as anywhere.
- Merck: "When handling fish, nitrile exam gloves should be worn" (S53, professional context).

**X5.7 First-timer mistakes (each sourced separately; no reputable single list found).**
- Stocking an uncycled tank (X1.10: S64, S53).
- Buying fewer than 12 and expecting no nipping (S21, S22).
- Slow, long-finned tankmates (S22, S32; betta and angelfish discussion in 7.4).
- Buying dyed fish (S21, S22).
- Skipping quarantine, or sharing nets between quarantine and display (S53, S52).
- Overfeeding (S38).
- Pouring the bag in (S25).
- A slow mix for shipped fish (S25).
- Letting the tank cool: feeding drops at 17°C (S14).
- Breeding GloFish to sell or trade the young (S28).
- Calcareous gravel or coral sand pushing pH over 8 (S61).
- Bare hands with cuts in tank water (S59).

### X6. Enrichment

**X6.1 Clear vs tannin water: the why.**
- For tannins, the evidence is indirect. Wild water is "brown, but clear and slightly acidic" (S14, secondhand). The species has evolved ion uptake "specializations" for "ion-poor acidic waters", with "a high maximum rate of uptake and a low Michaelis-Menten constant, which allows the tetras to take up Na+ at high rates even at very low water levels" (S4). Rio Negro dissolved organic carbon protected zebrafish against low-pH ion loss (S63, reused; no tetra tested). No study tests black skirts in stained vs clear water.
- Against needing it: the trade fish are captive bred and "unfussy regarding water chemistry" (S21); Seriously Fish suggests no botanicals or blackwater for this species at all (S21, re-read; the page's only setup advice is planting, floating plants and open water). Pushing pH down is a risk, not a benefit: "not especially tolerant of low pH", lethal at 4.0 (S4). Mycobacteria favor "low pH" and "low dissolved oxygen" (S45), relevant only to an over-loaded, acidic tank.
- Reading: the stain is a matter of looks for this species. What the sources tie to welfare and color is dim light, floating cover and dense planting (S21), not tannins. Tea-colored water would dim the light the same way floating plants do (inference; see Unsourced).

**X6.2 Schooling.** Startle waves (S9, section 5.2). Group size (X5.4). Shelter use, new emphasis from S1 (already in the source table): "both adult and juvenile white tetras were 3.8 times more likely to take refuge in shelters when temperature declined than green tetras." This is the one peer-reviewed measure of black skirt shelter use: they do use cover, and the plain white form used it more than the GloFish green form under cold stress.

**X6.3 Cover.** Plant thickets plus open water (S21, S22). Juveniles and adults live "in macrophytes of oxbow lakes" (S14). Shelter use (S1, above). Hiders "by nature" is a hobby-site line relayed by DFO (S14), weaker than S1.

**X6.4 Fin nipping and its tankmate consequences.**
- The sources say the fix usually works, not always: "this behaviour can usually be rectified by keeping it in a small shoal of at least 12 specimens. When maintained in these kind of numbers any squabbling is generally contained within the group." (S21, re-read; "usually", "generally"). TFH: "the fishes' energy is usually contained within the group" (S22).
- Who suffers: "slow-moving or long-finned tankmates" (S22); betta and angelfish fins (S32, lower tier); long-fin black skirts themselves (S32).
- Study context: the tiger barb result in S51 shows group size alone did not cut aggression in a known nipper at 8 fish. No black skirt study exists (F5 above, rechecked today).
- Consequence chain for the health guide: nipped fins are open wounds; fungi and bacteria are secondary invaders of damaged tissue (reused cardinal and ember findings), so a nipped tankmate becomes a fin rot case. General inference from those sources plus S22.

**X6.5 Feeding as enrichment.** No source tests food as enrichment for this species. Sourced pieces: the species "Relishes live or frozen foods" (S22), and is a "micropredator" (S22). Live Daphnia carries the Camallanus caution (X2.2, S52), so the enrichment guide's "Live daphnia give the school something to chase" needs the reputable-source rule beside it (S56). See Conflict 5.

### Conflicts with current guides

1. **black-skirt-tetra-cost-guide.mdx**, Vet Costs: "Seldom part of the budget. Black skirts stay healthy on clean water, with over-the-counter treatment when something breaks out." Sources: OTC misuse "usually does more harm than good" (S70); black tetra is a recorded mycobacteriosis host (S46, S45), a disease that needs diagnosis and has no reliable treatment (S45, S53). Same issue as cardinal Conflict 3. Prices for a rewrite are in X4.7 (observations).
2. **black-skirt-tetra-cost-guide.mdx**, Extras: "the cost of an ich outbreak is a bottle of salt or medication." Not wrong, but Merck's salt route is "5 g/L for one week at ≥24°C (75°F) if the fish species will tolerate it" (S52), and no source tests adult black skirt salt tolerance; fry are killed by 1 to 3 ppt (S14). The line should not imply salt is proven safe for the species.
3. **black-skirt-tetra-feeding-guide.mdx**, "A maintenance ration is small: about 0.5 to 1% of the fish's body weight a day". UF/IFAS gives 0.5 to 1.0% (S38) but Merck gives "3% of their body weight daily for maintenance" (S54). The guide presents one figure as settled. Owner call: name both, or drop the percentage and keep "what the school finishes".
4. **black-skirt-tetra-feeding-guide.mdx**, FAQ: "Can black skirt tetras go a day without food? Yes. Pet fish do well when fed on at least five days a week, so a healthy adult school misses nothing important on the odd day without a meal." Owner rule: never state how many days a fish can go without food. Also the source says "Fish should probably be fed at least 5 days per week" (S38), which is weaker than "do well".
5. **black-skirt-tetra-feeding-guide.mdx** ("Daphnia and bloodworm are the standard choices") and **black-skirt-tetra-enrichment-guide.mdx** ("Live daphnia give the school something to chase through open water"). Not contradicted, but Daphnia is a named Camallanus intermediate host (S52), and live foods should come from a reputable source (S56, S38). The guides carry no caution.
6. **black-skirt-tetra-enrichment-guide.mdx**, FAQ: "A school that size keeps its own squabbles in house, which is what stops the species from turning on the fins of other fish." Also the handling FAQ "Kept in a group of 12 or more, they turn most of that energy on each other". Sources say "usually" and "generally" (S21, S22), and the one group-size study on a nipper found no aggression drop at 8 fish (S51). The enrichment FAQ states it as certain.
7. **black-skirt-tetra-tank-setup-guide.mdx**, GloFish section: "In a comparison with plain white skirts, adult green GloFish tetras tolerated cold far worse". S1 says adult green tetras had "greatly diminished cold tolerance relative to unrelated adult white fish", but sibling juveniles were "not affected by FP transgenesis", so "background genetics, rearing history and/or life stage may play larger roles"; DFO reports company data of only "slightly impaired cold tolerance" against wild-type siblings (LD50 8.11 vs 7.94°C) (S14). The guide drops the "unrelated" qualifier. The FAQ "adult GloFish handle cold poorly" has the same issue. The 76 to 79°F advice still stands on the GloFish spec (S30).
8. **black-skirt-tetra-handling-guide.mdx**, "GloFish gives a shorter routine for its own fluorescent skirt tetras, 25 minutes in all, which starts with the sealed bag floating in the aquarium for 15 minutes." GloFish's method ends "pour all the water and your fish from the bag into your aquarium" (S31), which the guide's own step 3 rules out (S25). The guide presents the GloFish routine without that difference.
9. **black-skirt-tetra-handling-guide.mdx**, "The longer mix above gives the fish more time when shop water and tank water differ." For shipped fish PFK says a slow mix can be "a ticking time bomb" and advises moving the fish right after temperature matching or dosing an ammonia detoxifier (S25). The guide also discusses mail-order GloFish in the same paragraph. Same issue as cardinal Conflict 2.
10. **All six guides**, pH "6.0 to 7.5": Seriously Fish's current page gives 6.0 to 7.0 (S21, re-read 2026 revision); 7.5 comes from Aquarium Source (S32, lower tier) and a retailer; FishBase gives 6.0 to 8.0 (S17). Not a contradiction of the low end; the guides' upper figure rests on the weakest sources. Owner call.
11. **black-skirt-tetra-health-issues-guide.mdx**, "Artificially dyed skirt tetras ... The dye is injected". TFH describes an acid dip and dye bath (S22); section 11.3 already advised "dipped or injected". The handling guide has it right; the health guide does not.
12. **black-skirt-tetra-health-issues-guide.mdx**, quarantine FAQ and section: a month gives "slower problems like a microsporidian infection, time to show". Merck: quarantine "is not useful for some pathogens", naming mycobacteria, "which at this time cannot be detected with nonlethal tests" (S53); owner-version Merck says "at least 30 to 60 days" for valuable fish (S55). Not wrong about microsporidia, but the guide implies 30 days screens out slow disease generally.
13. **black-skirt-tetra-health-issues-guide.mdx**, opener: "It gets ich, fin rot and gill flukes of the genus Dactylogyrus". The peer-reviewed gill fluke records for this species are Diaphorocleidus armillatus (S48, S49), a different genus in the same family; Dactylogyrus comes from FishBase's disease list label (S18). Suggest "dactylogyrid gill flukes".
14. **black-skirt-tetra-health-issues-guide.mdx**, missing rather than wrong: no mycobacteriosis section, though black tetra is a recorded host (S46, S45) and the guide's "darker, keeps apart, refuses food" line fits it (X3.7). The site's other tetra guides carry an NTD-and-lookalikes section.

No source contradicted the tank size, temperature band, cold-limit figures, ich outbreak account, fry foods or sexing lines.
