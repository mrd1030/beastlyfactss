## Claim 1 (PRIORITY): bird fancier's lung from pet parrots, budgerigars, cockatiels, cockatoos, African greys in a home

Article wording: "Having pet birds in the home raises the risk, not only working with them professionally or breeding them" and the FAQ "having pet birds in the home is itself a recognized risk factor". The only case report cited (source 2) is about pigeons.

- Source 6 (Cureus 2024, abstract): "the patient's prior exposure to pet parrots at home was a significant factor." Case section: "She has had two parrots (Macaw and African Grey) for a few years." Also: "The most common causative antigens for HP are bird and avian protein exposure." Strongly positive avian protein antibody panel.
- Source 7 (BMC Pulm Med 2006): "HP can occur with exposure to many species of pet birds"; "budgerigars are the main source of HP in Britain"; HP is "an immune-mediated pulmonary disease caused by repeated inhalation" of dusts. It does not name cockatiels or parrots as specific causes.
- Source 1 (NHLBI): "Having pets such as birds in your home can raise your risk of hypersensitivity pneumonitis."
- Source 2 (Kila): lists "pet bird owners" among people at increased risk of BFL; does not mention parrots or budgerigars.
- Source 12 (snippet only): title "Shortness of breath secondary to a pet cockatiel" (1998). Not opened.

Verdict: supported. Source 6 covers a macaw and an African grey in a home; source 7 covers budgerigars and pet birds generally. No source opened covers cockatoos or cockatiels specifically in a person (source 12 would, if opened).

## Claim 2: powder down grows continuously, barbule tips break into keratin powder; which species

Article wording (line 63): "It's a specialized type of feather that grows continuously, and instead of shedding on a yearly cycle, the tips of its barbules break down on an ongoing basis into a fine keratin powder ... African grey parrots and cockatoos normally carry this powder on their feathers, and cockatiels and cockatoos produce it in large amounts." FAQ: "All three produce large amounts of this powder."

- Source 4 (Merck, bird owners edition), the only powder-down sentence: "lack of powder down in species like African grey parrots and cockatoos". Nothing on continuous growth, barbules, keratin, or cockatiels.
- Source 5 (Merck viral): "In the classic PBFD infection, the first indication of the presence of disease is a lack of powder down on the beak." (sentence reported as a paraphrase by the fetch tool, longer than its quote limit). Nothing on structure.
- Source 8 (LafeberVet cockatoo): "The umbrella cockatoo produces a substantial amount of powder down."; "Powder down feathers are obvious in white cockatoos and can be found in patches underneath the wings"; "compared to the scattered powder down feathers of other parrots."
- Source 9 (Lafeber owner article): "with some species (namely, Old World parrot species) producing more powder down than others."; "These 'dustier' parrots include cockatoos, cockatiels, and African grey parrots." The page does not say powder down grows continuously or describe barbule tips.
- Source 10 (VCA): "Cockatoos also naturally produce a lot of feather dust or powder down from their feathers".
- A search summary (not a fetched page, from Lafeber pet-bird pages) described the feathers as small white plumes with brush-like tips that break into powder. Not verified on an opened page.

Verdicts:
- Which species carry/produce it: partly supported. Cockatoos, cockatiels, African greys as the dusty trio is supported by source 9. "Substantial/large amounts" is supported for cockatoos only (sources 8, 10); for African greys and cockatiels, only "dustier" (source 9). The article's split ("greys and cockatoos carry it; cockatiels and cockatoos produce it in large amounts") is not stated by Merck in that form. Merck names only African grey and cockatoo, and only in a "lack of powder down" sentence.
- Continuous growth and barbule tips breaking into keratin powder: not found in any source opened. Stays unsourced.

## Claim 3: NHLBI wording

Article wording: "caused by breathing in substances that include bird droppings, feathers, and animal fur"; "diagnosed in people between 50 and 70 years old"; "progress toward pulmonary hypertension or heart failure".

- Source 1: "Bird droppings, feathers, and animal furs"
- Source 1: "people often are diagnosed with this condition when they are between 50 and 70 years old"
- Source 1: "can lead to pulmonary hypertension, permanent lung damage, or heart failure"
- Source 1: "Without treatment, hypersensitivity pneumonitis can cause permanent damage to the lungs"
- Source 1: "Having pets such as birds in your home can raise your risk of hypersensitivity pneumonitis."

Verdict: supported. Minor: NHLBI says "animal furs", the article "animal fur" (fine). "Often diagnosed between 50 and 70" is softer than the article's "tends to be diagnosed", which is acceptable. "Permanent lung damage" is also supported.

## Claim 4: FAQ on macaws, cockatiels, cockatoos and ventilation

Article wording: "Hypersensitivity pneumonitis ... occurs in macaws housed alongside cockatiels or cockatoos in poorly ventilated spaces, and improved ventilation and separation from powder-down species is critical for those birds."

- Source 3 (Merck): "has been reported in macaws (primarily blue-and-gold macaws)"
- Source 3: "powder down, such as cockatiels and cockatoos in poorly ventilated environments"
- Source 3: "Improved ventilation and separation from birds that produce powder down is critical."
- Source 3: "a history of being housed with feather down-producing birds in poor ventilation"
- Source 3: "removal of the bird from the offending environment"

Verdict: supported. Wording is close to Merck's; "has been reported" is weaker than "occurs", and it is primarily blue-and-gold macaws. Suggested softening: "has been reported in macaws, mainly blue-and-gold macaws, housed with powder-down birds such as cockatiels and cockatoos in poorly ventilated spaces". Also note the table row in the body ("Improved ventilation is critical anywhere birds that produce heavy powder down are housed") widens Merck's statement, which is about affected macaws.

## Claim 5: PBFD spread and species risk

Article wording: "spreads through direct contact with infected birds and through feather dust, dander, and dried droppings" and "cockatoos and African greys carry the most risk".

- Source 4 (Merck bird owners): "The virus spreads through direct contact with infected birds and through feather dust, dander, and dried droppings." This matches the article almost word for word; the article's sentence is a near-lift of Merck's wording (rewrite or quote-mark it).
- Source 4: "Psittacine beak and feather disease is caused by a circovirus."; "affects mainly young birds and rarely occurs in birds older than 3 years."; "Birds infected with this virus should be isolated."; "there is no cure".
- Source 5 (Merck professional): "Infected birds shed virus in their feathers, feather dander, feces, and oral secretions."; "Transmission occurs by inhalation and/or ingestion of the virus and can occur vertically."; "The virus is very stable in the environment, so fomites are a notable source of infection."; "It primarily affects young parrots."; "The virus was first recognized in the 1970s in cockatoos with beak and feather lesions." It also says PCR screening "has greatly decreased the prevalence of the virus in captive bred Cacatua spp." and that disease "is still seen in African greys, Eclectus parrots, lovebirds, and lorikeets, but is rare."

Verdicts:
- Transmission route: supported (source 4; source 5 adds inhalation, and feather dander).
- "Cockatoos and African greys carry the most risk": not found. Merck does not rank species by risk, and says cockatoo prevalence has dropped with screening. Suggested fix: cut the clause, or replace with "it primarily affects young parrots, and was first recognized in cockatoos" (source 5).

## Claim 6: Levoit Core300-P rated room size

Article wording: affiliate label "Levoit Core300-P Air Purifier (HEPA, up to 1073 sq ft)"; body "covers a typical living room or bird room".

- Source 11 (manufacturer): "Changes the air in a 1066 ft2 room once per hour with patented VortexAirTM Technology." and specs table "Ideal Room Size: 1066 ft2 (1 ACH) / 222 ft2 (4.8 ACH)".

Verdict: contradicted. The manufacturer figure is 1066 sq ft (at 1 air change per hour), not 1073. Also, 1066 sq ft is one air change per hour; at 4.8 ACH it is 222 sq ft. Suggested fix: change the label to "up to 1066 sq ft at 1 air change per hour" (or "up to 1,066 sq ft (1 ACH), 222 sq ft (4.8 ACH)"), and check that the label text in src/lib/data/affiliateProducts.js matches (not edited here). Note "typical living room or bird room" is fair only on the 4.8 ACH figure for a room around 200 sq ft; for a larger room the 1 ACH figure is a slow clean.

## Other statements checked in passing

- Kila quote "resulting from the repeated inhalation of avian proteins found in bird droppings, feathers, and serum": supported (source 2 abstract: "Bird fancier's lung (BFL) is a subtype of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)" ... "resulting from the repeated inhalation of avian proteins found in bird droppings, feathers, and serum.").
- Kila "Early recognition and intervention" / "irreversible pulmonary fibrosis" / "advised to remove the pigeons from his household" / "reduction of exposure to avian proteins, and the use of corticosteroid therapy": supported (source 2).
- FAQ "A 2025 case report ... specifically flags delayed diagnosis, tied to doctors not connecting ordinary respiratory symptoms to a bird in the home": partly supported. Source 2 says "Conventional chest radiographs often fail to detect early or subtle interstitial changes, leading to delayed diagnosis", diagnostic anchoring led to "delays in identifying other causes of respiratory symptoms, such as BFL", and stresses "taking a detailed environmental and occupational history in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory symptoms." It does not say owners should tell their doctor about the bird, nor that the cause is doctors not connecting symptoms to a bird. Suggested wording: delayed diagnosis happens, and a detailed exposure history helps.
- VCA (source 10): "Many cockatoo owners purchase a HEPA air filter to place in the room with their bird(s)." and "Misting them daily with water or taking them into the shower can help minimize powder down ... from shedding off their feathers and spreading throughout the house." Supports the HEPA and mist/shower table rows. Also "not recommended for people with airborne allergies."
- LafeberVet (source 8): "Provide frequent water baths or showers to maintain normal skin/feather quality and to manage the large amounts of powder dust produced by many cockatoo" supports the bathing row.
