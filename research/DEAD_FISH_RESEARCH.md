## 1. First steps when a fish dies

1.1 Take the body out promptly
- D2 (verbatim, written for euthanized fish): "Any euthanized fish or invertebrate should be promptly removed from its aquarium, pond, or other vessel and disposed of according to all pertinent federal, state, and local regulations".
- D3 (verbatim, on Mycobacterium): "Fish may be infected by eating infected dead fish or by other routes of contact." On fungal infection: "Prevention consists of removing all potential sources of the fungus, such as correcting poor sanitation and removing any dead and decaying matter."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. The reasons a source in hand gives are disease spread (tankmates eat the body) and decaying matter as a source of infection. D2's line is scoped to euthanized fish; it is the general principle, not a found-dead rule.

1.2 A decaying body raises ammonia
- D4 (verbatim): "Fertilizers and the decay of uneaten feed and organic matter contribute to ammonia, but in most aquaculture or hobbyist systems, the digestion of the feed eaten by the fish is the primary source of the compound."
- D4 (verbatim): "At low concentrations, ammonia causes stress and damages gills and other tissues. ... At higher concentrations, ammonia will kill fish".
- Verdict: PARTLY. Decay of organic matter contributes ammonia (D4), so a body left in the tank adds to the load. No opened source says how fast or how large a spike one dead fish causes. The hobby search extracts that say "within hours" and "18 hours" are not citable; write it without a timescale.

1.3 Test the water
- D1 (verbatim): "Many water quality issues that cause disease in fish, such as increased ammonia or nitrite, pH fluctuations, and low oxygen levels, are invisible to the human eye. For this reason, the best way to monitor water quality changes is to frequently test and keep records of your water quality parameters (e.g., temperature, pH, ammonia, nitrite, dissolved oxygen)."
- D3 (verbatim): "Excess ammonia in a system is very harmful to fish." and "When total ammonia levels are high (2 mg/L or higher), at least 50% of the water in the aquarium should be changed."
- D3 caveat (verbatim, old tank syndrome): "Simple water changes are not recommended, however, because an increase in pH may cause the ammonia present to become toxic, killing the fish." D3 describes that tank as having "high ammonia levels (often higher than 10 mg/L), little or no alkalinity, low pH".
- Verdict: SUPPORTED for ammonia, nitrite, pH and temperature. Nitrate is not named in either list (see Not found). A page should carry D3's caveat: a big water change on an old, acidic, high-ammonia tank can make things worse.

1.4 Take a water sample before changing water, if a lab may be involved
- D1 (verbatim): "water should be sampled as soon as the fish begin to act abnormally and before water changes are initiated." Also: "The water sample should be separate from the fish sample. The water that the fish are brought in will not correctly reflect what is occurring in your system."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. Useful order for a page: sample first, then change water.

1.5 Look for a cause
- D1 (verbatim): "When fish become sick or die, the first response should be to find out why." D1 lists the questions a lab will ask, including "Have there been recent additions? Which species and when?", "Were the new additions quarantined before being added to the system?", "When was abnormal behavior or death first noticed?", "Number of sick fish per day? Number of mortalities per day?", "When was the last water change? How much was changed?", "Has there been any change in feeding or system maintenance recently?", and physical signs ("Are the gills discolored, bloody, or frayed?", "Are there lesions or growths on the fish?").
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. D1's question list is a ready checklist for a "look for a cause" section.

1.6 Wash hands
- D3 (verbatim): "Mycobacterium can infect people, causing skin infection that can progress to more severe illness." and "It is always wise to wash your hands after working in any aquarium, and people with compromised immune systems should probably not handle aquariums directly."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED as general aquarium hygiene. D3 does not say it about handling a dead fish specifically.

## 2. Disposal

2.1 Do not flush
- D5 (fetch tool): "Don't flush the contents down the toilet because those pipes may lead to a waterway or wet area where the species could survive."
- D2 (verbatim): remains are disposed of "in a manner that will reduce the risk of disease spread, prevent pests and other nontarget species from gaining access to animal remains, and ensure human and environmental safety. Preventing environmental contamination by any life stage of fish that could hatch and/or survive outside an acceptable, enclosed body of water is an important consideration in confirmation of death and disposal of the animal's remains."
- Verdict: PARTLY. D5 is about aquarium contents and live organisms reaching waterways, not a dead fish. D2 gives the disease-spread and environmental principle for remains. No opened source says in so many words "a flushed dead fish carries disease into waterways"; the Edmonton page that says so was not readable (search extract only). The plumbing (clogging) reason: NOT FOUND in any opened source.

2.2 Household trash, bagged
- No opened source names the trash. D2's principle (prevent pests and nontarget animals from reaching remains, reduce disease spread) supports a sealed bag.
- D7 (verbatim, owner-opened) names the trash only for plants: "Freeze unwanted plants for 24 hours in a plastic bag, or heat in a microwave and then place in the trash." For live animals it points to the AVMA euthanasia guidelines and says "Disposal of live organisms should be considered as the last resort".
- Verdict: UNSUPPORTED as a named option for a dead fish. Edmonton's "garbage" advice is a search extract only; Hennepin's trash advice is for plants.

2.3 Burial (depth, distance from water, pets digging)
- No opened source. D2's "prevent pests and other nontarget species from gaining access to animal remains" is the principle behind burying deep enough that a dog or wildlife cannot dig it up.
- Verdict: NOT FOUND for any depth or setback figure. Search extracts gave county setbacks from wells (Washoe County, NV: 25 feet; a county health department: 100 feet for a well without a sanitary seal) and blog depths of 2 to 4 feet; none opened, none citable, and those are written for pets in general, not fish. Write burial without numbers, or with "check local rules", unless a source is found.

2.4 Follow local rules
- D2 (verbatim): "disposed of according to all pertinent federal, state, and local regulations".
- Verdict: SUPPORTED as a general statement.

2.5 Never release live fish into the wild
- D5 (fetch tool): "Dumping those contents into a nearby lake, river, or wetland may make you feel like you are freeing the animals, but you may be introducing completely new species into the natural environment." and "Don't purchase, sell, trade, plant, or release invasive species." Alternatives (fetch tool): "consider the alternatives to getting rid of aquarium plants and animals, ... such as donating them to a responsible hobbyist or disposing of them at a pet store."
- D6 (verbatim, owner-opened): "It is illegal to introduce non-native species into a free-living state, such as being released, planted or escaping into public waters because they can harm the environment, recreation, and the economy. Never release aquarium plants or animals into the environment".
- D7 (verbatim, owner-opened): "Make sure plants and animals cannot escape or are not released into the environment." and, quoting the Minnesota DNR, regulated invasive species "may not be introduced into a free-living state, such as being released or planted in public waters." It also names "dumping pets and aquarium water where they may flow to a storm drain or surface water" as a release to avoid.
- Verdict: SUPPORTED that release is harmful (D5, D6) and, for Minnesota, illegal for non-native species (D6). Write it as "in many states it is illegal" only with a second state in hand; otherwise "releasing it is harmful, and in Minnesota, for example, illegal". The Kansas FAQ and laws pages from the owner's search returned "not found" on 2026-11-04.

2.6 Out of scope, flagged: D5 also says (fetch tool) "One option is to freeze fish or other aquatic animals, gradually slowing their metabolism." That is about euthanizing a live fish, and the site's spotting-a-sick-fish-guide.mdx already rules out freezing a live fish. Do not use D5 for euthanasia; if the topic comes up, D2's aquatic section is the source to read.

## 3. When to call a vet or get a necropsy, and how to keep the body

3.1 When to call
- D1 (verbatim): "Without a thorough diagnostic workup, disease problems are often misdiagnosed, which leads to fish receiving inappropriate treatment that may cause more harm than good. ... Therefore, it is important to contact a veterinarian, fish health professional, or a fish disease diagnostic laboratory as soon as a disease problem is suspected".
- Verdict: PARTLY. D1 says to call as soon as disease is suspected. No opened source gives a threshold such as "two or more deaths" or "sudden losses"; the page can say "more than one death, or deaths with signs of disease" only as the site's own judgment, not as a sourced rule.

3.2 What makes a useful sample
- D1 (verbatim): "The best fish samples for diagnostic evaluation are the fish that are near death (moribund) and/or showing signs of distress or illness that are common to many of the sick fish. Dead fish are rarely acceptable for diagnostic tests. However, if the fish are in good condition, that is, with eyes that are clear and gills still red, they may have some diagnostic value. If they are obviously decomposed or malodorous, do not submit them." Also: "Three to five moribund fish will usually be sufficient."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED. Key point for a reader: a freshly dead fish with clear eyes and red gills can still be worth sending; a smelly or decomposed one is not.

3.3 How to keep the body: cold, not frozen
- D1 (verbatim): "If the fish are freshly dead, the carcasses should be kept moist with wet paper towels in an empty, heavy-ply plastic bag. Keep the sample cold packed with ice or ice packs in a Styrofoam® cooler and shipping carton. It is important not to freeze the sample, especially if tissues are to be submitted for histopathology (examination of tissues for disease processes at the microscopic level), as freezing will damage the tissues. The best way to avoid this problem is to never allow the fish to directly touch the ice or ice packs; instead, wrap the plastic bag containing fish in a layer of bubble wrap or a few extra layers of paper towels to cushion them from freezing temperatures."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED for "keep it cold, do not freeze, keep it moist in a bag, no direct contact with ice". The word "refrigerate" appears in D1 only for the water sample ("Keep the water sample refrigerated and in a dark location once collected"); for the fish D1 says "cold packed with ice or ice packs". "Put it in the fridge" is a fair plain-words reading of "cold, not frozen" but is not D1's wording.
- Conflict to note: hobby search extracts suggest freezing a body until disposal. That is fine for disposal only. If a necropsy might be wanted, freezing ruins it (D1).

3.4 Call the lab before sending
- D1 (verbatim): "most diagnostic facilities require prior notification that a sample is being shipped to their laboratory." Overnight shipping "should be verified for morning arrival. If fish arrive in the late afternoon they may not be processed the same day."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED.

3.5 How long results take
- D1 (verbatim): "the laboratory can make an initial diagnosis of water quality problems or parasitic infection at the time of examination ... Growth of bacteria on culture media usually requires 24 to 48 hours, and sensitivity testing to determine the proper antibiotic to use takes an additional 24 to 48 hours. If tissues are submitted for histology or virology, one to two weeks turnaround time is common."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED.

## 4. Is it dead, or resting or in shock?

4.1 Signs of death in fish
- D2 (verbatim): "Loss of movement, loss of reactivity to any stimulus, and initial flaccidity (prior to rigor mortis) may serve as indicators of death for fish and some aquatic invertebrates. More useful indicators for many fish include respiratory arrest (cessation of rhythmic opercular activity) for a minimum of 30 minutes and loss of eyeroll (vestibulo-ocular reflex, the movement of the eye when the fish is rocked from side to side)." Also: "The heart can continue to contract even after brain death or removal from the bodies of fish, so the presence of a heartbeat is not a reliable indicator of life, but sustained absence of heartbeat is a strong indicator of death."
- D2 (verbatim, general section I7): "Death must be confirmed before disposal of any animal remains."
- Verdict: SUPPORTED, with scope: D2 writes these indicators for confirming death after euthanasia, but the signs themselves (no gill movement for 30 minutes, no eye roll when gently rocked, limp body, no response to touch) are what an owner can check. A hobby search extract says "10 minutes after the last sign of gill movement"; D2's 30 minutes is the stronger figure and the one to use.

4.2 Resting or in shock versus dead
- NOT FOUND. No opened source describes how a resting or stunned fish looks next to a dead one. A hobby search extract says a fish can lie on its side or upside down from constipation and still be alive; not citable.
