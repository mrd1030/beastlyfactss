## 1. Identity

1.1 Scientific name and authority
- ITIS: "Hemigrammus erythrozonus", author "Durbin, 1909", status "valid", common name "glowlight tetra", credibility "verified - standards met". (S3) https://www.itis.gov/servlet/SingleRpt/SingleRpt?search_topic=TSN&search_value=162978
- FishBase: authority "Durbin, 1909". (S2) https://www.fishbase.se/summary/SpeciesSummary.php?id=10642
- GBIF: "Hemigrammus erythrozonus Durbin, 1909", status accepted. (S6) https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/2354398
- ETYFish: "Hemigrammus erythrozonus (Durbin 1909)". (S7) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- Seriously Fish does not record an authority ("Authority not recorded", fetch-tool report). (S1)

1.2 Original description (note: sometimes misattributed to Eigenmann)
- GBIF "publishedIn": "Durbin, M. L. (1909). Reports on the expedition to British Guiana of the Indiana University and the Carnegie Museum, 1908. Report No. 2. A new genus and twelve new species of tetragonopterid characins. Annals of the Carnegie Museum. v. 6 (no. 1): 55-72." (S6)
- The author is Marion Durbin (later Ellis). ETYFish: Durbin is the maiden name of Ellis, "who described many small tetras" (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S7)
- Eigenmann (1917) is a later redescription that FWS quotes (see 11C). It is not the original description. (S4)
- Type locality: not given in any page opened. A search summary named "Erukin, Guyana" but the page was not opened, so it is NOT recorded here as sourced. FWS gives only the Eschmeyer range line "Essequibo River, Guyana." (S4)

1.3 Name meaning
- ETYFish, species: "red-banded, from érythrós (ἐρυθρός), red, and zonus, Neo-Latin adjective of zona (L.), band", referring to the "cherry-colored streak" along the sides (fetch-tool excerpt). (S7)
- ETYFish, genus Hemigrammus (Gill 1858): the name refers to the "abruptly discontinued" lateral line of H. unilineatus, ending at mid-body (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S7)
- FishBase: "Hemigrammus: Greek, hemi = half + Greek, gramma = letter, signal". No eponymy on the page. (S2)
- Practical Fishkeeping: the species name "is said to mean 'with a red zone or stripe'" (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S8)

1.4 Family (sources disagree: this is a recent reclassification, not an error; same split the ember tetra showed)
- FishBase: "Acestrorhamphidae", common name "American tetras", subfamily Pristellinae (fetch-tool report). (S2)
- ETYFish lists the species under its Acestrorhamphidae page, subfamily Pristellinae (Géry & Boutière 1964). (S7)
- Wikipedia: "the family Acestrorhamphidae, the American characins" (uncited in the part read). (S12)
- de Melo et al. (2024): "a new classification of former Characidae into four families" (Spintherobolidae, Stevardiidae, Characidae, Acestrorhamphidae); "The family Acestrorhamphidae congregates 15 subfamilies", and Pristellinae is one of them (abstract read via aggregator; the page does not itself list Hemigrammus). (S5)
- ITIS: Characidae. (S3). GBIF: Characidae. (S6). Seriously Fish: breadcrumb "Characidae". (S1). FWS (quoting ITIS 2018): "Family Characidae". (S4)
- Reading: slower-updating databases (ITIS, GBIF) and Seriously Fish still say Characidae; FishBase, ETYFish and the 2024 phylogenomic classification place it in Acestrorhamphidae. Owner to decide which family the site writes; the neon, cardinal and ember entries should be checked for consistency.

1.5 Genus status
- Seriously Fish: "Hemigrammus has long been used as something of a catch-all for small characins" (the page adds that a revision is needed; fetch-tool paraphrase). (S1)
- A search summary says Mirande (2019) found Hemigrammus polyphyletic, but that paper was not opened. Not recorded as sourced. If the owner wants this, open Mirande 2019 before citing.

1.6 Disputed synonym (do NOT repeat without checking)
- Wikipedia: "Hemigrammus gracilis is a senior synonym." (S12). This conflicts with ITIS, GBIF and FishBase, which all treat H. erythrozonus as the valid name and list no such synonym (S2, S3, S6; the GBIF record shows a status of accepted, and FishBase's synonym list was not displayed in the fetch). Catalog of Fishes was not reached. Treat as unverified.
- Practical Fishkeeping gives the true early history: first exported from Guyana in the 1930s; "an early tentative ID as Hyphessobrycon gracilis" was rejected (fetch-tool paraphrase), and the species has "played the 'change the name game' to the full" (quote). (S8)

1.7 Trade and common names
- "Glowlight tetra" (ITIS, FishBase, Seriously Fish). Retailers also write "Glow Light" and "glolite". No other widely used trade name found in the pages opened.

## 2. Origin and wild habitat

- Distribution: "Known only from the Rio Essequibo, Guyana." (S1) https://www.seriouslyfish.com/species/hemigrammus-erythrozonus/
- FishBase: "South America: Essequibo River." (S2). FWS quoting Eschmeyer et al. (2018): "Essequibo River, Guyana." and noting "Only occurrences within Guyana represent known established populations". (S4)
- DISAGREEMENT on range: Wikipedia adds "the Essequibo River and the Courantyne River in Guyana and Suriname" with no citation. (S12) The two .gov/database sources and Seriously Fish say Essequibo, Guyana only. Use Guyana.
- Habitat: "is found primarily in forested areas, in tributaries off the main river channel." (S1)
- Water: "The water here is typically stained a dark brown with tannins from decaying organic material" and, per the fetch-tool paraphrase, is often highly acidic. (S1)
- Wild chemistry: no measured temperature, pH or hardness from a field site found.
- Not introduced anywhere: "This species has not been reported as introduced or established in the United States", and no introduction elsewhere. (S4)

## 3. Size, sexing, lifespan

3.1 Adult size (sources disagree)
- Seriously Fish: maximum standard length "40mm SL" (1.6 in). (S1)
- FishBase: "Max length : 3.3 cm TL male/unsexed", "Maximum length questionable", and "Maximum length reported to reach 4 cm TL" from another reference. (S2)
- FWS, quoting Eigenmann (1917): "the size range of specimens as 21-33mm". (S4)
- Practical Fishkeeping: adults "grow to around 4cm/1.6" in length." (S8)
- Aquarium Source: "around 1.5 inches in length when fully grown." (S10). Build Your Aquarium: "about 1½ inches". (S11)
- Wikipedia: "4 to 5 cm (1.6 to 2.0 in)", larger than neon and cardinal tetras (uncited). (S12)
- Retailer specs (observations only): LiveAquaria "Maximum Size: 2"" ; That Pet Place "Max Size 1.5"; Aquatic Arts "Average adult size: 1.2 - 1.6 inches (3 - 4 cm)".
- Summary for writers: about 1.5 inches (4 cm) is the referenced maximum; wild-caught specimens measured 21 to 33 mm in 1917; 2 inches is the high end in lower-tier and retailer pages.

3.2 Sexing
- Seriously Fish: "Mature females are more rounded in the belly than males, and tend to grow a little larger." (S1)
- Practical Fishkeeping: males "slimmer with a slightly sunken belly", females larger and fuller-bodied; color does not differ by sex (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S8)
- Aquarium Source: "Females are typically a bit larger and feature a plumper body shape", differences "subtle". (S10)

3.3 Lifespan in captivity (no authoritative source found)
- Aquarium Source: "The average glowlight tetra lifespan is between two and four years when given proper care." (S10)
- Wikipedia: "two to four years", tagged citation needed. (S12). Do not use alone.
- Seriously Fish, FishBase, Practical Fishkeeping (breeding article) give none. (S1, S2, S8)
- Aquarium Co-Op's "3-5 years" is for GloFish overall, not glowlights. Do not use. (S17)

3.4 Wild lifespan: no source found.

## 5. Tank

5.1 Minimum tank size and footprint (sources disagree)
- Seriously Fish: base "60 × 38cm" (24 × 15 in); a tank of about 68 L / 18 US gal "could house a small group of these comfortably" (fetch-tool paraphrase of the volume). (S1)
- Aquarium Source: "You can keep half a dozen fish in a standard 10-gallon tank!", but recommends 20 gallons or more if possible. (S10)
- Build Your Aquarium: "Minimum tank: 20 gallons, long or tall" (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11)
- Practical Fishkeeping Q&A: a 60 L tank is "a reasonable size" for a group of 10 glowlights (quote: "your tank is a reasonable size, leaving scope for more fish"; fetch-tool paraphrase of the context). (S9)
- Retailer specs (observations): Aquatic Arts, LiveAquaria, That Pet Place and PetSmart all list a 10 gallon minimum.
- DISAGREEMENT: 10 gal (Aquarium Source, retailers) vs about 18 gal / 60 cm base (Seriously Fish) vs 20 gal (Build Your Aquarium). The best-tier source sets the footprint at 60 x 38 cm.

5.2 Group size
- Seriously Fish: "Always buy a group of at least 6 of these, preferably 10 or more." (S1)
- Practical Fishkeeping Q&A: "A group of 10 of either of these would fill the mid-waters of your tank with activity and colour." (covers glowlight and ember tetras) (S9)
- Aquarium Source: "you should have at least six glowlight tetras". (S10)
- Build Your Aquarium: groups of at least 6, "Fewer than that causes stress, pale color, and hiding"; 10 or more better (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11)
- PetSmart listing (retailer, observation): "prefer to swim in schools of five or more".
- Research on shoaling group size for this species: none found.

5.3 Temperature
- Seriously Fish: "24-28°C" (75-82°F). (S1)
- FishBase: "Tropical; 24°C - 28°C"; FWS notes the source marks this as "assumed to represent recommended aquarium water temperature". (S2, S4)
- Aquarium Source: "74°F to 82°F (around 77 is ideal)". (S10)
- Build Your Aquarium: 73 to 83°F. (S11)
- Retailer specs: Aquatic Arts 72-82°F; That Pet Place 75-82; LiveAquaria 72-77°F.
- Consensus ceiling: 28°C (82°F), with 83°F the highest figure seen.

5.4 pH
- Seriously Fish: "5.5-7.5"; "tends to be more colourful when kept in acidic conditions." (S1)
- Aquarium Source: "5.5 to 7.5 (aim to keep it on the acidic side)". (S10)
- FishBase: "pH range: 6.0 - 8.0". (S2)
- Build Your Aquarium: 5.5 to 7.0 preferred (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11)
- Retailer spec: That Pet Place 6.5-7.5.
- DISAGREEMENT: lower bound 5.5 (hobby/husbandry) vs 6.0 (FishBase); upper 7.0 to 8.0.

5.5 Hardness
- Seriously Fish: "2-15dGH". (S1)
- FishBase: "dH range: 5 - 12". (S2)
- Aquarium Source: "up to 15 dGH" for general care; breeding "no higher than 6 dGH". (S10)
- Retailer spec: Aquatic Arts KH 3-10 dKH.
- KH: no reliable species figure.

5.6 Lighting
- No source gives a lighting spec for adults. Aquarium Source says the stripe "will really shine" under the right lighting (no intensity given) and that breeding eggs are "extremely sensitive to light". (S10) Practical Fishkeeping covers the breeding tank to keep out bright light: "Tetra species can lay light-sensitive eggs." (S8)

5.7 Plants, substrate, decor
- Aquarium Source: "add a wide variety of plants", "fine-leafed plants of varying heights", "some free swimming space in the center of the tank", fine sand on the bottom. (S10)
- Build Your Aquarium: floating plants, planted midground, sand or gravel both fine (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11)
- Wild: forest tributaries stained dark with tannins (S1), which supports leaf litter, dim cover and floating plants. Not stated as a requirement by any source.
- Aquarium Source: some aquarists add "a small bag of aquarium-safe peat" to the filter for tannins. (S10)

## 6. Diet

- Seriously Fish: "Easy to feed. It will readily accept just about anything offered." and "offer regular meals of small live and frozen foods such as bloodworm, Daphnia and brine shrimp". (S1)
- FishBase: "Feeds on worms, small crustaceans and plants" (referenced to Mills and Vevers 1989; quoted by FWS). Aquarium: omnivore per retailer specs. (S2, S4)
- Aquarium Source: "stick with a standard diet of micropellets or small flakes"; occasional freshly hatched brine shrimp and freeze-dried tubifex. (S10)
- Feeding frequency: Aquarium Source: "Feed these fish several times throughout the day, but keep the feedings light." (S10). Build Your Aquarium: two to three times daily (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11). No higher-tier source gives a frequency.
- Wild diet: FishBase line above only; no gut-content study found.

## 7. Behavior and tankmates

- Seriously Fish: "It is lively, quite colourful and peaceful." Good tankmate for "most livebearers, danios, rasboras, other tetras" and peaceful bottom dwellers; "Obviously, it isn't safe with larger species that may see it as food, such as angel fish." (S1)
- Aquarium Source: "Aggression isn't a huge problem."; leaves slow-moving or flowing-finned fish alone; "Angels are notorious for eating glowlight tetras."; named tankmates: peaceful tetras, cory catfish, cherry barbs, guppies, kuhli and clown loaches, mollies, danios, honey and sparkling gouramis. (S10)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "excellent for the community aquarium" (S8); "ideal for community tanks" (S9).
- Fin nipping: three lower-tier pages say they are not a problem. Build Your Aquarium: "Glowlight tetras are not fin nippers"; males may nip each other with no lasting harm (S11). Aquarium Source: leaves flowing fins alone (S10). A search summary cited a GloFish-focused guide (aquifarm.com) calling them "notorious fin-nippers", but that page was NOT opened. No authoritative source on nipping either way. Record as: no strong source; lower-tier pages say they do not nip.
- Betta: no opened page addresses glowlights with bettas. Gap.
- Shrimp: Build Your Aquarium says shrimp and snails are compatible (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11) Not a strong source; fish this size may eat shrimplets.
- Swimming level: PetSmart listing (retailer): "Swim level: Middle". Aquarium Source: free swimming space in the center. (S10)

## 8. Health

8.1 Common diseases
- FishBase disease list (quoted by FWS): "Fin-rot Disease (late stage)", "White spot Disease, Parasitic infestations (protozoa, worms, etc.)", "Fin Rot (early stage)", "Bacterial Infections (general)". "No OIE listed diseases were reported." (S2, S4)
- Aquarium Source: "Ich is highly contagious and potentially lethal." (S10). Build Your Aquarium: "prone to ich", so quarantine new fish (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11)
- Build Your Aquarium also warns against long-term aquarium salt for this species ("Moderate concentrations can stress or kill them", fetch-tool paraphrase). Low tier, unverified; VCA says salt use is "only suitable for scaled fish", and glowlights are scaled. (S11, S13)

8.2 Ich and the 86°F heat treatment (IMPORTANT for the shared ich guide link)
- VCA: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF." and "Not all fish can stand the heat, though." The parasite also "replicates faster in warmer water." Heat and salt are "less effective than chemical treatment because resistant strains can develop" (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S13) https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/fish-white-spot-disease-ich
- Glowlight temperature ceilings: 28°C / 82°F (Seriously Fish, FishBase), 83°F (Build Your Aquarium). 86°F is above every range found.
- No critical thermal maximum or lethal-temperature study for H. erythrozonus found. No source says glowlights tolerate 86°F.
- Plain reading: 86°F is 3 to 4°F above the top of every opened range, and there is no measured ceiling to justify it. The method cannot be stated as safe for this species from the sources found. Do not write it the way the cardinal guide does (that guide has a measured 50% mortality figure; glowlights do not).

8.3 Neon tetra disease (Pleistophora hyphessobryconis)
- Sanders, Watral and Kent (2012): the parasite "has been described in a broad range of fish hosts", named for its type host, the neon tetra Paracheirodon innesi, and reported in aquarium fish in several families. The paper does not mention Hemigrammus or glowlight tetras. (S14) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4117576
- Glowlight susceptibility specifically: no source found. A search returned nothing glowlight-specific.

8.4 Quarantine
- VCA: quarantine "for at least 30 days" before adding new fish (stated for ich). (S13)
- Practical Fishkeeping: "A quarantine or hospital tank is advisable for any new fish, especially online purchases." (fetch-tool paraphrase of the acclimation guide). (S15)
- The site's cardinal guide already uses 30 days.

## 9. Handling: netting, acclimation, transport

None of these are glowlight-specific; all are general small-fish guidance.
- Lights: new arrivals are kept dark; the article says "switch these off!" (S15)
- Float acclimation: add small amounts of tank water to a rolled-down bag; "Repeat this every few minutes" until it holds mostly aquarium water, "30-60 minutes"; net the fish in and leave the transport water behind, as it may carry waste or pest snail eggs (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S15)
- Drip acclimation is for sensitive species such as Caridina shrimp, about a couple of hours. (S15) Not needed for tetras by this source.
- Settling: give new arrivals an hour or two in the dark; they may take a day or two to start eating (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S15)
- Long trips: "Ammonia can rise and pH can drop" in transit (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S15)
- Bagging: "one-to three ratio" of water to air; bags in "a dark container" or wrapped in a towel or newspaper. (S16)
- Water-chemistry note for acclimation: wild-type fish are from soft acidic water (S1), tank-bred stock tolerates more (Aquatic Arts lists its stock as tank-bred; retailer observation).

## 10. Breeding basics

- Seriously Fish: "Quite easily bred, although you'll need to set up a separate tank". Water "soft and acidic in the range pH 5.5-6.5, gH 1-5, with a temperature of around 26.5 - 29 °C" (80-84°F). Eggs "will hatch in 24-36 hours, with the fry becoming free swimming a 3-4 days later." "Average brood size per female is 120-150." (S1)
- Practical Fishkeeping (John Rundle): bare tank, small sponge filter, 25-26°C, pH not above 7.2, DH not above 6.5; "More than 200 eggs can be laid by an adult pair."; "Within 24 hours the eggs will hatch."; "Parents will eat the eggs if left in."; fry live on the yolk sac about five days, then cultured infusoria, later brine shrimp nauplii and crushed dry food (fetch-tool paraphrase except where quoted). Described as a good first breeding project. (S8)
- Aquarium Source: female releases "100 to 150 eggs"; "Eggs will hatch in a day"; eggs "extremely sensitive to light"; water no higher than 6 dGH. (S10)
- DISAGREEMENT on clutch size: 120-150 average (Seriously Fish), 100-150 (Aquarium Source), more than 200 from a single selected pair (Practical Fishkeeping). Use "about 100 to 200".
- Egg predation: parents eat the eggs (S8); Build Your Aquarium agrees: egg scatterers, no parental care (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S11)

## 11. "Did you know?" candidates

A. A woman scientist named it, under her maiden name. The species is credited to "Durbin, 1909", which is the maiden name of Marion Durbin Ellis, who described many small tetras from the 1908 Indiana University and Carnegie Museum expedition to British Guiana.
- ETYFish: Durbin is the maiden name of Ellis (fetch-tool paraphrase). (S7) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- GBIF publishedIn: "Durbin, M. L. (1909). Reports on the expedition to British Guiana ... Annals of the Carnegie Museum". (S6) https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/2354398
- Verify on the desktop that the Durbin in the citation and the Ellis in ETYFish are the same person before the article states it; ETYFish says so, but no second source was opened.

B. The red stripe is a gap in the pigment cells. Eigenmann's 1917 description, quoted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, says "A broad stripe without chromatophores cherry-red in life extends from the head to the caudal". The species name itself means "red-banded".
- FWS (quoting Eigenmann 1917). (S4) https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Glowlight-Tetra.pdf
- ETYFish for the name meaning. (S7) https://etyfish.org/acestrorhamphidae/
- Careful: the quote says "without chromatophores" and "cherry-red in life". Do not explain the mechanism (blood color vs reflected light) because no opened source does.

C. A name-change survivor. Exported from Guyana in the 1930s, an early guess that it was a different tetra was rejected, and Practical Fishkeeping says it "played the 'change the name game' to the full." Today databases still split on its family (see 1.4).
- Practical Fishkeeping (S8) https://www.practicalfishkeeping.co.uk/features/how-to-breed-the-glowlight-tetra/
- de Melo et al. 2024 for the family split. (S5) https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W4402170922

D. (Backup) Its eggs are light-sensitive, so breeders cover the tank. (S8, S10)

### X1. Tank setup

**Tank size** (nothing new beyond section 5.1)
- Seriously Fish re-read: "A 60 ∗ 38 ∗ 30 cm (24 ∗ 15 ∗ 12 in), ~68 litres (~18 US gal) tank could house a small group of these comfortably." (S1, glowlight-specific). Fishkeeper gives no tank size: "Best kept as a shoal in a well-furnished aquarium". (S19)

**Temperature**
- "24 to 28°C" on Fishkeeper as well (S19), matching S1 and S2. No new ceiling source.
- Ich life cycle speed in this band, see X3.

**Water chemistry**
- Glowlight-specific, new: "Captive-bred specimens will acclimatise to a wide range of aquarium conditions. pH: 5.5-7.5, dH: 1-12 degrees." and "these fish are hardy and adaptable, thriving in tap water in most parts of the country" (UK). (S19)
- Seriously Fish re-read: "Although it will survive in slightly alkaline water, it tends to be more colourful when kept in acidic conditions." (S1)
- Hardness now has three ranges: 2 to 15 dGH (S1), 5 to 12 dH (S2), 1 to 12 dH (S19).
- Wild water (general Guyana, not a glowlight site): "Black waters are acidic, with high carbon dioxide and low oxygen content. White waters are turbid, with low carbon dioxide, high silica, and low acidity." (S34, citing Carter 1934); the Essequibo along the Iwokrama Forest "can be considered a white water river" while the Burro-Burro and Siparuni are "predominantly black water" (search-result text of S34; the extracted PDF confirms the black/white definitions). The glowlight's forest tributaries are described as tannin-stained by S1, which fits the black water class. No measured pH, hardness or temperature at a glowlight site was found.

**Filtration**
- Glowlight-specific: "At ~68litres (~18US gal) and a turnover of four to five times the volume an hour, look for a filter rated 270 to 340 lph" (about 71 to 90 US gal per hour, my conversion). (S1) First flow figure for this species; section 5.8 said no source.
- Seriously Fish's breeding tank: "A small air-powered sponge filter bubbling away very gently is all that is needed in terms of filtration." (S1)

**Substrate (short, species-specific)**
- Material: "Use a substrate of river sand and add a few driftwood branches" (S1, glowlight-specific). Aquarium Source: "a thin layer of substrate with fine sand." (S10, weak)
- Color effect: no glowlight source ties bed color to fish color. The nearest: "It does look particularly effective in a heavily-planted arrangement" but "can appear a little washed out in very spartan setups" (S1), and Fishkeeper's best color "in soft water with dim lighting and a tanin tint" (S19). For the cardinal, a pale bed in clear bright water lightens the fish (C11 in the cardinal file); not transferable as a glowlight claim.
- Inert vs calcareous: the glowlight survives slightly alkaline water (S1) and captive-bred fish adapt widely (S19), so a calcareous bed is less dangerous than for a cardinal but still works against the acidic water that brings out color (S1). General: "Even standard pea gravel contains sufficient calcium to boost the pH and hardness"; coral sand raises pH "to over 8.0" (S37, reused). pH 8 is above every glowlight range (S1, S2, S19 top out at 7.5 to 8.0).
- Depth: general, about 2.5 cm (1 in) of sand; about 7.5 cm (3 in) of inert gravel for rooted plants with liquid fertilizer (S37, S38, reused).
- Does the fish use the bottom? Sources disagree. Fishkeeper: larger shoals "will tend to occupy the lower reaches of the aquarium" (S19). PetSmart listing: "Swim level: Middle" (retailer, section 7); Aquarium Source: open swimming space in the center (S10); PFK: a group "would fill the mid-waters" (S9). Lab work measured "vertical area use" as part of the alarm response (S24, search result only), so a school dropping low can be an alarm or shy response (inference; see X6). Reading: mid to lower water, not a bottom forager; no source says glowlights feed off the substrate.

**Plants**
- "It does look particularly effective in a heavily-planted arrangement" (S1); "Glowlights appreciate the cover of aquatic plants" and are "equally at home in tanks furnished with bogwood and leaf litter" (S19). Breeding tank: "clumps of fine-leaved plants such as java moss or spawning mops" (S1).
- Salt and plants: "Plants in an aquarium or small ornamental pond may not tolerate increased salinity." (S29) Relevant because the health guide uses salt for ich in what is a planted display tank. See Conflicts.

**Lighting**
- Glowlight-specific, sources disagree: Seriously Fish "Use fairly dim lighting. Under these conditions the true beauty of the fish will be revealed." (S1) Fishkeeper "Lighting: No special requirements" yet "undoubtedly showing their best colour in soft water with dim lighting and a tanin tint" (S19). Aquarium Source: "Under the right lighting conditions, the stripe will really shine!" (S10, weak).
- Glowlight-specific, peer-reviewed: "The cones of the glowlight tetra, for example, continue to go through undiminished cycles of contraction and relaxation that mirror the previous light:dark cycle for at least two weeks in continual darkness." Equatorial species "rely on internal pacemakers to a much greater degree and are relatively insensitive to exogenous light signals." (S25) Reading (inference, owner review): the eye runs on a built-in day clock, which is a reason for a steady daily light schedule; the paper does not test welfare or recommend hours.
- Eggs and fry: "The eggs and fry are light sensitive in the early stages of life and the tank should be kept in darkness if possible." (S1)
- Light hours: none found (owner allows unsourced).

**Tankmates**
- Glowlight-specific: "One of the best tetras for the 'general' community setup." Good with "most livebearers, danios, rasboras, other tetras and peaceful bottom dwellers"; not "with larger species that may see it as food, such as angel fish." (S1, re-read)
- Glowlight-specific: "a peaceful and sometimes slightly shy species", "suitable for community aquaria containing fish of a similar size and disposition", and "a good dither fish for dwarf cichlids in larger aquaria." (S19)
- Glowlight-specific, peer-reviewed: lab studies used cichlids as glowlight predators: the convict cichlid as "a potential fish predator" (S21), and Jack Dempsey cichlids paired with tetras, which bit at them (S20, S22). Supports keeping glowlights away from mid-size and large cichlids; the angelfish line already rests on S1 and S10.
- Fin nipping, bettas, shrimp: no strong source either way; see X6 and Unsourced.

**Blackwater or clear water** (the how; the why is in X6)
- Seriously Fish's recipe: river sand, driftwood (dried beech stripped of bark as an alternative), "A few handfuls of dried leaves (again beech can be used, or oak leaves are also suitable)"; "Allow the wood and leaves to stain the water the colour of weak tea"; "A small net bag filled with aquarium-safe peat can be added to the filter to aid in the simulation of black water conditions"; maintenance "removing old leaves and replacing them every few weeks so they don't rot and foul the water." (S1, glowlight-specific)
- Peat: Seriously Fish's own neon and cardinal pages call peat collection "both unsustainable and environmentally-destructive" (C11 cardinal, N12 neon). The glowlight page does not. Source vs source within one publisher; see Conflicts.
- Wood caution (congener): a fatal swim bladder fungal infection in pretty tetras (Hemigrammus pulcher) in a public aquarium had as its "exogenous source of infection ... probably the ample wooden decoration and plants inside the aquarium." (S32) One case, a different species. Owner review whether it belongs in a guide.
- Chemistry: in hard, high-KH water tannins change pH little (C47 cardinal, weak); "Even a simple 20% weekly water change has the potential to disrupt or raise the pH and dilute the colouration" (S55, reused).

**Cycle the tank first** (owner decision: the single most important step at setup)
- "New tank syndrome is the most common cause of death for fish in new fish tanks"; 4 to 6 weeks for filtration to mature; seed with media from a healthy tank; stock in steps (S52, reused).
- Low pH slows the cycle: nitrification "proceeds best at a pH near 8" (S54); stalls are "Usually this is due to a low pH value (less than 7.0)" (S53, weak). Inference (unsourced): cycle before pushing the tank acidic with peat or leaves.
- Ammonia toxicity rises with pH and temperature; un-ionized NH3 is "approximately 100 times more toxic" (S41, reused).
- Glowlight angle: captive-bred and "hardy and adaptable" (S19), so the species is not as fragile as a wild cardinal, but no source exempts it from a cycled tank.

**What they eat**
- Wild: "Feeds on worms, small crustaceans and plants" (S2, section 6). No gut study.
- Staple: flakes and granules (S1), flake (S19), micropellets or small flakes (S10). "Flaked feeds may be too large for a small-mouthed fish to easily ingest." (S35)
- Live and frozen: bloodworm, Daphnia, brine shrimp (S1); daphnia and cyclops (S19).
- Disease risks of live foods (general): "Cyclops and Daphnia spp are common intermediate hosts for Camallanus spp" (S44, reused). Live tubifex: of 7 shop samples "6 contained infected oligochaetes" (S57, reused). The current feeding guide offers "freeze-dried tubifex" (from S10); no source opened says whether freeze-drying removes the risk. See Unsourced.
- Merck storage rule (S35, re-read today): "A feed container kept at room temperature should be discarded after 2 months of opening." "Commercial feeds stored in the freezer should be discarded after 6 months." "Commercial frozen feeds such as brine shrimp, bloodworms, mysids, or glassworms should be discarded after 1 year." "If thawed, these feeds should not be refrozen."

**Foods to avoid**
- Oversized flake for small mouths (S35); live tubifex and wild-sourced live crustaceans (S57, S44). No glowlight-specific avoid list exists.
- Diet balance (general): hepatic lipidosis "can occur for multiple reasons (eg, starvation, a high percentage of carbohydrates in the diet, a high amount of lipids, and rancidity)." (S35)

**Why they stop eating**
- Water first: "If you suspect that fish are getting sick, the first thing to do is check the water quality." (S42, reused); new tank syndrome lists decreased appetite (S52).
- Ich: "Lethargy and decreased appetite" are early ich signs (S13, re-read).
- Transport: farms withhold food before shipping and stress weakens immunity (S30); "they may take a day or two to start eating" after arrival (S15, section 9).
- Shy or under-schooled fish: small or lonely groups "spend most of their time in hiding rather than adorning your tank with color" (S10, weak); "sometimes slightly shy" (S19).
- Alarm cues (glowlight-specific, peer-reviewed): glowlights react to "conspecific skin extract (with alarm pheromone)" with antipredator behavior and fin flicking (S20), and to the odor of a predator that has eaten tetras (S21, S22). Whether this suppresses feeding was not stated in the abstracts read. Inference (owner review): a dead or injured glowlight left in the tank is a plausible reason the school goes quiet and stops feeding. The removal rule itself is sourced (X3).
- Internal parasites: Spironucleus "Weight loss (anorexia)" (S44, reused).

**When to worry (well fed vs underfed)**
- Well fed baseline: "Healthy fish should eat aggressively if fed at regularly scheduled times." (S42, reused)
- Female shape is not overfeeding: females are "more rounded in the belly" (S1), "fuller bodied" (S19), and a conditioned female is "very robust in the belly" (S8). Males are "slimmer with a slightly sunken belly region" (S8), so a slightly sunken male belly is normal, not underfeeding (reading of S8).
- Warning signs (general): Merck's illness list includes "Not eating", "Weight loss", "Swelling or bloating" and "Lethargy" (S36, re-read).
- Overfeeding: "be cautious not to overfeed them (this can lead to a rapid decrease in water quality)" and feed only what is eaten "within a few minutes" (S10, weak, the second part paraphrase).
- No source gives a body condition score for tetras.

**When you go away**
- Vacation steps: owner allows unsourced. Never state days without food (owner decision). Storage rule above applies to any food left for a sitter.

### X3. Health

**Signs that need action today**
- Ich: "fish infected with even a single Ich parasite should always be treated immediately because of its explosive reproductive rate"; untreated it "may result in 100% mortality." (S29)
- Dead fish out at once: dead fish should be "removed as soon as they are seen because mature trophonts will quickly abandon a fish once it has died and begin reproducing in the environment" (S29; the start of the sentence fell on a page break in the extract); for NTD, "Spores released from dead fish are a likely source of infection" (S28).
- Ich early signs: increased mucus, "Lethargy and decreased appetite", flashing, "Rapid breathing or gasping at the surface if the parasite has infected the gills" (S13, re-read).
- Ammonia: test "immediately any time fish appearance or behavior changes (including reduced feeding)" (S41, reused).
- Glowlight-specific behavior that is not a disease sign: fin flicking is the species' alarm display after it detects alarm pheromone (S20). A school fin flicking and bunching after a death or a scare is an alarm response, not by itself illness (inference from S20; owner review). Flashing (rubbing on objects) remains an ich sign (S13).

**What a healthy glowlight looks like**
- General: "smooth, unblemished scales, fins, and skin surfaces"; "Skin and fins should be intact, not bloody or ragged"; "Eyes should be bright, not cloudy"; "swimming normally and steadily" (S47, reused).
- Glowlight-specific: "the white fin tips will be a strong contrast to their bright orange body stripe" (S19); "lively, quite colourful and peaceful" (S1); eats readily: "It will readily accept just about anything offered." (S1). Color is best in acidic, dim, tea-tinted water (S1, S19), so a duller fish in bright, bare or alkaline water may be showing the setup, not disease (reading of S1 and S19).
- Stripe color wording: Fishkeeper says "bright orange" (S19), the 1917 description "cherry-red in life" (S4), ETYFish "cherry-colored" (S7). The guides say red or red-orange; both are sourced.

**Water quality**
- The ranges, now with Fishkeeper: pH 5.5 to 7.5, 1 to 12 dH (S19).

**Ich (and copper in soft water)**
- Ich basics from a stronger source than VCA (S29): "All species of freshwater fish are considered susceptible"; "The disease is highly contagious"; outbreaks "often appear when water temperatures are changing more rapidly"; "The disease is particularly severe when fish are crowded."
- Temperature and timing: "At warm temperatures (75°F to 79°F), the life cycle is completed in about 3 to 6 days. At these temperatures, chemical treatments should be applied daily, and a minimum of 3 to 5 treatments are required." "Treatments should never be discontinued until all mortality from Ich has stopped, and re-testing of fish may be necessary." (S29) The glowlight's whole range (75 to 82°F) sits in or just above that warm band, so a glowlight tank already runs a fast cycle (reading).
- Heat: "Most strains of ich cannot survive in water temperatures above 86ºF", and the method needs added oxygen and fish that tolerate heat (S13, re-read). No glowlight source supports 86°F (section 8.2 stands).
- Salt, two dosing sources: VCA "about a tablespoon of salt per 5 gallons of water", "only suitable for scaled fish", water changes afterward (S13). UF/IFAS: "at warmer water temperatures (75 to 79°F), use of 4 to 5 g/L (= 4 to 5 ppt) salt (sodium chloride) in a prolonged bath for 7 to 10 days is another effective treatment in smaller systems, provided the fish species can handle the increased salt concentration"; "theronts are intolerant to increased salinity levels of 3 to 5 ppt"; "Most freshwater fish can tolerate 5 ppt salinity for several weeks"; "Plants in an aquarium ... may not tolerate increased salinity." (S29) No source gives a glowlight salt tolerance. See Conflicts.
- Formalin: "usually applied at a concentration of 25 mg/L, which is equivalent to 1 ml of formalin per 10 gallons"; "half-dose" for sensitive species; formalin removes oxygen, so aerate (S29, partly paraphrase). Per RULES, doses only if the owner picks a source.
- Potassium permanganate: "not recommended as a treatment for Ich" (S29).
- Copper: copper sulfate is "toxic to fish, particularly in water of low alkalinity"; "NEVER use copper sulfate without first testing the total alkalinity of the water"; "Do not use copper sulfate if the total alkalinity is less than 50 mg/L"; "if you do not know the alkalinity of your water and cannot measure it, DO NOT USE COPPER SULFATE" (S29). Same 50 ppm rule in S39 (reused). Characids are the copper-sensitive group: in ion-poor water "all four characid fish species were more sensitive to copper than the catfish and the cichlids," with the lowest LC50 (12.8 µg/L) in the congener Hemigrammus rhodostomus (S31). Glowlight angle: a glowlight kept soft and acidic for color (S1) is the low-alkalinity case; a glowlight kept in harder tap water (S19) is less so. Copper is also toxic to invertebrates: "Most invertebrates are highly sensitive to copper and will not survive a copper treatment" (S40, reused). Owner decision: no medication is called shrimp-safe.
- Survivors as carriers: "Survivors of an Ich outbreak may also serve as reservoirs of infection" (S29); VCA agrees previously infected fish "can remain carriers" (S13).

**Fin rot and fungus**
- FishBase lists early and late fin rot for this species (section 8.1). General sources reused from the ember file: fin rot bacteria "usually Aeromonas, Pseudomonas and Flavobacterium" (ANGFA, S53 ember); "Beginning with white margin of fins, when all fins are erroded, tail turns red." (FishBase fin rot, S39 ember).
- Fungus (general): Saprolegnia and kin are "fungus-like protists" and "secondary tissue invaders that follow traumatic injuries" or poor water and low temperature (S48, reused); "Fungal spores are common in the aquatic environment, but do not usually cause disease in healthy fish" (S42, reused).
- Congener note: deep (systemic) fungal disease, not cotton-wool fungus, killed pretty tetras with "inappetence, exophthalmia, erratic swimming, eroded scales, anaemia of the gills and abdominal distension," traced probably to wood decor and plants (S32).

**Internal parasites** (general; all S44 reused)
- Camallanus: red worms "protruding from the anus"; Cyclops and Daphnia as intermediate hosts; fenbendazole or levamisole. Spironucleus: weight loss and anorexia; metronidazole. Tapeworms: praziquantel. Diagnosis is by wet mount or smear, a vet or lab job.

**Neon tetra disease and lookalikes**
- P. hyphessobryconis is "reported from many species of aquarium fishes in several families", "infecting some 20 species of freshwater fishes in four orders" (S28). A congener is on record: the paper cites Opitz (1942), "Mikrosporidienkrankheit Plistophora auch beim Hemigrammus ocellifer und Brachydanio rerio" (S28, reference list). No report names the glowlight.
- Transmission: "infection is initiated by ingestion of spores"; "Spores released from dead fish are a likely source of infection"; spores may be released during spawning and tank mates "quickly eat available eggs"; maternal transfer raised as a possibility (S28).
- Mycobacteriosis lookalike: Characidae (tetras) "may be more prone to the infection"; "There is no effective treatment for infected fish" (S49, reused); signs "variable and nonspecific" including emaciation, paleness and skeletal deformities (S45, reused).
- Heart disease in tetras (context only): valvular endocardiosis in four neons and one rummy-nose; "prevalence of endocardiosis in tetras of 4.3% and 5.7%" in two diagnostic labs' cardiac sections (S33). No glowlight case; probably not guide material.

**Swim bladder**
- The current glowlight health guide has no swim bladder section. Only the congener mycosis (S32), which presented as a swollen swim bladder, was found. Nothing glowlight-specific.

**Copper, medication and shrimp**
- See Ich above (S29, S31, S39, S40). Owner decision stands: shrimp come out before any medication goes in; nothing is called shrimp-safe.

**Quarantine** (owner decision: the single most important step for every fish added)
- "Preventing introduction of this parasite is one of the most important reasons that all incoming fish should be quarantined." "At the warm water temperatures required for many aquarium fish, active disease will often become evident 1 to 3 weeks after shipping. For this reason, a minimum 30-day quarantine period is recommended for new fish." (S29)
- "nets, siphon hoses, and other equipment that have not been disinfected should not be shared between tanks, especially in a quarantine area"; plants exposed to infected fish "may carry tomont stages." (S29) Useful for the netting and quarantine sections.
- Merck: "A 30-day period is the minimum time for quarantine, but longer periods may be necessary." (S46, reused)

**When a fish vet is worth it and how to find one** (general, reused)
- AAFV Find a Fish Vet map, North America only (S59). A local vet can consult an aquatic vet in any state (S58).
- Why not just medicate: fish "are often incorrectly treated with over-the-counter medications" and serial treatments usually do "more harm than good" (S43). What to bring: about a quart of tank water, not bag water; live sick fish rather than dead ones; history (S43).

**Species-specific conditions**
- The stripe: no source describes it fading as a disease sign. Color depends on water and light (S1, S19). Inference: a glowlight whose stripe is dull while the rest of the school is bright, in the same water, is the one to watch (owner review).
- Albino form: "Care for this is identical to the standard form." (S1). Fishkeeper: "an albino strain is widely available." (S19). No source on albino health differences.

### X4. Cost

**Captive-bred vs wild** (glowlight-specific, new and strong for this guide)
- "Don't expect to see any wild specimens for sale, though. All fish entering the trade are mass bred on commercial farms in various parts of the world. Most of the ones on sale in the UK originate from Eastern Europe." (S1, re-read)
- "Most specimens in the aquatics trade are captive-bred." "Widely bred in captivity, wild Glowlights are seldom seen" (S19).
- So the wild-vs-bred question the cardinal guide needs does not arise; a buyer is choosing between farmed batches. The albino is a selectively bred trade form (S1).

**Buying checklist**
- General (S47, reused): intact fins and skin, bright eyes, steady swimming; "The aquarium itself should be clean, and other fish in the environment should also appear healthy."
- Glowlight-specific cues: a lively, peaceful, colorful fish (S1); a group, not singles (S1: "at least 6 of these, preferably 10 or more"); store fish in a bare, bright tank may look "washed out" (S1), so color alone is a weak test (reading).
- Ich after shipping shows in 1 to 3 weeks (S29), so a healthy-looking shop tank is not proof; quarantine still applies.

**Shipping risks (transit only)**
- Bag water degrades "due to accumulation of carbon dioxide, ammonia, and other waste products during shipment" (S30). "Ammonia can rise and pH can drop" (S15, section 9). Stress "weakens the immune system of the fish, resulting in increased susceptibility to disease" (S30).
- Retail costs: see Observations.

**Ongoing costs (no electricity figures, owner decision)**
- Food storage rule (S35) caps how long an opened pot is usable: 2 months at room temperature. Frozen foods 1 year.
- Test kit refills: Merck recommends testing every two weeks (E13 neon, Merck "Routine Health Care of Fish", reused).

**Vet costs**
- AAFV and fish-vet consults exist (S58, S59); OTC overuse warning (S43). See Conflicts: the current cost guide says "Rarely a line item."

**Quarantine tank and adding fish later**
- Every addition gets a 30-day minimum quarantine (S29, S46). Survivors of ich can carry it (S29), and NTD spores come from dead fish (S28), which is the disease reason for buying the school at once.
- Shipping is charged per order (observations), so topping up a few fish online costs nearly as much to ship as a full school (arithmetic on observations, not a source).

**GloFish**: not relevant. No GloFish glowlight exists in any page opened; GloFish tetras are modified black skirts (S17, section 13).

### X5. Handling

**Netting**
- Glowlight-specific: the species has an alarm pheromone in its skin and reacts to "conspecific skin extract" (S20). Inference (owner review): a fish injured in the net releases alarm cue that unsettles the school, which is one more reason for slow, careful netting. Technique (net size, herding, cupping) remains unsourced, as in the other tetra files.
- Separate nets per tank: "nets, siphon hoses, and other equipment that have not been disinfected should not be shared between tanks" (S29).

**Drive home**
- Bagging one part water to three parts air, dark container (S16, section 9). Bag water degrades in transit (S30).

**Acclimation (float vs drip; the shipped-fish catch)**
- Float and mix over 30 to 60 minutes; drip for sensitive species (S15, section 9).
- Glowlight-specific: "Captive-bred specimens will acclimatise to a wide range of aquarium conditions." (S19) Supports the guide's position that drip acclimation is rarely needed.
- Shipped fish (reused from the cardinal file, C44 = S15): "Ammonia levels can increase due to fish waste and pH can drop due to increased CO2." After a long transport slow acclimation can be risky; move fish after temperature matching or add an ammonia detoxifier to the bag (partly paraphrase). Chemistry: the toxic un-ionized share of ammonia rises with pH (S41). See Conflicts.

**How many**
- "Always buy a group of at least 6 of these, preferably 10 or more." "It is a shoaling species by nature, and will fare much better when in the company of its own kind." (S1). "6 is a workable minimum" and larger shoals "can be breathtaking" (S19).

**Sexing**
- "Adults can be easily sexed, with the males being slimmer and having a slightly sunken belly region." (S8, re-read). Note the tension: Aquarium Source calls the differences "subtle" (S10, section 3.2), PFK says "easily sexed" (S8). "Mature females may appear fuller bodied." (S19)
- Spawning trivia: "during the act itself the pair often turn completely upside down." (S1)

**Fish tank granuloma** (general, reused)
- CDC: gloves when cleaning, especially over cuts; wash hands; children under 5 should not clean tanks; dump water away from the kitchen sink (S50, paraphrase in the cardinal file). MedlinePlus: bump 2 to 3 weeks after exposure, months of antibiotics (S51). Tetras among the families "more prone" to mycobacteria, which thrive in "acidic pH ... high fulvic acid, and high humic acid" (S49). A tea-stained, acidic glowlight tank fits that description (inference).

**First-timer mistakes** (each sourced separately)
- Uncycled tank (S52); buying too few, so the fish hide (S10, S1); skipping quarantine (S29, S46); angelfish or cichlid tankmates (S1, S20, S21); pouring bag water in (S15); sharing nets between tanks (S29); a heater set above the 82°F ceiling for ich (sections 5.3, 8.2); copper in soft water (S29, S39).

### X6. Enrichment

**Clear vs tannin water (the why)**
- For tannins, glowlight-specific: wild water "stained a dark brown with tannins" (S1); "Originating in forest streams where the water is the colour of black tea and the light levels low" (S19); best color "in soft water with dim lighting and a tanin tint" (S19); "Allow the wood and leaves to stain the water the colour of weak tea", "Under these conditions the true beauty of the fish will be revealed" (S1).
- For clear water, glowlight-specific: every fish sold is farm-bred (S1, S19), "Captive-bred specimens will acclimatise to a wide range of aquarium conditions" and thrive "in tap water" (S19); Fishkeeper lists lighting as "No special requirements" (S19).
- General evidence (reused): Rio Negro dissolved organic carbon protected zebrafish against low-pH ion loss (S56), not tested on any tetra; PFK presents blackwater benefits as long-standing association, and notes water changes dilute the color (S55). Mycobacteria favor acidic, humic-rich water (S49). Congener fungal case traced to wood and plants (S32).
- Where the sources agree: tannins are optional for this farmed species; acidic, dim, tinted water brings out color (S1, S19). No study tests glowlight welfare in stained vs clear water.

**Schooling** (glowlight-specific, peer-reviewed, the strongest new material)
- The school shares danger information. When one tetra detects alarm pheromone it fin flicks, and "The test shoals showed a significant increase in antipredator behaviour in response to fin-flicking stimulus tetras, but not to stimulus tetras that did not fin-flick." Fin flicking also deters predators: "Predators remained further away from the stimulus tetra, took longer to initiate a bite and displayed fewer bites towards tetras that fin-flicked." (S20)
- Glowlights inspect predators as a group, and inspecting teaches them. "Only tetras that were initially exposed to the tetra-diet cue and that had inspected the predator acquired the visual recognition of a convict cichlid as a predation threat." With the cue of a predator fed tetras, they inspected later, from farther away, and "in smaller groups" (S21, S22).
- Weak cues sharpen attention: a subthreshold alarm cue alone produced no response, but paired with the sight of an alarmed tankmate the response was as strong as to a full cue (S23, fetch-tool paraphrase).
- Shoal cohesion and vertical area use are the standard measures of glowlight alarm (S24, search result only).
- Reading for the guide (owner review): a bigger school spreads vigilance, and a school that bunches and sinks is showing an alarm response; the studies are about predation, not tank welfare, and none tests group size.
- Group size itself: 6 minimum, 10 or more (S1, S19).

**Cover**
- Plants and wood: "Glowlights appreciate the cover of aquatic plants" (S19); heavily planted looks best, spartan looks washed out (S1); "sometimes slightly shy" (S19).

**Feeding as enrichment**
- Small live and frozen foods "For the best condition and colours" (S1). Disease caveats in X2 (S44, S57). No study on foraging enrichment for glowlights.

**Fin nipping and tankmates**
- Not a black skirt issue here. No strong source either way: S1 and S19 do not mention nipping; S10 says glowlights leave flowing fins alone; S11 (lowest tier) says males nip each other harmlessly. Forum and a Petco vet Q&A (not opened, 520) report a glowlight nipping a betta. Stays a gap.
- Dither role: "a good dither fish for dwarf cichlids in larger aquaria" (S19).
