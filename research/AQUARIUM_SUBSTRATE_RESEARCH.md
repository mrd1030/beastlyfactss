## Part 1. General topics

### G1. Sand vs gravel, and grain size

- Both work; they differ in where the dirt goes. "more dirt may get trapped in the substrate if you use a larger grain size" and "very fine substrates, such as sand, don't allow much dirt to be trapped, so detritus tends to sit on top, where it can be sucked into the filter or syphoned off." (SB1)
- "Coarse substrates like gravel are easier to clean, but provide a poor medium for plants." "Fine substrates like silver sand are great for plant roots, but are more prone to turning anaerobic." (SB4)
- Gravel grain for planted tanks (manufacturer): "Different kinds and colours of gravel with a grain size from 0,8-4 mm will ensure a good water circulation" (about 0.03 to 0.16 in). (SB33) Aquasoil grain "2-3 mm". (SB34)
- Retailer figures (WEAK): "A common gravel grain size is between 2 and 6 mm, which covers most aquarium biotopes," gravel over 12 mm seldom used. (SB70) Discus.com for a planted discus tank: "Between three and eight millimeters is best." (SB44)
- The Merck pet-owner page leaves grain size to the species: "The size of the gravel you use depends on the types of fish you keep." "Some species like to sift fine gravel through their gills," while "others can get gravel stuck in their mouths or tend to swallow gravel easily." "Check with your veterinarian or an experienced fish keeper about what size gravel is best for your tank." (SB9)
- Texture: "Incorrect substrate size and texture can damage fish barbels and skin from repeated contact." "Incorrectly sized gravel can get lodged in the mouths of substrate scooping fish." (SB4)
- Fine natural gravel "Can harm catfish bristles and burrowing species"; colored gravels: "Coarse grains will affect catfish and burrowing fish." Planting substrates: "Useless for burrowing catfish or excessively dirty tanks." (SB5, Nathan Hill)
- Fish preference, the one controlled data point (zebrafish only): "The strongest preference was for gravel substrate and images of gravel attached to the bottom of the tank." Any combination with gravel was preferred over any with sand (p<0.05), and groups preferred "all substrate (p<0.01) and plant (p<0.05) enrichments over barren conditions." (SB13)
- Sand drawbacks: silver sand "Cannot be used for deep substrates as it can turn anaerobic" and "Strong filter flows may move it, leaving craters and sand drifts." "Many plant roots love it." (SB5) Discus.com (WEAK): sand "can compact, which will make it difficult for roots to grow into it," and "If the sand compacts, water circulation is reduced and pockets of anaerobic bacteria can develop." (SB44)
- No source gives a millimeter spec for "aquarium sand". See Unsourced.

### G2. Depth

Sources split by purpose. The figures:
- PFK FAQ: "For sand, most people go for a depth of around 2.5cm/1", but with gravel the norm is to go for a deeper layer of say 5cm/2" or more." Sand "tends to stagnate if used in a deep layer. About 2.5cm/1" should be plenty in most cases." (SB1)
- PFK Diploma (maximums): "Deeper substrates harbour more waste and are likelier to turn anaerobic than shallow ones." "At the maximum, coarse substrates should generally be no deeper than 5cm in a community tank." "For fine substrates, no deeper than 2.5cm substrate should be used." Exception: "aquascapes, where deep beds of highly porous planting substrate are used". (SB4) So: gravel up to about 2 in, sand up to about 1 in, deeper only for porous planting substrates.
- Planted: "Planted tanks usually require at least 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) of substrate." (SB35, WEAK) Inert gravel for plants: "Use a 7.5cm layer of any inert aquarium gravel or grit" (about 3 in). (SB3) Tropica: gravel "at least 3-4 cm in the front and 6-8 cm in the back" (about 1.2 to 1.6 in front, 2.4 to 3.1 in back) over "a layer of ½-1 cm substrate" of nutrient base. (SB33) Tropica Aquarium Soil worked example: about 8 cm (3.1 in). (SB34) Discus.com (WEAK): "Two or three inches is a good rule of thumb." (SB44)
- Vet-written pet care sheets: goldfish "1 to 2 inches of freshwater sand, gravel, or pebbles" (SB21); tetras "1 to 2 inches of sand, gravel, or pebbles" (SB22).
- Corydoras-specific: Ian Fuller: "fine smooth gravel or a course grained smooth sand, to a maximum depth of 15 millimeters" (about 0.6 in), because "this allows the catfishes to reach through to the base glass of the tank". (SB28) See species section.
- Slope: Merck: "Creating a slope to the gravel can also make the tank appear to be deeper front to back." (SB9) Tropica slopes front to back (SB33).
- Reading for writers: a thin bed (about 1 in of sand, up to 2 in of gravel) for an unplanted or epiphyte tank; 2 to 3 in where plants root into the floor. Depth past that is where the anaerobic warnings (G6) start, except for porous planting soils.

### G3. How much substrate per tank: the formulas

- Merck (veterinary): "Usually, gravel is placed in a tank at a proportion of 1 pound of gravel per 1 gallon of water." (SB9)
- PetMD (DVM-written, two sheets): "Aquariums need about 1.5 pounds of substrate for each gallon of water in the tank." "For example, a 10-gallon tank will require about 15 pounds of substrate to create a 1- to 2-inch layer." (SB21) Same rule on the tetra sheet: "use about 1½ pounds of substrate for each gallon of water in the tank." (SB22)
- Discus.com (WEAK): "If you want a 1 inch deep base: For every 1 gallon of water use 1 pound of substrate." "If you want a 2 inch deep base: For every 1 gallon of water use 2 pounds of substrate." (SB43; the page puts a dash where the colon is)
- Manufacturer method: Seachem gives no per-gallon rule. Its "Project Calculator" asks for "Desired substrate depth," aquarium width and length and returns bags (7 kg or 3.5 kg). (SB31, SB32) That is a footprint method, not a gallons method.
- Note for writers: pounds-per-gallon rules ignore tank shape. A tall tank has more gallons over the same floor, so the same rule gives it a deeper bed. The footprint method (length x width x depth) is what the manufacturer uses. My arithmetic, with standard tank dimensions that were not sourced this session: a 20 x 10 in floor (10 gallon) holds 200 cubic inches per inch of depth, about 20 per gallon; a 48 x 13 in floor (55 gallon) holds 624, about 11 per gallon. See Unsourced.
- Conflict between sources: 1 lb per gallon (Merck) vs 1.5 lb per gallon (PetMD). Not large; both are rules of thumb.

### G4. Bare-bottom tanks: when and why

- Cleaning and visibility: "A bare-based aquarium can be easier to keep clean, as any accumulation of solid waste will be visibly obvious." "It can also be easier to extract dormant pathogens on the base in the event of a disease outbreak." "This type of system is often used in hospital and quarantine tanks." (SB4)
- Substrate hides waste and pathogens: "Substrate can conceal waste like fish faeces and uneaten food" and "Substrates may harbour disease causing parasites and pathogens, making them difficult to remove." (SB4)
- Peer-reviewed (zebrafish, 25 L tanks, 2 cm gravel or sand vs bare, two 7-week trials): substrate "can also harbour elevated levels of waste products and unwanted bacteria," "the potential for pathogenic bacteria and fungi to grow on the increased surface area," and "Substrate was also associated with a greater Pseudomonas presence" (clear in the summer trial; tap water masked it in winter). "Overall, the addition of substrate as enrichment resulted in increased pH and nitrate and a greater presence of bacteria." The paper makes no recommendation for or against bare tanks. Its ammonia statements contradict each other (abstract says TAN rose with substrate; discussion says gravel and sand "reduced TAN compared to the control"). (SB12)
- The welfare side: zebrafish in groups preferred any substrate or plants "over barren conditions," and "zebrafish reared in barren conditions preferred structural enrichment over standard conditions." (SB13) In a research facility, egg numbers did not differ, but "the inclusion of gravel-type enrichment has a statistically significant effect on the quality of clutch," "resulting in a higher proportion of viable eggs." (SB14)
- Substrate as biofilter surface: goldfish "can survive in a 'bare bottom' tank without any substrate," but substrate enriches the tank and gives beneficial bacteria more surface area (paraphrase). (SB21) SB12 reports "increased nitrification in the presence of gravel, and potentially sand substrates".
- Quarantine and hospital tanks: Merck's quarantine build is "an inexpensive 10-gal. tank," with a "sponge filter, small aeration pump, and heater"; "Quarantine systems should be completely broken down and disinfected between uses." (SB11, SB10) Merck does not use the word "bare", but nothing in its build has a substrate; PFK names quarantine and hospital tanks as where bare bases are used (SB4).
- Discus: see species section (SB43). Breeding tanks for egg scatterers use a mesh base instead of substrate (SB50, SB53, SB55).
- Corydoras exception: Seriously Fish says breeding groups "seem to be less susceptible to disease when kept over a thin layer of sand, rather than in a bare-bottomed setup." (SB26, SB27)
- Optional for some species: PetMD betta supply list: "Substrate (optional)" (SB23). Angelfish "can be kept in a wide variety of setups, such as bare tanks, community tanks, and planted tanks." (SB64; the page has a dash where the comma is)
- Reading: bare is the default for quarantine, hospital, grow-out and breeding tanks (cleaning, visibility, disinfection). A display tank gets a substrate for welfare, plants and bacteria, at the cost of keeping it clean.

### G5. Gravel swallowing, lodged gravel, and impaction (goldfish and large cichlids)

- Mechanism: "Physical blockages occur in 'grubbing' fish like goldfish, which have evolved to take in mouthfuls of sand, silt and mud." "When placed into a tank with pea gravel, it may be swallowed, blocking the oesophagus." "Physical blockages will require a vet, as it is likely you'll damage the fish trying to remove any obstruction." "Avoid this by using either very fine substrates, or substrates larger than fish can ingest." (SB6)
- Lodged in the mouth (fish vet): goldfish forage by sucking up sand or gravel and "these are expelled unaided by the fish after several minutes"; lodging is rare because of the object's "relative size and shape"; it is often "dislodged and expelled before they reach the clinic"; "Sedation is usually required to allow large pieces of gravel to be grasped and carefully removed with forceps," "to avoid damaging the delicate lining of the mouth." Gravel problems are seen most in goldfish kept indoors. (SB24)
- Goldfish substrate: "the substrate should ideally be fine grit, very small rounded gravel or even sand" "as goldfish like to dig in the bottom for morsels of food." (SB7) PetMD: "Avoid gravel that's small enough for the fish to swallow." (SB21)
- The 1/2 inch figure: only SB25 (WEAK): "a good rule of thumb is to use gravel that is at least 1/2 inch in diameter," "Goldfish can sift through sand without ingesting harmful amounts," "A bare bottom tank eliminates the risk of gravel ingestion altogether." No stronger source gives a number.
- Merck lists swallowing as a species trait without naming species (SB9, quoted in G1).
- Large cichlids (oscar): no veterinary or peer-reviewed source found. Searches returned only forums and retailer blogs (oscars picking up and holding pebbles; advice to avoid gravel they could swallow). Seriously Fish: the oscar "has a tendency to dig into the substrate" and suggests "a soft, sandy substrate" (SB47). Impaction in oscars stays unsourced.
- No peer-reviewed fish gravel-impaction case report was found (searches returned turtle, horse and human cases only).

### G6. Depth, anaerobic pockets and hydrogen sulfide: is the "gas pocket" danger supported?

Verdict: supported in a narrow form. Hydrogen sulfide forms fast in thin, oxygen-starved layers of accumulated organic waste, and disturbing such a layer can release a toxic pulse. No source shows that an ordinary 1 to 2 inch aquarium bed that is cleaned routinely produces fish-killing gas. The danger tracks trapped waste, depth and neglect more than substrate type.
- Peer-reviewed (aquaculture waste, freshwater and marine): "H2S appeared only within one centimeter of the accumulated fish organic waste after oxygen was consumed," at "fine spatial (millimeter) and temporal (hourly) scales," and when disturbed it "was rapidly released into the water column and reached concentrations above the toxicity threshold for fish." The authors stress minimizing organic matter buildup. (SB15, abstract)
- Mechanism and moderators (Boyd): "anaerobic conditions are restricted to their sediments"; an aerobic layer at the sediment-water interface oxidizes sulfide, and "The absence of an aerobic layer at the sediment-water interface favors movement of sulfide into the water column." "Hydrogen sulfide is extremely toxic to fish." The toxic H2S share rises as pH falls: at 16°C about 12% at pH 8.0 and about 93% at pH 6.0 (from his table). "High concentrations of hydrogen sulfide are more likely in brackish water or seawater ponds," which hold more sulfate than freshwater. (SB16) Note the pH point: a soft, acidic tank (tetras, discus) would hold more of any sulfide as the toxic form; my inference from SB16.
- Aquarium-specific: "Deeper substrates harbour more waste and are likelier to turn anaerobic than shallow ones." "Deep sand can turn anaerobic without adequate passage of oxygen." (SB4) Silver sand "Cannot be used for deep substrates as it can turn anaerobic." (SB5) Sand "tends to stagnate if used in a deep layer." (SB1)
- Ponds (fish vet): gravel "deeper than 3 inches, you run the risk of anaerobic bacteria production"; "This can create pockets of hydrogen sulfide that can kill fish if mixed into the water supply." "Deep pockets of muck, if left undisturbed for long periods of time, can start to culture anaerobic bacteria." Stirring "will bring oxygen deep into the cracks and crevices, preventing anaerobic bacteria formation." (SB18)
- Capped soil: under a sand cap "escaping gases cause the dirt and sand to explode to the surface, making the water very cloudy and muddy"; gravel "allows the gases to escape more easily." (SB36, WEAK)
- Contrarian (WEAK, search result only, page did not load): aquariumscience.org "Anaerobic myth": "Poisonous Quantities of Hydrogen Sulfide gas are NEVER produced in ANY aquarium." Unsigned hobby site; no studies seen. Listed for completeness, not for citing.
- Malaysian trumpet snails as substrate stirrers: forum claims only. Unsourced.

### G7. Buffering substrates for hard-water fish: crushed coral, aragonite, coral sand

What they do:
- "Inert substrates and rocks don't alter the water chemistry, while non-inert, calcareous substrates do. These contain calcium and other minerals that increase the hardness and pH." Coral sand: use it only to "make your water rock hard and increase the pH of your water to over 8.0"; "Coral sand and gravel is calcareous, so you should only use it in situations where you want the pH and hardness to be kept high." (SB1)
- "Slow release of pH buffering minerals into the water (such as with coral sand)." Risk: "Excess mineral release into softwater (such as with coral sand in extremely acidic tanks)." (SB4)
- Chemistry (university extension, ponds): "Calcite (calcium carbonate) and dolomite (a combination of calcium and magnesium carbonates) both increase the total hardness (the calcium and magnesium concentration) and the total alkalinity (the carbonate and bicarbonate concentration) of water." "Calcite and dolomite increase the total hardness, total alkalinity, and pH, and act as a buffer to keep the pH constant." "Reaction time and solubility of limestone are directly related to the particle size." "Pond water with total alkalinity less than 20 ppm can benefit from liming." And never hydrated lime with fish: it raises pH "quickly and dramatically above tolerable levels". (SB17)
- So crushed coral and aragonite (calcium carbonate) add calcium but not magnesium; dolomite adds both (SB17). Relevant to mollies, where Seriously Fish says it is "the 'hard' minerals (calcium, magnesium)" that matter (SB59).
- Manufacturer directions (own product): "We use 1 pound of crushed coral per 10 gallons of water when mixed in with our gravel." In a filter, "smaller amounts" replaced more often. "The lower your pH is, the faster it dissolves." "As the pH rises, it slows down how fast the coral dissolves." It "significantly boosts KH" and adds some GH. Replacement: in the substrate "every 1 to 2 years, depending on how low the pH of the tap water is"; as filter media "after 1 to 3 months." Rinse until the water runs clear. Test pH and KH to know when to add more. (SB30; the page's en-dash ranges written with "to")
- Peer-reviewed side note: even non-calcareous gravel and silica sand raised pH in the zebrafish trial ("Addition of both gravel and sand substrate resulted in increased pH"). (SB12)
- When appropriate: hard-water species (guppy 8 to 30 dGH, platy 10 to 30, molly 15 to 30, swordtail 10 to 25; SB60, SB61, SB59, SB62) kept on soft tap water. Never in a soft-water tetra, discus or cardinal tank: "Inert substrates are of particular importance to those keeping fishes that like soft water, such as Discus." (SB1)
- A self-limiting pH ceiling (around 8.0 to 8.3) for aragonite and crushed coral: blogs and forums only, figures disagree. Unsourced.
- Aragonite cichlid sands (CaribSea Eco-Complete African Cichlid): retailer copy only in search results ("contain aragonite to maintain the high pH African Cichlids need"). No African cichlids on the site; not cited.

### G8. Calcareous rock, coated gravel, and the vinegar test (from the ember guide's general material)

- Pea gravel: "Even standard pea gravel contains sufficient calcium to boost the pH and hardness, so don't use it unless you want hard water." (SB1)
- Coated gravel: "Many coloured gravels, particularly the rough textured angular ones with a powdery colour coating, are made from white dolomite," "a naturally occurring mineral rich in calcium and magnesium," so it "wouldn't be a great choice at all if you want to keep fish that like neutral or soft water." (SB1) Also: "Artificially coloured gravel is often made from dolomite and then coated in paint and/or resin." (SB4)
- Rock: "Limestone and other calcareous rocks should be avoided by keepers of softwater aquaria." "Like seashells, these can leach calcium carbonate into the water". Vinegar: "Test for calcium carbonate by applying acid to the suspected rock to look for any fizzing." "Vinegar is often used for this purpose". Dragon stone shown as inert. "Soft water aquarists would also do well to avoid rocks with veins." (SB2) University outreach: limestone in vinegar shows "the fizzing and the bubbles being released." (SB40)
- Beach material (vet): avoid "items like shells/coral/sand collected from beaches that can alter pH." (SB23)
- Manufacturer inert claims: Flourite "is not chemically coated or treated and will not alter the pH of the water"; Flourite Black Sand "will not alter the pH of the water." (SB31, SB32)

### G9. Planted floors: inert plus root tabs, capped soil, aquasoil (the ember table)

- Inert plus root tabs: "insert root tab fertilizers into the ground if you are using inert substrate" (SB35, WEAK; Cryptocoryne and swords named in the search snippet only). Seachem on Flourite: Flourish Tabs "can be useful and helpful, but are not necessities"; "Gravel modifiers such as laterite are not necessary"; Flourite "is good for the life of the aquarium and need not be replaced." (SB31) Inert gravel plus liquid fertilizer, no planting substrate, also works (SB3).
- Capped soil: "only adding a 1/8 to 1/4-inch of dirt (just enough to barely cover the tank bottom)" then "at least a 2-inch cap of gravel to prevent the dirt from leaking into the water column." Gravel not sand as the cap (gas, see G6). Drawbacks: "Makes a huge cloudy mess if you move plants or have bottom dwellers that dig"; "Can lead to toxic ammonia spikes or unsightly algae growth". (SB36, WEAK) Pond soil or fertilizer bases "topped with gravel or grit." (SB3) Tropica's own method is a nutrient base under gravel (SB33).
- Aquasoil: Tropica: "Aquarium Soil is also a substrate that lowers pH and slightly affects water chemistry"; change "25-50% of the water at least twice a week during the first 4 weeks." (SB34, manufacturer) ADA Amazonia (WEAK): "originally acidic, with a pH level of 5.0-6.0, and it makes water more acidic"; "a large amount of ammonium (NH4) leaches into water"; add fish "two to three weeks after planting"; usable "for two to three years." (SB37) Amazonia Ver.2: pH "5.5 - 6.5" and "Helps to lower the hardness level of the water" (SB38). Tropica soil: "slightly lowers the pH"; soils "release larger amounts of ammonium in the beginning" (SB39).
- Planting substrate and nutrients (Hill): "Planting substrates are mainly designed to trap and slowly release nutrients to plant roots"; disturbing them can release nutrients into the water and "lead to an outbreak of algae." (SB5) "Planting substrates may initially contain a source of ammonia" (SB4).

(SB67, WEAK) "Bogwood and Mopani are notorious for leaching tannic acid into water." (SB2)

### G11. Cleaning

- Rinse first: most substrates "are very dusty and need to be cleaned thoroughly before they're used, otherwise the tank will turn extremely cloudy," "Powder-coated coloured gravels are a particular irritation here" (SB1). Merck: rinse "to remove any accumulated dust"; "Dust can prematurely clog filters and damage water pumps." (SB9) Sand: "Place around 5 to 8cm depth in a bucket at a time, and stir continuously and vigorously while flushing with cold water." "Don't try putting it in a sieve as you'll lose the lot!" (SB5)
- Routine (vet): "Cleanings should include stirring up the gravel or other substrate to release solid wastes that have been trapped"; every 2 weeks, "Stir top of substrate and remove debris." (SB10) Betta weekly: "siphon substrate with a gravel vacuum; change 15 to 25% of the water." (SB23)
- Gravel: vacuum "at least every two weeks, though weekly is considerably better"; "Improper cleaning will lead to nitrate spikes and disease hotbeds." (SB5) "you should be able to vacuum most of the tank floor while only removing about 20-30% of the aquarium water". (SB1) White gravel "only lasts one or two days before needing syphoning again." (SB5)
- Sand technique: "I like to gently rake my fingers through silver sand on a weekly basis"; "Then using a syphon hose, I skim just above the surface of the sand, removing the deposits." A gravel cleaner "will just lift the sand out of the tank"; "limit this to 25% of the total sand, in order not to disrupt filtration." (SB5) Retailer method: "you can periodically crimp the hose"; "vacuum the substrate back and forth in rows, as if you're mowing the lawn"; "clean about a third of the aquarium substrate" per change. (SB42, WEAK) Sand loss: "you'll need to top it up occasionally." (SB1)
- This resolves the ember file's unsourced "hover above the sand" item: SB5 is a source for skimming above sand.
- Planted beds: a gravel cleaner over "a costly planting substrate, such as laterite" risks "sucking it out" (SB1); "Some aquascapers suggest removing a section of the substrate every few weeks or months, and cleaning before replacing" (SB5); "Never leave the roots of plants behind when extracting them, as they'll decompose and churn out nitrates." "Rather than pulling plants out, try digging them out." (SB5)
- Use a net for uneaten food "rather than letting it settle on the base." (SB5)
- Undergravel filters: Merck describes the system, in which "the gravel acts as the filter medium" (SB9). Seriously Fish for every cory: "don't use undergravel filtration" (SB26, SB27).
- Mouth-siphoning: SB42 suggests it as a fallback to start a siphon. The fish tank granuloma risk is not tied to siphoning by any source opened (see Unsourced; the ember file lists the same gap).

### G12. Substrate color

- Tetras (vet-written): "Dark-colored, sandy substrates can make tetras' bright colors pop!" (SB22)
- Neon and cardinal: colors become "lighter in bright conditions with pale substrate or background and clear water" (SB56, SB57). Ember, zebra danio, swordtail: best "with a dark substrate" (SB58, SB50, SB62).
- Stress: in food fish, "black tank color increases stress sensitivity, at least in those fish species studied so far" (SB69). So a dark floor is a color effect; no source shows it lowers stress in aquarium fish.
- Bleher on corys: "only fine silica sand, ideally white or beige, but never black or any other colour." (SB8, no reason or data given on the page)

### G13. Corydoras barbel erosion and substrate: what the evidence shows

Verdict: contested, and unresolved by any controlled study. No peer-reviewed or veterinary source was found that tests what causes barbel loss in Corydoras. Both common explanations rest on expert opinion.
- Sharp grain (expert opinion, no data): Bleher: corys have a "specialised head and snout structure adapted for feeding in a fine substrate" and gravel "can damage their barbels," leading to "infection and loss of their barbels." (SB8) Fuller: "Any sharp edged sand or gravel can very quickly damage the delicate barbels," "this damage in turn can lead to bacterial infection and fugal attack which may in turn be fatal." (SB28) PFK Diploma: incorrect size and texture "can damage fish barbels and skin from repeated contact." (SB4) Hill: fine gravel "Can harm catfish bristles". (SB5)
- Dirty substrate (husbandry reference, no data): "These cats are sensitive to poorly-maintained or dirty substrates and can lose their barbels if kept in poor conditions"; "they can develop nasty infections of the barbels if kept in dirty or otherwise unfavourable conditions." (SB26, SB27)
- Against the sharp-grain story (WEAK): "In the wild, corydoras can be found on sharp substrate, so if their barbels start to erode, it may be caused by other factors like poor water quality." (SB29)
- The bacterial reservoir paper (SB12) studied zebrafish, not corys, and does not mention barbels. It shows more Pseudomonas with gravel or sand than bare (summer trial). It supports "keep substrate clean"; it does not show a cause of barbel erosion.
- Regrowth: amputation studies in other catfish and zebrafish (search result only) show fast regrowth; no abrasion study found.
- Safe framing for the guide: fine, smooth sand is the consensus choice (SB8, SB26, SB27, SB28, SB29), and keeping it clean is equally emphasized (SB26, SB27). No study settles which matters more.

### G14. Koi pond bottoms

- Bare liner (fish vet): "A liner bottom is one of the easiest substrates to keep clean." "They work well with a bottom drain." "Yes, a plain bottom is aesthetically unpleasing, but a bare bottom pond is easiest for most owners to clean." (SB18)
- Gravel and small rocks (fish vet): "You will need to stir the gravel/small rocks frequently to keep them from cementing together." "A pond vacuum will be of little use and will keep collecting all these small stones." "This substrate collects lots of debris and is hard to clean." Over 3 inches deep risks anaerobic bacteria and hydrogen sulfide (G6). (SB18)
- Large rocks: the vacuum "will work slightly better if the substrate is too large to fit up the hose"; "Multiple layers of stones will be more likely to collect debris than only one layer of rocks"; "Adding rocks on top of a liner, either sheet plastic or sprayed, will erode through the liner faster". (SB18)
- Muck: disturbed anaerobic muck can "release hydrogen sulfide"; "get your fish out of the way first!" (SB18)
- Trade magazine: "Rocks and pebbles in the bottom of the pond are bad for circulation." "The mere ability to vacuum the floor is reason enough to leave the deepest floor barren, without rock." "as the sludge falls into the crevices between the rocks, it stops the water flow." "Without water flow between the rocks, there is no oxygen." Bottom drains direct flow and remove waste; floor jets are "a good second option." (SB19)
- Vet-written care sheet: underlayment, then liner, then "A shallow layer of smooth-sided pebbles, rocks, or gravel are all suitable choices." Daily removal of debris and food with a net or skimmer; skimmers checked weekly; water changes "(no more than 10 to 25% of the pond's total water volume) every two to four weeks." (SB20)
- Merck: a pond hole "ideally lined with sand, both to act as a cushion and to help prevent frost heave" (under the liner, not as a substrate). (SB9)
- Liming a pond is a pond-chemistry tool, not a substrate (SB17); never hydrated lime with fish.

Guide vs source:
1. oscar-fish-tank-setup-guide.mdx: "smooth, pea-sized or larger gravel" vs SB6 (pea gravel swallowed by grubbing fish, blocking the oesophagus) and the site's own goldfish guide ("Small pea gravel ... is the size that causes trouble"). Seriously Fish suggests sand (SB47). The two guides contradict each other.
2. corydoras-catfish-tank-setup-guide.mdx: "about 2 inches deep" vs Fuller's 15 mm maximum (SB28) and PFK's 2.5 cm maximum for fine substrates (SB4).
3. corydoras-catfish-tank-setup-guide.mdx: "manufacturers flag some sharp-grained options as unsuitable for bottom-dwellers like cories": no manufacturer page found. Unverified.
4. corydoras-catfish-enrichment-guide.mdx: "Sharp substrate is a secondary factor. Not ideal, and not the primary cause" and "a corydoras on gravel is a corydoras that cannot do its characteristic behavior": stated more firmly than any source. Bleher, Fuller and PFK hold the sharp-grain view (SB8, SB28, SB4), Seriously Fish the dirty-substrate view (SB26, SB27); fine gravel is allowed by SB26, SB27, SB29. No study settles it (G13).
5. Cory guides citing Vanderzwalmen (SB12): accurate, but the study animals were zebrafish and the Pseudomonas difference showed in the summer trial only. The guides do not say so.
6. angelfish-tank-setup-guide.mdx: "delicate fins that tear easily against rough substrate": no source; a midwater fish (SB49).
7. bristlenose-pleco-tank-setup-guide.mdx: "sharp substrate can injure the soft underbelly and barbels": only general PFK lines (SB4, SB5); species page says décor "largely unimportant" (SB48).
8. koi-tank-setup-guide.mdx: "pump damage" and "injure mouths and gills" unsourced; the rest is supported (SB18, SB19, SB20).

10. swordtail-tank-setup-guide.mdx: keeps the unsourced fin line and omits Seriously Fish's sourced "dark substrate" (SB62).
11. guppy, molly, platy, swordtail tank guides: "Fine gravel or sand ... Avoid sharp or coarse gravel, which can damage fins" remains unsourced (already on their kept lists). The crushed coral lines now have sources (SB30, SB17). "mineral block" (guppy) still has none.
12. discus-tank-setup-guide.mdx: no contradiction; no calcareous-substrate warning although SB1 names discus as the fish it matters most for.

Source vs source (for the writer to resolve, not guide errors):
13. Substrate quantity: 1 lb per gallon (Merck SB9) vs 1.5 lb per gallon (PetMD SB21, SB22) vs 1 lb per gallon per inch (Discus.com SB43, WEAK) vs a footprint calculator (Seachem SB31).
